I'm working on a term paper for my philosophy of action seminar, and I'm trying to figure out whether hyperbolic discounting actually dissolves the problem of akrasia or just gives it a different name. The standard philosophical analysis treats weakness of will as a failure of practical reasoning: an agent judges that option A is best, all things considered, yet chooses option B. Meanwhile, hyperbolic discount models predict that preference orderings over intertemporal rewards will reverse as the delay to the smaller reward shrinks — so the agent who prefers the larger-later reward at t1 and the smaller-sooner reward at t2 isn't contradicting a stable ranking; she's just expressing different temporal slices of a non-exponential discount function. If that's correct, then it looks like there's no single "better judgment" being overridden, which suggests akrasia isn't a rational failure but a predictable feature of preference dynamics. To save the philosophical concept, it seems you'd need to invoke some kind of cross-temporal authority — a master preference for linear consistency or a unified diachronic self — but that strikes me as an unexplained normative postulate rather than an empirical finding. So my question is: do philosophers who study akrasia reject the transitivity failure implied by hyperbolic discounting, or do they accept the dynamics but insist that rational evaluation applies to the agent's long-horizon curve rather than to her local temporal parts? And if it's the latter, isn't that normative privileging of one curve over another just a presupposition that the model itself can't justify? I want to be careful not to conflate the mechanism with the normative evaluation, but I'm genuinely unsure where the boundary is drawn in the literature.
