After a long argument last night with a friend about higher-order evidence and epistemic akrasia, I find myself holding the following package of views, and I want to know whether they actually cohere.

First, I hold that epistemic akrasia—believing P while judging that one's total evidence does not support P—is always irrational; rational belief must align with one's all-things-considered assessment of the evidence.

Second, I think that in cases of peer disagreement with someone I regard as equally competent and well-informed, I should often suspend judgment or split the difference, even when I can fully trace and reject the specific errors in my peer's reasoning. The bare fact of disagreement functions as higher-order evidence about my own reliability, and I should not treat my confidence in my error-detection as immune from that symmetry-breaking pressure.

Third, if I learn that my belief in some proposition was produced by a recognized cognitive bias—say, motivated reasoning or an availability cascade—and I have no independent mechanism for correcting that bias, I should reduce my confidence in the proposition, regardless of whether the particular arguments I originally advanced for it still appear intact.

Fourth, I want to resist what I take to be an illegitimate bootstrapping move: I should not discount my belief-forming processes solely on the basis of higher-order evidence that is delivered by those very processes themselves. If the only access I have to the claim that I am unreliable in domain D is through the cognitive machinery whose reliability is in question, that doubt undermines itself and should not be treated as legitimate grounds for revision.

Fifth, I think rational belief assessment should be invariant under the chronological ordering of considerations: if the same body of first-order and higher-order evidence is available, it should not matter whether I happen to think about my own fallibility before or after I evaluate the arguments for P.

My friend insisted that (2) and (4) pull in opposite directions—one demands that I take disagreement seriously as external higher-order evidence, the other blocks internal self-doubt—but I am not sure that is a real conflict rather than an apparent one. She also suggested that (1) and (3) together commit me to a form of akrasia in disagreement cases, since (2) seems to require me to withhold belief even when my all-things-considered judgment (including the disagreement) still favors P. Meanwhile, (3) and (4) seem to leave me with almost no legitimate route to self-correction: (3) demands revision upon bias detection, but (4) makes such detection rarely legitimate unless it comes from outside the system, which in introspective cases it rarely does.

So: is this a consistent package? Which commitments genuinely conflict, which tensions dissolve on closer inspection, and what is the cheapest way to restore coherence—what does it cost me? I am not signaling which view I am prepared to abandon; I want an audit of what commits me to what.
