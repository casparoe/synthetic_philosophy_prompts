I am a high school senior in an advanced ethics and public policy seminar, and for my final paper I am trying to defend a specific position about welfare conditionality that goes slightly beyond the usual debates over workfare and benefit fraud. Rather than claiming that unconditional transfers encourage free-riding or that the "undeserving poor" lack moral virtue, I want to argue that the central problem is what I call "reciprocal invisibility": the state's structural inability to observe, in anything close to real time, whether a beneficiary is engaging in reciprocal social contribution. On this view, strict workfare rules, job-search logging, and behavioral conditions are not primarily punitive or disciplinary mechanisms; they serve an epistemic function, correcting an information asymmetry by making reciprocal effort visible and verifiable to the community that funds the safety net. The undeserving poor, in this framing, are not people with bad characters but people who are epistemically opaque to the social contract, and conditionality is therefore a requirement of transparency rather than of desert.

What I need is an honest map of how exposed this position actually is. What would have to be true for the "reciprocal visibility" defense of welfare conditionality to be wrong? I want you to identify which of its underlying assumptions could fail—perhaps about the state's legitimate claim to transparency, about whether contribution can be measured without distorting the very behaviors being measured, or about whether epistemic access carries any independent moral weight against autonomy—and to specify what the world, or our moral reasoning, would have to look like in each case. Please distinguish evidence or arguments that would be genuinely decisive (for instance, if unconditional transfers produced measurably stronger social cohesion, or if visibility mechanisms systematically misclassified deserving recipients due to structural barriers) from evidence that would merely be administratively awkward or politically unpopular. I also want to know what defenders of this view, including myself, should concede as genuinely counting against it, not merely what critics assert. Does the position risk collapsing into standard paternalism, or into a justification for surveillance-state oversight? Is it well insulated by qualifications, and if so, is that insulation intellectually honest or a defensive retreat? Finally, are there conceptual distinctions or formal considerations—from social choice, information economics, or moral philosophy—that would show this view either collapses into a rival account or makes distinctive predictions that its rivals do not?
