I am a senior in high school and have been asked to deliver the opening remarks at our school's annual art exhibition, titled "The Copy and Its Shadow." The audience will include parents, fellow students, several art teachers, and a visiting conservator who specializes in attribution science; roughly half the room will understand technical terms like provenance and pentimento, and the other half will not. I want the speech to turn on a specific philosophical tension rather than serve as decoration: I am interested in why an original painting retains value over a perfect forgery even when no observer can distinguish them, but I want to press the problem into two less-discussed corners. First, suppose two artists working in complete isolation produce works that are genuinely identical in every visible and material respect, and only one is later recognized as the work of a named master; does the recognition alter the object itself, or only our relation to it? Second, if value tracks causal history—the specific sequence of choices that led to the work—then why do we not treat a natural geological formation that happens to mirror a famous sculpture as art "by accident," and should we? The philosophical framework here needs to do real structural work: it should determine what I am able to say about the student works on display, several of which are deliberate homages to well-known pieces, and several others that are technically conventional but produced through genuinely unconventional processes. The speech should be approximately five minutes, should avoid asserting a definitive resolution that would alienate either the experts or the families, and should not ask the audience to accept any named theory or citation they could not verify on the spot. Please write the speech.
