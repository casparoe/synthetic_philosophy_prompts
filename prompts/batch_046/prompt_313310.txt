Write an essay on the following controversy. In his well-known 1930 essay on economic possibilities for future generations, Keynes predicts that within about a century economic growth will solve the problem of scarcity, allowing humanity to reduce labor to roughly fifteen hours per week. He then asks what people will do with abundant leisure, warning that the love of money may persist as a kind of disgusting morbidity even after it is no longer necessary. The standard interpretation treats Keynes as arguing that material abundance is largely sufficient for the good life, provided we overcome this residual moral corruption. But there is a less discussed tension: Keynes appears to assume that leisure is primarily instrumental—a recovery from labor—rather than autotelic, an activity valued for its own sake. Meanwhile, modern social phenomena, sometimes discussed under the label of workism, suggest that for many people labor has become a primary source of identity, social recognition, and meaning, not merely a means to subsistence.

Your task is to defend one of the following positions, or a qualified intermediate view, and reach an explicit verdict.

Position A: Keynes is essentially right. Once scarcity is eliminated, the main obstacle to the good life is indeed the moral pathology of avarice; if we cure that, leisure will naturally become fulfilling because human beings have innate capacities for culture, friendship, and contemplation that scarcity suppresses. The modern phenomenon of workism is not a deep structural feature of human psychology but a contingent cultural artifact of scarcity-thinking, and it will dissolve as abundance makes labor genuinely optional.

Position B: Keynes's vision fails because it treats the good life as a negative problem—the removal of scarcity and greed—rather than a positive one: the cultivation of practices and institutions that make leisure meaningful. Abundance without prior ethical and aesthetic education does not produce flourishing; it produces boredom, status competition through consumption, or the colonization of leisure by work-like metrics of productivity. The persistence of workism reveals that labor fulfills needs for recognition, narrative coherence, and social contribution that Keynes's model of leisure does not replace. Therefore, reducing work hours is not sufficient and may even be harmful without parallel transformation of education, civic life, and cultural norms.

Weigh the arguments on both sides carefully, but do not end on a non-committal note. You may defend a hybrid verdict—for example, that Keynes is correct about the economic precondition but wrong about the automaticity of the moral transition, and that abundance therefore requires a deliberate politics of leisure—but you must specify exactly what that politics entails and why it is not merely an afterthought to Keynes's economic argument.

In your essay, avoid treating Keynes's argument as a mere historical artifact; use it as a live framework to assess whether economic abundance can ever be sufficient for human flourishing.
