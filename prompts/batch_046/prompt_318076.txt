A philosophy podcast I listened to recently summarized L.A. Paul's argument about transformative experience in roughly the following way. Paul distinguishes revelatory value—the value of discovering what an experience is like—from experience value, the value of actually living through it. For genuinely transformative choices, such as becoming a parent, these two values come apart in a way that undermines standard decision theory. Because the experience is both epistemically transformative, so you do not know what it is like in advance, and personally transformative, so your core preferences will shift, you lack both the phenomenological data and the stable utility function that rational choice requires. The host claimed that Paul therefore concludes deliberation about these choices is necessarily inauthentic: authenticity demands acting on your true preferences, but the preferences that will matter after the transformation do not yet exist, and the ones you have now will be obsolete. The host also said Paul holds that testimony from people who have undergone the transformation is epistemically useless—not merely limited—because without first-person acquaintance you cannot even understand the descriptive terms others use, which makes social norms and expert advice about transformative choices entirely void. Finally, the host concluded that Paul advocates a kind of radical epistemic humility in these cases: you should refuse to assign subjective probabilities to outcomes altogether and make the choice on purely non-cognitive or existential grounds.
