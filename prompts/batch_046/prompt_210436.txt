As a novelist constructing dialogue in which every spoken line carries a submerged political meaning, I find myself returning to Grice's distinction between what is said and what is implicated, yet I am struck by a conceptual difficulty that standard expositions rarely address. Consider a dramatic situation in which a speaker addresses an interlocutor within a scene, calculating that her utterance will generate a specific conversational implicature that the interlocutor will accept as true, while the author simultaneously engineers the scene so that the reader—possessing information the interlocutor lacks—recognizes that the very conditions for that implicature's generation are undermined, and therefore perceives the speaker's intended implicature as false, even as the speaker remains unaware of this failure. The conceptual issue I want explained is this: within Grice's framework of speaker meaning, cooperative principle, and calculability, can an utterance bear two distinct implicatures calibrated to two separate audiences—the in-fiction listener and the extra-fictional reader—without collapsing the speaker's meaning into the author's meaning? More precisely, does Grice's requirement that the speaker intend the hearer to recognize her intention allow for a nested structure in which the primary hearer is meant to recover one implicature, while a secondary audience is meant to recover the speaker's failure to secure that implicature, and if so, how should we describe the speaker's psychological state: is she still cooperating with the cooperative principle relative to her immediate interlocutor, or does the presence of the second audience retroactively alter the maxims governing her utterance? I want a rigorous conceptual explanation—not a bibliography—of how Grice's apparatus handles, or fails to handle, this bifurcated audience structure, and where exactly the boundary between speaker implicature and authorial irony must be drawn.
