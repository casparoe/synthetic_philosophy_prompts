I take the view that the scale argument for assigning serious moral weight to insects—vast numbers multiplied by a low but non-negligible probability of sentience—fails not because insects are certainly non-sentient, but because insect consciousness, if present, is structurally fragmented: it lacks a unified integrative architecture or diachronic subject of experience, so there is no single “who” whose suffering accumulates within an individual over time or across the enormous population. Moral aggregation, on this account, requires a unified subject, and insects provide only distributed, modular affective states. What would have to be true for this to be wrong? I want an honest map of its exposure: which empirical findings about neural integration in insects (e.g., robust cross-modal binding, persistent self-modeling, or global broadcasting analogues) would be decisive; which philosophical arguments about aggregation without unified subjects would be merely awkward rather than fatal; and whether defenders could retreat to a weaker claim—that fragmentation reduces but does not eliminate moral weight—without insulating the view into unfalsifiability. Please distinguish what critics would cite from what proponents of this fragmentation thesis should themselves concede as damaging, rank the defeaters from fatal to cosmetic, and assess whether analogies to human split-brain or severe dissociative cases support or undermine the claim that unified subjecthood is necessary for moral aggregation. In particular, say what this position predicts that its rivals do not, and whether it currently risks anything substantive or has already been qualified into safety.
