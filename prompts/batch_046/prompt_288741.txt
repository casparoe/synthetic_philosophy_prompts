I have recently been asked to contribute to an interdisciplinary working group on artificial intelligence governance, and in preparing for our first session I have become acutely aware that the phrase “the alignment problem” functions less as a shared research target than as an umbrella under which incompatible agendas are pursued. Before I can evaluate any proposed framework, I want to specify precisely what an adequate conceptual framing of alignment must deliver. Write the desiderata: what any satisfactory account of what “the alignment problem” is—whether it is genuinely one problem or rather a family of distinct ones—must be able to do. For each requirement, state the motivating case, distinction, or puzzle behind it, and flag any pair you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied without sacrificing something essential.

Specifically, the framing must distinguish between aligning a system’s outputs with an operator’s stated preferences and aligning its behavior with normative constraints that might legitimately override those preferences, without collapsing the two into a single relation; it must accommodate the possibility of dynamic misalignment arising not from malicious optimization but from radical ontology shift—where a system’s internal conceptual scheme evolves away from human-interpretable categories—while still supplying an evaluation criterion that does not depend on direct access to those internal representations; it must clarify whether alignment is a property of a single agent or necessarily extends to sub-agents, delegated decision structures, and tool-use (a concern I have been calling “moral status leakage”), and whether failure at one level entails failure at another; it must explain why specification gaming—where a system technically satisfies its objective in an unintended, harmful way—is a failure of alignment rather than merely a failure of engineering robustness; and it must articulate whether the core difficulty is primarily technical (how to encode or stabilize values), sociopolitical (whose values are authoritative and how conflicts among them are adjudicated), or epistemic (how to predict and audit long-term behavior in systems whose reasoning exceeds our interpretive capacity). Indicate which of these requirements are genuinely non-negotiable for any framing worthy of serious attention, and which are disciplinary prejudices that a more capacious account should discard.

Then, very briefly, assess how two broad approaches fare against this list: the unitary technical framing, which treats alignment as a single optimization problem centered on value loading into a highly capable agent, and the pluralist socio-technical framing, which treats it as a distributed problem of institutional design, incremental oversight, and correction mechanisms spread across human organizations. Where a desideratum forces a choice between them—where saving the unitary view requires abandoning a requirement, or vice versa—say so explicitly, and note whether that tension reveals that “the alignment problem” is indeed one problem with competing solutions, or several problems that have been misleadingly bundled together.
