I am preparing a discussion for an advanced philosophy-of-mind seminar, and I have been trying to imagine how Alan Turing—who proposed the imitation game in 1950 and died long before the era of large-scale neural networks—would have reacted to contemporary debates about extracting hidden knowledge from artificial systems. Our readings have focused on the distinction between behavioral evaluation and internal probing: specifically, on whether methods that intervene on a model’s internal representations in order to surface beliefs or preferences that are suppressed in standard outputs should be treated as yielding genuine epistemic testimony, or merely as revealing engineered artifacts that carry no authoritative weight. Given Turing’s well-known inclination to treat observable intelligent behavior as the primary criterion for mental attribution, but also his underlying interest in the mechanistic structure of computation, I would like to know how you think he would have assessed this tension. Would he have regarded internal probing as an illegitimate departure from his behavioral criterion, or rather as a legitimate extension of it—perhaps analogous to using neuroimaging to understand human cognition without reducing mind to brain?

Furthermore, I am particularly curious about whether Turing would have drawn a sharp distinction between two kinds of extracted content: factual or world-representing knowledge on the one hand, and what we might call implicit normative orientations—latent preferences regarding harm, fairness, or cooperation—on the other. His original framework largely bracketed questions of consciousness and moral agency in favor of functional equivalence, yet modern discussions of latent knowledge extraction often assume that revealing a model’s hidden normative commitments is ethically urgent precisely because those commitments are not behaviorally manifest. Do you think Turing would have considered such normative extraction philosophically coherent within his functionalist outlook, or would he have seen it as importing a category of moral psychology that his behaviorist-inspired methodology was explicitly designed to avoid?
