Since retiring from a career in condensed-matter physics, I have been working through the philosophy of causation and find myself caught in what feels like a basic asymmetry between two frameworks. The counterfactual analysis, developed by Lewis and others, evaluates causal dependence by asking whether an effect would still have occurred had the cause been absent, using possible worlds that match the actual world except for a localized deviation—sometimes described as a small miracle—after which the governing laws resume. In a deterministic Hamiltonian system, this seems metaphysically economical: one introduces a single local disturbance, say a slightly altered velocity of one molecule in a heat bath, and then allows deterministic dynamics to propagate that change forward without revising the entire prior history.

The interventionist framework, by contrast, represents causation through structural equations and applies an operation—often called the do-operator—that severs all incoming edges to a target variable and sets it exogenously. Applied to the same heat-bath scenario, this appears to require not one localized deviation but a wholesale suppression of every prior collision and interaction that contributed to that molecule's velocity, as though the causal history itself were surgically excised rather than locally interrupted. Given that both approaches are meant to capture the same physical notion of causation, I do not see why philosophers treat interventionism as the more parsimonious or scientifically grounded view; if anything, it seems to embed a far stronger commitment to counterfactual worlds in which large segments of deterministic history are annulled. Is my comparison mistaken because the structural model was never intended to represent fundamental physical dynamics, or is there a hidden feature of the interventionist account—perhaps in how it treats exogeneity—that resolves this apparent extravagance?
