I work in software engineering without any formal training in philosophy, and I have been trying to model democratic membership as a distributed access-control protocol rather than a static registry. The position I want to examine holds that the demos should be defined recursively: anyone presently subject to collectively binding decisions and capable of revocable, verifiable participation is included, and the boundary can be updated only by a supermajority of existing members who must treat any proposed exclusion as a system failure requiring explicit justification rather than a default design choice. This avoids treating citizenship as a legacy database record, but I need to know how exposed it actually is. What would have to be true for this recursive-consent view of the demos to be wrong? I am not asking for the standard all-affected-interests objection or routine complaints about stateless persons; I want the deeper defeaters. Which concrete cases—temporary workers bound by decades-long infrastructure commitments, future generations locked into present climate decisions, or individuals who reject the verification mechanism itself—would force the view to concede structural error rather than merely deploy an exception handler? Which intuitions about belonging, such as birthright, territorial presence, or shared fate, does it implicitly predict away, and are those predictions fatal or only awkward? If there are formal results in democratic theory or social choice—perhaps showing that non-manipulable boundary rules are impossible, or that recursive membership is inherently unstable—please identify them and explain whether they strike the core mechanism or only its implementation. Rank the defeaters from fatal (the protocol must abandon its recursive structure) to merely awkward (it needs a patch). Also, distinguish which objections a committed defender of this view would themselves concede as genuinely damaging, and which they would dismiss by accusing the critic of importing a non-protocol, communitarian conception of membership. I am particularly interested in whether this framing retains a genuinely distinct vulnerability or whether it collapses into standard liberal inclusionism once all the engineering metaphors are stripped away.
