Question: In The Present Age and related writings, Kierkegaard develops a critique of the modern press and the phenomenon he calls 'the public.' Explain his account of how the press generates this abstraction, why he considers it ethically dangerous, and how it relates to his broader concept of 'levelling.' In your answer, distinguish his view from the claim that modern media merely spread false information.

Student response:
Kierkegaard argues that newspapers create an abstract entity he calls 'the public,' which is not a real gathering but a phantom formed through anonymity. Because writers address no particular reader and readers never meet, opinions circulate without anyone taking personal responsibility for them. This is ethically dangerous because it allows individuals to hold views without the decisive commitment that true ethical existence demands, which is why Kierkegaard concludes that genuine ethical life becomes practically impossible in modern society. The press also drives what he terms 'levelling,' a process by which qualitative distinctions — above all between the excellent individual and the ordinary member — are flattened into quantitative sameness. Specifically, the press achieves this levelling by removing class-based barriers to information, so that social rank no longer determines what one knows and everyone is reduced to the same informational level. Although some readers think Kierkegaard mainly fears that newspapers spread falsehoods, his deeper concern is this structural detachment from personal commitment. Therefore, the public and levelling together show that modern communication threatens ethical seriousness rather than merely corrupting knowledge.
