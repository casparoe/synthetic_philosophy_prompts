I'm a doctoral student in comparative philosophy working on affective normativity in early Chinese thought. I've been trying to map a conceptual tension in Zhuangzi's treatment of death—specifically, whether the metaphysical claim that death is merely one transformation (hua) in an endless process genuinely entails the normative posture that a sage should meet it without grief or fear. The standard interpretation treats this as a straightforward inference: if personal identity is not a fixed substance but a temporary configuration of qi, then death involves no real loss, and mourning is based on a false attachment to permanence. But I worry this collapses two distinct moves—an ontological denial of persistent selfhood and an ethical recommendation of emotional equanimity—that may operate independently, and that commentators too quickly assume the second follows from the first.

Could you explain the conceptual architecture that links, or fails to link, these two claims? In particular, I want to understand whether the sage's acceptance of death is meant to be a logical consequence of transformation-ontology, or whether the affective stance—exemplified in the well-known episode where Zhuangzi drums on a tub rather than mourning his wife's death—functions instead as a practical enactment or discipline that realizes the ontology rather than deriving from it. How does the practice of "fasting the mind" (xinzhai) fit into this picture? Does it offer an independent pathway to equanimity that doesn't require prior metaphysical commitment, or is it inseparable from the view that life and death are continuous phases of a single process? I'm not asking for a textual commentary, bibliography, or close exegesis of specific chapters; rather, I want a clear conceptual map of how these commitments interact, and where a careful reader might legitimately resist the move from "all is transformation" to "therefore no grief is warranted."
