Question:
Moral Foundations Theory describes liberals as relying primarily on the care and fairness foundations, while conservatives draw on loyalty, authority, and sanctity as well. A normative move sometimes made in defence of this taxonomy is to argue that because conservatives activate all five foundations, their moral profile is descriptively “more complete,” and that this descriptive completeness gives them normative priority: the conservative framework is closer to an objective moral architecture because it does not arbitrarily suppress innate modules. Evaluate this normative move. Specifically: (1) does distributing moral weight across more descriptive categories entail that the distribution is normatively superior? (2) Is there a hidden equivocation between “balance across evolutionary modules” and “balance as a moral virtue”? (3) Could a defender of the liberal profile reply by accepting the descriptive taxonomy but denying that evolutionary ubiquity confers normative authority?

Student answer:
MFT shows that liberals only use care and fairness, while conservatives use all five. This means conservatives have a more complete moral sense. It is not an is-ought fallacy to prefer the conservative view, because evolution has already validated all five foundations—if they were not genuinely moral, they would not have survived across cultures. A liberal who ignores loyalty or sanctity is therefore morally blind in the same way someone who ignores smell is sensorially blind. The theory’s implicit ideal is balance, so Haidt’s framework actually supports the conclusion that the conservative profile is closer to objective morality. The liberal position is thus not a reasoned disagreement but a kind of perceptual deficit.
