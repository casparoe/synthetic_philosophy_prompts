I facilitate a discussion group of about fourteen adults at a local library, meeting for seventy-five minutes every other week. None of us have formal training in history or philosophy, but we have noticed that every account of the past reads like a story with an inevitable arc, even though people living through the events had no idea where things were headed. I want an activity that lets us feel how backward-looking storytelling hides the confusion people actually experienced.

Design a structured session for this group. Split us into two teams and give both teams an identical set of cards describing a prolonged crisis—say, a multi-year drought and food emergency in one region during the 1940s—along with weather records and policy responses. One team receives the cards in strict chronological order; the other receives the same cards arranged thematically (economic measures, weather patterns, political statements). Give each team twenty-five minutes to construct an explanation of why the crisis ended as it did, then require a vote on two questions: was the outcome predictable from the first card, and should the decision-makers have seen it coming?

After the vote, reveal a hidden deck of "near-miss" cards—abandoned policies, weather patterns that almost occurred, and local actions that could have altered the result—which both teams were missing. Have the groups revise their explanations. Then debrief: map the shift in confidence to hindsight bias and the narrative fallacy; show how chronological ordering encourages a sense of inevitability while thematic ordering encourages structural explanation; and be honest about what this card exercise cannot reproduce—perhaps the emotional weight of living without foresight—and tell me exactly how to supply that in discussion.

Please include a minute-by-minute timeline, the materials needed, rules or roles that keep both teams equally engaged, an incentive scheme that requires no real money, what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, and a frank note on which feature of historical explanation the activity misses and must be added verbally.
