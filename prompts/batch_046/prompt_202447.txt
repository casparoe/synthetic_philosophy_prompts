I'm a doctoral student in visual culture and American studies serving on the acquisitions committee for a small public archive in a deindustrialized Midwestern city, and we face a steady stream of community-produced visual works—murals, posters, zines, and digital prints—from neighborhoods undergoing rapid redevelopment. We have about thirty minutes per proposal, nobody on the committee has read W. E. B. Du Bois, and right now we vote on instinct. I want you to translate Du Bois's claim that all art is propaganda—specifically, that legitimate art must combine truthful representation, formal beauty, and an explicit commitment to justice—into a reusable, step-by-step decision procedure my committee can actually run. Structure it as an ordered checklist: the precise questions to ask about a submitted work, what counts as a sufficient answer to advance or to stop with a rejection, and the explicit nodes where the question is factual versus one of values that must be signed off by a designated committee member rather than resolved mechanically. You must build in a filter for what I will call "extractive propaganda"—work that is aesthetically powerful and factually accurate yet ultimately promotes the displacement or commodification of the very community it depicts—so that the procedure can reject a beautiful, truthful piece on values grounds. Apply the procedure to three submissions: a hand-painted mural documenting a factory closure that was commissioned and paid for by the closing firm; an anonymous zine mapping vacant lots through abstract, non-representational imagery that refuses any explicit political message; and a community poster campaign against a proposed highway expansion that contains one demonstrably false statistic about traffic fatalities. Make sure one of these yields an answer the committee will resist accepting, and clearly state what the procedure cannot do—where it will misfire, and where Du Bois's framework itself strains or breaks when applied to collective, anonymous, or digitally circulated works. End with a one-sentence summary of the procedure's core logic, alone on the final line.
