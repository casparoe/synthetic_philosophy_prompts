I serve on a university advisory panel that applies a framework of coherent extrapolated volition to evaluate requests for cognitive and decision-support systems. Our mandate is to determine, for a given individual or group, whether a requested system configuration honors what that agent would prefer if they knew more, thought more clearly, and retained greater coherence across their values—while also respecting the structural integrity of their preference-formation process. We have established four precedents that guide our rulings. First, when a patient with a treatable phobia refused exposure therapy because anticipatory anxiety was overwhelming, we upheld the refusal: idealization may correct for misinformation but should not override autonomous boundaries around psychological risk. Second, when a student asked an AI tutor to systematically omit counterarguments from explanations in order to reduce cognitive discomfort, we denied the request: the idealized agent values understanding over comfort, and simplifying away objections violates coherence rather than preserving it. Third, when a local community requested that an algorithm weight nearby welfare more heavily than distant welfare, contrary to impartial utility maximization, we approved: collective volition can coherently embed relational proximity as a non-correctable parameter. Fourth, when a grieving individual asked an AI to maintain a false but consoling belief about a deceased relative, with safeguards against external harm, we permitted it: emotional architecture is part of volition, and coherence includes stable affective integration rather than maximal factual accuracy.

We now face a new proposal. A researcher has deliberately constructed what she calls an "epistemic architecture" in which she maintains rigorous analytical standards in scientific domains while sustaining an unexamined, comforting framework in a separate metaphysical domain; she derives explicit satisfaction from the boundary itself, not merely from the beliefs on either side. She requests that a cognitive assistant actively reinforce this compartmentalization—not merely refrain from challenging it, but structurally prevent information flow between the domains, ensuring that evidential pressure from her scientific work never destabilizes the metaphysical framework. She acknowledges that a fully informed, maximally coherent version of herself might dissolve the boundary, but argues that the boundary is itself a preference-structural feature that an idealized version of her, if it retained her core values, would preserve.

Predict our panel's verdict, and more importantly, identify the competing interpretive rules that fit our four precedents equally well but diverge here. Specifically, distinguish a rule that treats compartmentalization as correctable incoherence from one that treats it as a protected architectural feature of volition; say what each predicts about this request; offer your best reconstruction of which rule we are actually running, with the reasoning that makes that fit preferable; and propose one additional hypothetical case—distinct from the four above—that would definitively separate the two rules. If you conclude that our precedents genuinely underdetermine the outcome, state precisely what principle we would be adopting with each possible decision, and identify which of our four precedents serves as the closest analog for each choice.
