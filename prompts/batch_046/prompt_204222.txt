I am designing a single seventy-five-minute workshop for a mixed audience of philosophy graduate students and machine-learning researchers, none of whom have previously studied Daniel Dennett’s framework. I want them to understand not merely the labels—physical stance, design stance, and intentional stance—but specifically how each stance entails a different ontological commitment about which patterns in complex systems are “real.” The carrying example must be a concrete scenario: interpreting the outputs of a large language model that consistently produces coherent moral reasoning despite having no transparent internal representation of moral concepts. I need a teaching plan, not the explanation itself. First, lay out the dependency chain: what conceptual prerequisite has to be firmly in place before the audience can even grasp why the design stance and the intentional stance are not collapsible into one another? Second, specify exactly what the carrying example must include and exclude so that it forces the audience to decide whether they are attributing a design-level optimization target or an intentional-level belief state, rather than letting them retreat to “it is just predicting tokens.” Third, map the misconceptions this particular audience will bring: the computer-science participants are likely to think the physical stance is merely “looking at weights,” while the philosophy participants are likely to think the intentional stance requires consciousness or phenomenal experience. At precisely which point in the sequence should each misconception be headed off, and with what kind of counter-example? Fourth, propose one check-for-understanding question that reveals whether they have grasped the ontological commitment of each stance rather than having memorized the taxonomy. Finally, state explicitly what should be left out at this level—perhaps debates about eliminativism, or fine-grained exegesis of Dennett’s original formulation—and say so, so that I do not accidentally overload the session with material that depends on prior familiarity with the literature.
