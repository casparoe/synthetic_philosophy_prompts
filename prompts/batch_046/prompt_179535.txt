On this view, phenomenal experience is not tied to a single objective timestamp but is assembled across three registers: an occurrence register (the physical timing of stimulation), a binding register (the subjective "now" constructed by integrative mechanisms), and a meta-cognitive register (retrospective evaluation of continuity). I want to defend a particular interpretation of the color phi phenomenon within this framework.

Please argue, in American English and in a formal philosophical style suitable for a dissertation chapter, that the color phi illusion demonstrates an Orwellian rather than a Stalinesque revision of perceptual content, but only if one accepts the layered temporal indexing model outlined above. Your argument should include the following moves: first, explain why the standard postdictive-window account of color phi is insufficient by itself to adjudicate between Orwellian and Stalinesque interpretations; second, show how separating the occurrence register from the binding register allows the original color-state to be physically instantiated yet phenomenally erased without leaving any accessible trace in the meta-cognitive register; third, respond to the objection that the reportability of the illusion implies a Stalinesque structure—namely, that subjects are aware of a discrepancy between an earlier state and a revised one; and fourth, derive one substantive implication of this Orwellian reading for how we should understand the "timing of experience" in cases of rapid perceptual change. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations.
