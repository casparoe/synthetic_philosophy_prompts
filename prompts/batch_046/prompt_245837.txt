Here's an argument I built for a seminar and now find suspicious because its conclusion dissolves a distinction I want to preserve. (1) Republican freedom is absence of domination: being subject to another agent's arbitrary power, regardless of whether that power is ever exercised. (2) For power to count as non-arbitrary, its exercise must be constrained by rules that genuinely track the interests of those subject to it, rather than resting on the power-holder's mere benevolence. (3) But "tracking interests" can only be cashed out in one of two ways: either (a) the subjects actually authorize or control those rules, which makes non-domination depend on a form of positive self-legislation, or (b) the rules are calibrated so precisely that they never actually interfere with anyone's choices, which makes republican freedom depend on the preservation of negative liberty by institutional design. (4) So any case of genuinely non-arbitrary power either collapses into democratic autonomy — a variant of positive liberty — or into a well-engineered absence of interference — a variant of negative liberty. (5) Therefore republican freedom is not a distinct third concept but a redundant hybrid of Berlin's two. (6) This feels wrong: the kindly-master slave lacks republican freedom despite having negative freedom, and the democratic citizen seems situated differently from both the autonomous agent and the unmolested hermit. Where does the argument go wrong? If I have to choose, I'd rather keep (1) and (6) than (3) or (5).
