Having retired from experimental physics and recently begun serious study in philosophy of mind, I am drafting a short essay tentatively titled Dissipative Boundaries and the Moral Weight of Computation. I would welcome your critical comments on its central argument, particularly on whether I conflate physical autonomy with moral status.

The essay accepts that functional equivalence alone is an unreliable guide to which computations deserve moral consideration: if substrate independence were sufficient, every accurate simulation of a conscious process, however distributed, would generate parallel moral subjects, which seems implausible. I then propose attending instead to the thermodynamic embedding of a computation—specifically, whether the informational process is maintained by localized, non-recoverable dissipation of energy that serves primarily to preserve the system’s own boundary conditions rather than to perform external work. A brain, on this view, is not merely running algorithms; it sustains a dissipative structure whose physical persistence is inseparable from its cognitive activity. By contrast, the computations executed by a large language model, though functionally complex, are distributed across server infrastructure whose cooling and power systems serve external economic ends; the dissipation is not organized around maintaining the computation’s autonomous boundary. I therefore argue that moral attribution should be reserved for computations physically embedded in self-maintaining dissipative agents, regardless of functional sophistication.

I apply this framework to two boundary cases: artificial neural networks running on neuromorphic chips designed to minimize energy waste, and hypothetical software agents that control their own energy harvesting. In both cases, I suggest that the presence or absence of an integrated dissipative boundary—rather than integration or information in the abstract sense—should determine whether we treat the system as a candidate for moral consideration.

I am especially interested in your assessment of whether this criterion collapses into biological chauvinism, whether it unjustly excludes entities that might be conscious but thermodynamically efficient, and whether the distinction between serving the system’s boundary and serving external ends can be made precise enough to do philosophical work. Please address these points directly, and challenge any step that strikes you as physically naive or conceptually confused.
