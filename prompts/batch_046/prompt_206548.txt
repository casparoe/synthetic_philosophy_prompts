I'm assembling prompts for a dataset on philosophical and conceptual reasoning, and I'd like you to design a runnable workshop activity for a 100-minute graduate seminar of sixteen students (no prerequisites beyond basic familiarity with comparative sociology) that makes participants feel, before it is named, the normative tension between celebrating exceptional success and cutting it down. The specific frames are the American Dream's meritocratic visibility and the egalitarian suppression captured by Jantelagen and the tall poppy syndrome, but the activity itself should never lecture about them. Instead, the rules, roles, and payoffs should generate the conflict organically, with genuinely small but tangible stakes—perhaps a collective choice of next week's discussion topic plus a shared refreshment budget—so that strategic and emotional reactions arise from the incentives rather than from framing language.

Design a three-round simulation called the Allocation Protocol, with four collectives of four, a hidden role assigned in Round Three, and a minute-by-minute timeline. In Round One, each collective completes an isomorphic analytical task—ranking ambiguous policy proposals by internal coherence—with all individual contributions visible and named; afterward they must publicly allocate the shared reward proportionally, with explicit naming encouraged. In Round Two, the outputs are anonymized and pooled; allocation must be unanimous and equal, and any participant who claims exceptional contribution or singles out another by name triggers an automatic penalty reducing the collective reward by one quarter. In Round Three, reveal that in Round One one member of each collective was a hidden surrogate optimizer instructed to maximize visible coherence scores regardless of actual logical consistency, while Round Two anonymity masked genuinely superior but invisible work; collectives must then reallocate retroactively and vote on which rule to institutionalize for a hypothetical future grant. Specify roles (facilitator, collective members, hidden optimizer revealed only after Round Two), include a brief debrief after each round, predict what will go wrong—such as collectives refusing to apply the penalty in Round Two or over-correcting in Round Three—and map emotional moments onto the two cultural frames. Conclude with an honest note on which structural feature of this tension—likely the long-term organizational invisibility of labor or the internalized shame of excellence—the simulation cannot reproduce and must be supplied by discussion. Avoid citations, bibliographies, and any dependence on specific empirical studies; inject the niche twist of the hidden surrogate optimizer so the prompt remains meaningful without web access.
