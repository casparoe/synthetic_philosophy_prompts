I'm a software engineer without formal philosophy training, and I'm trying to audit my own views on everyday conventions before I discuss them with a colleague. I think of local practices—tipping, queuing, and jaywalking (ignoring pedestrian signals)—as coordination protocols with three separable layers: behavioral syntax (the rule you follow), local semantics (what the practice means in that culture), and normative weight (whether it binds outsiders). Here are my commitments.

(a) The syntax layer is never intrinsically moral; tipping, queuing, or stopping at a crosswalk is a coordination solution, not a categorical duty.
(b) Outsiders nonetheless inherit a derivative normative weight: a visitor who breaks the syntax predictably imposes costs—confusion, economic harm to service workers, safety risks—so there is a conditional obligation to comply.
(c) The strength of this inherited obligation scales with the cost of deviation to locals and inversely with the burden of compliance for the visitor.
(d) Inheritance applies only to syntax; a visitor need not subscribe to the local semantics—understanding or endorsing why locals queue this way or tip that percentage is unnecessary, and behavioral conformance is sufficient.
(e) If a protocol's local semantics are structurally unjust—say, a tipping regime that shifts wage risk onto customers and exploits labor, or a queuing system that enforces ethnic hierarchy—then the inherited normative weight is voided for outsiders, regardless of how cheap compliance would be.
(f) There is a universal baseline against causing direct, foreseeable harm; but social friction or inefficiency alone does not reach that threshold, so not every broken convention violates (f).
(g) When an outsider judges a protocol unjust and opts out, they still owe a secondary duty of minimal disruption—violating it quietly rather than performatively—to avoid collateral burden on locals.

Do these hang together? I'm particularly worried about three pairs. First, (a) denies intrinsic moral status to protocols, yet (b) assigns outsiders a moral obligation to follow them—is that a contradiction, or just a distinction between intrinsic and inherited weight? Second, (c) makes obligation proportional to cost and ease, but (e) nullifies it for injustice regardless of those proportions—does (e) always override (c), and if so, does that collapse the scaling principle? Third, (f) restricts universal duties to direct harm, while (b) treats economic and social friction as sufficient for obligation—does (b) smuggle in a broader harm concept that (f) rejects?

Please rank the tensions by severity rather than listing them all as equally bad. For each, say which commitment I'd have to weaken or drop to restore consistency, and identify any apparent tensions that dissolve once the three-layer distinction is applied properly. Also tell me whether this syntax-semantics-weight framework actually clarifies the conflicts or just relabels them, and what the least costly revision to my overall set would be. I don't want a historical lecture; I want an audit of whether my own position is jointly tenable.
