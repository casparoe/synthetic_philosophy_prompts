I am constructing a diagnostic decision tree for a graduate seminar that asks whether labor and leisure possess intrinsic moral standing or only instrumental value, particularly under conditions of widespread automation. The tree should classify positions by the answers they give to concrete cases, not by the labels adherents choose. The root split should concern the source of work's value: does it reside in the product produced, in the formation of the worker's character, or in the social recognition granted? From there, branch through responses to three scenarios: first, a fully automated factory in which former workers receive a universal dividend but may voluntarily perform now-unnecessary manual labor—does this voluntary labor retain dignity, or is it mere nostalgia? Second, a jurisdiction that caps labor at thirty-five hours per week and treats systematic overwork as a public-health hazard structurally analogous to environmental pollution—does this cap protect a right to leisure, or does it illegitimately restrict the worker's autonomy to pursue a vocation? Third, the phenomenon of death by overwork—interpreted either as an individual failure of work-life balance or as structural violence enacted by employers and cultural norms—does this phenomenon reveal that overwork is always exploitative, or only contingently so? At the leaves, place the following named positions, each with one sentence describing its most awkward commitment: the Weberian vocation view, which holds that work is self-justifying regardless of output; the leisure-right view, which treats mandatory rest as a condition of human dignity; the karoshi-critical view, which sees overwork as systemic coercion disguised as personal dedication; the automation-liberation view, which argues that meaningful labor must be decoupled from necessary production; and a hybrid view centered on shadow labor—the invisible cognitive, emotional, and corrective upkeep required to maintain automated systems—which claims that true contemporary work has migrated into this hidden maintenance and that traditional categories of labor and leisure no longer map onto reality. In addition, identify at least two unoccupied combinations in the logical space—such as a view that accepts the thirty-five-hour cap as just yet denies that shadow labor counts as real work—and diagnose each briefly: is it incoherent, merely unexplored, or unstable? The tree must distinguish all five named positions; if any two share an identical path through every node, flag them and specify the further case that would separate them. The intended audience is philosophically literate but not expert in philosophy of labor: every node must be answerable without prior knowledge of the view's name. Do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations. Deliver the tree as labeled branches, leaf descriptions, unoccupied-branch diagnoses, and explicit flags for indistinguishability.
