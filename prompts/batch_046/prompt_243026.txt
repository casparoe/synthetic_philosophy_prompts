Design a decision-tree taxonomy for Hempel’s raven paradox that sorts respondents by their actual evidential commitments rather than by the label they prefer. The tree must have exactly seven internal nodes, each posing a concrete observational or logical commitment answerable yes/no/maybe without specialist jargon—beginning with “You observe a black shoe; does this provide any evidential support, however slight, for ‘all ravens are black’?” and proceeding through whether logical equivalence entails evidential equivalence, whether prior probabilities can differentiate the two formulations, whether only potential falsifiers count as evidence, and whether background domain knowledge can block an otherwise valid inference. From the answer combinations, locate at least six terminal positions in the logical space, including: (1) strict logical-equivalence confirmation; (2) Bayesian differential confirmation; (3) Nicod-criterion exclusivity; (4) falsificationist rejection of positive confirmation for universal claims; (5) a pragmatic/context-sensitive view where knowledge about shoe-color distributions neutralizes the inference; and (6) one coherent but currently unoccupied position that you construct to fill an empty logical slot. For every combination that remains unoccupied, note it and briefly diagnose whether it is incoherent, unstable, or merely unexplored. Flag any pairs of named positions that your tree fails to separate. Present the tree as nested branches with the separating question at each node, followed by a list of empty slots with diagnoses. The audience is advanced undergraduates who have encountered the paradox once but do not know the literature; keep every question case-based and never ask “are you a Bayesian?” End with \answer{[terminal leaves located, empty slots noted]}.
