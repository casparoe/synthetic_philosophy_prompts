I serve as the dispute-resolution officer for a cooperative farm collective, and every Thursday I have to rule on whether a member violated our communication charter when they made a claim about crop conditions, water usage, or equipment status during our weekly meeting. The charter says members must observe "assertoric responsibility," but it never defines the term. From my own reading I understand there are three competing philosophical standards: some say you should only assert what you know, others say only what is true, and others say only what you genuinely believe. I need you to turn these competing norms into a single reusable decision procedure—something like a flowchart in words with ordered tests—that I can apply in about fifteen minutes per case, writing my reasoning on a one-page form that a non-specialist member can read and appeal if needed.

Because our disputes often surface days after the original statement, the procedure must handle not just the moment of speaking but what I am calling "assertoric residue": the lingering obligation a speaker has when someone challenges the claim later, even if the speaker has since changed their mind or discovered new evidence. The procedure should specify, step by step, how I check whether the initial norm was satisfied, whether a valid challenge was raised within our forty-eight-hour window, and whether the speaker's response settles the residue or compounds the violation. Include a small set of concrete test sentences I can apply—substitution tests, challenge scenarios, or belief-state checks—without requiring me to interview the speaker's private mental states in an invasive way.

Please apply your procedure to three worked cases: (1) a member said "the north field is dry" based on a morning walk, but irrigation records later show it was wet; (2) a member repeated a rumor about pesticide drift that they privately doubted but thought might be important; and (3) a case where your procedure exonerates a speaker even though the collective will be angry about the practical outcome—explain exactly why the procedure gives that unwelcome answer. Finally, flag the points where judgment cannot be eliminated and where I will still have to make a call without algorithmic cover, especially regarding how strict the "knowledge" standard should be when a member is acting under time pressure during an emergency harvest decision.
