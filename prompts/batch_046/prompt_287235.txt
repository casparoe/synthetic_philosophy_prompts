I'm working through my own commitments on permitted harm by examining concrete cases, and I want to know whether they converge on a single principle or whether I need to abandon one of my judgments.

Here are my verdicts.

One: In the standard switch case, diverting a trolley from a track with five people onto a side track with one, by pulling a lever, is morally permissible. The death of the one is a foreseen side effect, not a means.

Two: In the footbridge case, pushing a large person onto the track to stop the trolley and save the five is not permissible. The person's body is used as a means of stopping the trolley.

Three: In the surgeon case, killing one healthy patient to harvest organs that will save five dying patients is not permissible. The killing is the means to the saving.

Four: In an institutional triage case, a hospital applies a pre-established protocol that reassigns a single ventilator by lottery when more than four patients need it; the prior occupant dies, but no individual agent targets them. Applying the protocol is permissible, though tragic.

Five: In a large-scale climate-intervention case, a government deploys stratospheric aerosol injection expected to prevent millions of heat-related deaths, knowing it will also cause drought-related deaths in the tens of thousands; the drought is a direct physical consequence of the cooling, not a mechanism exploited to achieve it. Launching the program is permissible, provided the intent is solely the cooling benefit.

Six: In a municipal-demolition case, a city demolishes a building—knowing it contains one sleeping occupant—to clear land for a flood barrier that will save thousands; the demolition is physically necessary for the barrier, and the occupant's death is foreseen but not intended. I judge this not permissible.

These six judgments feel stable to me, but they are hard to reconcile. Cases four and five accept foreseen harm in institutional or large-scale settings, yet case six involves institutional decision-making and foreseen harm and I reject it. Case five accepts massive foreseen side-effect harm, while case six rejects a structurally parallel individual foreseen harm. Meanwhile, cases one and two track the familiar means/side-effect line, but cases four through six seem to require more than that line.

So: is there a precise principle—perhaps a refined doctrine of double effect with added conditions on proportionality, directness, or institutional mediation—that accommodates all six? If there is, state it exactly, identify which clause is motivated by which of my cases, say what it predicts for a case I have not listed (specifically, a self-driving vehicle programmed to swerve away from five pedestrians onto a path where it will necessarily strike one, with the algorithm treating the impact as an unavoidable trajectory consequence rather than using the body as a brake), and tell me which of my six verdicts it must overturn. If no single principle saves all six, present the two best competing candidates, say what each costs in terms of overturned verdicts, and advise which cost is smaller.
