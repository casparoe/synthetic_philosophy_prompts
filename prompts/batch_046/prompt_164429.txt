Draft the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between two interpretive camps about Plato's philosophy of intoxication and civic training in the Laws. Camp One, the Sympotic-Pedagogical camp, holds that Plato genuinely prescribes regulated communal drinking as an active civic technology: the symposion operates as a controlled stress-test that reveals hidden dispositions for corrective training, with age-graded rules and a sober symposiarch designed to build rational mastery through practiced exposure to pleasure rather than through avoidance; they further hold that this is consistent with the Republic's asceticism because the Laws addresses citizens who will never be philosophers, not because it abandons philosophical norms. Camp Two, the Strategic-Containment camp, holds that the Laws permits wine only as a politically necessary second-best; the symposiarch is a damage-control officer, the age rules are harm-reduction measures, and any language of training or revelation is strategic rhetoric to make an unavoidable evil tolerable; on this view, the true Platonic norm remains sober philosophical contemplation, and the Laws' pragmatism represents a departure from rather than an application of that norm.

The joint document must contain four parts. First, a numbered agreed-claims section listing propositions both camps sign about the textual landscape—specifically the comparison of Scythian, Cretan, and Athenian practices in Laws I, the age-based drinking restrictions, the duties of the symposiarch, Plato's psychology of pleasure, reason, and thumos across the Republic, Timaeus, and Laws, and explicit agreement on claims that the texts do not support. Second, a disagreements table with precise formulations in wording each camp accepts: whether the symposion is a positive training ground for virtue or a containment mechanism for vice; whether the passage describing intoxicated choral performance should be read as licensing expressive release under rational supervision or as demonstrating that intoxication destroys the order required for true music; and whether the Laws' political pragmatism extends Platonic ethics to imperfect citizens or abandons it. Third, identify at least two cruxes: one about whether Plato's psychology allows pleasure to be instrumentally used to strengthen rational governance without corrupting the soul, and one about whether the symposion should be understood as a static epistemic device that reveals pre-existing character or as a dynamic technology that actively constructs new dispositions—together with, for each crux, a concrete interpretive case or argumentative test both sides agree bears on it, such as how to read the Athenian Stranger's analogy between wine and gymnastic training of the body, with each camp's predicted resolution recorded in advance. Fourth, diagnose whether part of the dispute is verbal—particularly whether disagreement over endorsement collapses into disagreement about which class of citizens the Laws is describing—specifying exactly which part and why. The statement should be formal, should avoid bibliographies or citations, and should require reasoning about Platonic texts accessible to a well-trained model without reliance on obscure recent scholarship.
