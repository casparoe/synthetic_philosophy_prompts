I'm delivering the opening remarks for a three-day interdisciplinary workshop titled "Consciousness Without a Center," convened at a university conference center with a deliberately split audience. On one side sit philosophers of mind and cognitive neuroscientists for whom Dennett's demolition of the Cartesian theater and his multiple drafts model are familiar terrain; on the other sit clinical psychologists and narrative therapists who work daily with dissociation, depersonalization, and disrupted self-narrative, and who have likely never encountered those technical debates. The workshop asks whether consciousness requires a unified "auditorium" or "screen" where experience comes together for a single inner observer, or whether it is better understood as distributed, competing narratives without a central audience.

I need the remarks to make that philosophical tension do real structural work rather than serve as ornament. Specifically, treat the Cartesian theater not only as a theory of consciousness but as a hidden rhetorical expectation: audiences instinctively expect a speaker to be a unified Cartesian self, delivering one authoritative "draft" from a privileged inner vantage point. The speech should subtly undermine that expectation in its very form—perhaps by refusing a singular authoritative voice, by shifting registers between scientific and clinical perspectives, or by modeling distributed authorship—while remaining fully intelligible and welcoming to the clinicians who are not present for metaphysics. Because the model being questioned denies a central observer, the address must avoid comforting claims about "the speaker's true self" or "a core consciousness witnessing this room," and it must establish coherence and authority through other means.

Please write a ten-minute address with an opening that acknowledges the occasion, a middle section that uses the tension between the Cartesian theater and multiple drafts to frame the workshop's stakes, and a closing that invites collaboration without collapsing back into the very unified-observer model being questioned. Do not include a bibliography, reading list, or citations; the speech should stand entirely on its own conceptual feet, speaking to experts without leaving the non-philosophers behind.
