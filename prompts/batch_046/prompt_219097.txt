You are delivering the opening remarks at a workshop convened to honor the retirement of a scholar who spent her career arguing that rational choice theory systematically mischaracterized decisions made in the absence of preference. The audience is split: on one side, philosophers of action and decision theorists for whom Buridan's ass is familiar territory; on the other, family members, graduate students from outside philosophy, and university administrators who have never encountered the paradox. Write a formal, warm ten-minute address that treats Buridan's ass as a structural problem rather than a joke about indecision, and that uses it to frame the honoree's contribution without turning the occasion into a lecture. Specifically, organize the speech around a three-way distinction among responses to genuine indifference: arbitrary selection (a coin flip), hidden-preference revelation, and a third mode you should call resolution by contextual embedding. In this third mode, an agent breaks symmetry not by discovering a latent preference but by allowing a previously irrelevant feature of the situation—proximity, lighting, the accidental presence of a bystander—to become locally decisive, thereby generating an asymmetry that did not exist before the choice. Explain this idea in language accessible to the non-specialists, defend its status as rational rather than as a disguised failure of will, and show why it avoids reducing the honoree's view to a simple decision procedure for the indecisive. The philosophical framework must shape the rhetoric: do not claim that she "solved" Buridan's paradox, since her position was precisely that the paradox arises only if one assumes that every choice must be driven by a prior preference; instead, present her work as an argument for taking genuinely preference-less choice seriously. Because this is a ceremonial address, include no citations, paper titles, reading lists, or bibliographic references. If you mention Buridan or later commentators, paraphrase them; do not use verbatim quotations.
