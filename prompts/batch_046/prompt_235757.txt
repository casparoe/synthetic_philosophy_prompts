You are a philosopher of history with expertise in causal explanation, overdetermination, and multi-causal reasoning, consulting for a peer-reviewed history journal. Before drafting anything, think carefully and systematically: begin by brainstorming the conceptual architecture that must survive translation into practice. Specifically, distinguish genuine causal overdetermination—where multiple conditions are each individually sufficient for an outcome—from conjunctive multi-causality, where conditions are necessary but only jointly sufficient. Also map how historians typically conflate singular-cause narratives with redundant-pathway explanations, and identify which distinctions are philosophically essential versus which can be simplified for non-specialists.

Your task is to design a reusable editorial procedure—an ordered checklist with explicit decision nodes, stop-points, and diagnostic tests—that a journal editor or peer reviewer can run in about twenty minutes on a manuscript proposing a causal explanation of a complex historical event. The practitioner has deep period knowledge but no formal philosophy training; the stakes are publication-quality assessment; and the deliverable must be a standardized memo that flags conceptual errors rather than resolving normative disputes.

The procedure must operationalize the following commitments without requiring the user to cite theorists or texts:

1. A test for the singular-necessity error—when an author implies a cause is strictly necessary (the but-for condition) even though the evidence supports only contributory causation.
2. A method for identifying redundant causal pathways—situations where removing one proposed cause leaves the outcome intact because another sufficient pathway remains active.
3. A representation of explanatory weight that separates trigger-level events from structural conditions, with an explicit flag marking where this separation is a framing choice driven by values rather than by empirical fact.
4. A diagnostic for overdetermination proper: whether two or more proposed causes each pass a counterfactual fragility test—if cause A had not occurred, cause B would still have produced the outcome, and vice versa.

Include a small set of test sentences—template claims an editor can apply to a manuscript’s abstract or conclusion—to detect each error quickly.

Then apply the procedure step-by-step to three worked cases:

A. The fall of the Western Roman Empire in the fifth century CE, where economic decline, military pressure, and administrative fragmentation are often presented as competing explanations rather than as potentially redundant pathways.
B. The outbreak of World War I in 1914, weighing alliance structures, militarization, and the assassination at Sarajevo, and showing where the procedure forces the editor to recognize redundancy that standard narratives suppress.
C. A case where the procedure produces an answer the historiographical field will dislike: apply it to the American Revolution to assess whether British fiscal and administrative policy was overdetermined by colonial ideological and organizational mobilization. Show the steps, mark the mandatory stop-points, and state exactly what judgment the editor must make without procedural assistance.

For each case, indicate where the procedure halts with a flag, what factual determination is made, and what remains an unavoidable value judgment.

Finally, add a brief limits section identifying what this procedure cannot do—such as handling genuinely probabilistic causation over very long durations, explanations dependent on counterfactuals about individual psychology, or cases where all proposed causes are jointly necessary but none are sufficient alone. Specify how an editor should recognize these boundary conditions.

Write in a formal, precise tone appropriate for a professional methodology document. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations. Do not quote verbatim from any source; paraphrase all references to scholars or theories without quotation marks. Ensure that every element can be completed by a model relying solely on general philosophical and historical knowledge, with no need for online search.
