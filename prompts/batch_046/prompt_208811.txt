You are a philosopher of history with expertise in institutional design and path-dependent processes, and I need you to help me build a practical tool. I advise a municipal heritage review board that receives frequent proposals to alter, remove, or preserve elements of our city's built layout—street grids, bridge placements, zoning boundaries, and public-square configurations. The board wants to know whether a given feature is genuinely path-dependent—locked in by a contingent historical accident that could easily have branched otherwise—or whether it is functionally convergent, meaning that similar geographic, economic, and social conditions would have produced essentially the same feature regardless of that accident. They do not want a lecture on standard technology-lock-in examples; they want a reusable decision procedure they can run in about thirty minutes per proposal, without requiring philosophy degrees.

Please begin by brainstorming the key conceptual distinctions that must survive in any practical translation—particularly the difference between strong lock-in, where irreversible physical infrastructure makes reversal prohibitively costly, and weak lock-in, where institutional inertia persists mainly because no actor has borne the cost of coordination, and the idea of a minimal-perturbation counterfactual: altering a single early decision node with the smallest plausible change and tracking whether the divergence propagates downstream or dampens out. Then turn this into an ordered procedure: the questions to ask in sequence, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure halts with a clear verdict of path-dependent or not path-dependent, and where it must hand the decision to a human because the issue is one of values rather than historical causation. Be explicit about the judgment calls dressed up as procedural steps, flag where the procedure will misfire—especially when a feature is path-dependent but also serves a present function that a convergent replacement could serve equally well—and state what the procedure cannot do.

Apply the procedure to two concrete cases. First, the radial street pattern centered on the site of a demolished textile mill: the mill's location was determined by a 1903 tax-abatement deal that almost went to a neighboring town, yet the radial layout now channels traffic efficiently. Second, a river-crossing bridge built at a narrow point that was selected not because it was the optimal engineering site—two wider, flatter crossings existed—but because a 1920s political coalition needed the contract for that ward; today the bridge is structurally adequate but creates a chronic bottleneck. In at least one of these applications, the procedure should yield an answer the board is likely to resist—perhaps declaring a feature path-dependent and therefore a poor reason for preservation, even though it carries deep local identity value—so that you can show exactly where the procedure ends and values begin. Write the whole thing in formal, polished language suitable for a policy manual appendix, and think carefully about how to keep the philosophical content intact while making every step executable by a non-specialist.
