For a workshop of about 15 philosophy-of-science graduate students, 80 minutes: design a structured activity around cranes versus skyhooks and greedy reductionism that makes participants feel why reducing a high-level phenomenon to its lowest-level components fails—not merely through information loss, but through hidden intermediate scaffolding. Assign them a concrete collective phenomenon (for example, the stability of monetary exchange or the authority of scientific consensus) and split the room into “builders,” who must reconstruct the phenomenon using only vocabulary and relations permitted at one assigned lower level (physical, biological, or individual-psychological), and “auditors,” who check for smuggled middle-level concepts such as “institution,” “trust,” or “norm.” Provide explicit rules about permitted terms, assigned roles, a timeline with check-in points, and a payoff scheme in which auditors score points for catching hidden scaffolding and builders score points for maintaining coherence without it. The debrief should map each discovered failure mode onto Dennett’s distinction: where a builder genuinely constructs upward with cranes versus where they unknowingly invoke a skyhook disguised as base-level vocabulary. Be frank about which feature of greedy reductionism—specifically, the ontological claim that middle layers are dispensable even when they are epistemically indispensable—this physical exercise cannot reproduce, and state exactly what the facilitator must supply in discussion. Also inject a fresh framing: ask the group to consider the “scaffolding debt” problem, namely whether a reconstruction that succeeds only by tacitly enriching the base vocabulary with structural relations borrowed from the very layer being eliminated demonstrates reduction or rather its opposite. Note what will predictably go wrong the first time (builders redefine restricted terms, auditors miss implicit institutional framing embedded in examples) and specify how to correct it in the moment. The conceptual force should emerge from the rules, roles, and failures, not from any lecture delivered afterward.
