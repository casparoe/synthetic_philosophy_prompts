I'm leading a 90-minute discussion for a reading group of colleagues from law and the natural sciences—intelligent readers, but with no background in 19th-century political economy. Our text is John Stuart Mill's posthumously published essays on socialism, particularly the argument that socialism should be treated as an open, experimental question rather than a revolutionary program. I want them to grasp his specific position: not that socialism is inevitable or desirable in all forms, but that cooperative association might eventually replace competitive individualism if it proves compatible with liberty and efficiency, and that any transition, if it happens, must be gradual, voluntary, and tested against evidence rather than imposed.

Plan the session's explanatory arc. First, lay out the dependency structure: what conceptual distinction has to be in place before the others can make sense—for instance, his separation of the mode-of-production question from the distribution-of-rewards question, or his distinction between revolutionary socialism and associationist socialism—and in what exact order they should be introduced so that each supports the next. Second, choose one carrying example or structural comparison that should carry the entire idea. Do not use a historical case study such as the Rochdale Pioneers; instead, construct an abstract or lightly fictionalized scenario that illustrates the difference between state expropriation and voluntary cooperative transition, and specify exactly what that example must contain and what it must deliberately omit so that it does not collapse into either a libertarian fairy tale or a Marxist parable. Third, map the misconceptions they will bring: I expect at least that Mill was a socialist in the modern party-political sense, that he believed capitalism was irredeemable, and that he thought equality required identical incomes. For each misconception, specify the precise point in the sequence where it should be headed off and how. Fourth, include one check-for-understanding question I can ask near the end that reveals whether they have understood the conditional, experimental structure of his view rather than simply memorized that he liked cooperatives. Fifth, say explicitly what should be left out at this level—for example, his intellectual debts to Fourier or Owen, or the detailed chronology of his revisions to the Principles of Political Economy—and explain why omitting it protects the arc rather than weakening it.

Finally, state what would change your mind about this plan: identify which feature of Mill's texts, or which pedagogical failure mode, would convince you that the proposed order is wrong, or that the carrying example actually misleads more than it clarifies.
