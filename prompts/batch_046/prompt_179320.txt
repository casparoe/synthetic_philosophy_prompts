In philosophy of history and social memory studies, a long-standing deadlock persists over what ought to happen when a community's collective memory conflicts with the documentary record. One position—call it mnemonic priority—holds that collective memory carries a normative and interpretive authority rooted in lived transmission and identity-formation: when archives contradict the memory, the contradiction should be mediated through the mnemonic framework rather than resolved by automatic archival override. The opposing position—archival primacy—maintains that collective memory is systematically distorted by present social needs and that only critical, evidence-based historiography can correct mythologization; on this view, when the record conflicts with memory, the record should give way only in the sense that it provides the corrective standard. Both sides acknowledge that archives are partial and that memory is fallible; both offer nuanced accounts of bias, power, and interpretation. Yet the debate has stalled: the same arguments about epistemic reliability and social function are repeated without convergence.

My working hypothesis is that the stall is not mainly about reliability but about a hidden ambiguity in what "giving way" requires—whether it means epistemic correction, normative authorization, or both—such that each side may be answering a different question under one heading. But I want a diagnosis rather than an adjudication. Is this disagreement verbal, substantive, or a case of two different problems—one about truth, one about constitutive authority—being treated as one? Do the sides differ about standards of evidence, about which cases count as decisive data, or about bedrock intuitions regarding the function of the past in social life? Or is the framing itself ill-posed, presupposing a single arbiter where plural authorities are appropriate?

To sharpen this, I am introducing a distinction I have been using informally: retrospective correction, meaning the revision of beliefs about what occurred, versus prospective authorization, meaning the grant of authority to a community to determine which past is operative for its future identity. Does this distinction expose the real fault line, or does it merely rename the stalemate?

Please diagnose the structure of the disagreement using what proponents of each view actually maintain, not as a survey of authors. Identify whether there is a hidden empirical or conceptual question doing the work, and say whether anything—a formal model of mnemonic reliability, an empirical study of how communities integrate contradictions, a clarification of authority types, or a reframing of the question—could genuinely move it, or whether the impasse is structural and should be expected.
