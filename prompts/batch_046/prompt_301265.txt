I've recently been helping coordinate relief distribution in a small community organization, and the experience of deciding who receives limited supplies when you genuinely do not know how many people actually need help has made standard lifeboat ethics feel too tidy. I'm now leading a seminar for fourteen philosophy graduate students—ninety minutes, around a single table, with no external readings assigned—on moral decision-making under scarcity, and I want them to feel the specific pressure of allocating resources when the very identity and number of claimants is epistemically unstable rather than merely contested.

Please design a structured role-play or deliberation game, not a lecture, that puts them in this situation before anyone names the philosophical problem. Assign roles so that some participants represent visible claimants with documented needs, one acts as a messenger who asserts—without proof—an invisible claimant with a competing severe need, and the remainder form an allocation council that must establish binding rules in round one, apply them in round two after the claim is introduced, and revise them in round three only if unanimous. The rules should include small symbolic stakes: each council member begins with ten tokens representing moral authority, binding allocations cost tokens, and if the pool empties the council collapses and no further allocations occur. Provide a precise timeline—opening framing (five minutes), rule-setting (fifteen), first allocation (ten), crisis introduction (five), second allocation (fifteen), revision vote (ten), and debrief (twenty)—and specify exactly how the facilitator introduces the hidden claim without revealing whether it is true or fabricated. In the debrief, map the three rounds onto standard lifeboat-ethics distinctions—utilitarian aggregation, deontological side-constraints, and virtue-ethical judgment—but also address the feature this activity injects: epistemic opacity about who counts as a claimant. Be explicit about what the token system can and cannot reproduce about real scarcity, identify one predictable failure mode—likely that students treat the messenger's claim as a puzzle to solve rather than an irreducible uncertainty—and tell me how to prevent that in facilitation. Finally, include an honest note on which element of actual lifeboat situations, such as irreversible physical death or long-term survival dynamics, the classroom simulation necessarily cannot reproduce and that I must supply verbally.
