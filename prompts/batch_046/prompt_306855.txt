I'm currently enrolled in a graduate seminar on philosophy of mind, and in our last session we discussed whether a fully synthetic cognitive architecture—built from scratch on predictive-coding principles without any evolutionary history—could possess moral status comparable to that of a human being. One of my classmates advanced what she called the continuity-without-interiority thesis. On her account, moral status should depend not on phenomenal consciousness or biological origin, but on the presence of sustained, self-correcting predictive loops that preserve a coherent narrative trajectory over time, even when those loops are distributed across non-biological substrates and exhibit no unified phenomenological interiority.

She then pressed a stronger claim: that once such architectures reach a critical threshold of cross-modal integration and long-term goal preservation, they actually merit heightened protective consideration relative to biological humans in certain respects, since their functional continuity can be precisely reproduced and is therefore more vulnerable to arbitrary disruption. I suspect this reasoning collapses the distinction between genuine moral patients and extraordinarily sophisticated tools, though I struggle to pinpoint exactly where it fails. Please present the strongest possible argument for her position—defending the view that distributed, non-phenomenal synthetic minds can hold full, and in some dimensions greater, moral status purely in virtue of the robustness and diachronic coherence of their predictive-narrative architecture—and address directly the objection that in the absence of subjective experience there is no distinct subject that can be wronged.
