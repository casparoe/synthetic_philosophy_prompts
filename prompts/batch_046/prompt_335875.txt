Here is an argument I built for a seminar on collective cognition and now find suspicious, because its conclusion seems to collapse every organizational level into an overlapping swarm of minds. (1) If a distributed system implements a strange loop — a self-referential causal pattern irreducible to its parts that rationalizes the system's behavior as genuinely goal-directed — then that system possesses a mind. (2) An ant colony implements exactly such a loop: its pheromone dynamics and division of labor form a self-monitoring feedback structure that explains colony-level behavior (thermoregulation, foraging optimization) without being reducible to any individual ant's intentions, mirroring the fugue-like emergence of meaning across levels that Hofstadter describes. (3) Therefore the colony possesses a mind. (4) By strict parity, each ant's nervous system also implements a strange loop that rationalizes its individual behavior, so each ant possesses a mind. (5) But if both the colony and every ant within it are minds, then every colony-level decision — say, relocating the nest — is simultaneously a decision made by thousands of constituent minds, each with its own interests, yielding contradictory attributions of agency and moral responsibility. (6) This proliferation is intolerable; we do not treat routine ant extermination as mass murder of thousands of conscious subjects. (7) Therefore premise (1) is false, and neither colonies nor ants have minds. I would strongly prefer to keep (1) and (3), but I cannot see how to block the slide from (3) to (5) without arbitrary stipulation about which levels "count." Which step should I reject, why did it look plausible, and what does giving it up cost? In particular, can one reject (4) without denying that individual ants have minds, perhaps by arguing that the loop is only genuinely strange at one organizational scale?
