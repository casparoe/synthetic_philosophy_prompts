I am preparing a graduate seminar session on nationalism and collective memory, and I find the standard classroom framing—are nations ancient or modern?—too thin once you start looking at diasporic communities that reconstruct continuity through digital archives, algorithmic translation, and virtual reconstructions of destroyed ritual spaces. Write a dialogue of about ten to twelve exchanges between two scholars who are equally rigorous and neither is a straw figure. The first is a modernist historian working in the Hobsbawmian tradition: she holds that every nation is a modern assembly whose antiquity is retroactively produced through standardized language, monument-building, and now, in her specific research, through diaspora newsletters from the 1990s and immersive virtual-reality reconstructions of ruined ceremonial sites that allow scattered populations to return to a homeland they never shared in physical space. The second is a postcolonial memory scholar who does not reject modernity but argues that labeling such practices invented traditions smuggles in an archival epistemology—one that treats the absence of state documents or census records as evidence of fabrication—while ignoring that many communities maintained identity through embodied kinship performance, oral transmission, and non-textual ritual that never aimed to produce a paper trail. They should converge on a concrete hypothetical case: a transnational group that claims an unbroken centuries-old ceremonial cycle, yet whose only evidence is a 1990s newsletter and recent crowd-funded virtual environments. The modernist should argue that these media are precisely the technologies of invention, creating affective continuity across rupture; the scholar should reply that calling them invented confuses the storage medium, archive versus body, with the ontological status of the community's self-relation, and that to apply the label is to judge oral societies by a literacy-based criterion they never adopted. Have them genuinely press each other: the modernist should ask whether the scholar's defense collapses into a romantic essentialism that denies all modern mediation; the scholar should ask whether Anderson's idea of imagination secretly depends on print-capitalism, census literacy, and the newspaper, thereby excluding the very oral societies it claims to describe. The dialogue should stay in the weeds—no introductory lecture, no request for bibliographies or further reading, and no citation of specific page numbers or obscure monographs. I want the disagreement to unfold through conceptual pressure on the categories themselves, not through appeals to authority, and I want it to feel like a debate that could only happen after both interlocutors have already agreed that nations are not simply old or simply new.
