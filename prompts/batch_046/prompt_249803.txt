I'm working through some puzzles for a philosophy of consciousness seminar that compares two ways of describing post-hoc revision in temporal experience: the Orwellian model, in which an original experience is preserved but later rewritten in the narrative that consciousness delivers, and the Stalinesque model, in which the original is erased entirely and replaced by a fabricated continuity. We've been using the color phi phenomenon as the central case, but I'm trying to apply the distinction to some less standard variants and finding my verdicts pull in opposite directions.

Here are my judgments on four cases, and I want to know whether any single principle can save all of them. First, in standard color phi—two differently colored dots flashed sequentially at separate positions with appropriate timing—the subject reports a single object moving and changing color midway. I want to call this Orwellian: both discrete dot-perceptions occur, but the brain inserts a transitional experience into the report after the fact. Second, in a backward-masked version where the second flash is suppressed before it reaches awareness, the subject still reports smooth motion and the intermediate color, yet denies any awareness of a second discrete stimulus. That feels Stalinesque: there is no preserved "original" second-dot experience to be rewritten; it was airbrushed out before consciousness ever registered it. Third, consider a "frozen present" scenario where a static image is removed, but subjects continue to report its presence for roughly two hundred milliseconds with no felt gap. Here I hesitate: the absence of the stimulus is never experienced as such, which sounds Stalinesque, yet the initial veridical perception is not erased but merely extended with false content, which sounds Orwellian. Fourth, take a predictive-processing variant in which the brain generates an erroneous color prediction for an upcoming dot, experiences the predicted color as the actual perception, and then—when the real dot differs—updates the report without any conscious feeling of error or correction. The erroneous intermediate experience seems to vanish without trace, suggesting Stalinesque removal, but the seamless correction feels like a rewritten history rather than a replaced one.

Is there a coherent principle—perhaps tied to whether subpersonal representations of the "original" remain accessible, or to whether the revision leaves phenomenological residue—that can classify all four consistently? Or must I abandon the distinction altogether, or drop one of my verdicts? If a principle can be saved, I want it stated precisely, with the clause that handles each case above, its verdict on a further case I haven't mentioned (say, a situation in which a subject is briefly shown contradictory motion cues and reports a single coherent trajectory but later, under hypnosis, recalls the contradiction), and an honest statement of which of my four judgments it cannot accommodate without ad hoc complication.
