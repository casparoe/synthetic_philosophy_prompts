I am a retired physical chemist who has only recently begun reading systematically in philosophy of mind, and I have been struck by the way defenders of constitutive panpsychism often respond to the so-called combination problem. In popular expositions, the relation between micro-level experiential properties and a unified macro-consciousness is presented as directly analogous to chemical bonding: just as individual hydrogen and oxygen atoms—none of which is liquid—combine to form water molecules that collectively exhibit liquidity and wetness, so too might minimal micro-subjects, each bearing only the faintest phenomenal properties, combine through some form of "phenomenal bonding" to constitute a single macro-subject such as myself. The analogy is explicitly invoked to rebut the objection that panpsychism faces a special, perhaps insuperable emergence difficulty: if physical chemistry resolved the appearance of macroscopic properties from microscopic constituents without recourse to vitalism, then the aggregation of consciousness should be no more mysterious than the aggregation of H2O.

Please audit this analogy rigorously. First, provide an explicit correspondence table mapping elements of the source domain—atomic structure, electromagnetic bonding, statistical mechanics, the emergence of liquid-phase properties, and the mereological relation between molecules and the fluid—onto the target domain of micro-phenomenal subjects, phenomenal bonding, phenomenal fusion, macro-experience, and the various formulations of the combination problem. Second, identify precisely where the mapping fractures: for example, chemical bonding depends on spatial adjacency and quantifiable interaction energies in ways that may lack any phenomenal counterpart; liquidity is an observable, third-person behavioral disposition of aggregates, whereas macro-consciousness is typically characterized as an intrinsic, first-person unified field. Does the analogy therefore smuggle in a form of mereological summation that conflicts with the non-summative, fused character that some panpsychists—distinguishing "phenomenal bonding" from "phenomenal fusion"—insist is required for macro-subjectivity?

Third, examine what the chemical picture quietly imports about parts versus wholes: in chemistry, the macro-property is usually analyzed as a statistical-mechanical pattern instantiated across the aggregate rather than as a new substance inhering in the whole as one being. Does this import undermine the panpsychist claim that the macro-subject is genuinely one conscious entity rather than a collective or "society" of micro-minds? Fourth, address the grain problem—the apparent mismatch between the smooth, continuous qualitative character of macro-experience (for instance, a uniform visual field of red) and the presumably discrete, minimal phenomenal grains of micro-subjects—by asking whether any chemical analogue (such as the transition from discrete atomic orbitals to continuous energy bands) genuinely illuminates this mismatch, or whether it merely obscures the qualitative discontinuity. Finally, evaluate whether the analogy's intended conclusion—that the combination problem is no more puzzling than chemical emergence—survives once these disanalogies are fully exposed, or whether the analogy was carrying the argumentative weight all along. If the chemical-bonding picture fails, suggest whether a different physical analogy—perhaps drawn from quantum coherence, superfluid phase transitions, or collective excitation modes—might better capture the intended relation between micro- and macro-experience without importing misleading mereological assumptions.
