You are an expert in 19th-century political philosophy, with particular depth in the development of liberal thought and its encounters with socialist economics. Before you answer, take a moment to brainstorm the relevant conceptual landscape—both the established terms that might be nearby and the possible gaps where no precise label exists.

I have been reading Mill’s later reflections on economic organization, and I keep encountering the same structural picture. He wants to replace the capitalist wage relation with cooperative ownership: the workers collectively own and manage the capital they use. Yet he refuses any centralized directing authority that would assign labor from above. Instead, he pictures many independent cooperative enterprises operating side by side, competing with one another for members and for custom, with individuals free to leave one association and join another, and with failure localized to particular cooperatives rather than system-wide. What is distinctive is the justification he offers. It is not primarily about equality or about ending exploitation in a moral sense; it is that only this kind of decentralized collective structure can preserve the competitive pressure, the diversity of social experiments, and the individual freedom of choice that he believes large-scale private capitalism actually undermines. So the abolition of private capital is paired, almost paradoxically, with the deliberate preservation of market-like dynamics—exit, rivalry, localized risk—inside a collectively owned framework.

My question is whether there is an established concept that captures exactly this configuration: socialist or collective property combined with an internally competitive, decentralized, voluntary architecture, defended on specifically liberal grounds rather than on egalitarian or efficiency grounds. I am aware of later “market socialism,” but that tradition usually involves state ownership with simulated market mechanisms, not Mill’s network of voluntary cooperatives. I am also aware of Mill’s own language of “partnership,” but that seems to describe capital-labor relations within a single firm rather than this system-level design. So does this particular hybrid—collective ownership plus liberal competitive pluralism—have a precise name in the literature? If there is no exact term, can you propose one, together with a clear definition and boundary conditions that distinguish it from market socialism, from libertarian socialism, and from Mill’s partnership idea? In either case, please spell out what the phenomenon is not, and where its edges lie.
