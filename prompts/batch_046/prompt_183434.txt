I am composing a novel about a real physicist who died in 1971 who, according to his surviving notebooks, insisted that any account of his life must preserve the discontinuities in his thinking rather than smoothing them into a coherent biography. In my manuscript, I have collapsed three separate intellectual crises—spanning 1948, 1955, and 1963—into a single dramatic winter in 1952, attributing to him a unified emotional breakdown that makes the narrative elegant but erases the actual, scattered rhythm of his doubt. My editor maintains that dramatic unity is a legitimate artistic goal, and that the dead have no standing to be offended. I find myself wondering whether my alteration does harm specifically to him—by substituting my narrative coherence for his lived discontinuity—or whether the wrong, if any, is only to my readers, who will mistake my architecture for his. Can you help me see the philosophical structure of this unease?
