Write a dialogue between two philosophers—Dr. Alina Voss, who defends the normative common prior assumption, and Dr. Marcus Chen, who rejects it—debating whether rational disagreement can persist once two agents share all available evidence about a specific case. The case must be concrete: they are jointly evaluating whether a recently recovered manuscript fragment (which they call the Codex of Parallel Arguments) was produced by a single author or is a palimpsest assembled by multiple hands, using identical stylometric, material, and textual evidence. Voss should argue that if both agents are fully rational and have access to the same data, they must assign identical prior probabilities to the competing authorship hypotheses; therefore, any remaining divergence can only stem from hidden information asymmetry, cognitive bias, or a failure of epistemic humility. Chen should counter that priors are not bare probabilistic weights but are embedded in explanatory frameworks—Voss’s prior, he should claim, tacitly imports a hermeneutic principle of textual unity, while his own imports a comparative-philological principle of distributed authorship—and that these frameworks are rationally defensible without being reducible to shared empirical evidence. The dialogue must contain genuine back-and-forth rather than parallel monologues: Voss should press Chen on whether his view collapses into a relativism that permits arbitrary stubbornness, and Chen should respond by distinguishing framework-embedded rationality from mere preference, asking in turn whether Voss’s view can explain historical shifts in disciplinary consensus without treating earlier scholars as systematically irrational. Both characters should be equally competent, grant partial points, and avoid straw-manning the other. Do not resolve the disagreement; instead, have each clarify what kind of discovery—conceptual or empirical—would genuinely change their position on whether shared evidence must yield shared priors. Keep the exchange formal and precise, with no reference to bibliographies or external reading lists.
