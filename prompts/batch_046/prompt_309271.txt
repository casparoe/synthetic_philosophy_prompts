I am presently drafting a novel in which the narrative structure relies upon a play-within-a-novel, and the characters within that inner drama frequently deploy moral language—"this betrayal is wrong", "justice demands we act"—within hypothetical and conditional constructions. In preparing the philosophical architecture of the work, I have found myself adopting a cluster of commitments about moral discourse that I had hoped were coherent, but which now seem to pull in opposing directions, and I should be grateful for an audit.

My commitments are these. First, I take expressivism about moral judgement to be substantially correct: to call an action wrong is primarily to express a non-cognitive attitude of disapproval rather than to ascribe a representational property. Second, I accept the force of the Frege-Geach problem: in embedded contexts—conditionals, negations, modal constructions—the speaker does not seem to express the relevant attitude towards the content of the embedded clause, yet the clause retains its meaning. Third, I am inclined to think that the solution lies not in attributing representational content to moral terms but in treating embedded uses as what I have come to call "dramaturgically suspended expression", where the attitude is present but bracketed by the fictional or hypothetical frame, much as an actor might voice a sentiment without personally avowing it. Fourth, I maintain that this suspension operates uniformly: whether the embedding is logical (a conditional) or dramaturgical (a character speaking within an inner play), the expressive mechanism is identical. Fifth, I nevertheless hold that there is a single, stable concept of wrongness that governs both suspended and direct expressions, and that without such stability the narrative logic of moral disagreement in my novel collapses into mere noise. Sixth, I believe that when my characters discuss moral philosophy in an unembedded, direct manner—say, in a salon scene outside the inner play—they are genuinely expressing attitudes, not merely echoing them.

Do these six positions hang together? In particular, I worry that my fourth commitment—the uniformity of suspension across logical and dramaturgical embedding—conflicts with my fifth commitment to a stable, unitary moral concept, since if the attitude is genuinely absent in embedded cases, what remains to secure conceptual identity except some representational core that my first commitment explicitly denies? I also wonder whether my third commitment, the notion of dramaturgical suspension, is merely a redescription of the problem rather than a solution to it. Which pair is in the gravest tension, and what is the least costly revision—must I abandon the uniformity thesis, weaken the anti-representationalism of the first commitment, or concede that the stable concept I require is itself an attitude-complex rather than a representational entity? I should also be curious whether my sixth commitment, distinguishing direct salon discourse from embedded dramatic speech, introduces an ad hoc boundary that undermines the uniformity I elsewhere insist upon.
