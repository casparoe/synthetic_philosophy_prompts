As a retired marine biologist who has recently begun studying philosophy of science and archaeological epistemology, I am assembling a dataset of conceptual prompts and would like your contribution to one entry. Please write a short story—approximately 800 to 1,000 words—in American English that dramatizes the problem of inferring institutional beliefs and social structures from material artifacts alone.

The protagonist should be a retired field scientist, perhaps an ecologist or oceanographer, who in later life has turned to philosophical archaeology. She is examining a hypothetical, non-real assemblage of objects from an invented early Holocene settlement I will call the Kharra salt-flat caches. The assemblage contains three specific finds: first, a set of standardized clay tokens bearing identical notches arranged in triangular patterns; second, a single obsidian blade, deliberately broken and wrapped in woven grass, that shows no microscopic wear from cutting; and third, a circular ring of post-holes enclosing an empty interior space with no domestic refuse whatsoever.

The philosophical tension should arise from a rigorous interpretive deadlock. One reading holds that the tokens record quantifiable exchange values, implying an emergent market institution grounded in beliefs about equivalence and deferred obligation. A rival reading—which must be treated with equal seriousness—proposes that the tokens are records of communal debt that were ritually destroyed: the broken blade symbolizes cancellation, and the post-circle marks the site of periodic collective erasure of obligation, with no belief in enduring quantifiable value at all.

Your story should show the protagonist recognizing that both interpretations are fully consistent with the physical evidence, and that choosing between them requires importing an auxiliary theory about social cognition—say, whether these people possessed a concept of trans-temporal contractual obligation—that is not itself recoverable from clay, stone, or post-holes. The narrative should avoid resolving the ambiguity with a sudden discovery or hidden inscription; instead, let the ambiguity itself become the dramatic center, perhaps through the protagonist’s realization that her scientific training in causal inference from traces does not translate cleanly into the interpretation of intentional institutions.

Please do not include bibliographies, citations, or references to specific scholarly works. The story should stand as a self-contained philosophical fiction, accessible to a reader without specialized archaeological knowledge, but precise enough that it does not collapse into generic allegory.
