Construct a decision tree for a graduate seminar on the problem of other minds that sorts philosophical positions by their answers to concrete diagnostic scenarios rather than by self-identified labels. The tree should be built from the logical space of three binary distinctions: (A) Is first-person phenomenal access the only direct route to knowledge of consciousness, or can third-person functional and behavioral data provide direct—rather than merely inferential—warrant? (B) Does mental attribution require a shared biological or evolutionary substrate, or is it substrate-independent? (C) If a being exhibits no observable behavior that typically indicates mindedness, is that absence always epistemically neutral, or can it provide defeasible evidence against attributing a mind?

These three distinctions yield eight logical combinations; map each combination to a named position where one exists (for example, Cartesian privacy, direct perceptual realism, analogical inference, functionalist inference-to-best-explanation, substrate-restricted animalism, eliminativism about other minds, synthetic-mind functionalism, and the view that absence of behavior is decisive). For any unoccupied combination, provide a one-sentence diagnosis of why it is incoherent, unstable, or merely unexplored.

For each internal node, phrase the branching question as a response to a specific scenario that a student can answer yes or no without knowing philosophical jargon: a locked-in patient communicating only through eye-tracking; a behaviorally perfect duplicate with no neural activity whatsoever; an octopus solving novel puzzles with a nervous system lacking any mammalian analog; and an artificial system with fully readable internal-state architecture but no evolutionary history that reports no qualitative experience. The tree should have exactly eight leaves.

Additionally, flag at least one pair of named positions that your tree cannot separate, explaining which shared commitment causes them to fall on the same leaf. Finally, include a brief note on how a synthetic case—an AI whose every internal state is transparent to an observer but which never behaves as if in pain—tests the boundary between answers B and C, and state whether any existing named view comfortably occupies that quadrant or whether it represents a genuinely unexplored position. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; the instrument should be usable by a student with no external resources. The audience is graduate students in philosophy of mind.
