I'm drafting a six-week prospectus for a summer workshop in ancient philosophy, and I want to pitch a project that is genuinely open but requires no archival or philological work beyond close reading. The standard literature on Aristotle's views on money and exchange tends to rehearse the familiar distinction in the Politics between natural household management (oikonomikē) and unnatural wealth-getting (chrēmatistikē), and usually concludes either that Aristotle offers a proto-critique of unlimited accumulation or that he is an unrecoverable elitist who naturalizes social hierarchy. I think both readings miss a conceptual ambiguity in his account of exchange justice that, once made explicit, reshuffles the interpretive options entirely.

Specifically, Aristotle treats exchange as a form of corrective justice governed by proportionality, but he also insists that natural acquisition is limited by need (chreia). The ambiguity is whether need functions as a binary threshold—either a household has satisfied its necessary requirements or it has not—or as a scalar magnitude with graded degrees of urgency. If need is binary, then any accumulation beyond the threshold is automatically unjust, and interest-bearing loans (tokos) are condemned because they treat money as self-breeding rather than as a medium for satisfying discrete needs. But if need is scalar, then surplus can be ranked by urgency, and the prohibition on usury might depend not on the ontology of money but on whether the lender's surplus need is less urgent than the borrower's present need. This would mean Aristotle's critique of chrematistics is more nuanced than the standard "money is barren" reading suggests, and it might even leave room for a limited defense of credit under conditions of unequal urgency—without making him a modern economist.

My proposal asks which interpretation—binary or scalar—is textually and philosophically more defensible, and whether resolving the ambiguity makes Aristotle's economic thought more coherent overall rather than more anachronistically palatable. The deliverable would be a conceptual map of the two readings, an assessment of which passages in the Politics and the Ethics support each, and an argument about what each interpretation costs. I will treat this as a philosophical rather than historical-reconstructive project: the method is conceptual analysis of the exchange-justice framework, not a survey of Hellenistic economic practice. Failure conditions are explicit—if the scalar reading cannot be squared with Aristotle's claim that money was invented only for exchange and not for increase, then the ambiguity dissolves and the standard reading stands; if both readings fit the texts equally well but the scalar one requires importing modern economic concepts, then the project fails to show that the ambiguity is genuinely Aristotelian rather than projected.

The scope is realistic for six weeks: one week framing the binary/scalar distinction in contemporary philosophy of economics, two weeks mapping it onto the exchange-justice texts, two weeks testing coherence against the usury prohibition, and one week drafting the result as a workshop paper of about 5,000 words. I will supply all precise citations and textual references myself; please do not include a bibliography, reading list, or citation requests in the proposal. Please write this in a formal register suitable for a workshop application, arguing that the question is both tractable and worth asking. Finally, please indicate how confident you are in the interpretive framing—especially in the claim that this ambiguity is present in Aristotle rather than imported from modern theory—and clearly identify the single point where your confidence is lowest.
