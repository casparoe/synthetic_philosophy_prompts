A colleague recently summarised the exchange between Avishai Margalit and David Rieff on remembrance and forgetting in roughly the following terms. Margalit, she said, defends an unconditional ethical duty to remember atrocities, grounded in an ethics of care directed towards the dead: because victims can no longer speak for themselves, we owe them remembrance as the only form of justice available when legal redress fails, and this obligation is strongest within thick relationships—families, communities, nations—where shared memory sustains moral identity. Rieff, by contrast, treats collective remembrance as a kind of political addiction; in societies emerging from mass violence, state-sponsored memorialisation freezes trauma in public space, turning private grief into a permanent instrument of retaliation, so he advocates institutional forgetting—specifically, amnesty coupled with the deliberate non-archiving of testimonies—to break cycles of revenge. From this characterisation, my colleague drew two objections. First, she argued that Margalit’s position collapses into moral sentimentalism: if remembrance is owed primarily within thick relationships, strangers possess no ethical basis to maintain museums, school curricula, or monuments concerning distant genocides, reducing universal commemoration to an act of optional charity. Second, she claimed Rieff’s prescription is self-defeating, since any bureaucratic apparatus that suppresses archives necessarily becomes its own form of organised remembrance—this time of what must be hidden—thereby reproducing the very fixation on the past that it seeks to eliminate. She therefore concluded that the dispute is largely rhetorical: both authors agree that unregulated collective memory is dangerous, yet neither offers a practical decision procedure—say, a clear criterion distinguishing healthy commemoration from toxic fixation—so the choice between them ultimately reduces to temperament rather than argument.

I suspect this summary is too neat and that it misrepresents both thinkers in ways I cannot identify precisely. Could you audit its fairness? Specifically: which parts accurately capture Margalit’s and Rieff’s actual claims, which parts conflate positions they keep distinct, and do my colleague’s two objections genuinely follow? I am especially interested in whether a defensible middle position survives the correction—perhaps one that separates a mnemonic obligation, owed to victims regardless of relational thickness, from commemorative practice, the public rituals and institutions through which a society discharges that obligation—and whether such a distinction answers both objections without collapsing into the rhetorical stalemate she describes.
