I have been working through some formal intergenerational welfare models for a course on rationalist ethics and longtermism, and I have become stuck on what seems to be a contradiction in the literature on pure time preference. I understand that Ramsey's rule decomposes the social discount rate into a pure time-preference component, an elasticity-of-marginal-utility term scaled by consumption growth, and a catastrophic-risk adjustment. I also take Parfit's argument—that discounting future welfare merely because it lies in the future is ethically arbitrary—to be largely correct, and I gather that this rejection of positive pure time preference is standard among longtermist economists.

My confusion arises when I try to reconcile this with Koopmans's representation result for stationary preferences over infinite horizons, which appears to require a constant exponential discount factor if an individual agent is to avoid dynamic inconsistency. If we genuinely set pure time preference to zero, and if we are modelling a post-scarcity civilisation with negligible consumption growth, then the Ramsey rate collapses to little more than the extinction hazard rate. In that case, does not any use of a constant exponential discount factor—such as those still employed in integrated assessment models for climate damage—covertly reintroduce a positive pure rate? Or is the mistake mine in applying Koopmans's individual-choice axioms to social choice, where one ought instead to use a Harsanyi-style aggregation of individual utilities with time-neutral weights?

I was debating this with friends on a rationalist forum and we wondered whether Weitzman's gamma discounting genuinely resolves the tension, or merely obscures it by endogenising the rate to growth-rate uncertainty. Could you identify the precise false presupposition in my reasoning? Please write your answer in British English.
