I'm a planner at a regional transit authority and every quarter I have to write a recommendation on whether to extend a bus line into a low-income suburb. The board has to sign off, there's an equity officer who co-signs, and I usually have about 90 minutes before the meeting. I want a reusable decision procedue — not just one-time advice — grounded in Carnap's philosophy of planning and value. Specifically I want to use his idea that value judgments aren't truth-apt but can be rationalized through means-end analysis, his internal/external question distinction (choosing a framework vs. asking within it), his concept of explication to pin down terms like 'equity,' and his inductive-logic notion that predictions should carry a degree of confirmation rather than being called 'likely.'

Write me a step-by-step flowchart in words that I can run in order. At each step include a concrete test or check I can apply with the data I actually have. The procedure should: (1) force explicit explication of the key terms in the proposal; (2) seperate internal empirical questions from external framework-choice questions; (3) apply means-end rationality to any value conflict; (4) flag pseudoproblems — questions that sound factual but are actually framework disputes in disguise; (5) assign approximate degrees of confirmation to ridership and cost predictions, with rules for what to do when I don't have numbers. Mark every point where judgment can't be eliminated, especially in picking the framework and weighting values.

Then apply it to three hypothetical cases: Case A — high predicted ridership but the board is ideologically opposed to subsidies; Case B — low ridership but strong equity claims with ambiguous data; Case C — the equity officer and I disagree on whether 'access' should be defined as travel time or job connectivity, and the ridership forecast is right in the middle. Make sure one case gives an answer I'll probably dislike (maybe the procedure forces a 'no' in Case A because the value framework hasn't been internally justified). Finally, say clearly what the procedure cannot do — for instance, it can't resolve framework choice by empirical fact alone, and it can't generate probabilities out of nothing.

Keep it practical — I don't need a bibliography, just something I can use repeatedly.
