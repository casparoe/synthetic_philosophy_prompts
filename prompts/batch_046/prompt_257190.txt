A philosopher of mind has delivered consistent verdicts on the unity of consciousness across the following neurological conditions: in a neurologically typical adult she affirms one conscious subject; in a classic split-brain patient whose hemispheres, isolated by complete callosotomy, generate contradictory verbal and manual responses she denies unity and describes two independent streams; in a patient with complete agenesis of the corpus callosum but intact anterior commissure and fully integrated behavior, memory, and first-person report she affirms unity; in an adult who underwent left hemispherectomy in childhood, with the right hemisphere subsequently assuming language and self-reference, she affirms a single continuous subject; and in locked-in syndrome with preserved internal awareness and cognition but near-total paralysis of voluntary movement she affirms unity.

Consider now a novel case: an adult split-brain patient (complete callosotomy) who has been fitted with an artificial bidirectional neural interface that transmits sensory and mnemonic signals between hemispheres at latencies comparable to natural callosal transmission. Each hemisphere retains independent episodic encoding and can initiate voluntary actions through separate spinal pathways. Under experimental conditions both hemispheres agree on perceptual judgments and can access each other’s declarative memories in real time, yet they maintain dissociable emotional valence responses—the left reports calm about a stimulus that the right simultaneously reports, via an independent response channel, as intensely aversive—and each refers to itself as “I” while describing the other as “my other half” rather than as an external agent or as part of itself.

What verdict should she deliver to remain consistent with her track record, and which of her earlier rulings is the closest analog? Identify at least two distinct principles that jointly accommodate all her prior judgments, state what each principle predicts about this new patient, give your best-guess verdict with reasons for preferring it, and describe one further hypothetical case that would discriminate between those two principles.
