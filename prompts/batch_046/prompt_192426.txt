After retiring from a long career in comparative physiology, I have recently begun working through the philosophy of mind and value theory in a structured way, and I find myself returning repeatedly to the problem of why pain is bad rather than merely informative. In a graduate seminar I am auditing, one of the instructors—who holds a representationalist view of consciousness but rejects reductive physicalism—has laid out a consistent pattern of judgments about suffering across a range of clinical and hypothetical cases. I want to understand the principle behind her track record well enough to predict her response to a borderline case I have constructed, but I am also interested in the ambiguity of her position: I suspect more than one rule could accommodate everything she has said so far.

Here are her verdicts. First, regarding a patient with complete congenital insensitivity to pain—no nociception, no withdrawal reflexes, and no protective behavior—she judges that there is no suffering whatsoever, only a dangerous absence of a biological alarm system. Second, regarding a classic phantom-limb case, in which vivid suffering occurs in the absence of any peripheral tissue damage or nociceptive input, she insists that this is genuinely bad suffering, fully on par with veridical pain, because the affective and self-implicating dimensions remain intact. Third, regarding pain asymbolia—where nociception and tissue damage are present, but the affective aversion is selectively absent, so the patient reports feeling the sensation clearly yet states that it does not bother him—she rules that this is not genuinely bad suffering, comparing it to hearing a loud alarm without feeling any urgency or threat. Fourth, regarding a decerebrate animal preparation that exhibits coordinated reflex withdrawal from noxious heat but lacks any cortical integration or behavioral evidence of an integrated phenomenal field, she denies that suffering occurs, describing the response as a spinal reflex arc without a subject for whom anything is bad.

Now consider a hypothetical scenario I will call pure evaluative registration, inspired by recent speculative models in interoceptive predictive processing but not corresponding to any documented clinical syndrome. In this case, a subject possesses intact nociceptive pathways, a robust minimal self-model, and a strongly motivating explicit judgment—"This signal indicates severe damage to my body and must be terminated immediately"—integrated with goal-directed behavior to avoid the stimulus. Yet, owing to a selective disruption of affective tagging pathways, the subject reports no phenomenal "hurting," no emotional distress, and no negative affect whatsoever; there is only the cold, urgent cognitive conviction that the state is harmful. I am less interested in the bare answer than in the reasoning: what rule or rules best explain her four earlier verdicts, what each predicts about pure evaluative registration, which prediction you take to be best supported, and—crucially—which further hypothetical case would most cleanly distinguish the competing rules from one another.

Please present your analysis by first identifying at least two distinct principles that fit her earlier judgments, then stating what each predicts for the pure evaluative registration case, giving your best-guess verdict with the reason for preferring that rule, and naming the decisive further case.
