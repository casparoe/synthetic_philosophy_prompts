Write the desiderata that any adequate theory of moral aggregation in rescue cases must satisfy, with particular attention to whether and how the number of lives saved should determine choice. For each requirement, give the motivating case or conceptual tension behind it: (1) the classic one-versus-many rescue and its symmetric variants; (2) near-parity cases in which the numerical difference lies within an epistemic margin of error, so that precise headcounts should not drive the decision; (3) heterogeneous harm profiles, such as saving five people from serious disability versus one from death, where aggregation across different goods is required; (4) the distinction between aggregating benefits within a single life and aggregating across separate lives, and why a theory must not collapse them; (5) consistency with intuitions about exact ties and near-ties without generating an implausible continuous weighting function; and (6) an explanation of why, if numbers genuinely do not count, saving the larger group remains permissible but not obligatory. Flag any pair of desiderata you take to be jointly unsatisfiable—especially whether accommodating near-parity indifference conflicts with maintaining a sharp boundary against full aggregation. Then briefly score the three main contenders—pure non-aggregationism in the Taurek vein, weighted prioritarian aggregation, and a threshold-sensitive view—against your list, identifying which single desideratum does the most damage to the purest form of each.
