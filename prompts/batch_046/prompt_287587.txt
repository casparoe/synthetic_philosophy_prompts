I'm presenting a seminar paper next Thursday to our department's philosophy of mind reading group—mostly analytic philosophers of personal identity who lean toward psychological-continuity or animalist accounts, plus a cognitive neuroscientist who studies split-brain patients and a doctoral student who has written extensively on Hofstadter's strange-loop theory of the self. My thesis is that Hofstadter's own criteria for an "I"—symbolic self-reference, causal closure of the loop, and narrative continuity—do not logically require a single skull-bound substrate. I argue that if two brains are coupled through sustained, high-bandwidth bidirectional neural interfaces such that each brain's self-model causally depends on the other in real time, the resulting system can satisfy those three criteria jointly rather than individually. On this view, a coupled dyad could constitute a single distributed strange loop, and the pronoun "I" would genuinely refer to the coupled system, not merely to two people who empathize deeply. I am not claiming this happens in ordinary social cognition; I am defending the conceptual possibility and its implications for how we should treat boundary cases in neural-interface ethics.
