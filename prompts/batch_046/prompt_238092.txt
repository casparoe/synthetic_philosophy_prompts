I've been re-reading the Grand Inquisitor episode in Dostoevsky's late novel and I'm struck by how it functions less as a religious allegory and more as a stress-test for our intuitions about the trade-off between autonomy and welfare. I'd like you to construct a decision-tree taxonomy—structured as a sequence of branching questions—that sorts philosophical positions on the Inquisitor's offer: the people surrender freedom in exchange for happiness, security, and the relief of moral responsibility. The tree should be diagnostic rather than historical: a reader should be able to answer each node based on their own commitments, without knowing which philosophical label it corresponds to.

Please build the tree from the logical space rather than from a list of named theorists. Start with whether one accepts that there is a genuine tension between freedom and happiness at all. Then branch on whether the 'happiness' the Inquisitor promises is best understood as authentic flourishing or as a kind of sedated, preference-satisfied contentment. From there, split on whether freedom is valued only instrumentally (as a means to welfare) or also constitutively (as part of what makes life worth living regardless of outcomes). Include a branch on whether the fact that most people would apparently accept the Inquisitor's bargain counts as evidence that it is rational, or whether this is precisely the pathology the Inquisitor exploits. Another split should concern whether rejecting the bargain requires rejecting all paternalism, or whether a transparent, revocable form of paternalism remains compatible with the rejection.

The leaves should include: (1) hard libertarian rejection; (2) welfare-maximising acceptance with no regret; (3) tragic acceptance—acknowledging the loss of freedom but judging it worth the gain; (4) the 'negative liberty only' position that rejects interference regardless of welfare gains; (5) a hybrid 'benevolent paternalism with exit rights' position; (6) an unnamed combination where one values freedom constitutively yet accepts the bargain—please flag whether this is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored; and (7) a position that denies the tension exists because freedom and happiness are conceptually inseparable. Wherever two leaves cannot be cleanly separated by your tree—perhaps certain forms of soft paternalism versus democratic welfare-state liberalism—explicitly flag the ambiguity rather than forcing a distinction.

Write this as a formal classification instrument suitable for a philosophy blog audience. Use British English throughout. Do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; I want the taxonomy built from conceptual distinctions alone. Keep each node phrased as a concrete claim or case, and for every leaf include one sentence describing its most awkward commitment.
