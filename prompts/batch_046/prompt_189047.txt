I serve as the faculty representative on the University Research Equity Board, and we must vote next month on the proposed Resource Stabilization and Equity Protocol. Under the protocol, any department whose per-capita research funding exceeds 1.8 times the university median must remit fifteen percent of its operating budget to central administration; the remitted funds enter a stabilization reserve rather than being transferred as direct grants to underfunded units, because eligibility rules and administrative overhead historically allow only about forty percent of such pools to reach lower-funded departments. The board needs a formal recommendation—approve or reject—together with reasoning that treats this not as a generic fairness debate but as an instance of prioritarian distributive reasoning complicated by what I will call transfer viscosity: the idea that institutional resources dissipate value as they move through bureaucratic layers, so that harming the well-funded does not automatically translate into proportionate benefit for the worse-off.

The memo should distinguish direct distributive leveling, in which resources are taken from the well-off and delivered to the worse-off with minimal decay, from indirect institutional leveling, in which resources are withdrawn into a high-decay pool whose eventual distribution is uncertain and partial. It should apply prioritarian analysis under this viscosity constraint, weighing whether the special moral urgency of benefiting the worse-off survives when the mechanism of benefit is so inefficient, and it should include an honest, fully developed account of the strongest consideration on the losing side—specifically, whether the symbolic value or long-run institutional equilibrium achieved by reducing visible funding disparity can justify an immediate distributive loss that the viscosity framework predicts will largely evaporate before reaching its intended recipients. Write in formal institutional voice, with an explicit bottom-line recommendation, an analysis readable by board members without philosophical training, and no bibliography, reading list, or citations; the reasoning must stand on its own without reference to texts the board cannot access.
