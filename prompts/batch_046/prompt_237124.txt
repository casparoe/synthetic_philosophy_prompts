I am designing a single 75-minute session for a philosophy-of-mind reading group of sharp non-specialists—mostly lawyers and engineers—who understand basic neuroscience but have not read Hofstadter. The objective is to render intelligible Hofstadter's claim that the self is not a private token inside one skull but a substrate-independent pattern that can, in principle, be distributed across multiple biological brains, with no privileged original.

Construct the teaching plan, structured as an ordered dependency graph rather than a lecture transcript. Specifically:

1. Conceptual prerequisites: Identify the two distinctions that must be secured before the distributed-self thesis can be evaluated rather than dismissed—one concerning the ontology of patterns versus substrates, one concerning the conditions for self-reference—and specify the exact order in which they must be introduced, with justification for that sequencing.

2. Carrying case: Propose one concrete scenario (neurological, technological, or speculative) that demonstrates partial, not binary, distribution: a situation in which two brains share enough of the relevant pattern that neither can claim exclusive ownership of the "I," yet neither is a perfect duplicate. State precisely what the case must include to sustain the pattern-identity claim and what it must exclude to avoid collapsing into either Cartesian dualism or eliminative materialism.

3. Misconception map: Anticipate the three predictable objections in the order they will arise—(a) the Cartesian recoil that an "I" cannot be in two places, (b) the cloning error that treats distributed selfhood as mere replication, and (c) the reductionist slide that dissolves selfhood into atoms. For each, specify the point in the arc where it should be intercepted and the conceptual redirect that absorbs it without breaking momentum.

4. Diagnostic check: Formulate a single end-of-session question or exercise that distinguishes genuine comprehension of pattern-based identity from mere acceptance (or discomfort with) the idea of brain duplication.

5. Deliberate omissions: List what must be left out—specifically, appeals to quantum consciousness, citations of particular neuroscientific studies, and comparative engagement with other philosophers of personal identity—and justify each omission for this audience.

The deliverable should be a formal plan with marked dependencies, not an essay or a list of readings.
