okay so i've been stewing on moral worth and acting for the right reasons, specifically whether there's any coherent version of "motive pluralism" that isn't just kantian monism in a wig. i have these verdicts about different agents and i cant square them without dropping something.

case 1: a woman jumps into a freezing river to save a kid. she doesnt think "this is my duty" or "utility maximized" — she just feels this overwhelming empathic pull and acts immediately. to me she has full moral worth, maybe more than someone who deliberates for thirty seconds.

case 2: a guy does the exact same rescue but he ran a quick mental cost-benefit and decided saving the kid is optimal for overall welfare. he saves the kid. i think he's morally good but his moral worth feels thinner, like he's treating morality as accounting software.

case 3: a firefighter pulls people from a burning building out of pure trained reflex — no emotion, no moral reasoning, just muscle memory from years of drills. i want to say she gets professional praise but not deep moral credit? but then why is that different from case 1 if both bypass deliberation?

case 4: a person with severe OCD checks on an isolated elderly neighbor every single day. it's part of their compulsive routine — they feel intense anxiety if they skip. the neighbor genuinely benefits and depends on it. does the compulsion cancel the moral worth? part of me wants to say yes because the motive is pathological; part says consistency and benefit should count.

case 5: an AI safety system detects carbon monoxide and evacuates a building. no intentions, no feelings, just pattern matching and actuators. clearly no moral worth, but is that just speciesism, or is there something in the architecture that's actually missing?

case 6: someone donates anonymously to a food bank, but only because they feel crushing guilt about being wealthy. if the guilt disappeared, they'd stop immediately. i think this gets shaky, parasitic credit — the act is right but the agent isn't standing in the right relation to it.

case 7: someone donates anonymously, feels zero emotional reward, finds it genuinely unpleasant, but does it because they think it's right. classic kantian hero, but i actually feel less sure this should outrank case 1.

my problem: every principle i try either collapses into "only deliberate rational choice counts" which kills case 1, or "any stable disposition that produces good counts" which gives too much to case 4 and maybe even case 5. is there a middle-ground principle — maybe about the agent recognizing the other as a source of reasons rather than as a trigger for feeling or calculation — that can order these? and if so, which verdict do i have to drop? also what does it say about a hypothetical case 8: a person with brain damage who can only act on immediate emotional impulses but consistently does the right thing across decades, with no reflective access to why? does that recieve partial credit or full? i want the principle stated precisely, with the clause each case motivates, and an honest admission of which intuition it cant save.
