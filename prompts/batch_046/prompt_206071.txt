I'm a doctoral student in philosophy of language working on conceptual change, and I've noticed that debates about Quine's critique of the analytic-synthetic distinction seem to have reached a peculiar stalemate: the Grice-Strawson revival and Boghossian's work on implicit definition have been thoroughly discussed since the 1990s, yet neither side has conceded ground, and recent discussions in conceptual engineering don't seem to resolve the original tension. My working theory is that the disagreement has stalled not because either side is wrong about the facts, but because "analyticity" is being asked to do incompatible work: in one framework it names a normative relation between a statement and the rules of a linguistic practice; in another, it names a cognitive default in conceptual architecture that makes certain revisions psychologically unavailable. Is this the right diagnosis—that the sides are answering different questions bundled under one term—or is there a substantive formal or empirical disagreement hiding behind it, perhaps about the structure of belief revision or the results of concept-acquisition studies? I want a diagnosis supported by what Quine actually argues about reductionism and the revisability of statements, and by what the defenders of analyticity actually claim about implicit definition and conventional meaning, not a historical survey. And I want an honest assessment of whether a formal result in belief revision, an empirical finding about conceptual cores, or a clear conceptual distinction could actually move the debate forward, or whether progress requires abandoning the unified question altogether.
