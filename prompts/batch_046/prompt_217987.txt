Construct a diagnostic taxonomy—structured as a decision tree—of the interpretations of what Descartes's cogito argument actually establishes about the self. The tree should begin from the shared premise that the inference from thinking to being is valid in some form, and should branch only through concrete commitments on which interpretations genuinely diverge, not through labels that a student could pick without understanding the case.

The tree must include the following positions as leaves:
- Standard Cartesian substance dualism: an enduring res cogitans whose essence is thought, established with first-person certainty.
- Deflationary occurrence view: only the occurrence of thinking is proved; no thinker, enduring or momentary, is established.
- Momentary Cartesian subject: a thinker is established, but only as a non-enduring particular existing at the present instant—something analogous to applying a bundle-style ontology to first-person awareness.
- Kantian formal subject: the I functions as a necessary unity of apperception but is not knowable as an object or substance.
- Unnamed hybrid: an enduring thinker is established, but the cogito does not determine that its nature is essentially mental; the argument proves existence without fixing essence.

The branching nodes should be answerable without knowing philosophical labels. Use concrete diagnostic questions such as:
- Does the argument prove that there is a thinker, or only that thinking is occurring?
- If a thinker exists, does the proof license belief that it persists through time, or only that it exists now?
- Does the first-person authority gained extend to the content and kind of one's mental states, or only to the bare fact that one exists?
- Is the I that thinks a particular substance, or a logical/formal condition required for the unity of thought?

For each leaf, provide: (a) the name or designation (use "unnamed" if no named tradition holds exactly that combination), (b) one sentence summarizing its core commitment, and (c) its most awkward obligation—the counter-intuitive consequence defenders must accept that opponents typically reject.

Then map the full logical space of combinations across your branch questions. Identify any unoccupied cells—for example, a view that denies any thinker but asserts diachronic persistence of mental content—and briefly diagnose whether the gap is due to incoherence, unattractiveness, or mere neglect. Also flag any pair of named leaves that your tree's questions fail to genuinely separate, explaining why the distinction collapses at the level of case-based commitments.

The audience is an upper-level philosophy seminar familiar with Meditations I-II but not with specialist secondary literature. Keep the tree to between six and ten leaves. Do not ask for bibliographies, reading lists, citations, or specific editions. Do not put any real textual passage inside quotation marks; paraphrase references to texts instead. Ensure every node turns on what one should say about a concrete scenario—such as whether the cogito would still apply to you after gradual neuronal replacement—rather than asking which theoretical label someone prefers.
