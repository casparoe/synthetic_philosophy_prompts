I am drafting a long-form feature on whether Gricean pragmatics can survive the rise of algorithmically mediated scientific discourse, and I have constructed an argument that seems to collapse Grice’s central distinction altogether. Here is the chain as I see it.

(1) For Grice, speaker meaning in indirect cases requires that an audience recover an intended implicature by recognizing that the speaker has flouted one of the conversational maxims—Quantity, Quality, Relation, or Manner—while remaining cooperative overall.
(2) This recovery depends essentially on attributing to the speaker a specific communicative intention: the audience must recognize that the speaker intends them to derive q from p, and that this recognition is part of their reason for deriving q.
(3) In contemporary scientific and technical communication, a growing share of text is produced or mediated by systems—predictive language models, template-driven reporting tools, or institutional style algorithms—that do not possess mental states or communicative intentions in the relevant sense.
(4) Nevertheless, human readers consistently recover indirect meanings from these outputs: an algorithmically generated risk assessment that describes a systemic failure as not entirely unexpected is read as implicating negligence; an anonymized peer-review template that notes the authors have certainly been ambitious is read as implicating overreach.
(5) If Grice’s account is correct, these recoveries are illusory, because there is no speaker whose intention could ground the implicature.
(6) But if they are illusory, then the same reasoning applies to any text for which the producer’s mental states are opaque or distributed—anonymous policy briefs, translated technical manuals, collective authorship—which would strip speaker meaning from a substantial portion of institutional knowledge.
(7) To preserve Grice, one might restrict the account to face-to-face conversation, but then it becomes a local theory of oral interaction rather than a general account of speaker meaning, and it offers no guidance for interpreting the texts that actually structure scientific and public life.
(8) The alternative is to allow that implicatures can be generated by conventional textual patterns or institutional norms without being anchored in a specific speaker’s intention.
(9) Yet once conventional patterns can generate implicatures, they can also generate literal content, and Grice’s boundary between what is said (conventionally determined) and what is implicated (intentionally calculated) dissolves.
(10) So either Grice’s framework eliminates speaker meaning from modern mediated discourse, or it abandons the said/implicated distinction that defines it.

I want to keep (1) and (2) intact—I am not prepared to jettison intentionalism about speaker meaning—and I also want to preserve (4), because the phenomenology of reading these texts seems undeniable. That leaves me squeezed between (5) and (9). Which step should I reject, and what does that rejection cost? In particular, is there a way to maintain that mediated texts carry genuine speaker meaning without making every conventional pattern an implicature generator, or does saving Grice require a sharper separation between speaker-based and system-based communication than this argument allows?
