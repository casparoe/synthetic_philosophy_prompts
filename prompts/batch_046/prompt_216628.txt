I am working through the prisoner's dilemma for an independent study, and I think I have resolved the one-shot case by extending the logic of iteration. In the standard iterated game, cooperation survives because strategies like tit-for-tat link future payoffs to present behavior. Now imagine a population where every pair interacts only once—there is no direct repetition—but every agent can access a complete, public record of every other agent's prior choices and can selectively partner with those who cooperated before. I am calling this structure distributed iteration: since each decision still carries a shadow of the future across the population, the evolutionary pressure should be identical to that in the repeated two-player game. Yet evolutionary biology still treats single-interaction populations as defection traps. What exactly distinguishes indirect reciprocity mediated by partner choice from true iteration, and why does that distinction prevent cooperation from becoming inevitable in my scenario?
