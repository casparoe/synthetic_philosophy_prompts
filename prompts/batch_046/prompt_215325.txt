hey so i was reading some old rationalist threads about consequentialist cluelessness and it got me wondering about the DDT ban in 72. like, the standard story is carson publishes silent spring, eagle populations crash, epa bans it, and then malaria comes roaring back in parts of africa and asia because we lost the cheap indoor spray. but i want to think about this through the cluelessness lens specifically, not just a naive cost-benefit.

the decision makers in 72 had decent data on raptor reproduction and some vague epidemiologcal models, but they clearly couldn't have predicted the exact trajectory of malaria resurgance over the next fifty years, or the knock-on agricultural effects when pests returned, or whether ddt resistence in mosquitos would have evolved faster under continued use. so analyze the 72 ban as a case study in consequentialist cluelessness. who actually faced the cluelessness — the epa? congress? the WHO? was it parameter cluelessness about malaria rates, or structural cluelessness about ecosystem feedback loops? and does the episode support the cluelessness objection, or does it cut the other way because the immediate harms to birds were visible while the distant harms were purely speculative?

i also want to push on something a bit more in the weeds: there's this asymmetry idea that cluelessness isn't symmetric between action and inaction — banning DDT is an intervention with unknown long-run effects, but keeping it legal is also an intervention with unknown effects. apply that to the ban. does it collapse the distinction between interventionist and status-quo positions? and what does the case show about whether consequentialists should default to some precautionary principle, or is that just cluelessness wearing a different hat?
