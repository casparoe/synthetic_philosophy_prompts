Next week I present at our philosophy of mind workshop a thesis that tries to split the difference between Searle’s biological naturalism and Dennett’s derived-intentionalism without collapsing into either camp. I argue that the Chinese Room shows formal syntax is insufficient for semantics, but that the missing ingredient is not specifically carbon-based biology; rather, it is membership in a participatory normative practice that confers deontic status on states. On my view, intentionality is neither an intrinsic biological property of brains, as Searle holds, nor a purely observer-relative ascription like that of a thermostat, as Dennett suggests, but what I call institutionally original: constituted by shared normative commitments that generate content in a way that is not reducible to functional role or physical substrate alone. I will defend this against the standard objections from both sides, and I need to be ready for a room that has heard these moves before.

The audience is precisely this: two senior philosophers firmly committed to Searle’s biological naturalism, who regard any departure from intrinsic brain-based intentionality as a slide into eliminativism; one cognitive scientist who is an explicit Dennett-style functionalist and treats intentional ascription as a predictive heuristic with no deeper ontology; and three graduate students in cognitive science who are agnostic but suspicious that the entire debate is a category mistake about information processing. They care about whether my middle position is stable or just a verbal compromise, they will press me on whether normative practices can do explanatory work without being mysterious, and they will be quick to ask whether my institutional-originality is just derived intentionality with extra steps.

Please prepare my Q&A defense sheet. List the objections in roughly the order they will come, phrased in the vocabulary each faction would actually use, and rank them by how damaging they are to the proposal rather than to my ego. For each serious objection, provide a concise reply I can deliver in about two minutes, flag the weakest point in that reply, and note what I should concede up front rather than defend to death. Identify the one objection I am hoping nobody raises—the one that targets the boundary between my institutional originality and Dennett’s derived ascription, specifically asking why normative practices are not just another layer of observer-relative interpretation—and sketch a survival strategy that does not collapse me into either biological naturalism or pure functionalism. Do not ask for bibliographies, citation lists, or references to specific texts I should read; I need answers I can generate from reasoning and conceptual analysis alone, without access to outside sources.
