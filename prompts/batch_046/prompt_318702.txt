The student wrote:

"Darwin's inversion reverses the design argument. Rather than inferring an intelligent cause from complex adaptation, he shows that a blind process—natural selection acting on heritable variation—can accumulate advantageous changes over deep time. He strengthens this through analogy to artificial selection: breeders possess comprehension and deliberately select traits, but nature achieves comparable results without any understanding because variation is random with respect to fitness and survival operates automatically. Hence nature exhibits the competence to adapt organisms without any comprehension of the end state.

Turing performs a parallel inversion. Instead of asking whether a computer possesses internal mental states or genuinely understands symbols, he proposes judging thought solely by observable behavior. His imitation game shifts the criterion from internal comprehension to external performance: if a machine's outputs are indistinguishable from a human's, we have no more reason to deny it thought than we do for another person. Like Darwin, Turing separates competence (passing the behavioral test) from comprehension (having subjective awareness).

The structural parallel is that both thinkers replace an appeal to hidden internal capacities—design intelligence or mental substance—with an observable mechanism or criterion. The disanalogy is that Darwin's account is genuinely causal and mechanistic, operating on populations across generations, whereas Turing's is operational and definitional: he sets a functional equivalence threshold rather than explaining how computation produces understanding. Additionally, Darwin's use of artificial selection is genuinely analogical—breeders and nature share a structural pattern—whereas Turing does not analogize machines to humans but instead establishes a direct behavioral equivalence."
