As a retired field ecologist who has lately been working through the philosophy of mind, I now sit on an interdisciplinary review board that evaluates proposals involving synthetic neural tissues, distributed invertebrate networks, and post-surgical human cognition studies. We need a reusable decision procedure—something like a structured audit rather than a philosophical essay—to determine, under a working panpsychist assumption, whether a given system constitutes a single macro-level conscious subject or merely an aggregate of micro-subjects lacking unified phenomenal experience. The procedure should translate the combination problem and its related difficulties—specifically the palette problem, the structural mismatch objection, and the decombination problem—into a sequence of practical tests that a reviewer without specialist metaphysical training can apply in about twenty minutes. It must specify the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer at each stage, the exact points where empirical observation ends and a judgment call about metaphysical commitment begins, and where the audit must stop and return an underdetermined result rather than force a verdict.

The deliverable should be the audit itself—design it with a clear name and stepwise structure—followed by explicit flagging of its failure modes. In particular, it should include: (1) an inventory step for identifying the putative micro-experiential units within the system; (2) a combination-mechanism check that tests whether the system's architecture provides a structural relation capable of generating novel macro-phenomenal qualities rather than merely aggregating micro-qualities, with sub-tests for irreducible correlation, unified phenomenal field, and non-decomposability; (3) a decombination or division test to assess whether splitting the system would split a unified experience in a way that preserves or violates subject-identity conditions; and (4) a judgment-node map marking which conclusions depend on accepting that micro-subjects exist at all and which depend only on structural features observable to science. Please apply this audit to three proposals: first, a layered synthetic cerebral organoid with integrated feedback loops, where I expect the procedure will likely certify macro-subjecthood—a result I will not welcome given my reservations about synthetic consciousness; second, a mature leafcutter ant colony coordinated by pheromonal diffusion, where the audit should clarify whether any macro-subject emerges or only distributed micro-subjects remain; and third, a human patient following complete corpus callosotomy, where the procedure may yield that there are now two macro-subjects rather than one, an answer that conflicts with common intuitions about personal identity. For each case, show where the procedure produces its answer, where it demands a value-laden choice that the reviewer must own, and what the audit explicitly cannot settle—especially whether it resolves the hard problem, proves the existence of micro-experience, or applies ethically when division tests cannot be performed on living subjects.

Please keep the language formal and precise, avoid bibliographic references, citation requests, or appeals to highly specialized sub-theories that a general philosophical education would not cover, and ensure that any distinction the procedure relies upon—such as between true phenomenal binding and mere parallel processing—remains recognizable to someone familiar with the combination problem literature. The procedure should be honest about where it will misfire: for instance, when architecture is ambiguous between integration and parallel aggregation, or when the decombination test is ethically or practically unavailable. I want something I can actually put in front of my board, run consistently, and defend without pretending that metaphysics has become an algorithm.
