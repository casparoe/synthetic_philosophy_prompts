(2) Rejecting statistical independence yields superdeterminism, which preserves parameter independence (no direct cross-wing influence) but at the cost of making experimental choice globally predetermined and therefore apparently illusory.
(3) If we instead preserve statistical independence, Bell’s result forces rejection of parameter independence: the outcome at wing A depends on the setting at wing B, holding the hidden variables fixed.
(4) On any interventionist account of causation, if manipulating B’s setting makes a difference to A’s outcome, there exists a causal pathway from B to A.
(5) Yet quantum mechanics includes a no-signaling theorem: the local statistical distribution at A does not depend on B’s setting.
(6) So the parameter dependence required by Bell (given statistical independence) must be causally efficacious—capable of altering outcomes—while being systematically invisible to every statistical test available at the receiving wing.
(7) But a causal influence that is in principle undetectable by any local statistical probe violates the evidential constraint that distinguishes causal claims from post-hoc correlations; calling it “causal” collapses into mere relabeling.
(8) Therefore we face a dilemma: either accept superdeterminism and abandon the possibility of controlled experiment, or accept that Bell’s “non-locality” is causally inert and hence not genuinely non-local—making the theorem an argument against locality only if we first beg the question by counting causally inert correlations as causal violations.
(9) Consequently, Bell’s theorem either undermines empirical science or fails to establish non-locality.

The argument feels suspicious because it slides from parameter dependence to signaling, then uses no-signaling to erase the very dependence it just posited.
