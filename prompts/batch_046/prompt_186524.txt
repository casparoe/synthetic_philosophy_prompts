I am delivering the opening remarks—roughly eight minutes—at an interdisciplinary workshop titled "Derived Minds: Intentionality in Artificial and Biological Systems." The audience is split evenly between philosophers of mind who have followed the Searle-Dennett exchange since the 1980s and machine-learning researchers who speak casually of "intentional stance" without having read the original papers. I want the remarks to use Dennett's wandering two-bitser—the coin-operated vending machine he introduces to illustrate purely derived intentionality—as the structural spine rather than as a decorative anecdote. Specifically, I want to frame the workshop's central tension around this precise question: if every representational state in a system traces back ultimately to its designers' choices rather than to any biological need or evolutionary history belonging to the system itself, is there a principled, non-arbitrary point at which we ought to stop calling those states derived and start granting them original intentionality? The speech should welcome both halves of the room without condescension, should avoid all bibliographies, reading lists, and citations, and should conclude by posing a concrete challenge to the participants: whether the distinction between original and derived intentionality collapses under pressure once we take seriously that human intentionality is itself partly scaffolded by language, culture, and technological extension—an idea I want treated as a substantive philosophical pressure rather than as a rhetorical escape hatch. Please write the remarks so that this framework genuinely shapes what can and cannot be said in the welcome, determining both the tone and the boundaries of the invitation.
