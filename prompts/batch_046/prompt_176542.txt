Hey — I'm covering belief paradoxes in classic cinema for an ideas column and Rashomon keeps coming up. The lottery paradox says you can rationaly believe each ticket will lose without believing none win; the preface paradox is the author who trusts every claim in her book but admits there's probably an error somewhere in it. In Rashomon the bandit, the wife, the dead samurai (through the medium), and the woodcutter each present a fully coherent account and seem to believe every detail. But taken together they're incompatible. So is Kurosawa just dramatizing the preface paradox — rational claim-by-claim belief, contradiction at the collective level — or is he arguing for something weirder, like that testimony should be handled with graded credences rather than flat belief? Does the woodcutter's final version resolve the contradiction or just add another losing ticket? I'm trying to avoid the usual 'everything is subjective' take and actually use the paradox machinery. Where does the film resist that reading, and what does it add to the philosophy that the papers miss — especially about whether holding contradictory beliefs is unavoidable when narrating trauma?
