I'm applying for a six-week interdisciplinary writing grant to develop a conceptual framework that borrows Fernand Braudel's well-known distinction among geographical, social, and event-level temporalities, but redirects it away from historiography and toward the philosophy of explanation. The precise question is whether long-duration geographic and climatic structures can be genuinely explanatory of institutional change without being reducible either to aggregate individual intentions or to deterministic causal laws, and if so, what logical form that explanation takes. Standard debates in philosophy of social science tend to treat structural and intentional explanation as competing frameworks, yet I think they miss a middle possibility: that slow-moving conditions operate as a distinct explanatory layer with its own criteria of relevance, counterfactual dependence, and scope conditions.

The proposal should state this problem sharply, explain why the usual determinism-versus-individualism dichotomy fails to capture it, and lay out a realistic method for a solo six-week project—namely, conceptual analysis paired with close logical examination of two or three comparative episodes, such as the emergence of feudal tenure patterns in parts of Western Europe and the persistence of irrigation-based administrative structures in arid regions, treating these not as empirical discoveries but as structured thought experiments that test whether a climate or geographic feature can figure in an explanation without being either a background constant or a direct cause of specific actions. I need a clear account of what success looks like: probably a taxonomy of explanatory roles that lets us distinguish when a slow structure is part of the explanation versus part of the backdrop, with conditions that avoid collapsing into mere correlation. I also need an honest failure condition—specifically, if the taxonomy proves redundant with existing causal-mechanistic accounts or so vague that it cannot adjudicate between explanatory and non-explanatory relations. The deliverable should be suitable for philosophers of science and historians, but the scope must stay within conceptual work; there is no archival component. Please do not include a bibliography, citation list, or literature review by citation, as I will supply references myself. And please avoid boilerplate openings; write it as a direct pitch to a skeptical grant committee that knows Braudel's general schema but doubts that historiographical metaphors belong in philosophy of science.
