I'm a high school senior and the outgoing President of our regional Model European Union conference. This Friday I must deliver the closing plenary address—about ten minutes, roughly 900 words—to an audience of fellow student delegates and invited faculty observers, including constitutional scholars and EU-governance researchers who have attended our committee sessions. I want the speech to be genuinely philosophical rather than ceremonial.

The assigned theme is ever-closer union, but I want to treat it as a conceptual problem about pooled sovereignty rather than an integration slogan. To that end, please build the speech around an idea I am developing: what I call an authorial deficit. The standard democratic-deficit critique focuses on distance—voters feel remote from Brussels. I want to argue that the deeper problem arises when sovereignty is pooled rather than transferred: citizens retain membership in national democracies, but no single democratic body holds clear title over EU decisions. The Union functions less like a federation and more like a sovereignty condominium—shared possession without a single owner—so accountability does not disappear; it becomes structurally plural. Citizens cannot easily say this law is ours because the author is distributed.

This framing should do real structural work in the speech. Because authorship is plural, I should not offer false comfort through a simple call for more European democracy or a nostalgic retreat to pure national sovereignty—both presuppose a single author that the condominium structure denies. At the same time, I must avoid despair: the delegates spent three days negotiating real resolutions on energy solidarity and migration quotas, so I need to honor that practical work without pretending it resolved the conceptual ambiguity.

Please write the address in my voice—earnest, precise, but not buried in academic jargon. Open by briefly acknowledging the delegates' actual work this week, then introduce the conceptual frame, then close with something that lands for both teenagers and scholars without artificially resolving the tension. Do not include citations, bibliographies, or references to named scholars; I will deliver this from memory. Do not quote philosophers verbatim—paraphrase any ideas plainly and without quotation marks. The philosophical point must shape what I can and cannot claim, not merely decorate the remarks.
