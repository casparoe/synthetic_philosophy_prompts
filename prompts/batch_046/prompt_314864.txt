I hold a reconstructed version of Berkeley's master argument that replaces the usual linguistic analysis of "conceive" with a claim about phenomenal simulation: that any conscious sensory representation of a material object structurally includes an implicit representation of being perceived, so that generating a mental model of an unperceived tree is not merely false but phenomenally impossible—analogous to trying to represent a square circle. This seems to neutralize the standard objection that we can clearly think about absent objects. What I want is an honest map of its exposure. Which empirical findings—say, regarding object permanence in pre-linguistic infants, or unconscious representation in blindsight—would genuinely undermine the premise that object-representation is necessarily phenomenally self-involving? Which metaphysical distinctions—between personal-level and subpersonal representation, or between phenomenal and non-phenomenal intentionality—does the argument depend on, and how vulnerable is it if those distinctions collapse? Are there conceptual counter-cases, perhaps involving abstract or mathematical entities, that show phenomenal simulation can be directed at non-perceived objects without contradiction? Please rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward: which ones should a defender of this view concede as damaging, and which can be dismissed as misapplying the notion of sensory representation? Finally, assess whether this reconstruction risks being insulated from refutation by defining "phenomenal" narrowly enough to exclude all counterexamples, and whether such insulation is a theoretical vice.
