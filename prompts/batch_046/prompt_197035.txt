I have been tracking the verdicts of a senior colleague, Dr. Elena Vasquez, who chairs our faculty's working group on conceptual conditions. Over the past eighteen months she has delivered rulings on several proposed transcendental arguments, and the underlying principle is not transparent.

She endorses the argument that temporal synthesis—the integration of successive representations under a unified consciousness—is a necessary condition for self-consciousness, treating it as structural rather than psychological.

She endorses the argument that intersubjective correctability, the possibility of being corrected by others within shared normative practices, is necessary for the concept of objective error; she regards denial of this condition as performatively self-undermining for anyone asserting that they are wrong.

Yet she rejects the argument that the actual presence of other minds is necessary for self-consciousness itself; she holds that a solitary embodied subject could, in principle, achieve self-reference without ever encountering another consciousness.

She also rejects the argument that moral obligation requires a transcendent, non-empirical ground of value, insisting that practical norms remain fully immanent.

A new proposal has now come before her. A recent argument contends that for any system—biological or artificial—to genuinely possess beliefs capable of being true or false, it must be embedded in both embodied spatial orientation and intersubjective practices of correction. Applied to present large language models, the argument concludes that such systems do not hold beliefs at all, but only produce syntactically structured outputs, since they lack both embodiment and genuine intersubjective standing.

What, in your view, will Dr. Vasquez conclude? I am less concerned with the verdict on artificial cognition itself than with the candidate rules that reconcile her four earlier rulings, the divergent predictions each makes about this case, your best-guess verdict with the reasoning for preferring that rule, and the further hypothetical case—perhaps a disembodied but socially interactive system, or a solitary embodied agent—that would best distinguish the competing principles.
