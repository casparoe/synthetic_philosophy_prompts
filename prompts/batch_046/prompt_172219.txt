I am a high school junior serving on the documentation committee for our town’s community heritage center. Once a month I help draft text for a rotating public exhibit drawn from resident-submitted narratives. Roughly one in three submissions conflicts materially with the municipal archive—dates, agents, causes, outcomes, or moral evaluation differ. I have forty-eight hours to draft a recommendation for each conflict: whether the display should privilege the community account, the archival record, a hybrid framing, or an explicit notation of disagreement, all within a two-hundred-word panel limit. The recommendation then goes to the center director for final sign-off. I do not have authority to suppress either source, but I must determine how they appear together.

Please construct a reusable decision procedure I can run on any such conflict. The procedure should be ordered—specific questions to ask in sequence, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where it yields a clear recommendation, and where it must stop and flag the question as one of values rather than recoverable fact, requiring the director’s judgment rather than mine. Include concrete tests or distinctions that do not require specialized historiographic training, and mark explicitly where judgment cannot be eliminated.

Ground the procedure in the philosophy of collective memory and historical representation—particularly the tension between commemorative truth and documentary truth, and between mnemonic sovereignty and archival fidelity. To keep the translation sharp, organize conflicts into three registers: correctable conflict (the accounts can be harmonized without loss), containable conflict (they must appear side by side with framing), and irreconcilable conflict (they demand a meta-commentary on the disagreement itself). Do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations to named scholars; I need concepts I can apply directly.

Then apply the procedure step-by-step to three cases.

First, residents submit that the 1962 flood was survived entirely through spontaneous neighborhood mutual aid with no government involvement; the archive records coordinated municipal emergency response and documents that some residents were turned away from shelters under segregation policies.

Second, the community remembers Margaret Voss, who donated land for our public library in 1951, as a founding benefactor essential to local family identity; the archive shows she acquired the land through a disputed eviction and donated it partly to secure a tax exemption, with private correspondence containing no reference to public benefit. Many elderly residents say that removing her commemorative status would cause them tangible distress.

Third, two unrelated families each claim that their ancestor was the first postmaster; records list only one. The families do not dispute each other but both request inclusion.

For each case, show the recommendation the procedure produces, including any point where it demands a values call rather than giving me an answer. Include at least one case where the procedure produces a recommendation you expect I will not like.

Also state clearly what the procedure cannot do—where it will systematically misfire, overstate its authority, or fail to resolve a conflict.

Finally, tell me what would change your mind about the design of this procedure: which argument or observed failure mode would lead you to revise the ordering of steps or the boundary between factual and values questions, and identify the reasoning step that is most likely to fail in practice.
