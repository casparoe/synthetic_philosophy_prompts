You serve on the jury of a biennial prize for contemporary lyric poetry. This year, submissions include works of unknown or ambiguous authorship—some may be human-written, others generated by language models fine-tuned on canonical corpora, and a few are reconstructed fragments from damaged archives where editorial restoration has altered lineation and diction. The jury must render a single verdict on whether a given work exhibits genuine poetic merit, but you have only thirty minutes per submission, no access to authorship metadata during evaluation, and you must justify your decision to a board that includes both literary scholars and museum conservators.

Your task is to operationalize David Hume's standard of taste—specifically the criteria he advances for qualified judges, including delicacy of sentiment, practice and comparison, freedom from prejudice, and the corrective function of good sense—into a reusable evaluation procedure your jury can run repeatedly without appealing to external authority. The deliverable should be a stepwise protocol: the questions to ask in sequence, what counts as a sufficient answer at each stage, explicit stopping rules that yield a negative verdict, and clear markers of the points where the procedure must hand off to irreducible human judgment rather than mechanical application. The protocol must remain usable by jurors who lack specialist training in aesthetics, must function when authorship is unknown, and must be honest about which distinctions it collapses for the sake of speed.

Then apply this procedure to three submissions. First, a forty-line poem in strict Petrarchan form that displays technical competence but evokes no particular emotional response in any juror. Second, a text generated by a model trained exclusively on the complete works of John Donne, which reproduces metaphysical conceits with high fidelity but contains one anachronistic image that only a specialist in seventeenth-century theology would notice; here the procedure should produce a verdict the jury is likely to dislike. Third, a reconstructed poem assembled from water-damaged manuscript fragments, where the conservator's choice to modernize punctuation and supply missing verbs has arguably improved readability but altered the original rhythmic structure. For each case, run the steps explicitly and state where the procedure breaks down or demands a value judgment that the protocol itself cannot resolve.

Conclude by stating what this procedure is structurally incapable of doing—particularly regarding works that derive their value from contexts outside the text itself, or from the very fact of their artificial or reconstructed origin—and identify one category of submission for which the protocol should never be used.
