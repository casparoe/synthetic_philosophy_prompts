Been noticing this weird pattern in arguments about value and I can't tell if it's one thing or three different things people confuse. You get situations where someone claims there's a real reason not to do something, but when you press them on who the reason is for, there's nobody there. Like, destroying a beutiful landscape that no conscious being will ever experience -- people say it's wrong, but not wrong for anyone. Or preserving the accuracy of some mathematical fact even in a branch of reality where no minds exist. Or avoiding the creation of suffering in a perfectly isolated simulation that nobody will ever recieve. The reason seems to float free of any reasoner, but people still treat it as genuinely normative, not just as this would be nice if anyone cared. I think there's talk about impersonal goods but that sounds like a bucket, not a specific concept. Is there an established name for this pattern, where a normative claim doesn't seem tied to any particular subject, and how is it meant to differ from just saying the world is better this way? I'm also wondering about the flip side: people who hear no one is harmed and immediately conclude so there's no problem. Is that a named fallacy or a specific theoretical commitment? If there's no precise label for the unanchored-reason thing itself, how would you carve it out from nearby ideas like agent-neutral reasons or intrinsic value, and what would you call it? I don't need a bibliography, just the conceptual map, and I want to know if I'm missing some obvious distinction that philosophers already draw.
