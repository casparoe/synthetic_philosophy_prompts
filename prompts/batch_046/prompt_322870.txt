I am finalising my philosophical profile on lifeboat ethics and scarcity triage for a graduate seminar, and should like an internal consistency audit before I present. Here is where I have landed.

First, I endorse a modified Hardinian separation thesis: in conditions of acute resource scarcity, our primary moral obligations run within the vessel rather than across vessels, yet I reject the stronger claim that inter-vessel aid is always counter-productive. Rather, such aid is permissible only when it does not depress the recipient vessel's carrying capacity below its survival threshold.

Second, I hold that triage in these contexts is governed by a lexical ordering. The first priority is to preserve minimal moral agency for every occupant—no one may be treated as a mere resource or means, even to save greater numbers. Only once that floor is secured may we maximise survivor numbers; any remaining burdens are then distributed by a vulnerability-weighted lottery rather than by desert or contribution.

Third, I accept a contractualist constraint in the Scanlonian mode: any distribution principle adopted must be justifiable to each affected party. I qualify this, however, with an epistemic clause—justifiability is assessed relative to the decision-maker's actual, fallible knowledge of carrying capacity, not relative to an idealised omniscient standpoint.

Fourth, I distinguish structural omission from acute omission. Failing to expand capacity before crisis—structural omission—carries retrospective moral blame, but it does not generate present-tense obligations to overfill the boat. Nevertheless, if an occupant's prior actions contributed disproportionately to the structural deficit, their present claim to scarce resources is weakened proportionally.

Fifth, I deny direct moral standing to future generations or to the dead within the calculus, yet I affirm what I term a legacy constraint: survival strategies that entail the deliberate destruction of a vessel's own cultural or burial records are impermissible, even when they would materially increase survival odds, because this violates a non-instrumental value that persists beyond the death of its bearers.

Please audit this package. Which pairs of commitments are genuinely in tension, which only appear to conflict, and how severe are the real tensions? For each genuine tension, state how severe it is, identify the least costly revision that would restore consistency, and rank them accordingly. Then tell me which single revision—if forced to make only one—would eliminate the greatest amount of conflict across the set. I am not seeking a general lecture on lifeboat ethics, but an audit of my specific position.
