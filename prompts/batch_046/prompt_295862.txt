I have been puzzling over a tension in democratic practice with my graduate seminar on political philosophy, and I would like your help reconstructing its implicit philosophical commitments. Consider the widespread institutional and social pattern in liberal democracies—particularly visible in first-past-the-post and ranked-choice systems—where strategic voting for the least objectionable viable candidate is treated as the fulfillment of civic duty, where casting a ballot for a non-viable or minor candidate is characterized as either irrational waste or an irresponsible failure to prevent the worst outcome, and where deliberate abstention is interpreted not as an expressive act of dissent but as mere apathy or a dereliction of citizenship. This triad is embedded not only in campaign rhetoric and media commentary but also in the technical design of ballots that facilitate full ranking of major contenders while making blank, truncated, or protest rankings administratively and socially conspicuous.

What philosophical theory of voting—encompassing duty, expression, and the evaluation of the lesser evil—does this pattern presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit view: does it require a consequentialist calculus in which only probable outcomes generate moral reasons, or can a deontological framework accommodate the asymmetry between strategic accommodation and principled refusal? Say whether the practice strictly needs this theory or remains compatible with several rival accounts, identify any internal inconsistencies (for instance, between treating the vote as an expressive act of personal identity and treating it solely as an instrumental contribution to aggregate outcomes), and indicate what an institutional design built on a genuinely expressive or duty-based conception—one that treated abstention or protest voting as morally on a par with strategic voting—would have to change. In particular, address whether the familiar “spoiler” logic quietly imports a theory of collective responsibility that sits uneasily with the individualist framing of the secret ballot.

Please also say what would change your mind: which argument about voter motivation, institutional design, or the nature of political obligation would lead you to conclude that this practice is philosophically coherent rather than contradictory, and flag the specific step in your reconstruction where your reasoning is most vulnerable to failure.
