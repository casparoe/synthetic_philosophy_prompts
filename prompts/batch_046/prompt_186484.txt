Write an essay addressing the following controversy in metaethics. Standard evolutionary debunking arguments claim that evolutionary selection shaped our moral beliefs for reproductive fitness rather than for tracking moral truth, so we lack reason to trust them as indicators of objective moral facts. Now distinguish between moral beliefs that were directly selected for—such as in-group loyalty or reciprocity norms—and those that arise as cognitive spandrels: unavoidable structural byproducts of practical-reasoning architectures that evolved to solve coordination and prediction problems. The precise question is whether this spandrel category saves a reason-based non-naturalist moral realism, on which moral truths are not causally efficacious natural properties but are necessarily connected to the structure of reasons for action, accessible only through the very practical-reasoning capacities that evolution anchored but never calibrated for morality.

Weigh both sides carefully. The debunker can argue that spandrel-access provides no independent reliability guarantee, since evolutionary pressure never filtered these outputs for alignment with non-natural moral facts. The realist can reply that if moral cognition is structurally continuous with logical or mathematical cognition—both also spandrels of practical reasoning—then evolutionary anchoring undermines moral knowledge only if it also undermines all normative cognition, which is implausible. Reach and justify a specific verdict—for instance, that debunking succeeds only against causal-naturalist moral realisms but collapses against reason-based non-naturalist views, or the reverse.

Present the essay as exactly five numbered points, each developing one stage of the argument (the spandrel framing, the debunker’s challenge, the realist’s structural-continuity reply, the assessment of the analogy to logic or mathematics, and the final justified verdict), with no introduction or conclusion outside the numbering. Each point should consist of one or two concise paragraphs.

Conclude by stating explicitly what would change your mind: identify the weakest link in your reasoning and describe the specific counter-argument, conceptual distinction, or empirical finding that would overturn your verdict.
