I am a retired physicist who, since leaving the laboratory, have been working through philosophy of life with the same habits I once applied to instrumentation problems. I have drafted notes for an essay that attempts to read Camus's notion of the absurd through the lens of measurement theory: specifically, I propose that the absurd is not a metaphysical discovery but a systematic calibration error that occurs when human-scale value-frames are projected onto a domain—the universe considered as a whole—for which they were not designed, producing a persistent mismatch analogous to using a thermometer calibrated for biological temperatures to measure stellar plasma. I then contrast this framing with contemporary hybrid accounts of meaning, which I understand to hold that meaning arises when subjective attraction aligns with objective value, and I ask whether those accounts resolve the calibration problem by restricting the domain of evaluation back to human-scale projects, or whether they merely suppress the error signal without correcting the underlying misapplication. I am writing to request comments on these notes, particularly on whether my analogy distorts Camus's argument by treating the absurd as an epistemic limitation rather than as an existential confrontation, and on whether my characterization of hybrid meaning-theories is fair given that I have read them only through secondary summaries. I would also welcome suggestions on whether the essay should engage with Nagel's claim that absurdity arises from a collision between serious commitment and detached doubt, since that framing seems closer to mine but I am uncertain whether it belongs to the same conceptual family as Camus's revolt. The intended audience is a discussion group with mixed philosophical backgrounds, so I want the scientific framing to illuminate rather than obscure the philosophical stakes, and I am especially interested in whether the essay's central analogy is philosophically productive or merely decorative.
