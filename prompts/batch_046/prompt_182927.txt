I've been thinking about the death penalty stuff and I keep coming back to this weird tension that my roommate thinks I'm overanalyzing. Retributivists usually say execution is okay if the punishment fits the crime, but there's this gap between legal guilt—like, the jury said guilty beyond reasonable doubt—and actual moral culpability, which is more like, did this specific person really do the thing with the right mental state, with no hidden coercion or misidentification? Our courts can only ever reach the first one, a procedural status, not the metaphysical fact. So please argue that this gap doesnt just weaken the death penalty case, it actually collapses the retributive justification for capital punishment specifically. The idea is that execution is irreversible, so it commits the state to a claim about actual culpability that our epistemic institutions cant guarantee, which violates the core retributive principle that punishment must be strictly proportional to real desert. Meanwhile life without parole is reversible if new DNA comes up, so it respects the constraint that we only punish actual culpability—if we're wrong, we can correct it without having destroyed an innocent life. Dont just fall back on "risk of innocent people dying" as a consequentialist worry; make it about the internal logic of retributivism failing on its own terms because it cant secure the metaphysical premise it needs.

Also, dont bring deterrence into this unless its to say it doesnt rescue the argument, since I'm specifically interested in the retributive angle and how the risk of error interacts with it differently than it does for other punishments. I'm trying to see if the irreversibility creates a kind of asymmetry that makes capital punishment uniquely incompatible with retributive justice, not just riskier in a generic sense.
