In popular management discourse and in some sociological commentary, labels such as "Baby Boomer", "Millennial", or "Gen Z" are treated as though they named epidemiological cohorts: a fixed birth-window is defined, a population is tracked longitudinally, shared environmental exposures are assumed, and distinct behavioural or attitudinal outcomes are read off. The analogy is used to license predictions about workplace values, consumption patterns, or political orientation based solely on birth year, and to defend the claim that these groups constitute real social kinds rather than marketing segments. I want a precise audit of this analogy. First, provide the correspondence table: what in the epidemiological cohort study maps onto the birth-window definition, the exposure history, the outcome measurement, and the control for confounding variables? Second, identify where the mapping breaks, paying particular attention to the age-period-cohort identification problem and to the fact that generational labels are often applied retrospectively and unevenly across national contexts. Third, say whether the analogy quietly imports an assumption of intra-cohort homogeneity that a rigorous cohort study would not permit, and whether it obscures the difference between a true cohort effect and a period effect experienced at different life stages. Finally, assess whether any ontological claim — that generations are natural or institutional kinds with causal powers — survives once the epidemiological framing is shown to be largely metaphorical, or whether the analogy was carrying the entire argumentative load.
