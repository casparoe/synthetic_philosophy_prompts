Following a recent clinical evaluation of temporal processing deficits that has made the architecture of conscious experience unexpectedly personal for me, I am preparing a two-page prospectus for a one-semester independent study in philosophy of mind and need it framed as a rigorous, tractable research proposal. My half-formed idea is this: Dennett’s attack on the Cartesian theater through the multiple drafts model successfully disperses the spatial metaphor of a central place where consciousness comes together, but it has not been systematically tested against the temporal version of the same problem. Neuroscience indicates that cross-modal perceptual binding operates within narrow temporal windows—roughly 50 to 200 milliseconds—yet the parallel-stream account offers no explicit mechanism for how disparate drafts are assigned to the same subjective now without either a distributed synchronizer that functions like a minimal temporal theater, or a fragmentation of consciousness into non-overlapping micro-narratives that no subject could recognize as unified.

Please turn this into a formal proposal containing the following elements. First, a precise statement of the research question: does the multiple drafts model, when combined with empirical constraints on temporal binding, implicitly require a functional equivalent of a Cartesian theater in the temporal domain, and if so, is that equivalent avoidable without collapsing the phenomenology of unified experience? Second, an explanation of why existing positions leave this open—Dennett’s critics have focused almost entirely on spatial localization and the homunculus fallacy, while defenders treat temporal continuity as an unproblematic byproduct of narrative stitching, with neither side engaging the neuroscience of temporal binding windows to see whether synchronization demands reintroduce the very centralization the model was designed to eliminate. Third, the method and timeline, month by month for one semester: Month 1, reconstruct Dennett’s model with explicit attention to temporal dynamics rather than spatial metaphors; Month 2, map the conceptual structure of multisensory temporal binding as a philosophical constraint rather than as empirical data to be verified; Month 3, develop two formal alternatives—Model A, a distributed clock with no central stage, and Model B, a fragmentation model that denies unified temporal experience; Month 4, test each against first-person phenomenological reports and against clinical cases of disrupted temporal integration; Month 5, draft the argument; Month 6, revise and finalize. Fourth, what the deliverable would look like: a 6,000-word paper arguing for one of three conclusions—that the model can absorb temporal binding without a theater equivalent, that it requires a minimal temporal synchronizer that is not metaphysically objectionable, or that it must accept a radical fragmentation of consciousness that conflicts with first-person reports. Fifth, what would count as failure—if the analysis shows that standard narrative stitching already solves the binding problem with no synchronization mechanism needed, or if the clinical and phenomenological data prove too contested to support any of the three conclusions. Sixth, an honest scope assessment: the risk is that empirical neuroscience will outrun philosophical analysis in a single semester, so the project will treat empirical findings strictly as conceptual constraints and will avoid claims that depend on unsettled experimental results.

Please do not include a bibliography, citation list, or reading recommendations—I will supply references myself. Also, do not request or depend on specific external sources. End with a one-sentence summary of the proposed project's central claim as the last line, beginning with "Summary:".
