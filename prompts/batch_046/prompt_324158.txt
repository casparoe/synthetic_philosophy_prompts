I'm planning a session for a philosophy reading group of advanced undergraduates who have already worked through Judith Jarvis Thomson's violinist case and Don Marquis's future-like-ours argument, but they keep collapsing the two into a single bodily-autonomy-versus-future-value standoff. I want them to see that the arguments have different logical structures and respond differently once bodily location is removed. The carrying twist is an ex utero dependency scenario: a fetus sustained in an artificial environment that requires continuous, biologically specific, and costly maintenance that only one designated person can provide, with no physical invasion of that person's body. Plan the teaching arc: the order in which the conceptual pieces must be introduced so that the separation between autonomy-based refusal and future-value harm becomes structurally clear rather than muddled; why the standard violinist case should be set aside once this example is introduced, and exactly what properties the ex utero case must have and must lack to do the work; the misconceptions the group will predictably bring—I'm expecting "without pregnancy there is no moral issue," "dependency always generates a duty to sustain," and "Thomson's point is really just about inconvenience"—and the precise point in the sequence where each should be intercepted; a single check question at the end that reveals whether they have grasped the structural divergence rather than just chosen a side; and what I should explicitly bracket—personhood, potentiality, actual biomedical timelines—and openly say so. I need the dependency map and the misconception map, not a resolution of the abortion debate.
