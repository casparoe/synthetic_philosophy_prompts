I am currently studying jurisprudence at sixth-form level and have found myself rather perplexed by the exchange between H.L.A. Hart and Lon Fuller concerning the relationship between law and morality. From my reading, Hart maintains a separation thesis: law and morality are conceptually distinct, though he allows in his principal work that any viable legal system must contain a minimal moral content—prohibitions on killing, theft, and deception—if human societies are to survive. Fuller, by contrast, argues that law possesses what he terms an internal morality, consisting of eight procedural principles—generality, promulgation, non-retroactivity, clarity, non-contradictoriness, feasibility, constancy through time, and congruence between official action and declared rule—and he insists that a system failing these conditions is not merely bad law but not law at all, amounting instead to mere managerial direction.

My difficulty is this. Fuller's eight conditions appear to be purely formal requirements of efficacy: they describe how rules must be structured so that people can actually follow them, rather than imposing any substantive moral standard on what the rules ought to contain. If that is so, then Fuller's internal morality seems to operate not as a moral constraint but as an instrumental criterion of good legal craftsmanship—rather like saying a bridge must obey physical laws if it is not to collapse. Meanwhile, Hart's minimum content addresses the substantive moral rules that law must include for social survival. Therefore, I wonder whether I have misunderstood the nature of the debate entirely. Is Fuller's position not simply a procedural complement to Hart's substantive concession, meaning that both accept law must contain morality, but differ only over whether that morality governs form or content? And if Fuller's principles are indeed only conditions of efficacy, why do philosophers treat his view as a genuine form of natural law rather than as a theory of legal engineering? I should be most grateful for clarification as to where my reasoning goes astray—in particular, whether I have wrongly reduced Fuller's procedural morality to a matter of mere functionality, and whether Hart's minimum content actually answers Fuller's challenge rather than leaving it intact.
