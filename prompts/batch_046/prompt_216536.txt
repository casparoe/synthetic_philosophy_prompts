A professor in my philosophy of economics seminar offered the following reconstruction of Aristotle's account of money and exchange in Nicomachean Ethics V and Politics I. He said that Aristotle distinguishes oikonomikē—household management oriented toward the good life—from chrematistikē, the unlimited acquisition of wealth through exchange. On his reading, money (nomisma) is purely a conventional instrument (nomos) introduced to solve the problem of commensurability: since goods such as shoes and houses share no intrinsic substance, human need (chreia) establishes a virtual equivalence relation—functioning almost like a preference-independent cardinal metric—that permits comparison prior to exchange, and coinage merely quantifies this pre-existing proportion. From this, the professor derived what he called a "labor-proportionality" standard for just exchange: the exchange-ratio should reflect the labor embodied in each good, with money serving as a neutral accounting unit that tracks this objective equivalence. He further argued that Aristotle's condemnation of usury (tokos) is not restricted to currency lending but applies to any capital return not derived from labor—so that commercial profit exceeding the labor-cost of goods handled is equally unnatural. Consequently, he concluded that Aristotle's ethics contain an implicit zero-interest, labor-value norm for all economic activity, making him an anticipatory critic of modern financialization rather than merely a commentator on household thrift. The reconstruction sounds partly familiar—Aristotle does call money barren and distinguishes natural from unnatural acquisition—but I worry it imports modern categories. Which elements are genuinely Aristotelian, which are common pedagogical simplifications, and which are subtle distortions? Specifically, does Aristotle's concept of chreia support anything like a cardinal equivalence metric, does his critique of tokos extend to trade profits in the manner suggested, and is there any textual basis for attributing to him a labor-proportionality principle? Finally, if those problematic elements are removed, does the conclusion that Aristotle anticipates a modern anti-finance position still stand?
