I'm preparing a long-form piece on why revolutions so often destroy their own architects, and I've walked myself into a logical corner I can't exit. Consider this argument I sketched with a historian colleague:

(1) Let's distinguish an "auto-immune" revolution—where its own normative logic demands the elimination of its creators—from one that is merely "institutionally recoiling," where administrative machinery built for defense eventually turns on leaders for purely pragmatic reasons (security, factional balance, resource scarcity).

(2) The French Revolution was institutionally recoiling, not auto-immune: Robespierre was executed by the Revolutionary Tribunal he had expanded, but the motive was consolidation amid war and factional rivalry, not because revolutionary virtue required his death.

(3) Therefore, 1789 does not count as a case of a revolution genuinely "devouring its children" in the structural sense.

(4) Yet the pattern holds across modern revolutions—1789, 1848, 1917, 1979—each building coercive institutions that eventually destroy their founders.

(5) If none of these were auto-immune, then "revolutions devour their children" is not a structural law but a coincidence of contingency.

(6) A coincidence repeated four times across different ideologies and contexts is implausible; there must be a structural driver.

(7) The only remaining structural driver is ideological: any project claiming to embody a unified popular will cannot tolerate plural leadership, so it must purge dissenters—including original architects.

(8) So if we deny that 1789 was auto-immune, we must either deny the structural pattern (implausible), or apply the ideological driver to all four cases—including ones like 1848 or 1979 where the ideological logic was thin or absent.

(9) That proves too much: it forces an ideological determinism onto revolutions that historians generally explain through contingency and class conflict.

(10) So we seem trapped: either accept that Robespierre died for revolutionary virtue (giving up the contingency/class-struggle interpretation), accept that the pattern is accidental (giving up historical structure), or accept that every revolution is ideologically auto-immune regardless of its actual content (giving up historiographical nuance).

Where does this chain break, if it breaks? If I must keep one claim intact, I'd rather preserve the distinction in (1) than surrender to (7). What does rejecting each vulnerable step cost?
