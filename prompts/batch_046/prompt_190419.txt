Write a proposal for a 3-month independent study on whether Dewey's experimentalist view of democracy survives if we stop assuming that democratic inquiry starts from a shared problem. Most people who invoke Dewey for participatory politics assume there's a common problematic situation that just needs better collective reasoning, but what if the conflict is that different groups literally describe the situation in incompatible ways—say, land-use disputes where one framing sees extraction and another sees relational obligation? Can Dewey's model still distinguish legitimate democratic outcomes from pure power consolidation, or does it collapse into proceduralism?

State the question precisely: does Dewey's logic of inquiry contain internal resources to evaluate adversarial contestation when participants don't share a common end or even a common problem-definition? Explain why existing readings—either Dewey is a pluralist who welcomes conflict or he's a liberal hiding behind scientific method—don't settle this, since both assume some shared framework for evaluation. Propose an approach: reconstruct the stages of inquiry from his writings on human nature and democratic life, but apply them specifically to cases where the problem-description itself is contested, using one concrete contemporary land-use conflict as a running example. Say why this is better than the obvious move of comparing Dewey to Habermas or Mouffe, which has been done to death. Describe what a result looks like: either Dewey offers a criterion for when value-conflict is still inquiry versus when it's just bargaining, or he doesn't and his democratic theory needs an external supplement he never provided. Give an honest scope check: three months, no archive access, one primary reader, so stick to published texts and a single case study; if Dewey's texts turn out too ambiguous on the inquiry-vs-politics boundary, you might spend the whole term on exegesis with no payoff. Mention one thing that could make it too big—trying to cover both Dewey's epistemology and his ethics seperately instead of keeping them tied to the democratic question.
