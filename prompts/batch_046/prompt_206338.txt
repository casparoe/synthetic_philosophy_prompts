I am preparing a comparative metaphysics module that treats al-Ghazālī's occasionalism not as a historical curiosity but as a live structural option for thinking about causation in light of modern physics. Before I evaluate particular reconstructions, I need the full specification: what would a reconstructed occasionalist theory of causation have to get right in order to count as an adequate competitor to contemporary dispositionalist or powers-based accounts? Give me the desiderata list with the motivating case or distinction behind each. The theory must be able to accommodate statistical regularities in nature—such as the predictable correlations observed in quantum mechanics—without collapsing them into Humean constant conjunction, and it must preserve a sharp distinction between metaphysical dependency (all existents depend on God for their being and continuation) and causal production (the denial that fire, medicine, or physical laws possess intrinsic efficacy to produce effects). It must also respect the results of conservation laws and symmetries in physics without treating those regularities as autonomous causal powers operating independently of divine action, and it must offer a coherent account of counterfactual stability—why, if God had not established this particular regularity, burning would not follow contact with fire—without committing to a realist ontology of unactualized dispositions or modal essences in created things.

For each requirement, flag whether it is non-negotiable for any view that genuinely inherits al-Ghazālī's denial of necessary connection (al-ṭabāʾiʿ) or whether it represents a negotiable preference of a particular interpretive camp—such as the continuous-recreation thesis versus the ʿādat Allāh reading. I am especially interested in whether any subset of these desiderata is jointly unsatisfiable: for instance, can one maintain both that there are strictly no secondary causes and that physical theory describes real modal structures that constrain what God can consistently will? Identify any such tension explicitly, and indicate which single desideratum does the most work in excluding the most popular non-occasionalist rivals. Keep the weight on the criteria and their motivating considerations rather than on a scorecard, though I do want a brief assessment of how three candidates fare: the standard "divine custom" reconstruction, the "intermittent-necessity" interpretation I am developing (which treats regularities as defeasible modal patterns sustained by divine will rather than as habitual repetitions), and a leading dispositionalist rival. A table is acceptable, but the conceptual labor should lie in the requirements themselves.
