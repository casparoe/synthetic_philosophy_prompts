I'm planning a session for a philosophy-of-science reading group—graduate students comfortable with basic probability but with no background in game theory—on the common prior assumption in Aumann's agreement theorem. I need a teaching plan rather than the lecture itself: the exact dependency order in which the components must appear (why a shared sample space and common knowledge of posteriors have to be in place before the disagreement result can even be stated, and why that ordering prevents the misconception that the theorem is about people being obstinate), the single carrying example that should thread through the session—I have in mind two epidemiologists updating on the same outbreak data but with different prior weights on airborne versus surface transmission mechanisms, and I want you to specify what this example must include (e.g., identical likelihoods, observable posteriors) and what it must exclude (e.g., hidden private evidence, incommensurable conceptual schemes) so it isolates parameter disagreement rather than structural disagreement—the predictable misconceptions they will raise in the order they will raise them (that any disagreement proves irrationality; that different priors license permanent divergence; that the result applies to deep theory choice rather than to estimation within a fixed model), the point in the arc where each misconception should be intercepted and absorbed, a check for understanding that distinguishes "they see why a common prior is a structural constraint on the belief space, not a psychological demand for consensus" from "they have only memorized the slogan," and an explicit statement of which formal machinery—common-knowledge operators, partition structures, or measure-theoretic formulations—should be left out at this level and acknowledged as omitted. Finally, flag any dependency in your plan that might accidentally smuggle in a niche extension of the result, such as non-partition information structures or correlated priors, so that I do not build the arc on material that requires specialist literature beyond what this audience possesses.
