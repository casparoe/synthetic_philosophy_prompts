In my graduate seminar on comparative moral cultures, I have been pressing students on a question that usually receives a sociological rather than a philosophical treatment: the contrast between a German folk-moral orientation, in which moral seriousness is identified with disciplined adherence to rationalized rules, institutional order, and the internalization of duty regardless of private inclination, and an American folk-moral orientation, in which moral seriousness is identified with the sovereignty of individual conscience, the right to dissent from collective norms, and the willingness to violate rules when they conflict with unmediated moral conviction. Rather than asking for a cultural diagnosis, I want a rigorous philosophical adjudication of which conception has the better of it.

Write an essay that weighs these two accounts of moral authority and reaches a justified conclusion. Construct the strongest philosophical version of each: present the German view not merely as obedience but as the claim that moral agency is constituted by participation in a shared normative order, such that unauthorized rule-breaking is a form of self-undermining moral failure because it dissolves the very conditions under which duty becomes intelligible; present the American view not merely as rebellion but as the claim that moral agency requires the capacity for direct, unmediated judgment, such that blind adherence to rules is itself a moral failure whenever it suppresses conscience. Then evaluate them directly.

In doing so, go beyond familiar objections and address a specific tension that is often glossed over: the problem of moral remainder, meaning the sense that even a fully justified conscientious violation of a just rule leaves a genuine moral cost that is not erased by the justification itself, versus the problem of normative dissolution, meaning the risk that if every agent reserves a private right to override institutional rules on the basis of individual conviction, the authority of the order collapses into arbitrariness. Examine whether a coherent middle position is available—for instance, by distinguishing the authority of procedural rules from that of substantive moral principles—or whether any such distinction inevitably collapses back into one of the two poles, because procedural authority either claims unconditional priority or implicitly appeals to conscience for its own justification. Please be explicit about the level of confidence you have in the conclusion you defend, and single out the precise point in your argument—whether concerning the phenomenology of duty, the epistemic reliability of individual conscience, or the conditions for institutional stability—about which you are least sure.
