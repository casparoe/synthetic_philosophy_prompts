I have adopted a specific version of the view that lying and misleading differ not in degree but in kind: lying involves an explicit breach of the conversational entitlement that a listener holds specifically against a speaker's direct testimony, whereas misleading can occur without the speaker ever exercising that testimonial authority. I extend this to institutional speech acts—such as central-bank forward guidance or diplomatic communiqués—where a speaker issues technically true but strategically ambiguous statements, knowing the audience will resolve the ambiguity in a false direction, yet never asserting the falsehood themselves. I want a map of what would genuinely count against this position, not merely the standard counterexamples about lies told with true beliefs or misleading via true implicatures. Specifically: which empirical results in experimental pragmatics or moral psychology—concerning, for instance, how ordinary people attribute blame for strategic ambiguity versus direct falsehood—would undermine the entitlement-breach account? Which formal features of speech-act theory, if shown to be symmetrical between assertion and implicature, would collapse the distinction? And what should a committed defender concede as genuinely damaging—say, if listeners treat strategic ambiguity as a worse betrayal than direct falsehood, or if the view cannot distinguish institutional ambiguity from ordinary conversational omission? Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, distinguish those that could be absorbed by refining the account from those that require abandoning it, and note whether the position risks anything substantial at all or has been insulated by qualifications that render it unfalsifiable.
