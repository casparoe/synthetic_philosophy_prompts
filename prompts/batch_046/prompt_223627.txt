Read Ursula K. Le Guin's short story "The Ones Who Walk Away from Omelas" through the separateness-of-persons objection to utilitarianism, but resist treating the narrative as a mere illustration of aggregate welfare. Focus instead on how the story's central architecture—one child's hidden suffering functioning as the necessary condition for the happiness of all others—tests two distinct formulations of the objection: the familiar Rawlsian claim that utilitarianism violates the distinction between persons by allowing sacrifices for the greater good, and a less commonly emphasized structural variant, which asks whether the objection is strengthened when the sacrifice is not merely summed into a total but institutionally embedded as an irreducible prerequisite for every other life. Where does the story depict the citizens as genuinely unable to integrate the child's pain into their own well-being without conceptual collapse, thereby supporting the separateness claim? Where does it instead portray the child as a fungible utility reservoir, precisely the reduction the objection condemns? Consider the walkers who depart: do they restore the child's separateness by withdrawing from the system, or do they merely relocate themselves outside the aggregate while leaving the structural exploitation intact for those who stay? Honor the text's deliberate ambiguity—Le Guin offers no description of the walkers' destination—so do not resolve the philosophical tension by fiat. Separate what is settled in the philosophical literature (the core Rawlsian formulation) from what remains genuinely contested (whether institutional embeddedness intensifies the separateness objection or transforms it into a critique of complicity). Begin with the critical reading itself, without preface or commentary, and do not present any contested interpretation as the consensus among philosophers working on this problem.
