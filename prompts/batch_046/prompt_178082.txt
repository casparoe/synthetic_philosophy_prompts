I've been developing a view of supererogation I'm tentatively calling the normative restructuring account. On this view, an act is supererogatory not because it merely exceeds a baseline of duty, but because it involves a voluntary restructuring of the agent's normative relationships—such as adopting an open-ended commitment to care for a particular stranger, or donating an organ when not required—such that performance transforms the landscape of reasons applying to that agent going forward, generating new obligations that did not exist prior to the act, while omission remains fully compatible with intact moral standing. The core asymmetry is that generic rational agents need not undertake these restructuring commitments, yet once undertaken they are not merely praiseworthy extras but genuinely alter what the agent owes; if this restructuring mechanism fails, then supererogation collapses either into concealed obligation or into non-moral excellence.

Tell me what would genuinely count against this position—what it risks and where it is exposed. I want to know whether there are intuitive cases that separate the restructuring effect from the moral praise we attach to heroic acts; whether formal considerations in deontic logic or rational-choice theory about self-binding commitments and future-self coherence undermine the idea that restructuring can be both voluntary and non-obligatory ex ante; and whether the view risks collapsing into a high-threshold form of act-utilitarianism or into a virtue-ethical account of character-based excellences that I am trying to avoid. Rank the potential defeaters from fatal to merely awkward—say, a demonstration that all apparently restructuring acts are either already required by prior consistency principles or are not genuinely voluntary, versus cases where some apparently supererogatory acts seem to restructure nothing at all. Also say which objections defenders of this view, including myself, should concede as genuinely damaging but think can be accommodated with qualifications, and whether the built-in asymmetry between omission and performance insulates the view so thoroughly that it becomes unfalsifiable and therefore theoretically weak rather than robust. I'd like the answer to distinguish what critics typically say about supererogation in general from what specifically threatens the restructuring mechanism I've described.
