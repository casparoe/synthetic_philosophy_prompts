Below are working notes for a brief interpretive essay I am drafting on Benjamin Franklin’s project of moral perfection, as recounted in his Autobiography. Rather than treating the famous list of thirteen virtues and the little book of black marks as a quaint episode of Enlightenment self-culture, I want to press a structural reading. Franklin approaches virtue not as an integrated state of character—something analogous to Aristotelian hexis or Stoic katorthosis—but as a set of discrete, modular skills acquired sequentially, one per week, with quantitative tracking of lapses. His persistent failure to master Order and Humility, together with his frank admission that perfection remained elusive, is usually read as conventional modesty. I propose instead that failure is built into the architecture of the method: because attention shifts from one virtue to the next before the previous one has been woven into judgment, the system lacks the corrective integration that phronesis or Stoic coherence would provide. On this reading, perfection does not function as an achievable endpoint but as an asymptotic limit—approached through successive approximations, measurable in the diminishing frequency of black marks, yet never finally instantiated. The moral life becomes an indefinite iterative project rather than a transformation of the self.

This framing raises a specific tension with Franklin’s practical deism. If the ideal of moral perfection is asymptotic, does it retain normative force, or does it collapse into a purely descriptive record of incremental improvement? I am also unsure whether comparing Franklin’s modular progress to the Stoic figure of the prokopton genuinely illuminates his failure, or whether it risks anachronism by imposing an integrated theory of virtue onto a deliberately empirical, piecemeal experiment.

Please comment on these notes. I would especially value your judgment on two points: first, whether the contrast between Franklin’s sequential tracking and the Aristotelian or Stoic emphasis on integrated virtue is both historically defensible and philosophically sharp; second, whether interpreting Franklin’s perfection as an asymptotic limit strengthens the interpretation or inadvertently dissolves the normative ambition that makes the project compelling in the first place. I am asking for conceptual and argumentative feedback only—not for citations, bibliographies, or source verification.
