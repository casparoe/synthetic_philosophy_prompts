Analyze the International Astronomical Union's 2006 reclassification of Pluto — precipitated by the 2005 discovery of Eris — as a test case for the thesis that science carves nature at its joints. Rather than summarizing the vote, map the dispute onto the natural-kinds debate with discipline: identify which properties of Pluto and the Kuiper Belt objects (mass, orbital dynamics, hydrostatic equilibrium, compositional similarity to other dwarf planets) are candidates for being the joints that a realist taxonomy should respect, and which are artifacts of observational history and cultural attachment (the century-long status of Pluto as the ninth planet, the pedagogical neatness of a nine-planet system). Examine the IAU's choice to adopt a dynamical criterion — having cleared the neighborhood around its orbit — instead of a purely geophysical one based on roundness. Does this decision track a pre-existing ontological boundary in the solar system, or does it construct an operational kind shaped by present-day research priorities? Then reverse the direction: ask what the Pluto episode reveals about theories of natural kinds. If planetary scientists continue to use the term planet for Pluto in geophysical contexts despite the IAU ruling, does that vindicate pluralism about kinds, undermine the idea of a single correct carving, or show that astronomical taxonomy was never in the business of tracking mind-independent joints? Be explicit about where the theory falls silent — for example, whether the dynamics of orbital clearing have analogues in biological taxonomy or chemical classification, or whether this case is sui generis. Finally, assess whether the episode strengthens realism about kinds, supports a conventionalist or pragmatic account, or reveals that the carving metaphor was misleading from the start for this domain.
