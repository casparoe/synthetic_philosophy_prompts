Consider a philosopher who consistently delivers these verdicts on suffering-pleasure trade-offs: (i) a single continuous century of extreme agony is worse than any finite population of mildly content lives, regardless of cardinal sum; (ii) distributing that same century of agony across a billion people, each feeling one ten-millionth, is not morally equivalent to the single case and is in fact far less bad; (iii) adding minor pleasures to a life already containing extreme agony yields no moral improvement whatsoever; (iv) removing minor discomforts from generally happy lives does yield improvement; (v) pleasure and pain do not share a measurable common scale, though both carry moral weight. Now suppose a technology fragments conscious experience into isolated, non-remembering temporal slices: a subject lives one year of bliss, interrupted only by two one-second slices of intense torture that bear no psychological connection to each other or to the bliss. The total suffering-time equals two seconds; the total well-being equals one year. Must she judge this fragmented life better or worse than the single continuous century of agony in (i)? Predict her verdict, identify whether her underlying rule is a lexical-threshold view, an incommensurability view with continuity conditions, or a hybrid, state which of (i)–(v) is the closest analog and why, and specify the minimal additional case that would distinguish a lexical-threshold rule from a continuity-sensitive incommensurability rule.
