I am preparing a seminar on comparative theories of well-being and have become interested in tranquilism—the position that only the absence of craving (taṇhā) and suffering (dukkha) carries intrinsic value, while pleasure, knowledge, and achievement are at best instrumentally useful and at worst distractions that rekindle craving. I come from a background in computational psychiatry and am fluent in its conceptual vocabulary: precision-weighted prediction errors, interoceptive inference, allostatic regulation, active inference, and the distinction between homeostatic set-points and allostatic overload. I want you to translate the core tranquilist thesis into this framework with as little loss as possible. Specifically, restate (i) the claim that well-being is defined negatively rather than positively, (ii) the rejection of hedonic pleasure as an intrinsic good, and (iii) the idea that craving itself—not merely its frustration—is a form of suffering, all using the language of predictive processing and regulatory dynamics.

Then provide the residue report. For each of the three claims above, say whether it maps cleanly onto a statement about minimized free energy, suppressed precision on interoceptive prediction errors, or stable homeostatic set-points; whether it can only be forced into that vocabulary by distorting either the neuroscience or the ethics (for example, by treating the cessation of craving as mere passive equilibrium rather than an active achievement); and whether any element—such as the Buddhist distinction between mundane contentment and the unconditioned cessation of craving associated with nibbāna—simply has no expression in computational-psychiatry terms. Finally, tell me whether the residue suggests that the neuroscience vocabulary is missing a normative distinction, or whether it reveals that the tranquilist claim was less precise than it appeared.
