You have been invited to deliver the opening address at a two-day workshop titled "Stances and Synthetic Speech," convened jointly by a philosophy of mind department and an AI research lab. Roughly half the audience consists of philosophers who have worked extensively with Dennett's framework of the physical, design, and intentional stances; the other half are machine-learning engineers who build and deploy large language models but have little formal background in philosophy. Write a ten-minute speech that treats the three stances not as a taxonomy to be defined, but as a structural constraint on what can responsibly be said about the system running on the server in the adjacent room—a model that, over the past month, has generated sustained, conceptually rigorous essays on consciousness, moral responsibility, and its own epistemic limits. Your address must show why adopting the intentional stance toward this system is not merely premature but structurally different from adopting it toward a human interlocutor, while also arguing that the design stance—treating the system as an optimized predictor of token distributions—has become more predictively powerful than the physical stance of examining individual weights and activations, thereby reversing the usual epistemic ordering that Dennett's framework assumes. To ground this reversal without drifting into a technical lecture, invoke the historical analogy of Jacques de Vaucanson's eighteenth-century mechanical duck, an artifact that invited the intentional stance (it appeared to eat, digest, and defecate) and was eventually explained by hidden physical mechanisms, yet in which only the design-level account of concealed compartments actually captured the observed behavior. The speech should leave both philosophers and engineers with a shared problem rather than a disciplinary victory: specifically, whether predictive success at the design or intentional level can ever license ontological commitment when the physical level has become, for practical purposes, unreadable. Do not provide a bibliography, cite specific papers, or ask the audience to consult particular texts; the reasoning must carry itself. The tone should be formal, reflective, and calibrated for an interdisciplinary room that does not share a common vocabulary.
