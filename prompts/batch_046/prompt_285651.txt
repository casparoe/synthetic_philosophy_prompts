I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy training, but I've been thinking about Buridan's ass in the context of deterministic decision systems, and I've constructed an argument that now strikes me as suspicious because it seems to collapse rational autonomy into either hidden determinism or pure arbitrariness. (1) If two options are genuinely equal in all merit-relevant respects, then by definition a rational agent has no reason to prefer one to the other, so perfect rationality in the face of true equality should produce paralysis. (2) In any deterministic system I work with—whether a scheduling algorithm or a neural network—ties are never left unresolved; they are broken by some hidden feature (memory address, hash ordering, firing latency, clock-cycle noise) that is external to the options' evaluated value. (3) So if human cognition is also a deterministic physical system, then whenever we appear to choose freely between genuinely equal alternatives, the selection is actually caused by one of these invisible asymmetries. (4) This yields a strict dichotomy: either there was never true equality (because a hidden merit-difference existed), or the resolving feature was merit-independent, in which case the choice was not justified by reasons but was merely the deterministic output of an arbitrary mechanism. (5) Applying this to moral and practical life—choosing between two identically needy charities, or two job offers with identical terms—means every such decision is either driven by an unrecognized bias or is rationally groundless. (6) But then there is no conceptual space for a choice that is both free and justified; rational autonomy disappears, leaving only hidden causation on one side and randomness on the other. (7) That proves too much, because we routinely treat symmetrical choices as legitimate exercises of judgment rather than as symptoms of hidden bias or irrationality. Where does the chain actually break? If I have to choose, I'd rather preserve (1) and (4), since they align with my intuition that equality should paralyze and that deterministic systems must resolve ties externally, but I want to understand the cost of saving them.
