Since becoming the primary caregiver for my mother following a stroke that left her conscious but unable to consistently will movement in her affected limb, I have found the causal exclusion argument impossible to treat as merely academic. Watching her form a clear intention to lift a cup and then seeing that intention fail—while the neural events in her motor cortex proceed on their own trajectory—raises a question that feels personal: what would it actually take for a theory of mental causation to count as adequate, rather than merely consistent?

Write the full list of desiderata that such a theory must satisfy. For each requirement, specify the concrete case or empirical result that motivates it, indicate whether it is non-negotiable or a preference of one camp, and note any distinction it must preserve—such as that between having mental causal efficacy at all and having the specific kind of efficacy required for moral responsibility, or between diachronic planning causation and momentary reflex causation. The list must include: consistency with the causal closure of physics without rendering mental properties redundant; accommodation of failed intentional actions like my mother’s; compatibility with neuroscientific evidence that particular neural events are sufficient for particular bodily movements; preservation of conscious deliberation in long-range decision-making; and an explicit stance on whether mental causation in artificial or non-biological architectures falls within the theory’s scope or lies outside it.

Then, identify which subset of these desiderata is jointly unsatisfiable under standard physicalist commitments—particularly in light of the overdetermination problem versus the exclusion problem—and state which single requirement does the most work in eliminating the major candidates: identity theory, emergentist dualism, non-reductive realization, overdeterminationist physicalism, and eliminativism. Do not provide a full scorecard for every contender; keep the weight on the criteria, their motivating cases, and the conflicts between them. A table is acceptable if it clarifies the conflicts, but the reasoning behind each tension should be explicit. Avoid bibliographies, reading lists, or requests for citations; the point is to specify what adequacy requires, not to catalog who has defended what.
