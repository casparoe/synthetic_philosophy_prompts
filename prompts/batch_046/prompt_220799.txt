Draft an adversarial-collaboration summary between a scientific realist committed to the no-miracles argument and a defender of the pessimistic meta-induction who holds that the history of mature science gives us a strong inductive reason to doubt that our current best theories are approximately true. I want a document both could sign in good faith.

Structure it in four parts. First, a numbered list of agreed claims — including about the formal structure of inference to the best explanation, about what needs explaining (predictive success, novel predictions, inter-theoretic convergence), about what standard no-go results such as underdetermination arguments and the problem of unconceived alternatives do and do not establish, and about the broad historical pattern of theory change in physics and chemistry since roughly 1800. Be precise and neutral; do not slip in evaluative language that either side would reject.

Second, a disagreements table with at least three cruxes, each stated twice — once in wording the realist would accept, once in wording the anti-realist would accept. The cruxes should be: (i) whether predictive success is best explained by approximate truth or by some non-truth-involving feature such as empirical adequacy combined with selection effects; (ii) whether the historical base rate of theory overturn applies uniformly across mature and immature sciences, or whether there is a structurally relevant distinction that blocks the induction in mature domains; (iii) whether structural realism is a stable middle ground or whether it collapses into either full-blown realism or anti-realism once its commitments are made explicit.

For each crux, specify a concrete historical case or argument both sides agree bears on it — for example, the retention of specific structural relations across the transition from classical to relativistic mechanics, or the fate of phlogiston theory versus the retention of quantitative chemical-composition relations — and record in advance what each side predicts about how that case should be interpreted. Include also the evidence or reasoning each side thinks would genuinely move them.

Third, a brief section asking whether any portion of the dispute is verbal: for instance, whether disagreement over “approximate truth” dissolves once we distinguish ontological commitment from structural commitment, or whether it remains substantive even after that distinction is drawn.

Finally, ask what each side counts as the single strongest argument or finding that could cause them to abandon their view, phrased so that the other side could recognize it as a legitimate potential defeater.

Write in formal, polished American English. Do not ask for bibliographies, reading lists, page citations, or references to specific editions. Do not put any formulation in quotation marks unless you have fetched and verified the exact wording from a source; paraphrase familiar positions instead. Make sure the document reads as a constructive joint text rather than a verdict on who is right.
