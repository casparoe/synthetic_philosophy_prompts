I'm designing a decision-tree handout for an advanced undergraduate seminar on nationalism that asks how old are nations and sorts respondents by their substantive commitments rather than by label recognition. Please build a taxonomy with five branching nodes, each node phrased as a concrete scenario or yes-no claim that someone could answer without knowing the terms modernist, primordialist, ethno-symbolist, or invented tradition. The first node should ask whether a community lacking mass literacy and anonymous membership can still be a nation. The second should ask whether a tradition explicitly invented in the nineteenth century can become genuinely binding on members who know its origin, or whether concealment is essential. The third should ask whether bureaucratic devices—censuses, ethnographic maps, standardized languages—create the categories they claim to record, and if so whether that creation undermines any claim to antiquity. The fourth should ask whether a polity symbolically continuous with a medieval kingdom but structurally reconstructed in 1870 counts as the same nation, and whether the continuity must be causal, narrative, or both. The fifth should ask whether recognizing that all nations involve some imagination makes the concept useless for political legitimacy, or merely descriptive. At each leaf, place the nearest named position—Anderson-style imagined community, Hobsbawm-style critical invention, Smith-style ethno-symbolism, perennialism, radical constructivism, or unnamed—followed by a one-sentence description of its most awkward commitment. Then identify any logically possible combinations that appear unoccupied, especially ones that combine modern conditions with genuine pre-modern continuity, and briefly diagnose why they are empty: incoherent, unattractive, or unexplored. Finally, flag any two named positions that this tree fails to separate, explaining on which node they converge. The audience should be students who have not read Anderson or Hobsbawm directly; do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or requests for citations.
