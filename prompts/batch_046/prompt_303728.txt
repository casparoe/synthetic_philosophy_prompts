Having retired after thirty years in experimental physical chemistry and only recently begun serious study of philosophy of science and archaeological epistemology, I have taken on the role of external grader for an interdisciplinary graduate seminar on inference from material remains. I composed the exam question below for that seminar, and I present it together with a student answer that I ask you to assess exactly as an instructor or examination TA would: assign a specific grade and justify it with precise, point-by-point comments rather than a vague overall impression. The question concerns a hypothetical late-Neolithic assemblage—standardized clay tokens bearing varied seal impressions scattered across domestic contexts, an unsealed cache of identical tokens recovered from a collapsed storage room, and ceramic vessels of uniform volumetric capacity but distinct decorative motifs concentrated near that room—and asks whether these support an archaeologist's inference of a centralized redistributive bureaucracy; in particular, it requires distinguishing indexical standardization, arising from peer calibration and shared craft norms, from directive standardization, arising from an administrative protocol, and identifying the evidentiary gap that most seriously weakens the bureaucratic reading. Please deliver your evaluation as exactly five numbered items, each consisting of one or two sentences, with no introductory paragraph and no concluding paragraph.

Question: Evaluate the archaeologist's bureaucratic interpretation of the assemblage described above. In your answer, explain how indexical and directive standardization differ in this context, propose at least one alternative social arrangement that could produce the same material pattern, and specify the single missing class of evidence that would most undermine the claim of centralized redistribution.

Student answer: The student correctly notes that uniform token weights and vessel capacities do not by themselves prove administrative control, since ritual exchange networks or kin-based craft traditions can generate peer-level standardization without any central authority. They also properly observe that the domestic scattering of sealed tokens is at least as consistent with decentralized household accounting as with feed-in to a central store. However, they claim that the absence of seal impressions on the cached tokens positively demonstrates that those items circulated only inside the institution for internal accounting, which mistakes a missing feature for direct evidence and ignores possibilities such as unfinished goods, perishable seal materials, or a separate temporal phase. They further assert that the volumetrically identical vessels function as calibrated extraction measures, thereby proving the institution possessed coercive bureaucratic capacity; this conflates shared technological convergence or communal portioning norms with state-level measurement, and it assumes without argument that uniform capacity implies surplus extraction rather than ritual redistribution. Finally, they argue that the diverse seal impressions found in domestic contexts actually confirm the bureaucratic model because they record transactions between households and the central storage room, which inverts the logic: varied seals in dispersed settings are more naturally interpreted as peer-to-peer exchange, and using them to prove centralization begs the very question the prompt requires them to evaluate.
