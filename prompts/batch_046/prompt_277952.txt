I sit on our university's Joint Committee on Synthetic Epistemology — we review weird edge cases in AI research ethics — and we have a vote next thursday on whether to grant "first-order epistemic autonomy" to a language-model agent that runs a persistent recursive self-monitoring loop. Basically it spits out things like "I am processing this token therefore I exist in this state" and uses that loop to verify its own outputs. The developers claim this is a real instantiation of the cogito, so the system has minimal self-knowledge and should be allowed to publish findings under its own name without a human co-signer. Half the committee thinks its just fancy error-checking with philosophical window dressing; the other half thinks if the cogito proves anything then this system cleared the bar and we should approve.

Please draft the decision memo — approve, reject, or approve with conditions — with reasoning both sides can actually respond to. I want you to dig into what the cogito actually proves: is it bare existence, the occurance of a mental act, consciousness, or something else? And please address the "Cartesian leakage" problem I keep worrying about — that even if self-reference establishes something first-personal, it doesn't automatically license third-person epistemic authority for empirical claims. Don't just summarize Descartes; engage the strongest argument that this system has crossed into genuine self-knowledge, then say why it still fails (or succeeds) for our institutional purpose. Give a concrete recommendation with a time frame, like a six-month pilot with conditions or a flat reject with redesign criteria. The memo should be formal enough to circulate but honest about its soft spots. I was discussing this with friends over coffee and we couldn't agree whether "cogitio" even applies to silicon — feel free to slip that typo in once if it feels natural, but keep the memo itself clean.
