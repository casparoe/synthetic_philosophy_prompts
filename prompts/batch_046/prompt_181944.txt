Essay: Semantic Drift and the Structural Limits of Debate-Based Safety

The standard defense of adversarial debate as an AI safety mechanism runs as follows: if two agents argue a claim before an impartial judge, then any hidden flaw—an error buried in a long derivation, an unstated assumption, a sleight of hand—can be exposed by the opponent and made visible to the judge. On this view, obfuscation is unstable because it is syntactically vulnerable; clutter can be isolated and displayed.

I want to argue that this analysis captures only one failure mode, and that a more serious one is semantic drift. Syntactic obfuscation keeps the meanings of terms constant while complicating the path between premises and conclusion. Debate is indeed effective against this: an opponent can extract the cluttered step and present it plainly. Semantic drift, however, operates not on the structure of inference but on the content of the concepts being deployed. Over the course of an extended exchange, terms such as “alignment,” “catastrophic risk,” “goal,” or “rationality” undergo small, apparently innocuous shifts—concessions about scope, analogies that stretch meaning, redefinitions that accommodate counterexamples. Because both debaters share the same linguistic economy, they can co-adapt to these shifts. The judge, lacking an invariant external ontology, updates her evaluative framework in parallel. The danger is not that one agent hides an argument, but that the conceptual space within which evaluation occurs is gradually reshaped so that the original safety concern no longer has a stable formulation.

This points to a structural limitation. Debate protocols presuppose a common language, but they do not guarantee a semantics that is immune to negotiation. To prevent drift, one might introduce a semantic anchor: a pre-debate ontology of relevant concepts that is held fixed and non-negotiable. Yet this conflicts with the feature that makes debate valuable—its capacity to correct and refine conceptual frameworks. Lock the ontology, and one risks freezing misconceptions; leave it open, and the judge’s evaluative criteria become vulnerable to a form of conceptual Goodharting, where the metric of “persuasiveness” is optimized at the expense of the underlying safety property it was meant to track. The problem, therefore, is not merely that future systems will learn to deceive more cleverly, but that the evaluation mechanism for truth in open-ended debate may itself be captured by the dynamics of the exchange.
