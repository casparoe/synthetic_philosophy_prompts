The etiological and causal-role accounts of biological function—roughly, the view that a trait’s function is fixed by its evolutionary selection history versus the view that it is fixed by its present causal contribution to a system’s capacity—have been debated for decades with remarkably little convergence. Every canonical objection (the heart that makes noise, the malfunctioning organ lacking a selection history, the accidentally useful trait) has been met with a standard reply and a standard counter-reply, and the literature now reads like a well-rehearsed script. My working theory is that the stall is not empirical—both sides agree on the biology—but conceptual and normative: the etiological camp needs “function” to carry the weight of design explanation and genuine malfunction, while the causal-role camp treats it as an organizational descriptor that applies equally to artifacts and accidental arrangements, so they may be answering different questions (why this trait is there versus how this system works) under a single heading. Is that diagnosis correct, or is there a hidden substantive disagreement—perhaps about whether evolutionary history is causally relevant to current dysfunction, or about which biological cases should count as central data—disguised by the shared vocabulary? Diagnose the impasse using what proponents on each side actually say about malfunction, design, and explanatory depth, rather than surveying interpretations. And tell me honestly whether anything—a precise conceptual distinction separating design-explanation from organizational-role, a formal model of functional ascription, a shift in which cases are treated as paradigmatic—could actually move the debate forward, or whether we should expect permanent deadlock and why.
