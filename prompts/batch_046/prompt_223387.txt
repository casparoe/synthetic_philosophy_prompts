Write a decision tree that sorts philosophical positions on karma understood specifically as a theory of moral desert—that is, a framework according to which present or future states of well-being or suffering are deserved in virtue of prior actions, independent of forward-looking consequentialist justification. Design the tree for an advanced undergraduate seminar in moral philosophy, so each branch node must be a concrete claim or thought-experiment that a student can answer without already knowing the label of the view it leads to. Begin with the root question: does moral desert require diachronic personal identity across states of being, or can it be satisfied within a single biological life?

From there, construct at least six further branch nodes covering: (1) whether proportionality is quantitative (suffering exactly balances harm) or qualitative (suffering transforms the moral character of the agent); (2) whether the mechanism requires a metaphysical substrate such as rebirth, a subtle body, or only a causal-continuity relation; (3) whether backward-looking retribution is sufficient or whether desert must also include a corrective or educative function; (4) whether the scope of desert is agent-indexed (only the original wrongdoer can bear it) or state-indexed (the causal chain can transfer desert to a successor being); (5) whether pleasure and suffering are commensurable on a single scale of desert, or whether extreme suffering carries lexical priority over any compensatory pleasure; and (6) whether a secular analog—say, social reputation and institutional punishment—can fully substitute for the metaphysical mechanism without loss of normative force.

The tree should terminate in named or clearly described leaves: for example, classical retributive karma with rebirth; transformative karma without agent-identity; secular single-life desert theory; Western meritocratic analog; agent-indexed diachronic desert; state-indexed causal-transfer desert; incommensurable suffering-priority karma; and a deflationary view that rejects diachronic desert altogether. For each leaf, provide a one-sentence statement of its most counterintuitive commitment. Additionally, flag any pair of leaves that your tree cannot cleanly separate—particularly where secular analogs and metaphysical versions converge on the same practical judgments—and briefly diagnose whether the gap is conceptual, normative, or merely unexplored. Do not ask for bibliographies, citation lists, or references to specific texts; the tree should stand on its internal logical structure alone.
