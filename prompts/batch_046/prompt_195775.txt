I am not a philosopher by training, but I have been working through a practical problem on my own using H.P. Grice’s idea that what a speaker means can outstrip what their words literally say, governed by expectations of cooperation. I sit on a volunteer community mediation panel that handles about ten disputes a month—mostly over text messages, meeting remarks, and verbal promises—where one party insists “that is not what I meant” and the other insists “that is what anyone reasonable would have taken you to mean.” I need a reusable decision procedure, essentially a step-by-step checklist with clear stopping conditions, that my panel can run in roughly twenty minutes per case without formal training in logic or linguistics. Our recommendations are reviewed by a lawyer, so the procedure must show exactly where a rule produces an answer and where human judgment must take over because the issue is one of values or context-reading rather than fact.

The conceptual translation I want is this: Grice’s cooperative framework assumes that speakers and listeners share a purpose, but in mediation cooperation has usually broken down. So I want the procedure built around three filters applied in strict order. First, the literal filter: what the sentence asserts if interpreted at face value with standard grammatical rules. Second, the cooperative-implicature filter: what a reasonable listener should infer if the speaker were still observing basic norms of relevance, informativeness, and clarity, given the shared context available to both parties. Third, the adversarial-withdrawal filter: whether the record contains specific markers—hedging language, irony indicators, ambiguous reference, prior open conflict, or changes in audience—that allow the speaker to plausibly deny they were cooperating at all, and therefore deny that they intended the implication. At each filter, specify what counts as a sufficient answer, where the procedure stops with a “no binding commitment” verdict, and where it must hand the decision back to the mediator.

The procedure must handle hard cases: messages without vocal tone; promises embedded in conditionals (“I would help if my schedule allows”); statements made in groups where not everyone shares the same background context; and cases where one party claims “implicature overreach”—that the listener invented a cooperative implication the speaker never intended. Please build in a specific check against this overreach: before Filter Two can apply, the mediator must identify at least one piece of independent, shared-context evidence (a prior agreement, a visible agenda item, a reply that acknowledged the point) rather than relying solely on the listener’s private inference.

Please apply the procedure to three worked cases. The first should be a direct text message—something like “Sure, I’ll handle the fundraiser setup”—where the speaker later claims sarcastic intent but the procedure binds them to commitment despite that claim. The second should involve a conditional promise made during a meeting with multiple attendees present. The third should be a case where the procedure produces an answer I will likely dislike: for example, finding that a speaker whose message sounded genuinely apologetic was in fact not committed to any concrete remedial action, because the withdrawal conditions in Filter Three defeat the apparent implication. For each case, show the output of all three filters and flag precisely where a mediator’s personal judgment enters.

Also specify what the procedure cannot do. It should not claim to read hidden mental states, resolve legal liability, or apply across cultures where cooperative norms differ radically. Please be explicit about its misfire points—especially where Filter Three might wrongly excuse a speaker who was in fact being deliberately ambiguous.

Finally, please say clearly what would change your mind about this design. For instance, would a demonstrated case in which speaker intention consistently diverges from listener inference across a broad sample make you abandon Filter Two in favor of a purely speaker-centered rule? Or would evidence that adversarial contexts make cooperative inference impossible cause you to collapse the three-filter structure? And identify the specific step in your reasoning—the adversarial-withdrawal check, the overreach safeguard, or the shared-context requirement—where you are least confident and most likely to be wrong.

Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citation requests. Do not ask me to verify specific page numbers. Build the answer directly from the conceptual structure you outline.
