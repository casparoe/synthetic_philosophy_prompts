In discussions that recruit Aristotle’s Politics I and Nicomachean Ethics V for modern economic criticism, a common analogy maps his distinction between oikonomia—household management directed toward natural use and sufficiency—and chrematistike—wealth-getting directed toward unlimited monetary accumulation—onto the contemporary distinction between a circular, regenerative economy (where resources cycle back into productive use within ecological limits) and a linear, extractive financial economy (where capital is converted into ever-accumulating monetary stock detached from material regeneration). Those who use it argue that Aristotle’s claim that money is barren, in that it does not reproduce like grain or livestock, anticipates the ecological and ethical critique of financialization: just as chrematistike breaks the natural cycle of use and need, modern finance treats renewable stocks as infinite flows of extractable value. Please audit this analogy in detail: provide an explicit correspondence table (household or polis, natural limit, currency, regenerative cycle, unlimited accumulation, and the role of exchange justice or antipeponthos); then identify the precise points of breakdown—especially Aristotle’s teleological embedding of economics within the politics of the polis, his qualified acceptance of retail trade (kapēlikē) within natural limits, the fact that his sterility argument concerns biological reproduction rather than ecological depletion, and the absence in his framework of any concept of intergenerational sustainability; examine what modern normative commitments (ecological limits, sustainability, anti-rentier politics) the analogy quietly imports that are foreign to Aristotle’s own priorities; and tell me whether the conclusion—that Aristotle’s framework genuinely supports a circular-economy critique of financialization—survives once these disanalogies are on the table, or whether the analogy was carrying the normative load all along. In doing so, separate what is settled in Aristotelian scholarship (for instance, the textual distinction between natural and unnatural acquisition in Politics I.8–10, the argument that tokos is unnatural in Ethics V.5, and the role of proportional reciprocity in exchange) from what is genuinely contested (for instance, whether chrematistike is vicious in all cases or only when unlimited, whether Aristotle’s exchange justice is best read as proportional reciprocity or as a form of distributive correction, and whether his economic thought can be detached from his political teleology). Do not present a contested interpretive view as the consensus.
