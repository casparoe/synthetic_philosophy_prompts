Here is an argument I built for a seminar and now find suspicious because the conclusion wipes out a distinction I take seriously. (1) An action has moral worth only if it is done for the right reason, where that means the agent is moved by recognizing the act as morally required. (2) For a recognition to genuinely be the motivating reason, it must be counterfactually exclusive: in the nearest world where the agent lacks that recognition, the act does not occur. (3) Human motivation is always psychologically overdetermined; for any prosocial act there are sufficient non-normative causes—habit, affect, anticipated reward—that would produce the same behavior even without the normative recognition. (4) So the recognition of rightness is never counterfactually exclusive in actual agents. (5) Therefore, by (1) and (2), no human action ever possesses moral worth. (6) Yet we clearly distinguish the clerk who returns change from duty from the one who does it only for the security camera; if (5) were true this distinction would be groundless. (7) Hence (1) is false and acting for the right reasons is unnecessary for moral worth. I would rather keep (1) and (6) than accept (5) or (7). Where does the reasoning break—whether in a hidden shift between normative and explanatory reasons, in the demand for exclusivity, or elsewhere—and what is the price of repairing it? Give your answer as a numbered list of exactly five items, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion.
