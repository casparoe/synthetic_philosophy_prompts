I have been tracking the case judgments of a particular information-ethics scholar—let me call her Dr. R—across a series of proposals involving data collection, anonymization, and secondary use. Her record so far is as follows.

First, she endorsed a public library's plan to digitize and publish its 1980s borrowing logs after stripping borrower names but retaining book titles and checkout dates, arguing that this served a legitimate archival purpose and that anonymity protected the borrowers' interests.

Second, she opposed a corporate wellness program that used wearable biometric monitors to optimize office lighting and temperature, even though employees gave explicit opt-in consent, HR was contractually barred from viewing individual metrics, and the data was never used for evaluation or promotion.

Third, she permitted a sociolinguistic study that scraped only publicly visible social-media posts—without contacting users—to map regional dialect patterns, maintaining that public visibility implied acceptance of observational research uses.

Fourth, she rejected a smart-city pilot installing acoustic sensors in public parks designed to detect gunshots through local processing; she granted that no audio was stored or transmitted beyond the alert, but insisted that the technology necessarily intercepted conversational content in a space where people reasonably expected acoustic privacy.

She is now being consulted on a university proposal to construct an aggregate "emotional climate" model of campus buildings. The researchers would apply sentiment-analysis algorithms to publicly geotagged social-media posts made within university structures, outputting only building-level stress indices for architects and planners. No individual user would be identified, no raw post retained, and no interaction with posters required.

Please predict Dr. R's verdict on this proposal. Then identify at least two distinct principled rules—such as one emphasizing contextual integrity of information flows, one emphasizing whether technology extracts latent psychological dispositions not available to ordinary observation in that setting, and one emphasizing structural vulnerability and the conditions for genuinely voluntary consent—that are each consistent with her four prior judgments. For each rule, state precisely what it predicts about the campus emotional-climate case, note which of her earlier verdicts it accommodates most naturally and which it must strain to explain, and indicate where the rules diverge. Offer your best-guess prediction of her actual verdict, with explicit reasons, but be honest about where her track record genuinely underdetermines the answer. Finally, propose one further hypothetical case—distinct from all five above—that would best discriminate between the rules you have identified.

Separate what is settled in privacy scholarship from what remains genuinely contested among specialists, and do not present any particular contested framework—such as the view that contextual integrity is the dominant analytical lens—as if it represented the consensus.
