I'm drafting a scene for my third novel—slow, philosophical, about people who build systems they don't understand—where my protagonist Mara gives opening remarks at a workshop called "When Intention Fails." The audience is split right down the middle: engineers on one side who think alignement just means the model does exactly what the prompt intends, and philosphers on the other who think it's about embedding actual human values into the weights. Mara used to work in this space before she walked away, and she needs to speak from the narrow definition—intent alignment versus value alignment—without letting either camp off the hook, which means she can't promise long-term safety or ethical outcomes, she can only talk about execution fidelity, and that limitation should shape every paragraph she speaks.

I'm playing with this concept I'm calling the "residue gap" for the book, basically the values that never make it into the intent specifcation because you can't encode what you haven't named yet, and I want her to drop it in casually, like she's thinking out loud in front of strangers who know the math but not what responsibility actually weighs. Don't cite anyone, no reading lists, just write it as her speaking for about five minutes—tired, she's been up since four, maybe she leaves a sentence hanging or mispronounces "eudaimonia"—so it lands as a real moment rather than a lecture. The philosophical idea should do structural work: because she accepts only the narrow, intent-only framing for the sake of argument, there's a whole range of promises she literally cannot make, and I want the speech to show that absence rather than decorate around it.
