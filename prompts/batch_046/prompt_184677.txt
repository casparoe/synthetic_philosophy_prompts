I am a doctoral student in comparative philosophy working on non-Western epistemologies and philosophy of mind. I am drafting an essay that offers a non-standard reading of Jain syādvāda—the seven-mode scheme of qualified predication (syāt asti, syāt nāsti, syāt asti-nāsti, syāt avaktavya, and their compound forms). Rather than treating it as a proto-paraconsistent logic or a mere tolerance doctrine, I propose interpreting it as an "epistemic etiquette": a normative framework for coordinating conflicting standpoints (nayas) without collapsing them into a single evaluation context. On this reading, the seven modes are not competing logical values assigned to one context, but indexed discourse roles: each naya licenses a specific qualified assertion about the same object, and the etiquette requires retaining all seven as simultaneously appropriate rather than selecting a winner.

To distinguish this from familiar Western analogues, I contrast it with epistemic contextualism, which resolves conflicts by shifting assessment contexts; syādvāda, I claim, holds the object fixed but multiplies the standpoints. I then apply the framework to the contemporary debate over whether consciousness is fundamental: from a reductive physicalist naya, ultimate consciousness receives syād nāsti; from a phenomenological naya, syād asti; and the intermediate modes capture relational or inexpressible qualifications. My concern is that this may be only redescription.

(b) Does the comparison to contextualism hold, given that contextualism shifts evaluation parameters while syādvāda preserves simultaneous validity across standpoints? (c) Does the application to the consciousness debate generate philosophical traction or merely relabel existing positions?
