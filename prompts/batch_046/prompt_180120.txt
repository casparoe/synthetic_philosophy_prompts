Could you set out the most important implications of the problem Clément Marot confronted when translating Ovid's Metamorphoses under severe formal constraint—preserving an elegant acoustic and syntactic surface, often characterized as his ton beau, even as Latin quantitative meter and precise semantic architecture were necessarily reshaped—for my own work, which has recently become personally urgent. I am translating a long, argumentatively dense philosophical poem into a language that imposes strict syllabic count, mandatory internal rhyme, and frequent syntactic inversion; I have begun to notice what I call prosodic debt, the hidden structural obligation that accrues whenever a formal feature of the source, such as a logical conjunction embedded in word order, is sacrificed to preserve an acoustic pattern in the target text. I want to understand whether Marot's preservation of form is best read as compensation for inevitable semantic loss, as an independent aesthetic claim that actively reconfigures the source's meaning, or as a generative distortion that reveals hidden structure. In particular, I am trying to decide whether I should treat my own accumulating prosodic debts as errors to minimize, as neutral translations of conceptual weight into acoustic weight, or as productive reinterpretations. What are the most important implications of Marot's practice for each of these three stances, and which stance, in your view, forces me to abandon the very idea of a faithful translation?
