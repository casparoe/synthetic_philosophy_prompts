As a journalist covering philosophy of science and the hidden architectures of belief, I am preparing an interactive feature—something like a branching decision-tree graphic—for readers who work in interdisciplinary research but have never formalized how they justify their methodological commitments. Over coffee with a cognitive scientist friend, I realized that researchers often behave like implicit epistemologists: some treat calibrated instrument readouts as stopping points for justification, others treat every claim as supported only by its coherence with the wider predictive model, and a few seem comfortable saying that asking “why?” could in principle continue forever. I want a taxonomy, presented as a tree, that sorts these stances on the epistemic regress problem without requiring the reader to already know the labels foundationalism, coherentism, or infinitism, and without relying on any bibliographic references.

Please construct the tree so that each internal node is a concrete question about justification that a working scientist could answer without knowing philosophy jargon. The first split should ask whether justification can ever terminate in beliefs that are not themselves supported by further beliefs—whether anchored by private experience, by public reproducible protocols, or not at all. From there, branch into whether such anchors, if they exist, are treated as incorrigible or as fallible; if fallible, whether the anchor is a mental seeming or an institutional calibration practice (this latter distinction is central to my feature, because it separates traditional modest foundationalism from a view I am tentatively calling practice-anchored foundationalism). For the non-terminating paths, split between holistic mutual-support structures and linear infinite chains; within the holistic branch, distinguish pure coherence from structures that allow privileged nodes—such as predictive-checkpoint beliefs—without granting them basic status; within the linear branch, distinguish whether the endlessness is a vicious failure of justification or a benign feature, and further whether the chain is practically traversable or only an abstract structure. Every leaf should name the position it represents—strong rationalist foundationalism, modest phenomenal foundationalism, practice-anchored foundationalism, pure coherentism, structured coherentism with privileged nodes, benign practical infinitism, theoretical infinitism, and regress skepticism—along with one sentence describing its most awkward commitment. You should also map the logical space: list the answer-combinations your tree permits but that appear unoccupied, and for each diagnose whether the gap is due to incoherence, unattractiveness, or mere neglect. Finally, flag any pair of leaves that this tree genuinely cannot separate, explaining what further case or distinction would be needed to pull them apart. The deliverable should be formal enough to serve as the backbone of a magazine explainer, and it should depend on no specific texts, dates, or citations—readers should navigate it entirely from the conceptual structure you provide.
