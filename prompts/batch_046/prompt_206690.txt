At a recent museum-ethics panel, a curator defended retaining a major retrospective of a filmmaker convicted of serious crimes by insisting that the institution must “separate the art from the artist.” She deployed the phrase both to dismiss the objection that the exhibition implicitly honors the perpetrator and to accuse critics of committing a category error. I am trying to understand exactly what work this slogan can be made to do. Please lay out the distinct precise theses that “separate the art from the artist” might express—distinguishing, for example, ontological independence of the work from its maker’s moral status, epistemic irrelevance of biography to interpretation, normative autonomy of aesthetic judgment, and practical institutional separation of display decisions from biographical knowledge. For each reading, indicate whether it is a platitude, a substantive and defensible claim, or false. Then specify which reading the curator’s argument actually requires, and note where people who invoke the slogan in such debates typically smuggle in a stronger claim than they can defend. In particular, address how the slogan functions differently under solitary authorship (e.g., a painter working alone) versus collective or institutional production (e.g., a feature film or an architectural project involving dozens of collaborators), since the panel concerned a filmmaker. Finally, tell me which of these readings, if any, genuinely supports retaining the retrospective despite full knowledge of the crimes, and which ones are being illicitly used to end the argument rather than to advance it.
