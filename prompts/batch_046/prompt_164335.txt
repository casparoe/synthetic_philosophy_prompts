Here is an argument I sketched about sphexishness that seems to erase the distinction between rigid instinct and human deliberation, though I find the conclusion hard to accept. (1) A system is sphexish when it executes a fixed routine R upon encountering trigger conditions C, ignoring contextual differences that do not match C. (2) A system counts as genuinely flexible — and thus as a candidate for free will — only if it can suspend or revise R in response to such contextual differences. (3) Any suspension of R must be governed by a higher-order rule R' that determines when revision is warranted. (4) If R' is itself fixed, then the system's flexibility is sphexish at the meta-level: it rigidly applies R' to generate only the appearance of adaptability. (5) To avoid meta-level sphexishness, R' must be revisable by R'', which must be revisable by R''', and so on, producing an infinite regress of governing rules. (6) No finite system can complete an infinite regress of governing rules. (7) Therefore every finite system, including any human agent, contains an unmodifiable sphexish core that ultimately governs all apparent behavioral revisions. (8) Consequently, the difference between a sphex wasp and a reasoning person is merely quantitative — greater complexity of routines, not a qualitative escape from mechanical rigidity — so free will is illusory. (9) Yet we do seem to draw a real, not merely quantitative, distinction between instinctive rigidity and reasoned choice. Which step should I reject? I want to keep (2) and (9) if possible. Is the error an equivocation in (3) between behavioral revision and normative assessment, or does (5) illegitimately assume a hierarchical, rule-based architecture that distributed or embodied cognition could avoid? And if the argument is valid, what is the least costly premise to abandon — (6), with its commitment to finitude, or (7), with its claim about an unmodifiable core?
