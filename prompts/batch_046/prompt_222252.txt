I'm a backend software engineer with basically no formal philosoophy background — I learn this stuff from podcasts and wikipedia — and I'm putting together a take-home exam for a small reading group I started. We just went over the dogmatism paradox, but I don't want people to just recite the standard formulation about knowing p and then hearing not-p. I want the question to actually make them do work. So here's my specific framing: use a distributed systems analogy. Picture a cluster of database nodes that reach consensus on a transaction being committed; then one node discovers its own vote was corrupted by a bad log entry. The cluster now has to choose: roll back the transaction (admitting the prior consensus wasn't real knowledge) or keep it committed (treating the prior state as knowledge that survives the defeater). The exam should ask students to map this scenario onto the dogmatism paradox with precision — they need to identify which part of the system corresponds to epistemic closure, which part is the defeater, and wether holding onto the commitment despite corruption is genuinely dogmatic or just rational stability. The model answer needs to be around 500 words and must take a committed stance: either argue that rollback is required and that calling it dogmatism confuses knowledge with justified belief, or argue that holding firm is correct and the paradox only arises if we misapply closure to defeasible entitlements. Don't just list both sides; pick one, defend it, and say exactly where the other view goes wrong. I also want a marking scheme for an upper-level seminar: four or five specific conceptual moves each worth points, totaling about 20, plus a one-line note on the typical mistake for each. No bibliographies or citation requests — this should be answerable from reasoning and the scenario alone.
