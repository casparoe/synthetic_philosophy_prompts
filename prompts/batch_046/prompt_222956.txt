I am writing this for an independent study in political economy, and I have been trying to understand why the socialist calculation debate remains so resistant to empirical resolution. In our course we read the classic exchange in which Mises and Hayek argue that rational economic calculation requires private property and real market prices to reveal dispersed local knowledge, while Lange replies that a central board could use trial-and-error pricing rules to approximate market outcomes without capitalism. The debate is usually treated as purely theoretical, but I recently came across Project Cybersyn—the cybernetic management system designed by Stafford Beer for Salvador Allende’s Chile between 1971 and 1973—which appears to be an actual institutional attempt to build the machinery that one side of the debate was imagining.

Here is what I find puzzling, and I would like an analysis that treats Cybersyn not merely as a failed technological experiment but as a live case study in the calculation debate. Beer did not attempt to replicate Lange’s simulated market with iterative price adjustments; instead, he designed a recursive Viable System Model in which state factories retained local autonomy and reported real-time production data through a computer network, with higher-level systems monitoring variance rather than dictating output. So was Cybersyn an implementation of Lange’s market socialism, or was it an entirely different answer to Mises—one that tried to preserve local knowledge not through prices but through distributed cybernetic feedback? I want you to map the actors carefully: who held decision rights, what counted as economic calculation in Beer’s architecture versus in Lange’s board, and whether Hayek’s critique about tacit, context-specific knowledge applies differently to a system that decentralizes information rather than centralizing prices. Then tell me where the theory breaks down against the episode: the coup in 1973 ended the project before full deployment, but was there also a conceptual failure—did Beer confuse information transmission with economic calculation—or does the episode actually strengthen Hayek’s position by showing that even computer-enhanced decentralization cannot substitute for the price mechanism? Finally, what does this case reveal about the debate itself: was Lange asking the wrong question all along, or does Cybersyn show that the socialist calculation problem was never about computation but about the institutional locus of entrepreneurial discovery?
