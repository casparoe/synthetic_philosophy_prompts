I spent the evening in a long argument with a close friend about whether "national character" is a coherent concept at all, and whether it is ever intellectually respectable to generalize about peoples. She opened with a precise challenge: if national character is meant to be an explanatory kind—something that causally accounts for behavioral differences across populations—then it collapses either into discredited biological essentialism or into redundant sociology, since institutions, class structures, and historical contingency do all the actual explanatory work. I replied that she was presupposing an overly realist ontology; I proposed instead that we treat national character as a regulative ideal, a shared normative self-image that shapes conduct without rigidly determining it, much as personal character can be aspirational rather than purely descriptive. She conceded that this avoids false essentialism, but objected that it renders the concept explanatorily useless and politically dangerous, since policymakers invariably slide from talk of regulative ideals to descriptive claims about what a people "are really like." I then introduced a distinction I had recently encountered: between distributive generalizations, which attribute a trait to every member of a group, and collective generalizations, which attribute emergent properties to a group as a structured whole—like saying an orchestra plays with a restrained style without attributing restraint to each musician. I argued that respectable generalizations about peoples are always collective, not distributive, and that recognizing this saves comparative cultural analysis from both statistical falsehood and essentialism. She countered that collective properties still require grounding in individual dispositions, and that once one attempts such grounding one either reverts to distributive claims or admits that the "national" framing is arbitrary—why privilege the nation over region, profession, or generation? We went in circles and left it unresolved.

Please adjudicate our exchange point by point: assess the strength of her explanatory-kind challenge, my regulative-ideal reply, her objection about the normative-to-descriptive slide, my collective-versus-distributive distinction, and her final reply about grounding and arbitrariness. Say who is right about what, and what both of us are missing. In particular, please separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among philosophers and social scientists who work on these issues, and do not present any contested view as the consensus.
