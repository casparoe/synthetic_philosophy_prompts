Take the 2014 Scottish independance referendum and analyze it through Hobsbawm's invention of tradition and Anderson's imagined communities. The Yes side constantly refered back to the Declaration of Arbroath in 1320 as proof Scotland was always a sovereign nation, framing the vote as "restoring" independance rather than breaking away. Meanwhile it's well known that modern Scottish national iconography — tartans, kilts, the romantic Highland image — was largely standardized or invented in the 18th and 19th centuries, and that "Britain" itself was an imagined community constructed after 1707. I want you to map the campaign onto the theory: which rhetorical moves are classic invented-tradition stuff, which features of the referendum it explains well (like why ancient symbols still move people), and where it falls silent or gets it wrong — for instance, does it have anything useful to say about the economic arguments or about the fact that a lot of voters just didn't care about history? Then flip it: what does the referendum's failure, and the fact that Scottish identity persisted inside the UK rather than dissolving, show about Anderson's claim that imagined communities are durable? Does it support the idea, or suggest that communities can be re-imagined downward? One more thing: be honest about what's actually settled among historians versus what's genuinely contested. Don't present the "all national traditions are invented" line as if every scholar agrees, since I know there's real pushback on that. Separate the settled parts from the parts people still argue over, and don't pretend there's a consensus where there isn't.
