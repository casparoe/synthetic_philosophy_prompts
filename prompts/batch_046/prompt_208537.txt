Policy discussions about digital privacy often rely on an analogy to real property: personal data is like a house, consent mechanisms are like locks and doors, unauthorized access is trespass or theft, and the appropriate remedy is to strengthen individual ownership rights and market exchange. The analogy is used to argue that privacy protection should be modeled on property law rather than on rights of personality or contextual norms. Audit it: lay out an explicit correspondence table (data subject to homeowner, information flow to entry, consent to key, etc.); identify the precise points where the mapping collapses, particularly with respect to the non-rivalrous, infinitely replicable, and relationally constituted nature of information versus the rivalrous, spatially bounded, and individually possessed nature of real estate; specify which normative features of property—exclusivity, alienability, and clear boundaries—the analogy quietly imports into the privacy debate and whether those features actually fit; and determine whether the conclusion that privacy is best protected by proprietary control and voluntary exchange survives once the disanalogies are explicit. In doing so, consider whether framing privacy instead as the maintenance of contextually appropriate informational boundaries—where the harm lies not in unauthorized entry but in the disruption of social norms governing flow—offers a more accurate account without relying on the property metaphor.
