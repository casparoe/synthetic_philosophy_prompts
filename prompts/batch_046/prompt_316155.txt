In covering philosophy of science, I keep encountering a peculiar stall in the Bayesian literature on old evidence—cases such as Mercury’s perihelion being well known long before general relativity arrived. The formal results are uncontroversial: Bayesians can show that P(E|T) exceeds P(E) regardless of when the agent first learned E. Yet the philosophical dispute has barely shifted in decades. One camp, roughly in Howson’s spirit, insists that if an agent already assigns E high credence, the theory cannot genuinely confirm it; the other, closer to Garber’s resolution, treats confirmation as a static logical relation between propositions, so the diachronic fact of prior belief is simply irrelevant.

My working hypothesis is that the disagreement is not about mathematics at all, but about a hidden divergence over what an epistemic agent is. The first camp seems to be describing an embodied, historically situated learner for whom learning requires an actual change in belief state; the second describes an atemporal logical construct for whom only the present credence function matters. If that diagnosis holds, are the two sides answering different questions—one normative-descriptive, the other purely formal—under the single heading of confirmation? Or is there a bedrock intuition about epistemic surprise or explanatory relevance that neither side can defend?

Please diagnose the structure of this impasse: is it verbal, substantive, or a case of two research programs running parallel? Does a hidden empirical question about how scientists actually revise beliefs do the work, or is the core conflict about standards for what counts as a reason? And given your diagnosis, what—an explicit formal distinction between diachronic bookkeeping and synchronic informativeness, a reconception of the agent in question, or an argument that the problem is ill-posed—would count as genuine progress, or should we expect no resolution?
