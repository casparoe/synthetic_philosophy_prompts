I am writing this after a long and somewhat frustrating argument with a colleague in our aesthetics reading group, in which we kept returning to whether hedonism can survive an honest phenomenology of pleasure. I work as an acoustic engineer and composer, so my conceptual intuitions are shaped less by utility curves than by how we model sound: spectral envelopes, formant structures, attack-decay profiles, and the psychoacoustic phenomenon that two tones with identical pitch and amplitude can be experienced as utterly different because of timbral color—an irreducible qualitative dimension that resists decomposition into constituent frequencies. I want you to take the classical heterogeneity problem for hedonism—the worry that bodily relief, intellectual satisfaction, and sensory enjoyment share no introspectible common feature; Mill’s distinction between higher and lower pleasures; Sidgwick’s concern that qualitative differences undermine any common quantitative metric; and the modern eliminativist suggestion that “pleasure” is merely a folk-psychological label masking heterogeneous neural events—and restate each major position (pure quantitative hedonism, qualitative pluralism, the common-currency view, and the eliminativist move) in the vocabulary of auditory phenomenology and spectral analysis. For each translation, specify what maps cleanly onto acoustic analogues, what must be distorted to fit—say, treating timbre as decomposable into pure sinusoidal components—and what has no expression at all in that framework. Then deliver the residue report: does the failure of translation reveal that hedonism lacks a dimension comparable to timbral space, or does it show that my acoustic vocabulary is too domain-specific to capture normative structure? I am not asking for a bibliography or a history of the debate, but for an honest conceptual translation whose value lies precisely in what it cannot carry.
