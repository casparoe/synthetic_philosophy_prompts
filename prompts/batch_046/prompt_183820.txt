As a retired experimental physicist who spent several decades working on precision measurement, calibration protocols, and error-correction algorithms, I have recently begun studying formal epistemology with particular attention to Nozick’s tracking theory of knowledge and the well-known failure of epistemic closure under sensitivity and adherence conditions. The standard literature rehearses familiar skeptical counterexamples—disguised mules, brains in vats, and the like—but I find these distant from the structural problems that actually arise in scientific practice, where knowledge is rarely formed through direct observation alone. Instead, belief propagates through cascades of instrumentally mediated inferences: detectors record signals, statistical filters process outputs, computational reconstructions generate representations, and theoretical conclusions are drawn from premises that no human observer could verify unaided. This suggests what I will call an “instrumental closure” problem: if Nozick’s counterfactual conditions apply differentially across nodes of a coupled agent-instrument system, then the failure of closure for direct perceptual beliefs may be only the surface manifestation of a deeper breakdown in technologically mediated reasoning chains.

Given this framing, I am not seeking a survey of established objections to tracking theory, nor a bibliography of recent modal-epistemological literature. Rather, I want a defended prioritization of the genuinely open questions at this intersection, tailored to someone with my background—comfortable with counterfactual reasoning about physical systems, but without extensive training in formal modal logic or the finer points of contemporary semantics. Among the unresolved issues I see, which should take precedence: whether tracking conditions must be indexed to the agent alone, to the instrument-agent coupled system, or to each inferential node separately; whether closure failure has structurally different implications for scientific reasoning than for ordinary empirical knowledge; whether a principled middle position can preserve deductive transmission of knowledge without requiring the omniscient sensitivity that tracking conditions seem to deny; and whether recent formal analyses of modal proximity and safety conditions can be adapted to evaluate instrumentally mediated beliefs without collapsing into either pure reliabilism or unrestrained closure? Please rank these—or substitute others you judge more central—and defend the ordering with explicit criteria: tractability for a researcher with scientific but not specialist philosophical training, upstream dependence relations (which answers would reshape the others), and what specific kind of progress—whether a conceptual distinction, a formal result, or a revised framework—would cause you to revise the ranking.

I intend to use this agenda to guide about a year of independent study and eventually a long-form essay on knowledge transmission in technologically mediated science. I am therefore less interested in which debates are historically prominent than in which lines of work, if pursued seriously, would most alter how one understands the relationship between counterfactual reliability and deductive inference in contexts where human cognition is distributed across calibrated machinery. Treat this as an allocation problem for limited intellectual resources: tell me where the years should go, what makes a question worth that investment rather than merely unresolved, and which apparent open questions are in fact downstream of others or unlikely to change the broader picture regardless of their answer.
