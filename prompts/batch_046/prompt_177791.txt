For my grad seminar I'm trying to build a decision tree on whether genuine moral dilemmas are actually possible, and I want students sorted by what they actually think, not by which philosopher they happen to like. Start with a concrete case: a surgeon has one liver and two patients with identical need—picking one means the other dies. Then branch with questions a student can answer without knowing any labels: is there a hidden correct choice the surgeon just can't see? Is there genuinely no right answer? Does the surgeon still owe something to the one who dies—some moral remainder, regret, or guilt? Build the tree out in logical space first: combinations of yes/no on hidden correct answer, presence of moral remainder, and whether the conflict is between obligations or just competing reasons. Then place the actual positions in that grid: the no-dilemma view, tragic-choice-with-remainder, pure-conflict-no-remainder, and one niche view I want included—call it the 'hidden-weight' view, where dilemmas only occur when the correct weight of competing obligations is epistemically opaque to the agent, but an omniscient observer could resolve it. Note any empty slots—like maybe nobody holds both 'there's always a hidden answer' and 'moral remainder is rational'—and diagnose briefly why that combo is incoherent or just ugly. Flag pairs of leaves your tree can't really seperate, especially if they differ only on metaphysical ground the questions miss. Keep it practical for a handout: no bibliography, no citation list, just the tree, the case, and the reasoning. Oh, and feel free to leave a typo or two—this is a draft, not a journal article.
