As a retired experimental physicist who has only recently begun to study philosophy seriously, I am seeking guidance on a decision facing my former institution. A bequest is sufficient either to preserve our original 1960s analog computation laboratory—its equipment, handwritten logbooks, and the specific spatial environment in which a generation of researchers developed a distinctive mode of reasoning—as a permanent historical site, or to liquidate the property and fund ten full scholarships for students in quantitative disciplines. I find the two outcomes genuinely incomparable: safeguarding that particular material culture of thought seems to protect an irreplaceable form of intellectual life, while the scholarships represent an immediate, transformative benefit to individuals. I have recently encountered a suggestion that when goods resist evaluation on any shared scale, rational choice is not a matter of discovering hidden weights, but of committing to one option through the decision itself, thereby shaping one's practical identity. This strikes me as elegant, yet I fear it risks reducing a serious moral tension to an exercise in self-definition. I would be grateful for a careful assessment of whether my dilemma exemplifies this kind of conflict, and whether that framework resolves the difficulty or merely disguises it. Please respond in American English, with clarity and directness.
