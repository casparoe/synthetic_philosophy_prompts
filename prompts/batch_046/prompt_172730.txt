Write a poem structured as a six-item archival accession list—items 1 through 6, each introduced by its number—using blank verse or free verse across roughly 20 to 30 lines. Items 1, 2, 3, 5, and 6 should describe concrete surviving documents (a land deed, a census roll, correspondence, a photograph, meeting minutes) in the voice of someone examining the shelf. Item 4 should appear only as its number followed by a line of white space or the single centered word “Unlisted,” with no descriptive content. The formal gap should perform conceptual work: the poem should ask whether item 4’s silence is an accidental absence (the record was never made) or an active erasure (destroyed or withheld), and it should treat the power to classify that gap as “never existed” rather than “removed” as itself an exercise of authority. Without naming specific theorists or providing a bibliography, render the idea that archives do not neutrally preserve the past but actively structure what can be said about it, and that the provenance of what survives is inseparable from the institutional authority that kept it. Leave the ambiguity of item 4 unresolved; do not resolve the tension with a sermon about recovery or a shrug about inevitable loss. Present the poem first, with no introductory commentary or framing. After the poem, include a brief, clearly separated note identifying where you take liberties with contested ideas—particularly if you treat every archival absence as structurally produced by power rather than contingent—and invite pushback: if the premise that silence always signals authority is confused or overreaching, say so plainly rather than forcing the poem to fit.
