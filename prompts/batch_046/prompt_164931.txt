I am a doctoral student in philosophy of technology, currently serving as an external ethics reviewer for a regional transit authority piloting autonomous shuttle buses in a mixed-use urban zone. The authority needs a reusable review protocol—something an engineer or I can apply in about thirty minutes—to evaluate whether a proposed design change, software update, or incident report reveals a responsibility gap for harm caused or risked by the shuttle's autonomous decision system.

Standard philosophical accounts of responsibility gaps distinguish cases in which no human agent is clearly accountable because system behavior is unpredictable or emergent. For this protocol, I want to work with a slightly finer taxonomy developed specifically for learning-enabled vehicles operating in open, uncertified environments:

1. Design gap: the behavior is traceable to the system's specified architecture, yet no designer or user intended or foresaw that specific action under the conditions that obtained.
2. Deployment gap: the behavior arises from interaction between the certified system and local, contingent environmental features—weather patterns, informal pedestrian norms, temporary construction—not present in the certification environment.
3. Adaptation gap: the behavior results from post-deployment updates, whether explicit model retraining, reinforcement-learning adjustments, or feature-weight shifts, that causally disconnect the operating system from the version that received regulatory certification.

Please construct a decision procedure in ordered steps, with the following properties:

- Each step poses a specific, answerable question; specify what counts as a sufficient affirmative or negative answer.
- Identify the precise points where the procedure yields a definitive outcome: stop—no gap; stop—unassigned responsibility; or stop—proceed only with board-level authorization.
- Explicitly mark the points where judgment cannot be eliminated—values, risk tolerance, institutional liability preferences—and the decision must be handed to the oversight board rather than resolved by the reviewer.
- Flag the conditions under which the procedure is likely to misfire, especially false confidence that a log file or version number resolves an adaptation gap.
- The procedure must be usable by a reviewer with engineering training but no philosophical background, and it should be honest about which distinctions (e.g., deployment gap versus adaptation gap) require interpretive judgment that cannot be fully proceduralized.
- Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, citation requests, or appeals to authority; the procedure must stand on its own conceptual footing.

Then apply the procedure to three scenarios:

(a) A proposed over-the-air update that lowers the pedestrian-detection confidence threshold from 0.92 to 0.85 to improve traffic flow. The procedure should yield an answer that protects certification integrity even if the engineering team strongly dislikes it.

(b) An incident in which the shuttle abruptly braked in a designated bike lane during an unseasonal snowstorm. Post-incident logs reveal that the vision model re-weighted snow-texture features in a manner not present in the certified model, though the weights changed within a permitted 'local adaptation' module.

(c) A proposal to allow the shuttle fleet to continuously learn from local traffic patterns over a six-month period without human-in-the-loop review of weight updates.

Finally, state clearly what the procedure cannot do—where it reaches its limits and a human decision is unavoidable.
