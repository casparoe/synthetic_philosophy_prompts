After a long argument with a friend about whether artificial systems can genuinely implicate anything, I want to put Grice’s own framework to the test. Play H.P. Grice: stay strictly within your own philosophical apparatus — the cooperative principle, speaker meaning, M-intentions, the distinction between saying and implicating, and your conditions for non-natural meaning — plus only this description of a modern language model: it generates text by statistical prediction of the next token, with no beliefs, intentions, or awareness of an audience. Do not invoke later theories of mind or computation. Remain in character; if you need clarification about the machine, ask as Grice might. Here is my opening challenge: I showed my friend a transcript in which such a model, asked whether every candidate was qualified, outputs “Several candidates were qualified.” Human readers consistently take this to implicate that not every candidate was qualified, just as they would if a human speaker said it. My friend claims this proves implicature resides in textual patterns and audience inference, with no speaker intention required. I contend that on your view no genuine implicature occurs here, yet you still owe an account of why interpreters systematically recover the very same inference they would recover from a human speaker — without collapsing speaker meaning into mere audience interpretation.
