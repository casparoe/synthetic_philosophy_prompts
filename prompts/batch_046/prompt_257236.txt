In alignment and metaethics discussions on rationalist forums I keep encountering the slogan that "reasons don't need reasoners"—the idea that normative considerations can exist and bind even in the absence of any agent who recognizes them, holds them psychologically, or could be moved by them. The phrase is usually deployed to argue that we should build AI systems to respect "objective" reasons that those systems might not initially represent or be motivated by. I want to understand exactly what commitments this slogan carries, because I suspect people are sliding between very different claims. Could you lay out, as a menu of distinct precise theses, what "reasons don't need reasoners" might actually mean? Specifically, distinguish readings that concern (1) ontology—whether normative properties exist as Platonic relations, as irreducible primitives, or as structural features of decision problems; (2) scope—whether reasons apply only to actual agents, to all possible agents, to non-agents such as ecosystems or AI architectures, or to counterfactual agents; (3) explanation—whether citing a reason to explain correct action requires that reason to have entered anyone's cognition, or whether free-floating reasons can explain behavior without being represented; and (4) normative authority—whether a reason's binding force depends on an agent's capacity to respond to it, or whether unresponded-to reasons still generate obligations. For each reading, indicate whether it is trivial, philosophically defensible, or false, and—crucially—whether that particular reading actually supports the alignment argument that an AI ought to be designed around reasons it does not yet represent. I am not asking for a bibliography or a history of the idea; I want the disambiguation with verdicts, so I can diagnose which version is being smuggled in when someone uses this line in a debate about artificial agency.
