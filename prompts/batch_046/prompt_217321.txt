Consider a long-stalled debate in the philosophy of collective memory: whether narratives of defeated or tragic national events can be morally separated from the social-psychological functions they serve. One camp—call them the Functionalists—argues that these four cases share a deep structural form. Each transforms a military or political defeat into a story of moral endurance, provides a framework for collective continuity after trauma, and performs necessary psychological work for group identity. From this view, criticizing the narrative on grounds of historical distortion misses its purpose; the function is independent of factual accuracy, and societies need such myths to recover coherence.

The opposing camp—the Moralists—rejects that separation. They point out that the Confederate narrative explicitly embeds white supremacist valorization; that Masada can be mobilized to glorify collective suicide over pragmatic survival; that Kosovo mythology has served ethnic exclusion and territorial claim; and that the Dunkirk narrative often obscures strategic failure behind emotional triumph. For the Moralists, retaining the myth for social cohesion necessarily retains its normative framework; you cannot extract the psychological benefit without also transmitting the moral corruption embedded in the story's structure.

The debate has been stalled for decades. Both sides cite the same four episodes. Both acknowledge that defeated groups need narratives. Both agree that historical accuracy has value.
