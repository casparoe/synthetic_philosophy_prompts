Having recently served on a university committee that reviews institutional honors, I have become preoccupied with a particular discursive pattern—one that seems to sit at the intersection of performative language and institutional reasoning, but that I cannot name with confidence. The committee has the authority to revoke an honorary distinction; the vote does not report that the distinction has vanished, but actually removes it. Nevertheless, in the official minutes, in subsequent administrative justifications, and even in my own retrospective thinking, we consistently slide into treating the revocation as if it had revealed a pre-existing unworthiness, as though the decision were a descriptive judgment about a state that was already there rather than the constitutive act that created the new institutional reality. I have observed an analogous slide in disciplinary proceedings: once a board formally declares a finding of misconduct, colleagues tend to re-interpret prior behavior as having always manifested that misconduct, and they treat the declaration as causally downstream of the offense—evidence that the offense was real—rather than as the institutional act that established the offense's official status. What strikes me as philosophically peculiar is not mere retrospective rationalization or self-deception, but a specific explanatory inversion: the performative effect is taken as its own prior cause and justification, so that an act of social constitution is read backward as a discovery. I am familiar with Austin's distinction between performative and constative utterances, and with the idea that some declarations create institutional facts rather than describe them, but those frameworks do not seem to isolate this particular backward-looking denial of constitutive force. Does this phenomenon have an established name—perhaps in the literature on speech acts, institutional ontology, or the philosophy of social reality? If the nearest candidates are concepts like retrospective rationalization, performative contradiction, or the creation of status functions, I would like to know precisely how my case differs from each, especially since the slide I am describing need not involve insincerity, logical contradiction, or ignorance of the rules. And should there be no adequate term, what would be the appropriate coinage, together with its boundary conditions—distinguishing it, for instance, from ordinary causal reasoning about past events or from the strategic rewriting of institutional history?
