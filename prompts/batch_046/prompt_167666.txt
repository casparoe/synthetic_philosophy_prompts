I'm drafting a one-semester philosophy-of-mind research proposal—roughly 6,000 words—for a seminar that asks for an original framing rather than a literature review. My half-formed idea concerns split-brain patients, but I want to avoid the well-worn "one consciousness or two?" debate that has dominated discussions since the classic commissurotomy studies. Instead, I want to propose that the entire dispute presupposes "the unity of consciousness" is a single binary property, when the clinical and behavioral data actually suggest it should be decomposed into at least three conceptually independent dimensions: informational integration across hemispheres, narrative self-model coherence, and unified volitional control over action. In ordinary subjects these dimensions are so tightly correlated that treating them as one thing is harmless, but split-brain cases appear to dissociate them—some tasks show preserved emotional coherence despite informational isolation, others show independent motor intentions that are nevertheless woven into a single behavioral stream through cross-cueing, and still others reveal a left-hemisphere "interpreter" generating unified narratives for actions it did not initiate. The existing positions—whether defending a single divided subject or arguing for two co-conscious minds—leave this open precisely because both camps cite selectively from different behavioral domains without a principled taxonomy for weighing them against one another.

The proposal should state this decompositional question precisely: does analyzing split-brain phenomena through these three dimensions dissolve the standard puzzle cases, sharpen the genuine disagreements, or reveal that one dimension should be privileged as constitutive of consciousness's unity? The method would be conceptual and case-based rather than empirical: I would examine the major categories of split-brain behavior (independent hemispheric responses, cross-modal cueing, emotional consistency, confabulated explanation, and naturalistic integration) and map each onto the three dimensions to see where they align and where they come apart. I need the proposal to explain why this conceptual approach makes progress where purely empirical or neuroanatomical approaches stall—namely, that the disagreement is not about which hemisphere is active, but about which kind of unity matters philosophically. I also want a clear account of what the deliverable looks like: a framework that classifies split-brain phenomena without forcing them into a binary subject-count, plus an argument for whether any one of the three dimensions should be treated as fundamental. What would count as failure? If the dimensions turn out to be perfectly co-variant across all relevant cases, or if there is no non-arbitrary reason to separate them, then the decompositional project collapses and the standard framing was adequate after all. Finally, the proposal needs an honest scope assessment: this is not a neuroscience project, I will not be generating new clinical data, and the risk is that the conceptual distinctions become ad hoc unless tightly constrained by the actual behavioral reports—so I need to specify which classes of reported phenomena I will cover and which I will deliberately set aside to keep the project finishable in one semester. Please do not include a bibliography or citation list; I will add references myself, and I want the framing to stand or fall on its conceptual architecture alone.
