I am presenting to the monthly joint seminar of a university teaching hospital’s Palliative Care Division and Philosophy of Medicine group. The audience includes attending physicians who direct terminal sedation, a hospice ethicist committed to dignity-preservation, and philosophers of medicine: one is a hedonist about well-being, another has defended the view that meaningful suffering can be constitutive of a good death, and a third works on narrative identity in dementia care. I defend clinical tranquilism: in end-of-life decision-making, the only morally relevant considerations are the absence of suffering and the discharge of craving, where discharge means restructuring the patient’s environment and temporal orientation so that an appetitive drive ceases to register, rather than either satisfying its content or pharmacologically suppressing the mental state that carries it. Positive goods—relational presence, narrative coherence, consciousness-preservation—have no independent moral weight. I apply this to a patient with advanced dementia who repeatedly attempts to leave her unit stating she must get home, showing no behavioral distress signals but persistent wayfinding. I argue we should neither restrain her, which suppresses the craving by force and risks suffering, nor sedate her, since there is no suffering to suppress; instead, the unit should be redesigned as a safe looping corridor that allows continuous movement, thereby discharging the craving structurally without addressing its content. The clinicians will worry about family distress, institutional liability, and the appearance of neglect; the philosophers will worry that I have erased well-being, dignity, and the value of consciousness itself.

Prepare me for the objections in the order they will actually come, phrased as these interlocutors would phrase them: which have real force, which I should concede in part, which I should hold firmly, and what reply must land for the proposal to survive the session. Identify the weakest point in my own position—likely the boundary between discharge and disguised suppression, or my dismissal of positive goods—that I should prepare to defend rather than hope they miss. Include the objection I am hoping nobody asks, probably about whether my framework permits withholding analgesia from a suffering patient when that suffering is tied to a meaningful life-project, and sketch the thirty-second reply I need ready. Before listing objections, briefly restate the two moves in my argument they will target: first, that positive states have no independent moral weight here; second, that discharge and suppression are clinically and morally distinct operations on craving. Do not ask for citations, bibliographies, or reading lists; work only from the conceptual framework as I have laid it out.
