Argue that if moral status genuinely tracks sentience as a continuous gradation rather than an on-off property, then entities in the intermediate zone—such as insects with minimal nociceptive architecture or simple thermostatic feedback loops—do not merely possess a smaller quantity of the same kind of patienthood that mammals enjoy, but instead bear a structurally fragmentary status that is incommensurable with full-blown moral patienthood. From this premise, derive the stronger conclusion that standard aggregationist frameworks, including total and average forms of utilitarianism, are conceptually misapplied whenever they treat these intermediate cases as scalar contributions to a sum, since doing so implicitly reintroduces the binary distinction between patient and non-patient that the gradation thesis was meant to eliminate. Construct the argument from first principles: begin with the physiological continuity from reflex arcs through nociception to integrated experience, show why a purely scalar metric of status fails to capture the normative difference between integrated suffering and fragmented feedback, and explain why heterogenous populations containing both fully sentient beings and these intermediate entities cannot be evaluated by a single aggregative calculus without either collapsing the gradation back into a threshold or treating incommensurable values as interchangeable. You need not invoke named authors or specific empirical studies; the task is to make the conceptual case that gradation in sentience forces a corresponding pluralism in the logic of moral status, not merely a redistribution of weights along one axis.
