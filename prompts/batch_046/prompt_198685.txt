I work at a worker-owned manufacturing collective that recently replaced its flat year-end bonus with an algorithmic “contribution index.” The system aggregates peer ratings on reliability and fairness, then applies an exponential decay weight so that actions older than about eighteen months contribute almost nothing to the current score. Two years ago, my colleague Marcus caused a serious equipment failure through clear negligence; he was never formally disciplined, only informally shunned by the team. Over the past twelve months he has been exceptionally diligent and now holds the highest index score in our unit. I have been consistently reliable for years, but my score is lower because peers rate me as steady but unremarkable, and my older positive contributions have largely decayed away. Marcus is now the leading candidate for a leadership bonus and promotion that I was also being considered for. He argues that our own system’s logic makes his past failure mathematically irrelevant, and that holding it against him now contradicts the idea that people should be judged by their present character rather than by historical mistakes they have outgrown. I feel he never truly made amends for the damage and disruption he caused, yet I also recognize that the decay rule was adopted specifically to prevent old errors from permanently defining anyone. Should I support his promotion, and is there a deeper flaw in using exponential decay to determine what people deserve—treating moral weight like radioactive half-life—or am I simply resentful that consistency is being undervalued?
