He's writting about an 1890s factory strike where the workers left almost no written traces of their own — just management ledgers, a police report, and a census sheet that lists occupations without names. Leo wants to use what he calls "structural absence mapping": comparing gaps across these elite sources to triangulate the shape of worker resistance. He says that's still microhistory, just with bureaucratic lenses rather than personal ones. I told him that's not history from below, it's "negative prosopography" — treating workers as holes in the data rather than agents with intentions. Leo replied that demanding first-person testimony is elitist because it only works for literate people, and that his method at least tries to recover agency without inventing quotes. I said attributing intentions to people using categories they never used themselves — like reading "absent from roll call" as organised strike action — is ventriloquism with extra steps. He then argued that my standard would leave us with almost nothing for pre-literate societies, and that admitting silence is honest but also abandons them to elite narratives. I think he's missing that I'm not demanding quotes, I'm demanding we don't confuse bureaucratic absence with intentional resistance.
