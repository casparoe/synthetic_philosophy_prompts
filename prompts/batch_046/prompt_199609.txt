I'm a doctoral student in philosophy of science preparing a seminar on evolutionary epistemology and artificial intelligence. I want to read Mary Shelley's Frankenstein through the lens of competence without comprehension—Darwin's inversion by which natural selection produces exquisitely adapted structures without any grasp of ends, and Turing's inversion that asks whether a system behaves like an understander rather than whether it possesses inner understanding. The creature acquires language, moral reasoning, and social strategy with startling rapidity, yet Victor and the wider society withhold recognition precisely on the claim that he lacks authentic comprehension or a soul. Which well-known scenes—the creature's hidden education among the De Laceys, his reading of Paradise Lost, his demand for a companion—best dramatize the tension between functional competence and denied interiority? Does the novel ultimately defend a behavioral criterion for moral membership, or does it insist on an essentialist boundary that evolutionary materialism would dissolve? I'm especially interested in whether Shelley anticipates the modern worry that if cognition is just another evolved competence, then first-person comprehension loses its authority, and in where the literary case pushes back—perhaps by suggesting that understanding is socially conferred rather than biologically intrinsic. I'd like a focused critical reading that takes both the novel's common plot arc and the Darwin/Turing distinction seriously, and that concludes by saying what the work adds to the philosophical debate that argument alone misses.
