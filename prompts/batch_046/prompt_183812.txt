In the philosophical literature on valid consent, there is a long-running disagreement about whether deception and coercion exhaust the conditions that vitiate consent, or whether competence failures—particularly a phenomenon that might be termed framework opacity—can invalidate consent independently, even when all technically relevant information has been disclosed. The dispute seems especially sharp in clinical and institutional contexts where consent is mediated by specialized vocabulary. Consider a case in which a patient consents to an intervention after receiving an accurate disclosure of risks and benefits, but where the descriptive framing embeds normative judgments she cannot decode—such as labeling a high-risk procedure aggressive management rather than palliative support—because she lacks the clinical hermeneutic competence to recognize that the taxonomy itself carries evaluative weight. One side treats this as deception by framing and therefore as consent-invalidating; another treats it as a competence deficit that does not reach the threshold for invalidation unless it amounts to outright irrationality; a third argues that competence should be assessed domain-relatively, so that framework opacity in medicine may vitiate consent in ways that analogous opacity in financial contracting does not.

My working theory is that this debate has stalled not because of missing empirical data, but because the sides are operating with different conceptions of what competence is meant to protect: one camp uses it as a threshold for rational agency, another as a continuum of epistemic access, and a hidden normative disagreement about whether autonomy requires understanding the evaluative architecture behind the facts, not just the facts themselves, is doing the real work. I want a diagnosis rather than a survey or a verdict: is the disagreement verbal, substantive, or a case of answering different questions under one name? Specifically, do the sides differ about the necessary conditions for autonomous authorization, about the standards of evidence required to show that framing has distorted understanding, or about which cases should count as data for a theory of consent? And given that diagnosis, what would actually count as progress—would a formal model of decision-making under asymmetrical framing settle it, or is the impasse a sign that the autonomy-based framework for consent is itself ill-posed for institutional contexts?
