I have recently been appointed to an institutional review board overseeing clinical neuroscience trials, and a particular protocol has compelled me to reconsider whether our standard framework for valid consent can survive forms of deception that are embedded in system architecture rather than expressed in discrete false statements. The study employs a real-time predictive neurofeedback loop: participants are accurately informed of physical risks, withdrawal rights, and data-use policies, yet they are told that the feedback they receive is a post-hoc analysis of their recorded neural states, when in fact the system uses anticipatory modeling to generate stimuli that shape those very states before they fully occur. There is no explicit threat or reward structure that would traditionally count as coercion, and every participant scores well on conventional competence screenings that test comprehension of disclosed information. Nevertheless, the misrepresentation concerns not a peripheral detail but the causal logic of the intervention itself, which raises the question of whether consent can be valid when a subject understands the risks of an experience without understanding that the experience is being actively constructed by the mechanism to which she is consenting.

Please describe the most important implications of this scenario for what makes consent valid, particularly with respect to deception, coercion, and competence. I am especially interested in whether deception should be evaluated not only at the level of individual propositions but also at the level of systemic causal structure; whether coercion might operate architecturally through the design of feedback loops rather than interpersonally through threats or incentives; and whether competence requires a form of meta-cognitive grasp of how predictive systems intervene in one's mental processes, beyond the standard ability to recall and weigh disclosed risks. I do not need a literature review, reading list, or citations—only a rigorous conceptual analysis of how these three conditions should be reconceived if consent is to remain meaningful in contexts where the object of consent is itself a dynamically shaping mechanism.
