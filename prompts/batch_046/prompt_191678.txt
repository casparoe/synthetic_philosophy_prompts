In our philosophy-of-physics reading group, discussion of the Born-rule problem in Everettian quantum mechanics has stalled in a way that feels structural rather than merely hard. The decision-theoretic camp—following Deutsch and Wallace—argues that rational agents in a branching universe are constrained by preference axioms and symmetry conditions to assign credences proportional to squared amplitudes, so no further physical explanation is needed. Their opponents—drawing on self-locating uncertainty, envariance, or direct measure-theoretic weighting—reply that this addresses only how to bet, not why high-amplitude branches contain more observers or more frequent records; they demand a physical account of objective branch weights. Everyone accepts the same unitary dynamics and the same statistical data; no one disputes the formalism. My working hypothesis is that the deadlock is conceptual: one side treats probability as an indexical rationality constraint, the other as a statistical law over an ensemble of actual worlds, and they pursue different questions under the single heading "the Born rule." Is that right, or is there a disguised formal disagreement—say, about whether the principle of indifference applies across decohered branches with unequal amplitudes, or whether the decision-theoretic axioms implicitly presuppose the very measure they claim to derive? Diagnose the stall: is it verbal ambiguity about "probability," a substantive conflict over explanatory standards, bedrock intuitions about whether physics must supply frequencies, or genuinely different problems pursued under one label? And tell me honestly: given your diagnosis, could any conceptual distinction, formal result, or clarification of indexical reasoning actually break the impasse, or is the disagreement a sign that the underlying question is ill-posed?
