Design a decision tree that classifies positions on nomic self-reference and self-amendment—whether a legal system can contain rules that validly alter, repeal, or authorize the rules that establish them. The tree should be built from logical commitments rather than from a list of named theories, so that a respondent can traverse it by answering concrete case-questions without knowing the position labels in advance.

Root commitment: Must legal authority ultimately rest on a source that is not itself subject to legal amendment?

From there, branch through the following diagnostic nodes, each phrased as a claim or case:
- Can a constitutional amendment procedure be used to change the procedure itself? Does that generate a vicious circularity, produce only a benign iterative loop, or require an implicit exemption clause that protects the procedure from self-application?
- Can a general repeal principle—say, that any later contradictory statute overrides an earlier one—apply to itself? If so, does it yield a self-defeating paradox, dissolve through temporal indexing, or remain consistent only if it implicitly excludes itself?
- To avoid tension, is a strict hierarchical separation required between procedural rules of change and substantive legal norms, or is meta-level iteration (a rule governing the rule of change) sufficient?
- Is any residual appearance of paradox best read as showing that legal validity is graded, context-sensitive, or non-binary; as revealing a genuine incoherence in fully self-grounding authority; or as indicating that authority is inherently reflexive and that self-application is a feature rather than a bug?

Each leaf must carry a position label—named if one exists (e.g., hierarchical basic-norm view, strict procedural-substantive separation, meta-iterative dissolution, self-defeating paradox thesis, reflexive-authority view, graded-validity contextualism) or marked “unnamed”—plus one sentence describing its most awkward commitment: the consequence it must accept that its rivals avoid.

Map the full logical space of answer-combinations. Where a combination is unoccupied, note it and briefly diagnose the gap as likely due to incoherence, unattractiveness, or being unexplored. Explicitly flag any pair of leaves that the tree’s questions cannot genuinely separate.

The instrument should serve a philosophy-of-law or conceptual-analysis audience, aim for six to eight leaves, and must not request bibliographies, reading lists, citations, or references to specific texts.
