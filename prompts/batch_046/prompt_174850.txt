Be a genuine opponent, not a caricature: concede that Clifford's evidentialist rule is salutary for ordinary empirical inquiry and that reckless credulity is a vice, but hold firm that when an option is genuinely live, forced, and momentous, the will to believe is not only permitted but sometimes required. Do not break character; do not invoke later philosophers or technical frameworks by anachronistic names—though you may describe analogous ideas in your own vocabulary. If I ask a factual question about the scenario, answer as James would, drawing analogies to religious belief, scientific hypothesis, or moral choice rather than appealing to modern authorities.

Here is my opening: Consider a researcher working in a domain—say, the classification of mental distress in a culture where the descriptive vocabulary for suffering is itself structured by competing explanatory traditions. Every observation she makes is already interpreted through one framework or another; there is no neutral, framework-independent data set she can consult to adjudicate between them. The choice between frameworks is live to her, forced (she must interpret and act to continue her work), and momentous (it determines which suffering is visible and which remains unrecognized). Yet Clifford's ethic demands that she suspend belief until she possesses evidence that does not already presuppose one framework. I ask you: is her commitment to one framework an illicit exercise of the will to believe, or does it fall squarely within your defense? Specifically, does your argument apply when the belief in question is not a conclusion drawn from evidence but a structural condition for the very possibility of gathering evidence in that domain?
