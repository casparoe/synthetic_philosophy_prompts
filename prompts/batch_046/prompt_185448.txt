I am preparing a single session for a graduate reading group in public-health policy, composed of students who have read a brief excerpt from Plato's Laws on symposia but have no background in ancient philosophy. They arrive with two default assumptions: that Plato is either a prudish opponent of all intoxication or an uncritical celebrant of wine, and that any modern comparison is anachronistic. I want them to understand Plato's regulatory framework for drinking parties not as moralistic prohibition or hedonistic license, but as a civic technology: alcohol is permitted under strict conditions of dosage, age, and social supervision precisely because disinhibition functions as a diagnostic mirror of character, revealing dispositions that sober ritual keeps hidden, and the legislator's task is to design the conditions under which this revelation is educative rather than destructive.

Plan the session's explanatory arc with explicit dependency ordering: first, the conceptual distinction that must be secured before the regulatory framework can even be introduced—specifically, the difference between using a substance to escape one's character and using it, under controlled conditions, to expose it; second, the single carrying example or scenario that should anchor the discussion—specify what features it must include (for instance, a supervised setting with graduated dosage and observable behavioral consequences) and what it must exclude (for instance, unsupervised recreational consumption or clinical addiction treatment); third, the misconceptions the students will predictably generate in sequence—likely that this view contradicts Plato's Symposium, that it amounts to a proto-prohibitionist stance disguised as moderation, or that diagnostic drinking is merely state-sanctioned vice—and the precise point in the arc where each misconception should be intercepted and dissolved; fourth, the check for understanding that would demonstrate they have grasped the conceptual structure rather than merely being made uncomfortable by the analogy to modern harm-reduction policy; and fifth, what parts of Plato's broader legal theology, the dialogue's dramatic framing, or the specific institutional context should be explicitly left out at this level and noted as omitted.
