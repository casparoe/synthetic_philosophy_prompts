As a journalist writing on the science of decision-making, I am assembling material on how bounded rationality is taught through embodied experience rather than lecture. Design a 90-minute workshop simulation for 14 graduate students in behavioral science—title it "The Threshold Market"—that makes participants feel why satisficing is rational rather than lazy, specifically by running them into the tension between procedural and substantive rationality. The setup: students work in asymmetric pairs as buyers with hidden multi-attribute preference cards and a fixed budget of attention tokens that can be spent either searching for exact values or committing to an aspiration threshold. Inject a novel mechanism: aspiration levels are socially contagious within each pair—if one partner lowers their threshold after a failed round, the other’s hidden utility for that option shifts downward, but only if the pair adopted a satisficing rule; pairs that pursue full optimization suffer a hidden cognitive tax deducted from final scores, with the tax rate revealed only at debrief. The market-maker controls information release round by round. Specify the timeline (10 minutes rules and roles, three 15-minute trading rounds, 15 minutes of silent strategy revision, one final round, 30-minute debrief), the small real stakes (each point equals a raffle entry), roles with asymmetric information, and rules that force strategy selection before the tax is known. In the debrief, map specific moments—such as the instant a pair stops searching and suddenly finds acceptable goods—onto Simon’s concepts of bounded computation and aspiration-level adjustment; be explicit that the simulation cannot reproduce the long-run ecological formation of aspirations in natural environments, which the facilitator must supply. Note honestly what will predictably go wrong: optimizers will often accuse the game of being rigged, and some pairs will collude to pool search costs; advise how to handle this without breaking the procedural frame, and identify the one feature of substantive rationality—counterfactual comparison with all unchosen options—that the activity structurally cannot stage and must be addressed conceptually afterwards.
