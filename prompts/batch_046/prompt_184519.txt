I have been thinking on my own about whether two things that are exactly the same in every respect except where they sit could still be genuinely different things. I came across the idea of two black spheres in an otherwise empty universe, and the broader claim that if two items share every property they ought to be one item. Without any formal training, I want to feel why this is puzzling rather than just accepting an answer.

Please design a 75-minute workshop activity for about ten adults in a community reading group with no philosophy background. We should play as curators of a strange museum that collects not physical objects but pure qualities—exact roundness, perfect blackness, and so on—without any built-in space, location, or time stamps printed on the registry cards. Each participant receives one card describing a quality in full detail. Midway through, every participant receives a second card that is identical in every printed property, except that we are told it arrived at a different moment of entry, though the cards themselves carry no date. The rules should force practical choices: do we file the second card under the same entry, open a new file, charge double storage, or invent our own coordinate system just to keep them apart? There should be small stakes—perhaps curation points that determine who designs the final display—so that our filing behavior reveals whether we are treating them as one quality or two. Do not lecture us beforehand; let us argue and decide first.

Then provide a debrief that maps our filing decisions, any coordinate systems we invented, and the moments of disagreement onto the metaphysical problem, naming the principle and the counterexample only after we have lived through them. Include the exact rules, card materials, token economy, and payoff scheme. Tell me what will predictably fail the first time you run this and how to handle it. Be explicit about which feature of the philosophical case this game cannot replicate and that you will need to supply in discussion. Do not include citations, a bibliography, or a reading list, and do not ask us to prepare anything in advance.
