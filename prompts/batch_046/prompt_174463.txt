I'm trying to figure out what actually makes something an 'a' for me, going case by case, and I want to see if there's a real principle or if I'm just being wierd and inconsistent.

Here are my gut calls:
- Normal Arial lowercase a: yes.
- Handwritten one with a square bowl and barely-there tail: still yes.
- That same shape sitting alone rotated 90°: no, that's just a rotated symbol, not an a.
- But if the whole alphabet grid is rotated 90° together, then in that context I read it as an a again.
- An a where the bowl and stem are totally disconnected and floating apart: no, broken.
- Except in one font where every letter is split by a vertical gap as part of its design; there the split a still counts.
- Same parts arranged horizontally like a b missing its bottom loop: I say no.
- A face alphabet where facial features have the same relational layout (two eyes as bowl, nose as stem, mouth as tail): in that system I do call it an a.

So I seem to care about relational structure, but orientation and connectivity rules shift depending on whether it's a global system feature. What's the cleanest principle that captures all this, and which verdict should I ditch? Also predict for a case I haven't said: an a formed entirely in negative space where the background shapes the bowl and stem.
