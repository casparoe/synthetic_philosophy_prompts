Write a one-page research proposal for an interdisciplinary philosophy-archaeology workshop, framed around a precise question in the domain of reconstructing beliefs and institutions from material culture. The specific problem: when archaeologists encounter a repeated, highly standardized configuration of grave goods in a well-defined microdomain—say, Late Neolithic burial assemblages in the Levant in which miniature limestone vessels are consistently placed in the same spatial relation to the deceased, with no accompanying iconography, text, or obvious practical function—what justifies interpreting this pattern as evidence of a shared institutional belief rather than merely a stable practical convention or a technological habit? Frame the conceptual challenge by distinguishing, in philosophical terms, the inference from material regularity to "belief" (a mental-state reconstruction) from the inference to "institution" (a collective-status reconstruction). Explain why existing approaches in archaeological theory—whether mentalist accounts that treat objects as direct expressions of individual cognition, or practice-theory accounts that reduce patterns to social reproduction—tend to blur or collapse this distinction, leaving the epistemic status of the vessel-placement pattern ambiguous. Propose an approach that deploys the concept of institutional fact—where collective recognition assigns a status function that need not be transparent to every participant—to determine whether such a burial configuration could constitute an institution (with rules of correct use and collective acceptance) without requiring that every individual involved held a specific propositional belief about its meaning. Specify the method: a conceptual analysis of the conditions under which material repetition warrants institutional rather than mentalistic interpretation, applied to the Levantine assemblage as a test case, with explicit criteria for failure (e.g., if the belief/institution distinction turns out to be a false dichotomy that cannot do independent explanatory work in this microdomain) and for success (a framework that lets archaeologists classify ambiguous assemblages without over-committing to either mental-state attribution or pure functionalism). State the scope—approximately 600 words, suitable for a workshop pitch rather than a grant application—and indicate that the deliverable should be usable for both philosophers interested in collective intentionality and archaeologists concerned with inference from silent material records. Do not include a bibliography, citation list, or requests for specific readings; the proposal should stand on its conceptual framing alone, and any references will be added separately.
