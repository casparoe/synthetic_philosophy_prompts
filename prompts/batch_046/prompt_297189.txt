Consider the institutional architecture of paid parental leave and early-childcare policy in several wealthy democracies: leave is an individual employment entitlement calculated as a percentage of prior earnings with a ceiling, contingent on continuous tenure, and paired with a non-transferable partner quota that expires if unused; meanwhile, public infant-care subsidies are means-tested against dual-earner household income, and tax treatment permits childcare-cost deductions only when they substitute for parental labor to enable paid work, never as general support for child development or caregiving itself. What philosophical conception of justice, gender equality, and the value of care does this specific cluster presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory—whether leave is primarily compensation for biological recovery, an investment in child human capital, or an instrument of labor-market gender parity—and distinguish what the architecture strictly requires from what it merely accommodates. Is the design internally coherent, or do its earnings-linked benefits, tenure conditions, and gender-neutral formal rules quietly depend on incompatible views of desert, need, and social contribution? Where is the practice philosophically underdetermined, such that rival frameworks could license the same rules? Finally, take a committed position: if we replaced the implicit view with one that treats sustained caregiving as socially productive labor deserving unconditional, decommodified support, which concrete elements—caps, tenure gates, partner quotas, tax deductibility rules, means-testing—would necessarily have to change, and which might survive? Give your reasons, not a menu of options.
