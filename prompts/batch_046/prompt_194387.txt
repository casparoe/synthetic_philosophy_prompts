I'm delivering the opening remarks tomorrow at the retirement symposium for Dr. Elena Vasquez, who spent thirty-five years working on sequential rationality and models of self-prediction. The audience is split evenly: her former students and colleagues in decision theory and formal epistemology, who will recognize every structural move, and her family—children, grandchildren, and old friends—who have heard her mention “the absent-minded driver” at holiday dinners for decades without ever seeing the payoff matrix. I need a fifteen-minute address built around one specific, nonstandard variant of the absent-minded driver problem that I will lay out for you now, and I want that variant to do real structural work: it must shape what I can and cannot say about her career, rather than serving as a decorative analogy.

Here is the variant. A driver must take the second exit to reach his destination (payoff 4). If he reaches the first exit and deliberates whether to take it, he will be absent-minded and miss the second exit entirely (payoff 0). The twist is that he can, at the first exit, leave a physical marker—say, a folded note on the dashboard—but the presence of that marker at the second exit tells him he already passed the first exit without taking it, which updates his belief about his own prior reasoning and changes the payoff he assigns to continuing versus stopping. Specifically, seeing the marker makes him think he was in a “continuation” frame, which lowers his confidence that the second exit is correct, reducing its payoff from 4 to 2, while not seeing the marker gives him full confidence. The standard resolution relies on backward induction, but this marker-variant introduces a reflexive loop: the driver’s belief about his own prior mental state becomes part of the payoff structure itself.

I want the speech to use this loop to force me into a precise middle space. I should not be able to claim Dr. Vasquez was purely strategic—because then the marker of her own prior planning would have undermined her confidence at the crucial moment—nor purely spontaneous—because then she would never have reached the second exit with payoff 4 in the first place. The logic should make it impossible for me to praise her either as a cold calculator or as someone who simply forgot to plan, and instead require me to describe a career of long-range commitments—grant trajectories, mentoring lines, institutional building—that depend on being, in a carefully structured sense, “absent-minded” about the immediate temptations of the first exit. The speech needs to include one concrete, non-technical image the family can hold onto—perhaps something about leaving notes for one’s future self that change what that future self can believe—and one precise observation the decision-theory side will recognize as a genuine point about reflexive belief-updating in sequential choice. It must land as affection and intellectual respect, not as a lecture.
