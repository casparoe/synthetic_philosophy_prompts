Over the past year, as I have moved from graduate study in philosophy into applied ethics work, begun weighing whether to have children, and assumed stewardship of a modest philanthropic fund, I have found my practical reasoning repeatedly stalling on a problem I can only call consequentialist opacity. Rather than asking for a survey of the literature, I want to test whether my intuitions across concrete cases cohere around a single normative principle, and if they do not, which ones I should abandon.

Here are my verdicts.

First, career choice. I was recently offered a position in synthetic biology governance, where my work might influence publication norms for dual-use research with potential effects on pandemic risk over decades. The causal chain is long, the sign of the effect is genuinely unclear to me, and I have no credible way to estimate magnitude. My intuition is that I should decline the role and take a local public-health position with measurable, near-term benefits—not because I have calculated expected value, but because consequentialist reasoning seems to lose its authoritative grip when I am simultaneously clueless about both sign and magnitude.

Second, reproduction. I am deciding whether to have a child. The child’s lifetime emissions, potential for future good or harm, and influence on others are enormous and span centuries. Here, however, my radical ignorance about long-term effects does not feel like a reason to refrain. I think it is permissible to proceed based on personal and relational reasons, and that consequentialism should not require me to solve an intractable forecasting problem before making this decision.

Third, institutional experimentation. A municipality I advise is considering a universal basic-income pilot. The pilot could generate valuable evidence, or it could trigger labor-market distortions that compound unpredictably across generations. Despite this deep cluelessness about long-term systemic effects, I think we should proceed if near-term welfare indicators are promising, treating the unknown long tail as a reason for careful design rather than for inaction.

Fourth, charitable allocation. I must divide ten thousand dollars between bed-net distribution, where mortality effects are well-estimated, and a longtermist fund that aims to shape artificial-intelligence development over the next century. Here I feel the bed-net donation is more clearly demanded, yet I cannot stabilize my judgment: I keep reversing myself, unsure whether the other option is genuinely worse or merely more opaque.

Fifth, contractual commitment. A close friend asks whether to sign a five-year contract with a defense-technology firm working on autonomous targeting systems. The near-term work is legal and technically interesting; the long-term contribution to either strategic deterrence or catastrophic escalation is radically uncertain. My intuition is that she should not treat this long-term opacity as decisive; she should evaluate the immediate moral character of the role and her own integrity, rather than attempting to maximize expected global utility across an unknowable future.

These five verdicts feel like they issue from the same person, but I cannot extract the rule. In cases one and five, I seem to say that cluelessness about long-term effects undermines or at least displaces consequentialist obligation; in cases two and three, I say it does not. In case four, I am paralyzed. Is there a coherent principle—perhaps distinguishing actions that are personally irreversible from those that are institutionally revisable, or separating domains where the agent controls the causal pathway from those where she does not, or drawing a boundary between life-structuring choices and allocative ones—that captures cases one through three and forces me to drop either case four or case five? Or am I simply inconsistent?

If a principle does emerge, please state it precisely, indicate which clause addresses which case, deliver its verdict on a case I have not listed—specifically, deciding whether to take a job lobbying for nuclear-energy expansion, where near-term climate benefits are clear but long-term proliferation and waste risks are both enormous and poorly understood—and be explicit about which of my five intuitions it cannot accommodate without distortion.
