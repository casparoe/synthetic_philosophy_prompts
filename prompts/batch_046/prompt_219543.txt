In popular ethics writing and classroom discussion, Kant's universalizability tests are frequently presented as nothing more than the Golden Rule—treat others as you wish to be treated—recast in the technical language of maxims, rational agents, and logical contradictions. The analogy is typically used to argue that the categorical imperative is essentially common-sense reciprocity burdened with redundant philosophical apparatus, so that applying it requires no special training and that its deliverances will largely reproduce pre-theoretical moral intuitions. How far does this correspondence actually hold? Provide an explicit mapping: what in the Golden Rule corresponds to the maxim, the rational will, the contradiction in conception, the contradiction in will, the distinction between perfect and imperfect duties, and the idea of a kingdom of ends? Then locate the precise fractures: does the Golden Rule's dependence on subjective preference map onto Kant's requirement that moral laws be valid independently of inclination? Can the difference between undermining the possibility of an action (contradiction in conception) and undermining a necessary end (contradiction in will) be captured by a reciprocity-of-desire framework? And does the analogy quietly import a sentimentalist or consequentialist structure that Kant's account explicitly excludes? Finally, judge whether the conclusion—that Kantian ethics is merely formalized common sense—survives once these disanalogies are visible, and suggest whether a different analogy would serve the comparison better. In responding, separate what is well established in Kant scholarship from what is genuinely contested, and avoid presenting any disputed interpretive claim—such as whether the Golden Rule can be reconstructed as an anticipatory version of the Formula of Universal Law—as if it were the consensus view.
