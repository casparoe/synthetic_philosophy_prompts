Here is an argument I have constructed for a seminar on the philosophy of social science, and I find it suspicious because the conclusion seems to undermine the discipline entirely. I would like to know where, if anywhere, the reasoning fails.

(1) Methodological individualism holds that every adequate explanation of a social phenomenon must be reducible, without explanatory loss, to statements about the beliefs, intentions, and actions of individual human beings.

(2) Consider the claim: "The tenure committee rejected Dr. Aris's application because the discipline was undergoing a methodological shift." To satisfy (1), this must be translated into statements about particular individuals—say, Professor Blake believed X, Dr. Chen preferred Y, and so on.

(3) However, identifying which individuals count as "the committee" and which preferences count as "the discipline's shift" requires prior reference to social roles (committee-member, disciplinary norm) and collective entities (the discipline) that are themselves in need of reduction.

(4) Any attempt to complete the reduction without such reference forces a purely physical description—"the bodies in Room 302 cast ballots"—which strips away the very explanatory content that made the original claim informative. This illustrates what we might call the non-unique realisation problem for social roles: the same physical bodies could realise a different social entity, or none at all, so physical description alone cannot fix the explanatory content.

(5) Therefore, methodological individualism demands either an infinite regress of translations (each social term requiring further social terms to identify its individual realisers) or an acceptance of explanatorily empty physical descriptions.

(6) An infinite regress is not an explanation, and an empty description explains nothing; hence, on the individualist programme, no social explanation can ever be completed.

(7) But sociology, economics, and history routinely provide completed explanations that invoke social structures; therefore, either those disciplines are systematically illusory, or methodological individualism is false.

(8) The claim that those disciplines are systematically illusory is absurd.

(9) Therefore, methodological individualism is false.

Where does this argument go wrong, if it does? If I must choose, I would prefer to retain (1) and reject (8), but I want to understand the price of that choice. In particular, please identify the precise step at which the reasoning slips—whether it is an equivocation between ontological and explanatory reduction, a hidden modal slide, or a failure to distinguish weak from strong individualism—and explain what the standard positions (the weak individualist who allows supervenient structures, the holist who accepts irreducible collective intentionality, and the eliminativist about social entities) each have to pay in order to reject that step. Please do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; I want the analysis to stand on its own.
