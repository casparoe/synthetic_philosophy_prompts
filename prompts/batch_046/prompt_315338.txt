I am preparing a long-form piece on the philosophy hidden inside immigration bureaucracy, and I want to examine one institutional mechanism that is concrete enough to dissect but rarely treated as a philosophical object. In a number of European states, the granting or renewal of residence permits has been tied to “integration contracts”: applicants must complete state-mandated courses and pass assessments in constitutional loyalty, gender-equality norms, and language proficiency, with failure not simply delaying naturalization but triggering active removal proceedings. The design treats cultural assimilation not as a private aspiration but as an enforceable precondition of legal belonging.

What implicit theory of political membership and moral equality does this practice presuppose? Reconstruct the philosophy embedded in the mechanism: does it assume that democratic legitimacy requires cultural homogeneity, that individual autonomy can be contractually traded for residency rights, or that the liberal state may legitimately rank ways of life by their compatibility with public norms? Carefully separate what the practice strictly requires philosophically from what it merely fits well with, and indicate whether a multicultural or pluralist theory of citizenship could license the same bureaucratic form without endorsing assimilationism.

Then bring in Michael Dummett’s well-known arguments against assimilationist immigration controls. Dummett maintained that requiring immigrants to suppress or surrender their cultural identity as a condition of full membership violates the liberal state’s duty of neutrality and treats newcomers as less than full moral equals. Does the integration-contract framework necessarily conflict with Dummett’s position, or can it be reinterpreted—say, as protecting immigrants’ autonomy through education rather than constraining their culture? If one took Dummett’s premises seriously, what concrete institutional changes would follow: the abolition of culturally weighted testing, the separation of welfare access from assimilation metrics, or a reconception of membership itself that requires no preconditions of cultural conformity? Finally, where is the practice philosophically underdetermined: are there rival theories of justice, identity, or state legitimacy that could justify the same policy instruments while reaching opposite conclusions about immigration?
