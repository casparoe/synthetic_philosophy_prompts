I'm applying for a small four-month research grant and need a two-page proposal. My idea is about Sen's liberal paradox — you know, the one where even minimal liberal rights (like two people deciding over books or bed colors) clash with Pareto efficiency. Most people either bite the bullet and say rights trump efficiency, or they say Sen's "personal sphere" idea is too vague to apply in real life. But I think everyones missed something: what if the paradox only bites because we treat liberal authority as authority over outcomes inside fixed domains? I want to test whether reinterpreting liberal rights as "procedural veto powers over framing" — not over what you end up with, but over how the choice gets described and bounded — lets us escape the contradiction or just pushes it back one level.

The existing literature (Sen's original proof, Nozick-style responses, some welfare-economics fixes) doesn't settle this because they all keep the object of rights constant: either you control the outcome or you don't. Nobody has seriously formalized a version where the right is specifically about the framing of the decision problem, and then checked whether the impossability result still holds when preferences can shift with framing.

My method is mostly conceptual and formal, not empirical. Month one: reconstruct Sen's original proof with the standard outcome-based interpretation so I can see exactly where the contradiction enters. Month two: build a minimal formal model where agents have veto rights only over the "agenda" or framing of choices that affect them, not over final allocations, and derive the corresponding social choice conditions. Month three: run the proof through — does the same cyclic preference structure still generate the conflict? If it does, map where exactly the framing-veto fails to block it. Month four: write up, including a concrete illustrative case (maybe collective climate-policy choices that impinge on personal lifestyle domains) and an honest failure condition: if framing-veto collapses into outcome-control whenever preferences are stable, then the reinterpretation is just cosmetic and the paradox survives untouched.

What I'd deliver is a short monograph-style paper with the formal result and the conceptual argument, plus a one-page brief for non-philosophers explaining why framing rights matter for democratic design. If the project fails, it fails because the formal distinction turns out to be unstable — which is still a result, just not the one I want.

The scope is realistic for one person over four months with no lab or travel costs, but I need to flag one risk: if the formalization requires heavier social-choice machinery than I anticipate, I might only get through months one and two. I'd rather be honest about that than pretend I can solve it all in a summer, and I can adjust the deliverable to a rigorous partial result if needed.
