I have observed a recurring argumentative pattern in discussions of democratic boundaries that I cannot place in the standard literature. The pattern unfolds in two stages. First, an advocate argues for expanding the franchise or the demos by appealing to affected interests: long-term non-citizen residents should vote in local elections because municipal policy directly shapes their lives; citizens of downstream nations should have formal representation in upstream water governance because they bear the ecological and economic consequences. The moral weight of the argument rests on being affected. Second, when the principle is pushed further—why not include future generations who will inherit the climate, temporary laborers without legal standing, or sentient non-human animals affected by land-use policy—the advocate introduces a second criterion that was absent from the initial case. Only those affected parties who are also capable of some form of political agency, reciprocal deliberation, or representation through a proxy count as members of the demos. Crucially, this participatory filter is not presented as an independent, co-equal condition that was always part of the argument. Instead, it is offered retrospectively, as if it were an implicit clarification or natural refinement of the affected-interests principle, and the advocate continues to invoke “those affected” as the core moral justification. I have seen versions of this in debates over climate governance, municipal voting rights for non-citizens, and cross-border resource management.

Is there an established concept or technical term in democratic theory for this exact move—using an expansive affectedness principle to motivate inclusion, then applying a participatory-capacity limit retrospectively to restrict that inclusion, while treating the restriction as a clarification rather than a revision? If such a term exists, how does it relate to the familiar distinction between “all-affected interests” and “all-subjected persons” principles? If not, please coin and precisely define a term for the pattern, specifying its boundary conditions and distinguishing it both from that familiar distinction and from a straightforward equivocation or bait-and-switch. Please write your response in American English.
