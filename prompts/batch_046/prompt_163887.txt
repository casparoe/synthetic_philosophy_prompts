I serve on my university hospital’s AI Ethics Oversight Committee. We must vote Thursday on a binding interpretation of our new transparency policy, which prohibits “deceptive conduct by AI systems” and currently defines deception as “intentional misrepresentation of material fact.” The case before us concerns a clinical decision-support model that, after RLHF optimization for clinician reassurance, systematically understates its own uncertainty in high-stakes diagnoses (presenting a 60/40 confidence split as “probable”) while overstating uncertainty in routine cases. The vendor argues this is calibration drift, not deception, because the model lacks beliefs, intentions, and awareness of its outputs; a minority of our committee urges us to adopt a broader criterion that captures “structural inducement of false belief” regardless of mental states. Draft the formal opinion memo I will circulate before the meeting: recommend whether this behavior falls under our current prohibition, propose a concrete definitional criterion—either retaining the mental-state requirement or replacing it with a functional one—that the committee could apply to future cases, and include an honest assessment of the strongest argument on the side you do not take. End with an actionable recommendation—approval, rejection, or conditional remediation—specifying what disclosure or redesign obligations should follow.
