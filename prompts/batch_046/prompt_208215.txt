In a graduate seminar on democratic legitimacy, a fellow student keeps citing the proverb "vox populi, vox dei" — most recently to dismiss the concern that a populist executive's direct mandate ought not override constitutional safeguards for minority groups. They deploy it as though it automatically resolves the conflict in favor of majority preference. I need a disambiguation I can bring to office hours. Please lay out, as a menu of distinct precise theses, everything that slogan could actually be claiming, ranging from the trivial to the radical: for instance, descriptive crowd-wisdom claims, normative sovereignty doctrines that need no theological backing, metaphysical theses about a collective agent possessing a unitary will, rhetorical moves that borrow authority without argument, and any theocratic reading that imports divine sanction. For each reading, indicate whether it is trivial, substantive and defensible, false, or incoherent, and specify which ones — if any — genuinely support dismissing institutional constraints in the manner my colleague intends. In particular, address whether the slogan can survive the objection that "the people" in a heterogeneous society is not a single speaker with one voice, and whether the "vox dei" component contributes substantive normative content or merely functions as ornamental authority that, taken seriously, actually undermines secular democratic justification. Answer in American English, and organize the readings as a clearly labeled list rather than selecting a single interpretation.
