I am writing a short essay for my philosophy of value seminar on whether "better than" is transitive. Rather than relying on standard sorites cases, I want to develop what I call a "dimension-shifting spectrum." I imagine a sequence of options A0 through A100, where each successive option is marginally better than the previous one along a shared evaluative dimension—initially predictive accuracy, then gradually simplicity, then explanatory breadth. By A100, the criteria have shifted so thoroughly that comparing A100 directly to A0 seems to invoke incommensurable standards, even though every adjacent pair was comparable. I take this to suggest that transitivity can fail because the metric of comparison is path-dependent, not merely because of vagueness at the boundaries.

Before I finalize the draft, I would welcome focused comments on three points. First, is this genuinely distinct from a standard continuum fallacy, or have I just redescribed vagueness in dynamic terms? Second, does the dimension-shifting move avoid the standard aggregation reply—that we should weigh all relevant dimensions at every step? Third, could the argument collapse into the claim that "better than" is ambiguous across contexts, rather than genuinely intransitive within a single ordering?

I am not looking for citations, a bibliography, or a reading list—only substantive feedback on the coherence and defensibility of the central claim. The essay is capped at 1,500 words, so I want to be sure the argument is sound before I commit to it.
