I was arguing late into the evening with a friend about whether omissions can ever be genuine causes rather than mere background conditions, and we realized our disagreements tracked specific case judgments more than any explicit theory. I want to know whether my intuitions form a coherent pattern or whether consistency forces me to abandon some of them.

Here are my verdicts, given as concretely as I can. First, a reservoir engineer fails to open a spillway gate during an unprecedented storm, and a downstream valley floods; I think her omission genuinely caused the inundation, not merely permitted it. Second, a botanist neglects to water a rare orchid in a climate-controlled greenhouse, and the plant dies within days; I judge the neglect a direct cause of death. Third, a legacy systems administrator omits a redundant checksum in an updated protocol for a hospital network, and months later a single corrupted packet triggers a cascade failure that delays emergency surgeries; I feel the omission belongs in the causal chain leading to the harm. Fourth, a bystander at a shallow public pool notices a child struggling but does nothing, and the child drowns; here I think the bystander failed to prevent death but did not cause it. Fifth, a coalition of industrialized nations declines to deploy carbon-capture infrastructure that was technically feasible and economically viable, and over the subsequent decades regional temperatures rise, eventually correlating with a specific catastrophic hurricane; I am inclined to say the omission contributed to the background conditions but did not cause that particular storm. Sixth, a physician withholds a requested placebo from an anxious patient who has no organic pathology, and the patient's anxiety subsequently worsens; I do not think the withholding caused the worsening—the underlying condition did—despite the temporal sequence and the counterfactual that administration might have eased symptoms.

What puzzles me is that cases one through three feel like robust causation by omission, case four feels like pure non-prevention, case five feels like diffuse contribution lacking specific causal power over an individual event, and case six feels like no causation whatsoever, even though in every instance a bad outcome occurred and someone failed to perform an action that would have averted or mitigated it. Is there a precise principle—perhaps involving normative role obligations, counterfactual proximity, or institutional proximity to the harm—that captures all six judgments? Please state the principle with enough specificity that each clause is motivated by one of these cases, predict what it says about an unlisted case—say, a museum curator who declines to install humidity controls and a Renaissance painting gradually deteriorates—and be explicit about which of my six verdicts the principle cannot accommodate without contradiction or ad hoc revision. If no single principle saves them all, tell me which one I should overturn and why.
