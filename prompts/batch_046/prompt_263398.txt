Having spent thirty years in experimental physics before turning to philosophy in retirement, I find myself repeatedly drawn back to Sartre’s analysis of radical freedom and bad faith, yet I am struck by how rarely discussions specify what an adequate theory of these phenomena is actually required to deliver. Before I assess the competing interpretations—existentialist, analytic, or naturalized phenomenological—I want the full desiderata specified. Write the list of requirements that any theory of radical human freedom and self-deceptive bad faith must satisfy to count as conceptually adequate, with the motivating case or tension behind each. The theory must explain the phenomenology of being “condemned to be free” without collapsing freedom into mere psychological determinism or cognitive bias; it must preserve Sartre’s distinction between facticity and transcendence rather than dissolving one into the other; and it must account for what I will call “asymmetric recognition”—the common pattern in which an agent exercises explicit, reflective choice in one domain of life (say, career) while simultaneously treating another domain (say, intimate relationships) as fixed by nature or social destiny, without reducing this pattern to ordinary hypocrisy or compartmentalized belief. It must also distinguish bad faith structurally from Freudian repression and from simple factual error, explaining specifically why bad faith involves a “lie to oneself” that is sustained rather than corrected by the very awareness that makes it possible. Include the empirical constraints the theory should respect—particularly regarding human cognitive limitation and the social embedding of self-interpretation—without requiring citation of specific neuroscientific or psychological studies. For each requirement, indicate whether it is non-negotiable for any Sartre-informed account or whether it reflects the preference of a particular camp (e.g., absolutist versus situated-freedom interpretations). Then identify any subset of these desiderata that appear jointly unsatisfiable—perhaps the conjunction of absolute ontological freedom with a fully socially constructed self-image—and explain why that tension is internal rather than merely apparent. Finally, briefly score Sartre’s own account, a compatibilist revision, and a naturalized phenomenological alternative against the list, but keep the weight on the criteria themselves: I want to know which single requirement does the most work in excluding candidates, and whether the list reveals that “radical freedom” and “bad faith” are ultimately explicable within one framework or require a divided theory.
