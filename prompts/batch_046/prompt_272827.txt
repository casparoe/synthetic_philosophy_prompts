Design a final-exam essay prompt for an upper-level seminar on the philosophy of history and earth-system science, together with a model answer of about 600 words and a grading rubric that distinguishes A, B, and C performance.

The essay should ask students to treat the Anthropocene not as an uncontested geological label but as a boundary dispute in which the choice of starting date and the authority to decide are conceptually entangled. Require them to compare three specific periodization proposals: the mid-twentieth-century Great Acceleration anchored in radionuclide and carbon-emission signals; the Orbis Spike proposal that places the boundary near 1610 on the basis of the Columbian Exchange and corresponding carbon-dip signals in Antarctic ice; and a more recent polymer-stratigraphic horizon thesis that identifies the first global appearance of synthetic microplastics in deep-sea sediments—dated around 1950 in some North Atlantic cores and closer to 1970 in others—as the definitive stratigraphic marker.

The prompt must also require students to address what I will call stratigraphic sovereignty: the conflict over whether ratification of epochal boundaries belongs exclusively to the International Commission on Stratigraphy, or whether climate scientists, earth-system historians, and scholars of intergenerational legal equity possess co-equal authority. Crucially, the essay should ask whether selecting one boundary over another is merely an evidentiary disagreement or whether it encodes a normative judgment about which form of harm—colonial ecological violence, industrial metabolic rupture, or petrochemical material persistence—ought to be treated as epoch-defining.

Provide the exam question itself, calibrated for a 90-minute sitting and a 1,500-word response, then the model answer. The model answer should not simply catalog the three proposals; it should argue that the residue—those aspects of the debate that cannot be resolved by stratigraphic evidence alone—reveal either a genuine conceptual disagreement about historical agency or a disciplinary turf battle disguised as geology. Conclude the answer by assessing whether the concept of stratigraphic sovereignty is a useful analytical tool or a category mistake that imports political theory into a domain governed by rock-layer correlation.

Finally, deliver the marking scheme. It should specify that an A answer integrates all three boundary proposals with the sovereignty problem, identifies the normative residue, and takes a defensible position on the category-mistake question; a B answer describes the proposals and mentions sovereignty but treats it as an administrative footnote rather than a conceptual crux; and a C answer collapses the Anthropocene debate into general climate-change discussion without distinguishing the boundary proposals or addressing authority. List the exact conceptual moves that must be present or absent for each band.
