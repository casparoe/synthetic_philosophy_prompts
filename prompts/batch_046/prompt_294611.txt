I'm delivering the opening remarks at an interdisciplinary workshop convened to reflect on the half-century legacy of the evolutionary tournament studies. The audience is split evenly between evolutionary biologists, who understand cooperation through kin selection and direct reciprocity, and formal game theorists, who treat it as a rationality problem in repeated games; neither camp is patient with being reduced to a footnote in the other's framework. I need a ten-minute address that takes the structural distinction between the one-shot and iterated prisoner's dilemma as its organizing principle—not as decoration, but as the explanation for why the two disciplines consistently talk past each other. The speech should turn on a specific, somewhat counterintuitive claim: that in the iterated setting, the evolutionary stability of cooperation depends less on reciprocity per se than on the informational architecture of the interaction, specifically on whether agents can condition their next move on observed history while lacking any capacity to commit to future strategies. Please construct an address that renders this claim intelligible to the biologists without reducing it to metaphor, respects the formal rigor the theorists expect, and avoids declaring either framework victorious. Do not include citations, bibliographies, or requests for further reading.
