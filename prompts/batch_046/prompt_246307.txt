I'm opening our little interdepartmental workshop tomorrow on — well, on what we keep calling "the species problem," though I think that name hides more than it reveals. The room will be half evolutionary biologists who've spent thirty years arguing about reproductive isolation versus phylogenetic trees, and half philosophy grad students who mostly know the debate from one chpater of a textbook and maybe a blog post. I need about five minutes of opening remarks, not a welcome mat, and I want the framing to actually constrain what we talk about for the next two days.

Here's the knot I want to put in front of them: people treat species failure like it's one puzzle, but I think it's at least three different breakdowns that get smushed together. First there's the continuum problem — drawing a sharp line through a smooth gradient, your classic sorites-type mess. Then there's the criterion problem: do we privilege interbreeding, evolutionary branching, or ecological niche, and why does every choice feel like it leaves out some obvious case? And then there's this third thing I've been calling the inheritance gap — we build taxonomies on inherited traits, but in plenty of organisms those traits are horizontally transferred or stay phenotypically plastic, so the taxonomy tracks genealogy perfectly but misses the functional kind that actually drives conservation decisions. I probably made that term up; if there's a real name for it you can drop it in, but please don't turn this into a literature review — the biologists in the room can cite papers in their sleep, and the grad students will zone out if I start naming chapters.

The speech needs to work for both crowds without either feeling talked down to. The experts shouldn't hear me explaining Darwin; the students should leave seeing why this isn't just semantics or wordplay. Keep it casual — we're stuck with bad conference coffee, I'm speaking from notes on my phone, and there's probably a typo in my outline. Make the framing do real structural work: by the end they should understand why these three failures aren't interchangeable, and why fixing one doesn't touch the others.
