I'm working through a tension in epistemology centered on the bootstrapping problem and the threat of easy knowledge, particularly where reliabilist and virtue-theoretic accounts try to block epistemic circularity without collapsing into skepticism. In discussion with a study partner we sketched an argument that initially seemed tight, but its conclusion now strikes me as intolerable: it forces a binary choice between global skepticism and an implausibly uniform, non-defeasible conception of justification across all domains of a faculty. I need a diagnosis that takes a definite stand rather than surveying options.

Here is the argument as we formalized it:

(1) For any belief B held by subject S, if B is genuinely justified then S must possess some reason R for B that is not itself justified solely by the very faculty F that produced B.
(2) Consider any basic cognitive faculty F—say, perceptual tracking, memorial retention, or inductive projection. The only considerations S can actually marshal in favor of F's reliability are further deliverances of F itself (for instance, noticing that perceptions usually cohere with later perceptions, or that retained memories match present experience).
(3) So for each F, either S lacks any non-circular reason for trusting F, or S bootstraps F's reliability from outputs of F.
(4) If bootstrapping is illegitimate—because it yields knowledge that is too easy, lacking the independent verification required for genuine justification—then S is never justified in relying on F.
(5) But S is plainly justified in relying on at least some applications of F.
(6) Therefore bootstrapping must be legitimate after all.
(7) Once S legitimately bootstraps the reliability of F, S acquires a global entitlement: the knowledge that F is generally reliable.
(8) If S knows that F is generally reliable, and if justification is transitive across the outputs of a known-reliable source absent specific defeaters, then every particular belief produced by F inherits justification automatically.
(9) Hence S is either justified in every belief F produces, regardless of domain or context, or in none of them.
(10) Yet justification is clearly local and defeasible: I can be justified in believing the weather is cold via perception while remaining unjustified in believing a quantum field equation via the same perceptual faculty, and I can lose justification for a particular belief when I acquire a specific defeater without losing my general trust in the faculty.
(11) So (9) is false.
(12) By reductio, (6) must be false: bootstrapping is illegitimate.
(13) But then by (4) S is unjustified in relying on any F, contradicting (5).

This loop seems to collapse unless one step is rejected. I am inclined to preserve (1) and (5) and to reject (9), but I am unsure whether the real damage is done earlier—perhaps in the slide from (7) to (8), where domain-general reliability may be illicitly converted into unconditional local justification, or perhaps in (1)'s implicit demand for a reason available to S in a manner that externalist or anti-individualist frameworks deny. Give me a committed answer: identify the failing step, explain precisely why it looked plausible, and spell out the cost of abandoning it—especially whether doing so forces me to accept some form of easy knowledge after all, or whether it requires giving up the idea that faculty-trust needs any independent reason. Argue for one diagnosis and defend it; do not lay out every escape route and leave me to choose.
