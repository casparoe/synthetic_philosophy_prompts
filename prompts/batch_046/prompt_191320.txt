I'm working through a problem set for a seminar on personal identity and split-brain phenomena, and I've constructed an argument that seems to dissolve the unity of ordinary consciousness into fragments, which strikes me as clearly wrong but I can't isolate the misstep. Here is the reasoning: (1) Call a mental state S reflectively owned by a subject only if that subject can acknowledge S as hers without relying on external observation or third-person inference. (2) In the classic split-brain case, the left hemisphere cannot reflectively own the motor intentions executed by the right hemisphere; it learns of them only by watching the left hand move and then confabulating an explanation. (3) So, by (1), those motor intentions do not belong to the left-hemisphere subject. (4) But the same relation holds within an intact brain: a subject's implicit biases, procedural skills, and autonomic emotional adjustments are not reflectively owned in that direct first-person way; they are accessed only through behavioral observation or indirect inference. (5) Therefore, if (1) is the correct metaphysical criterion for belonging to a subject, then an intact person is not one subject but a federation of many—one for each domain of mental life that fails the reflective-ownership test. (6) That conclusion is absurd; we do not treat ordinary people as loose collections of sub-personal agents. (7) Hence (1) is false, and reflective transparency cannot be necessary for mental-state ownership.

But I worry that giving up (1) costs me the best argument for saying split-brain patients contain two subjects rather than one. If I reject (1), do I also lose the split-subject interpretation? Or is there a more subtle flaw—perhaps an equivocation between epistemic access and metaphysical belonging, or a hidden shift from "does not belong to the left hemisphere" to "belongs to a separate right-hemisphere subject"? I want you to diagnose exactly which step fails, explain why it seemed plausible, and then price the alternatives: what does the unity theorist, the split-subject proponent, and the bundle-theory sympathizer each have to pay if they reject the step you identify?
