Write a short research proposal—hard limit of 650 words, formatted as a single-page pitch—for a three-month independent-study grant in philosophy. Frame the precise question as follows: in public commemoration, is the ethical wrong of retaining a statue of a historical wrongdoer structurally different from retaining a street named after the same figure, and does that difference depend on whether the object functions as an attributive symbol (its representational content conveys praise that can be reinterpreted) versus an indexical designation (it fixes reference to a location regardless of the name-bearer’s properties)?

Your proposal must do four things. First, state the question so that it has a determinate answer, and explain why existing debates—whether focused on historical justice, democratic representation, or the expressive meaning of monuments—leave this specific structural comparison unsettled rather than resolving it by treating both cases as identical instances of “public honor.” Second, lay out the approach: analyze the metaphysics of the commemorative object (how its meaning is fixed, whether reinterpretation can occur without physical alteration, and what modal changes to the object would change its ethical status) and argue why this philosophical angle advances the issue where empirical historical surveys or political polling stall; also explain why you reject the obvious alternative of collapsing the two cases. Third, describe what a result would look like—e.g., a principled distinction that explains why retaining a renamed-but-unchanged street might differ ethically from retaining an unchanged statue with a new plaque—and specify what would count as failure, such as the distinction collapsing once we consider hybrid cases (a named statue, a street with a representational marker). Fourth, give an honest scope assessment: what conceptual work can realistically be completed in three months without field research, and where the project risks overreaching into sociology or urban planning.

Do not include a bibliography, literature review, or requests for specific citations. You may refer to general positions (e.g., “the view that renaming is merely cosmetic”) but do not invent named scholars or page references. Write for a funding committee that includes non-specialists but expects rigorous conceptual framing.
