For a feature I am writing on sleep, consciousness, and the limits of first-person testimony, please write a dialogue between two philosophers of mind. One should defend Norman Malcolm’s view that dreams are not genuine experiences, on the grounds that the criteria for correctly attributing an experience—waking observation, behavioral expression, and intersubjective verification—are absent during sleep, leaving dream reports as reconstructions formed upon awakening. The other should defend Daniel Dennett’s opposing account, according to which dreams are experiences but largely narrative confabulations assembled in the brief interval of arousal, so that the “dream” we recall is not a continuous inner movie played back but a constructed story. To keep the exchange from rehashing only the classic mid-century debate, set the conversation in a hypothetical research lab that has developed a non-waking brain-computer interface allowing sleeping subjects to emit real-time, minimal signals about internal states without awakening—thereby raising whether Malcolm’s verification criterion collapses when testimony can occur inside sleep itself, and whether Dennett’s assembly model can accommodate “lucid micro-signals” that leave no subsequent waking trace. Each interlocutor must respond directly to the other’s arguments rather than talking past them; neither should be a strawman. The dialogue should be rigorous and polished, should not request bibliographies, citations, or reading lists, and should treat the BCI technology as a conceptual extension rather than a reference to specific recent studies.
