If Rousseau—whose well-known critique of public theatre in Geneva argued that spectacles corrupt civic virtue by substituting artificial emotion for genuine moral sentiment and fragmenting collective attention—were alive to observe contemporary immersive performances mediated by predictive affective computing, in which biometric feedback loops adjust a procedurally generated narrative in real time and each audience member becomes an embodied co-performer, how might he extend his argument? Would he regard the dissolution of passive spectatorship as a restoration of active citizenship, or rather as a more insidious corruption in which the shared public square is replaced by individually calibrated emotional regimes? In particular, consider whether his distinction between authentic compassion and theatrical pity survives when the spectacle is tailored to each viewer's psychometric profile, and whether this represents a qualitative break from the collective corruption he feared, or merely its algorithmic intensification.
