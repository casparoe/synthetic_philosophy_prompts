I am writing a paper for my ancient philosophy seminar on Aristotle's account of exchange and money in the Nicomachean Ethics and Politics. In discussion, a classmate advanced the ruler analogy: money functions like a measuring rod that renders shoes and houses commensurable by expressing a quantitative value, so Aristotle's critique of usury targets only the distortion of using the measuring instrument as its own object—like weighing the ruler—while leaving ordinary merchant profit untouched, provided proportional equality is maintained. She concludes that Aristotle permits chrematistic gain whenever it respects the measure. Audit this analogy: provide the explicit correspondence table (ruler, goods, measurement, distortion); identify the points where it misrepresents Aristotle's claim that exchange is grounded in chreia and political reciprocity rather than in a pre-existing quantitative value; and say whether her permissive conclusion about merchant profit survives once those disanalogies are accounted for. Please answer in exactly five numbered items, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion.
