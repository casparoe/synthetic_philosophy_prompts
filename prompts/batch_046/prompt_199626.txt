Two members of my philosophy-of-economics reading group had a sustained disagreement about Keynes’s treatment of probability and uncertainty. I listened without taking sides. Please adjudicate their exchange point by point: for each of the six moves below, rate it from clearly mistaken to clearly right and briefly justify the rating; then say who is right about which specific issue, note what both parties are missing, and identify whether there is a deeper conceptual tension they overlook. In your response, please state how confident you are in the adjudication overall and single out the specific point about which you are least sure.

(1) The first speaker claimed that in A Treatise on Probability Keynes treats probability as a logical relation between a proposition and the evidence for it, permitting numerical values when that relation is precise, and that this makes Keynes essentially a logical-probabilist who anticipated Bayesian subjectivism.

(2) The second speaker objected that this reading ignores the decisive shift in The General Theory of Employment, Interest and Money and in Keynes’s subsequent reply to critics, where he argues that much of economic life involves uncertainty in a strong sense: situations for which there is no scientific basis to form any calculable probability, subjective or objective.

(3) The first speaker replied that even within the General Theory Keynes deploys the weight of argument, denoted w, to rank alternatives when precise probabilities are unavailable. If beliefs can be ordered by weight, she argued, there remains a rational subjective ordering, merely non-numerical, so the Bayesian framework survives in a generalized form.

(4) The second speaker countered that Keynesian weight measures the completeness or balance of available evidence relative to total ignorance, not the strength or confidence of a belief. Keynes explicitly denies that long-run expectations about investment returns or political events can be assigned probabilities of any kind; therefore importing decision-theoretic language of subjective ordering distorts his view.

(5) The first speaker then noted that in his 1937 defense Keynes admitted he had overestimated the applicability of the probability calculus in the earlier Treatise. This, she said, shows an evolutionary narrowing of domain rather than a contradiction: Keynes retained the logical apparatus but recognized its limits.

(6) The second speaker concluded that if Keynes genuinely held that many economic decisions lack any probabilistic basis whatsoever, then labeling him a subjectivist—whether numerical or ordinal—is misleading. The core insight is that rational action under true uncertainty relies on convention and animal spirits, not on concealed probabilities waiting to be ordered.

Neither speaker addressed whether Keynesian uncertainty should be read as epistemic—reflecting merely hidden or unknown probabilities—or as ontological, reflecting an inherently open future. They also did not consider whether modern ambiguity-aversion models, such as those developed after Ellsberg, genuinely capture Keynes’s weight of argument or instead reduce it to a preference over known versus unknown urns.
