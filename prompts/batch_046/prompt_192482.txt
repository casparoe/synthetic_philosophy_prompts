As a novelist drafting a work in which a protagonist deliberately suppresses inherited memories of political violence, I find myself navigating the philosophical tension between Avishai Margalit's claim that groups bear an ethical duty to preserve the memory of wrongs done to them and David Rieff's argument that strategic forgetting is sometimes a precondition for individual and collective recovery. Rather than requesting a general comparison, please describe the most important implications of this tension for the narrative ethics of fiction—specifically, whether an author who grants a character the freedom to forget thereby risks complicity in historical erasure, or whether such narrative omission can be defended as an exercise in moral autonomy that respects the character's psychological integrity. Address, in particular, what this debate implies for the reliability of a narrator who frames absence as presence: if remembering is obligatory, does aesthetic smoothing of traumatic detail constitute a betrayal of both historical accountability and reader trust? Conversely, if forgetting is ethically permissible, what formal mechanisms—ellipses, temporal jumps, or unreliable testimony—must a novelist employ to ensure that silence is interpreted as a deliberate ethical choice rather than an authorial failure of imagination?
