As someone who recently joined the board of a neighborhood cooperative and watched rational, well-informed members deadlock over values that do not reduce to self-interest or misinformation, I have been revisiting Spinoza’s democratic theory with unexpected urgency. His Political Treatise treats democracy as the most natural form of government because it expresses the collective power of the multitude, yet he also maintains that citizens guided by reason share adequate ideas that ought to dissolve faction into common agreement. I am trying to locate a precise conceptual tension between these commitments.

Please provide an example of a conceptual issue—drawn from democratic or collective decision-making—where Spinoza’s view that democracy expresses the multitude’s collective power conflicts with his rationalist expectation that citizens will converge through shared adequate ideas. The case should turn on disagreement that is genuinely irreducible: not a product of ignorance, false belief, or class antagonism, but of incompatible affective orientations that reason alone does not resolve. Rather than merely labeling the tension, show exactly where it arises and why Spinoza’s framework seems to lack the resources to accommodate it.

Please also say what would change your mind: which argument or consideration would convince you that Spinoza’s account can absorb this kind of affective pluralism without contradiction, and where your reasoning is most likely to fail.
