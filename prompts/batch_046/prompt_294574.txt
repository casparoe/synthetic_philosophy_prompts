I am a high school student in an advanced course on social choice, and I keep noticing what looks like a philosophical contradiction in how government agencies actually treat human choice. In applied welfare economics, consulting firms and public agencies routinely build cardinal measures of social welfare using revealed-preference methods: they infer citizens’ valuations of infrastructure, environmental goods, or risk reduction solely from observed market behavior, travel-time savings, or property-price shifts, and they systematically reject direct stated-preference surveys whenever those surveys conflict with observed choices, dismissing the surveys as distorted by “hypothetical bias.” Yet inside the very same bureaucracies—behavioral public-policy units—analysts routinely treat many observed choices as corrupt indicators of true welfare. When individuals select high-interest payday loans, skip free preventive-care appointments, or repeatedly click “agree” on opaque digital terms-of-service contracts, the analysts do not infer that these people genuinely prefer debt, illness, or data extraction; instead, they invoke “friction,” “present bias,” or “bounded rationality” to discount the revealed choice and substitute an analyst-reconstructed “true preference,” which is then used to redesign choice architecture. So the institution treats revealed preference as epistemically authoritative gospel in cost-benefit analysis, but as unreliable noise in behavioral-intervention design. What implicit theory of utility, preference, and welfare does this double standard presuppose? Does it require that there exists an objective, rational “true preference” separable from observed behavior, with revealed preference serving only as a fallible tracker of that hidden baseline? Or can the split be sustained without any metaphysical commitment to hidden mental states—perhaps purely as a pragmatic division between predictive modeling and normative evaluation? Reconstruct the implicit philosophy of value at work here: is utility an internal mental state, a normative standard of coherence, or merely a predictive construct?
