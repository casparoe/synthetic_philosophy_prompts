I have been trying to formalize a tension within global workspace theory (GWT) between access consciousness and phenomenal consciousness, and I have constructed the following argument that now strikes me as proving far too much. I would like to know precisely where it fails—whether there is a hidden equivocation, a scope shift, or an invalid modal slide—and, if a premise must be abandoned, which rejection is least costly.

(1) On GWT, a mental representation becomes access-conscious when it is broadcast within a global workspace, thereby becoming available to all cognitive subsystems—memory, reasoning, report, and action-guidance—within the agent's architecture.

(2) Phenomenal consciousness either reduces to access consciousness or is necessarily co-extensive with it; there is no further "phenomenal residue" that outstrips global functional availability.

(3) Now consider a nested, locally recurrent module—say, a prefrontal micro-circuit—whose representations are broadcast among all of its own internal sub-modules but never reach the brain-wide workspace.

(4) Relative to that module's own architecture, those representations are globally available: every constituent subsystem within the module can access them for integration and control.

(5) By (1) and (2), therefore, that module possesses its own access-conscious states, and hence its own phenomenal consciousness.

(6) By iteration, any sufficiently integrated neural loop with internal broadcasting qualifies as an independent conscious subject.

(7) So a single human brain contains thousands of distinct phenomenal subjects, and in split-brain patients we should recognize not one divided mind but two fully intact macro-subjects—alongside countless micro-subjects—rather than a single disrupted one.

(8) This proliferation is plainly absurd; we do not suppose that every cortical column enjoys its own private inner light.

(9) Therefore, either (2) is false and phenomenal consciousness outstrips access, or (4) equivocates on "global" (system-relative versus agent-relative availability).

The chain feels valid, yet I am reluctant to surrender (1) or (2), since abandoning them seems to reintroduce a mysterious phenomenal surplus that GWT was designed to eliminate. Is the error in (4)—that local broadcasting is not the right kind of global availability—or is there a concealed slide from functional availability within a subsystem to phenomenal presence for a subject? If I must choose, I would prefer to preserve (1) and (2) and reject something in the nesting inference, but I want to understand exactly what commitment that choice entails. Where does the argument go wrong?
