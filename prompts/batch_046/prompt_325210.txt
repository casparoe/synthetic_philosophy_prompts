In a comparative ethics course I'm taking, we've been examining how German and American folk morality diverge on the authority of rules versus the authority of individual conscience. During a discussion of bureaucratic ethics, one student invoked the maxim 'Ordnung muss sein'—there must be order—to argue that moral failure is essentially a failure of procedural adherence, and that social coordination collapses without near-unconditional rule-following. Another student countered with the American folk refrain that 'your conscience is your only real guide,' implying that any institutional command that conflicts with private conviction is automatically illegitimate. I am trying to determine what precise claims are hiding in each slogan, because the argument keeps sliding between them without anyone specifying what is actually being defended.

For 'Ordnung muss sein,' please separate the possible readings: is it an empirical claim about the conditions for social coordination, a normative claim that rules possess intrinsic moral value independent of their content, a psychological claim about the cognitive costs of constant deliberation, or a category mistake that conflates legal regularity with moral justification? And for the conscience-based counter-slogan, does it commit one to a form of moral intuitionism, to the stronger claim that institutional authority is always subordinate to private judgment, or merely to a procedural veto right? For each slogan, indicate which interpretations are trivial platitudes, which are substantive and defensible within their respective folk traditions, which are demonstrably false, and—crucially—which interpretations are actually being invoked when someone uses them to settle a dispute about whether rule-following or individual conscience 'has the better of it,' especially in borderline cases like a civil servant refusing an unlawful order versus one enforcing a regulation that is technically valid but substantively harmful.
