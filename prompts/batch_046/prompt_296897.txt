And here's the student's answer, copied more or less verbatim (I left their typo):

Hacking distinguishes interactive kinds, where the classification affects the people classified, from indifferent kinds, where it doesn't. Psychiatric categories are mostly interactive. Looping occurs when people become aware of a diagnosis and begin acting in line with it, which forces clinicians to revise the criteria. When DSM-III introduced social anxiety disorder in 1980, people started recognizing themselves in the description, prevelance rose, and the manual eventually had to widen the boundaries. Schizophrenia is different—Hacking treats it as an indifferent kind because of its biological basis, so the loop is broken; patients don't reshape the category through self-awareness. Also, the effect runs only one way: the label alters the patient, but the altered patient behavior doesn't feed back into revising the DSM, it just leads clinicians to apply the same label more often.
