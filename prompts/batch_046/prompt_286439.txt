I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy training, and I've been trying to organize my own thoughts on scientific modeling before joining a discussion group. Here is the package I've landed on, and I need an audit of whether it actually hangs together.

(1) I accept what is called Friedman's F-twist: for a scientific model, the realism of its core assumptions is epistemically irrelevant, and predictive accuracy is the sole criterion that matters.

(2) In my engineering work, I systematically reject black-box predictive models that lack interpretable structural assumptions, because I have seen unrealistic internal representations cause catastrophic failures outside the training distribution, even when in-sample predictions were excellent.

(3) I do not think this split is disciplinary special pleading. The difference is that economic models are evaluated post-hoc on observed outcomes, whereas engineering models are used prospectively to guide interventions, so the epistemic risk profile is genuinely different.

(4) I also believe that the best scientific models combine deliberately false simplifying assumptions with explicit, formal boundary conditions that specify exactly where the falsehood is harmless.

(5) Finally, I hold that when predictions fail, the correct response is always to preserve the core false assumptions and revise only the boundary conditions or calibration terms, because the tractability of the model depends on keeping those false assumptions intact.

Which of these commitments are genuinely in tension, and which only appear to conflict? In particular, does (1) undermine (4) and (5) by making structural boundary conditions epistemically superfluous? Does (3) actually justify the split between (1) and (2), or does it collapse on inspection? Rank the tensions by severity, indicate for each whether the least costly revision targets (1), (3), or (5), and tell me which single revision would clean up the most inconsistencies with the least damage to the overall view.
