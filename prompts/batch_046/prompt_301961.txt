I have been trying to understand whether a scientific claim can fail in the short run and still be correct in the long run, using Malthus as my test case. I constructed an argument to see if "right but early" is a coherent idea, but the conclusion seems to swallow everything. Here are the steps I wrote out.

(1) Malthus held that population grows geometrically while food production grows arithmetically, which means population must eventually outpace resources.
(2) For over two hundred years, this crisis has not arrived globally; instead, technological change has repeatedly expanded the food supply faster than population in many places.
(3) Those who defend Malthus say he was not falsified but only right but early — the underlying pressure is real, but technological rescue has postponed the reckoning.
(4) To make this work, one must separate the core mechanism (the mismatch in growth rates) from the predicted timeline (the actual crisis).
(5) I call this move predictive debt: the theory takes out credibility now by promising that verification will come later, and if the deadline slips, the debt is never called.
(6) But if predictive debt is allowed for Malthus, it must be allowed for any long-range theory whose outcome has not yet occurred.
(7) That means present counterevidence never actually falsifies such a theory; absence now is always compatible with presence later.
(8) If no observation can ever contradict them, long-range theories fail the basic requirement that distinguishes science from speculation — exposure to refutation.
(9) So I seem forced to choose: either Malthus is unscientific along with every other long-range prediction, or the right-but-early defense is illegitimate and Malthus was simply wrong.
(10) I resist both options, because the population-resource pressure feels real, and I do not want to abandon long-range scientific forecasting altogether.
(11) Where is the mistake? Is there an equivocation between falsifying the mechanism and falsifying the timeline? Or is one of these premises — perhaps the idea that predictive debt applies universally — the one I should reject, and if so, what does giving it up cost me?
