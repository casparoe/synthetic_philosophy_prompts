Please explain the conceptual issue of institutional overdetermination in archaeological inference, particularly as it arises in the reconstruction of economic beliefs from standardized material culture. Consider a case in which a uniform corpus of storage vessels with standardized volumetric markings and seal impressions can be interpreted with equal evidential support as (i) the material trace of a centralized redistribution bureaucracy operating under a unified ontology of state property and administrative authority, or (ii) the residue of a decentralized network of reciprocal obligations in which identical metrics served purely social functions of debt tracking and status negotiation, with no collective commitment to bureaucratic personhood or centralized ownership. The difficulty is not standard underdetermination of theory by data, but a deeper circularity: to classify an object-pattern as an institutional trace rather than a functional byproduct already requires a prior model of collective intentionality, authority, and belief that is precisely what the archaeological reconstruction aims to establish. Analyze whether this circularity can be broken by adopting a principle of institutional parsimony—favoring the reconstruction that minimizes novel ontological commitments about shared mental states and collective agency—or whether such parsimony merely displaces the problem into contested priors about the evolutionary and cognitive baseline of human social organization. Your explanation should clarify why the inference from physical residue to institutional ontology is conceptually distinct from ordinary evidential reasoning, and whether the circularity is vicious or benign.
