Here is the answer (about 450 words):

Malthus claimed that population grows geometrically while food production grows only arithmetically, so positive checks—famine, war, disease—are inevitable. This clearly failed in Britain and globally after the Industrial Revolution, because synthetic fertilizers, mechanization, and trade let food supply outpace population for nearly two centuries. By Popper's criterion of bold predictive failure, the theory was falsified.

Yet the theory can be rescued if we read it as an asymptotic claim rather than a timeline-bound forecast. Once technological subsidies are exhausted—if soil degradation and climate limits eventually halt yield growth—the geometric-arithmetic gap will reassert itself. So Malthus was not wrong about the dynamics; he was merely early, applying a long-run structural logic to a temporary technological exception.

This rescue requires keeping the mechanism intact. The mechanism is not general resource scarcity, but specifically that population pressure activates positive checks when per-capita food falls. Even so, the underlying logic is essentially the same as any resource-depletion model: when inputs are fixed and demand grows exponentially, collapse eventually follows. Even if modern medicine suppresses those checks, the structural pressure remains, waiting for the technological boundary condition to lift.

There is a tension, however: Malthus himself denied that technology could ever increase carrying capacity, even temporarily, treating agricultural productivity as a fixed constant. Because he ruled out any temporary improvement, revising his theory to accommodate technological rescue actually violates his original formulation. Still, the revision is philosophically legitimate. Long-run predictions outlast any single researcher, so they are immune to direct falsification. Their untestability within a human lifespan means we should evaluate them by structural coherence alone, not by historical outcomes like the 20th-century yield boom.
