I'm auditing this course on philosophy of mind and me and my friends got into a huge debate about uploading after I mentioned that LessWrong post about gradual neuron replacement. One friend laid out this argument that seems to prove either that identity isn't transitive or that any digital copy of me is literally me, which feels like it proves way too much. Here are the steps:

(1) Imagine a clinical protocol where every month we replace 5% of your cortical synapses with synthetic analogues that match the exact input-output profile, done slowly enough that your phenominal reports stay continous—no blackout, no "this isn't me" moment.

(2) After 20 months you're fully synthetic but have reported continuous selfhood the whole time. Denying you're still you requires picking an arbitrary cutoff—say, 63% replaced—where you suddenly died, which is implausible.

(3) So gradual functional replacement with maintained phenomenal continuity preserves personal identity.

(4) Now run a variant: instead of copying synapses, at each step we train a predictive model on your remaining biological tissue and let it drive the synthetic parts. By month 20 you're running on a continuously-updated model that never literally copied your neural architecture.

(5) From the inside, there's no reportable difference between "my synapse was replaced" and "a model predicted my synapse's output"—both feel like smooth continuation.

(6) So if (3) holds, predictive-model replacement should preserve you too.

(7) But once the month-20 model is finished, we can instantiate it ten times on different servers. Each reports your memories and says "I'm me."

(8) By (6), each instance is you. By transitivity, they're identical to each other, but they clearly aren't—different locations, diverging futures.

(9) So reject transitivity, reject (6), or reject (3). Rejecting (3) makes gradual replacement kill you arbitrarily. Rejecting transivity saves identity but breaks logic. Rejecting (6) requires saying there's a hidden metaphysical difference between replication and prediction that never shows up in experience.

My friend thinks the error is in (5)—that phenomenal reports can't distinguish these cases so they can't ground identity claims—but I'm not sure that's right either. Tell me which specific step to reject, whether it's the slide from (3) to (6), the use of transitivity in (8), or something else; explain why that step looked plausible at first; and then price out what each of the three options (giving up (3), giving up (6), or giving up transitivity) actually costs for someone who wants to defend gradual uploading without accepting that ten copies of me are all literally identical. I'm especially curious whether there's a hidden equivocation between causal continuity and functional continuity between (3) and (6) that makes the slide look valid when it isn't, and whether that saves us from the conclusion without giving up on uploading altogether.
