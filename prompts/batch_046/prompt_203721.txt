I run a legal philosophy seminar for about 10 grad students, 90 minutes on Tuesday, and I need an activity that makes them actually feel teh Hart-Fuller tension before anyone names Fuller's internal morality or Hart's rule of recognition. No lecture up front — the rules and payoffs should force the problem.

Split them into Codifiers (3) and Adjudicators (4) in a fictional state, Nordia, with 3 silent observers. The Codifiers draft a 5-rule Charter that includes standard provisions plus one clause you should invent: call it procedural reciprocity, meaning officials must apply rules in ways that preserve citizens' capacity to predict and respond — it's like Fuller's congruence principle but embedded as a positive charter duty, not an external moral check. The Codifiers also set an amendment gate (3/5 vote) and decide whether to include an emergency suspension clause.

Then deal three Crisis Cards — invent them, don't use the usual vehicle-in-park. Each should describe a literal application of Nordia's rules that seems to destroy the charter's own purpose: for instance, a ban on dangerous gatherings applied to a protest that is literally preventing government collapse, or the amendment gate exploited to dissolve judicial review just before a vote. The Adjudicators must rule in writing: apply text strictly, invoke procedural reciprocity to bend the rule, or declare the rule is not law because it violates the charter's internal structure.

Now for the mechanic — and I want a committed take, not a buffet of options: Adjudicators earn Stability Points for textual consistency across rounds and Legitimacy Points for outcomes that keep observers' hidden public trust scores high, but they don't know which metric is weighted more heavily until after round 3. The observers have hidden roles: two are positivist citizens who only value predictability, one is a natural-law citizen who values minimal decency in outcomes. If Stability and Legitimacy diverge by more than 5 points after round 3, trigger a constitutional collapse event.

Then debrief: map each crisis card onto Fuller's claim that extreme procedural failure breaks legality vs Hart's worry that judges invent law under cover of interpretation. Be frank — which feature of Fuller's view, maybe the idea that legality has an internal morality of aspiration, does the game reproduce well? Which feature, probably the long-run cultural erosion when judges routinely smuggle moral judgment into interpretation, can't be reproduced in 90 minutes and I need to supply in discussion?

Use American English. Don't ask for a bibliography, reading list, or citations — I want this runnable Tuesday without anyone needing to look up obscure texts. Keep it casual; there's a typo or two in here and that's fine. Design the three crisis cards specifically for Nordia's charter, tell me definitively which scoring mechanic best captures the institutional risk and why, and make sure the idea shows up in what happens, not just in what I say afterwards.
