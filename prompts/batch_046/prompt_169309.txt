As a recently retired entomologist who has begun studying philosophy with a small group of former scientific colleagues, I have become preoccupied with the scale argument concerning insect sentience. Given that the global population of insects likely exceeds ten quintillion individuals at any given time, even a minuscule per-capita probability of conscious experience—say, one in ten thousand—might, by simple expectation, generate more aggregate moral weight than the entire class of mammals. I am struck by how closely this resembles epidemiological burden modeling, where prevalence multiplied by severity yields total harm, yet moral patienthood does not obviously behave like an infectious agent. For our upcoming session, I want to move past broad slogans and map the actual logical space of responses.

Please construct a decision tree with exactly four binary branch nodes. Each node should pose a concrete judgment about a specific case or aggregation rule, phrased so that a respondent can answer it without already knowing which philosophical position it represents. The tree must begin from a shared starting point—acceptance of the scale problem, including the population figures and the conceptual possibility of invertebrate sentience—and terminate in one of six named positions. The required terminal positions are: (1) linear expected-value aggregation across all insects regardless of neural architecture; (2) a threshold or cutoff view that assigns moral weight only when individual credence exceeds a specified level; (3) a taxonomically graded view that weights different orders according to observable neural complexity; (4) an epistemic skeptic who rejects the argument on the grounds that we possess no justified credence assignments at all; (5) a precautionary practitioner who supports mitigation measures despite not knowing the probabilities; and (6) an anti-aggregationist who denies that moral consideration scales with population size.

To force genuine separation, embed these three diagnostic comparisons in your nodes: (a) the trade-off between assigning 0.1 percent credence to sentience in ten billion beetles versus 99 percent credence in a single chimpanzee; (b) the distinction between taxa with demonstrated nociceptive learning (for instance, certain hymenopterans) and those with only reflexive withdrawal (for instance, aphids); and (c) a practical policy choice between a broad-spectrum pesticide that eliminates 99 percent of an unknown-sentience pest population and a targeted method that eliminates 10 percent but requires observable suffering. For any combination of answers that does not map onto one of the six positions above, identify the empty cell and briefly diagnose whether it is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored.

Keep the language precise but accessible to retired scientists without formal philosophical training; avoid unnecessary jargon, and do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or requests for citations. Finally, explicitly flag any pair among the six terminal positions that your four nodes genuinely cannot distinguish—if two views yield identical answers on every branch, state that limitation openly rather than manufacturing a spurious distinction. The deliverable should be the tree itself, with each branch labeled by its diagnostic question and each leaf labeled by the position it represents.
