I serve on the university’s Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee, and next week we must issue a formal recommendation on a proposal that has split the board precisely along a philosophical fault line. The research team wants to run a study they call “Interfacial Translation”: they would implant a bidirectional cortical bridge between a macaque’s anterior cingulate and insular cortices and a human volunteer’s language-production networks, so that nociceptive and affective states in the macaque are mapped in real time onto syntactically structured first-person utterances spoken by the human partner—phrases such as “this sensation is overwhelming me” or “I want this to stop.” The macaque would still be physically restrained and subjected to calibrated noxious stimuli, but the team argues that the bridge turns raw neural data into articulated self-report, giving researchers direct access to subjective states that ordinary behavioral assays cannot capture. Half the board treats this as an elaborate form of telemetry with a human interpreter acting as a passive transducer, meaning standard welfare safeguards apply and the study can proceed with the usual analgesic conditions. The other half contends that the bridge creates a temporary distributed rational agent—part macaque affect, part human linguistic autonomy—so that harming the macaque component now harms a being capable of first-person rational articulation, which collapses the Kantian distinction between direct duties to rational nature and mere indirect duties regarding non-rational animals. I need you to draft the committee’s decision memo—approve, reject, or approve with conditions—addressed directly to the principal investigators. The memo should state a clear bottom line, provide reasoning the researchers can actually answer (for instance, by redesigning the interface or the consent structure), and include an honest, non-dismissive treatment of the strongest consideration on the losing side. Do not include a bibliography or citation list; the memo should stand on conceptual argument alone.
