I'm working through case studies for a graduate seminar on moral luck, and I've been tracking my professor's implicit judgments across a range of scenarios. Her record so far is as follows. First, she holds that two agents with identical negligent mental states are equally blameworthy regardless of whether a pedestrian is actually killed, though she concedes that the agent who causes death owes greater reparative obligations. Second, she maintains that being raised in a culture that valorizes violence somewhat diminishes blameworthiness for violent acts relative to an otherwise identical agent raised in a pacifist culture, assuming both possessed comparable capacities for critical reflection. Third, she denies that having an aggressive temperament through no fault of one's own attracts blame, yet affirms that failing to pursue available treatment does. Fourth, she judges that inheriting a fortune and donating it is less praiseworthy than earning an equivalent sum through labor and donating it, even when the intentions and efforts involved in the act of giving are held constant.

Next week we turn to what I am calling reception luck — the case of a philosopher who develops and publishes a rigorous ethical argument in good faith, with no awareness that in a distant historical context her reasoning will be systematically distorted to justify large-scale harm. The philosopher's reasoning, character, and intentions are held constant across worlds; only the subsequent cultural reception differs. Given her prior verdicts, what should she say about whether this philosopher bears any moral stain or diminished praise? I am less interested in a simple yes or no than in the competing rules that could accommodate her earlier judgments, what each predicts here, which rule she is most likely running, and which further case — perhaps involving a scientist whose benign invention is later weaponized — would best discriminate among the remaining candidates.
