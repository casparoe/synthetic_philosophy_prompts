Two of my coworkers—both software engineers like me, with no formal philosophy background—got into a long argument over lunch about whether societies are rational to preserve "lost cause" narratives. One of them, reaching for an analogy from our field, said these myths work like lossy compression algorithms: the full historical record (military defeat, internal coercion, moral failure, strategic misjudgment) is too noisy and large for collective memory to store losslessly, so a culture applies a codec that discards inconvenient data and preserves a high-signal core—heroism, sacrifice, group identity—that can be decompressed into solidarity when needed. He pointed to four cases: the Lost Cause of the Confederacy, the Masada narrative of heroic resistance, the Kosovo Field myth of pure national martyrdom, and the Dunkirk "spirit" of miraculous deliverance. In each case, he said, the compressed version omits surrender, coercion, or strategic reality, but the retained signal still does useful work for cohesion and morale.

The other colleague pushed back hard. She said lossy compression in engineering is governed by an explicit loss function and is at least auditable: you know exactly which frequencies you are discarding and why, and you can measure the reconstruction error. National myths, she argued, have no transparent "codec spec"—there is no documented loss function—and the omissions are not random noise but systematically favorable to existing power structures. She noted that the Masada story often suppresses the internal violence of the Sicarii and the coerced nature of many deaths, while the Kosovo narrative erases the multi-ethnic reality of the battlefield to serve modern exclusivism. She added that Dunkirk was a strategic withdrawal made possible by German restraint and naval logistics, not a supernatural triumph, and that framing it as a miracle risks mislearning about alliance politics and supply chains. She concluded that calling these myths "rational compressions" is a category error: they are not optimized representations but corrupted backups that prevent error correction and hide distributive harm.

He replied that even corrupted backups have utility when the original storage is permanently lost—archival destruction, generational turnover, and destroyed records mean the "uncompressed" history is often unrecoverable anyway. He argued that the Dunkirk myth, however inaccurate about logistics, produced the social capital that sustained Britain through the Blitz, and that the Masada story, however simplified, serves a genuine existential function for groups facing perceived annihilation. He said if a representation is systematically false but systematically useful, and if the cost of full fidelity is social fragmentation or collective despair, then the compression is rational in exactly the way we rationally use JPEG instead of RAW: we accept artifacting because the storage and transmission costs of perfection are prohibitive.

She countered that this treats "utility" as a black box without asking who pays the storage cost and who benefits from the artifacts. She said the Confederacy myth's "social capital" was extracted at the expense of Black Americans through Jim Crow and disenfranchisement, and that treating that as a rational tradeoff requires a moral framework that ignores distributive harm. She also argued that the analogy to image compression fails because JPEG is value-neutral—no one is oppressed by a compressed pixel—whereas mythic compression allocates honor, blame, and belonging, so the "codec" is never politically innocent. She added that if the original data is truly lost, the honest response is epistemic humility, not the confident deployment of a convenient fiction.

They asked me to referee. I want to understand whether the compression analogy holds up, exactly where it collapses, and whether lost causes are more like efficient data structures or like corrupted backups that societies keep running out of inertia rather than design. Could you go through their exchange step by step, rate each move, say where both sides are missing something important, and tell me whether the analogy survives once the disanalogies are on the table?
