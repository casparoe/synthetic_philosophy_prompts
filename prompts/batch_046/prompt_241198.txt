Here is an argument I developed for a seminar on Benacerraf’s dilemma, and I now suspect it proves too much—perhaps even self-defeating. I would like to know exactly where it slips.

(1) Benacerraf’s challenge rests on the premise that knowledge requires a causal-informational link, which abstract mathematical objects cannot provide.
(2) If this premise is sound, it applies not only to numbers and sets but to any causally inert entity, including modal properties, logical relations, and the reliability conditions that underpin epistemology itself.
(3) Hence we face a choice: either abandon the causal-reliability requirement globally, or accept that we lack knowledge of the very premises used to derive Benacerraf’s conclusion.
(4) Abandoning it globally appears to dissolve the dilemma altogether, allowing Platonism to stand without cost.
(5) Retaining it globally renders the argument self-undermining, since grasping (2) and (3) requires knowing abstract epistemic relations that are themselves causally isolated.
(6) There seems to be no stable intermediate restriction that saves mathematical ignorance without also undermining logical and modal knowledge.

Which step is weakest, and what does giving it up cost the standard positions—causal nominalism, modal structuralism, and fictionalist anti-realism? Please respond in no more than 300 words, in at most two paragraphs.
