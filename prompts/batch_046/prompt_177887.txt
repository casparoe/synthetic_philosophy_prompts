I am designing the final essay examination for an upper-division seminar on the metaphysics of normative reasons, and I am dissatisfied with prompts that allow students to reproduce textbook summaries of whether reasons are mental states or external considerations. To force genuine application, I want a single 90-minute essay question on free-floating rationales—normative considerations that appear to guide outcomes without being anchored to any agent’s mental states, intentions, or deliberative perspective—embedded within a constructed but intelligible extension I have been developing: what I call “architectural rationales.” These are coherence and revision conditions built into autonomous regulatory systems that update their operating principles in response to structural constraints they do not represent, understand, or intend, yet that seem to exert genuine normative pull on the system’s trajectory. The exam question should ask students to evaluate whether such architectural rationales are genuinely free-floating reasons, requiring them to distinguish normative explanation from merely functional or causal explanation, to determine whether the absence of an individual or collective reasoner eliminates normative force, and to assess whether stretching the concept of “reason” to cover these structures renders it explanatorily empty.

Please provide: (1) the exam question itself, formulated so it cannot be answered by reciting the standard mental-state versus external-reason debate; (2) a model answer of approximately 700 words that executes the required analytical moves with precision; and (3) a marking scheme out of 25 points, structured around five must-hit moves, each with its point value and a one-line note on the specific misconception or shallow response that should cost marks—such as treating structural coherence as automatically normative, conflating the absence of a particular reasoner with the absence of any normative structure, or reducing architectural rationales to disguised designer intent. The difficulty should lie in applying the distinction between free-floating rationales and instrumental regularities, not in recalling author names or canonical formulations, and neither the question nor the model answer should depend on bibliographic references or verbatim quotations from specific texts.
