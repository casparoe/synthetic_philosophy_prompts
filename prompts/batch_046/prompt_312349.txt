I am a novelist developing a manuscript on the ethics of posthumous dramatization. Before you compose anything, I want you to adopt the combined perspective of a professional moral philosopher and a literary craftsman, and to think carefully about the conceptual architecture. First, take a moment to brainstorm: move beyond the familiar debates about factual distortion or privacy invasion in biopics, and inject the less-discussed problem of narrative teleology—the way a dramatized life necessarily imposes coherence (exposition, crisis, resolution) onto a life that may have ended in institutional erasure, ambiguity, or contradiction. Now consider the specific case: a screenwriter is adapting the private journals of a recently deceased public figure who left a legally binding directive that any portrayal must preserve her contradictions without resolving them into redemption, tragedy, or heroic arc. Write a short story that unfolds entirely in the editing room, using only the present-tense negotiations between the screenwriter, the estate's lawyer, and the footage itself. The philosophical concept—what we owe the real dead when we dramatize them—should be embedded in the narrative machinery rather than stated as thesis. Force the reader to experience the paradox that honoring the dead's request for incoherence may itself require a coherent artistic form, thereby risking betrayal. Do not cite philosophers, name specific theories, or provide bibliographies; build the tension from the situation and dialogue alone.
