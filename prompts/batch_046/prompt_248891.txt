I have noticed a recurring maneuver in discussions of aesthetic testimony—both in seminar rooms and in critical prose—that I do not have a precise term for, and I am writing to ask whether it is recognized in the literature and, if not, how it ought to be carved.

The pattern runs as follows. A subject has a direct, first-order aesthetic experience of a work: she finds a particular minimalist composition monotonous, or a conceptual installation emotionally inert, or a late-modernist novel stylistically alienating. She then receives authoritative testimony—whether from a critic, a mentor, or a peer presumed competent—that the work in fact possesses a profound, subtle, or otherwise valuable aesthetic quality. Rather than simply replacing her own judgment with the testimony, or dismissing the testimony as incompatible with her experience, she preserves both by treating her own negative response not as false but as premature, unripened, or conditionally incomplete. She describes her experience as occurring 'before the work had fully disclosed itself,' or 'outside the framing conditions required for its effect,' thereby relegating her direct judgment to a provisional or preparatory stage rather than to a contradictory evaluation. The testimony is accommodated not by overriding experience but by temporalizing or conditionalizing it: her present judgment is held in abeyance as an incomplete version of what she expects eventually to feel, should the proper conditions obtain.

I have observed this in at least two variants. In one, a student who initially experiences a large chromatic painting as oppressive comes to characterize her initial reaction as 'pre-encounter affect,' maintaining that she had not yet inhabited the temporal duration necessary for the painting's field to operate upon her. In another, a listener who finds an atonal quartet grating describes his discomfort not as an aesthetic failure of the music but as a symptom of his own 'not-yet-adapted perceptual apparatus,' expecting that repeated exposure will transform his present resistance into appreciation without his ever having been wrong about what he initially felt.

This is not, I take it, the standard deference to aesthetic testimony, in which a recipient typically adopts an expert's judgment in place of her own or suspends judgment altogether. Nor is it the hermeneutic circle in any familiar sense, since the revision is not of interpretation toward coherence but specifically of the authority-status of one's own experiential report. It also differs from mere self-deception or wishful thinking, because the speaker often articulates a structured expectation of future convergence and treats her current state as epistemically subordinate rather than erroneous.

Does this configuration—direct aesthetic experience retained but downgraded to an immature or conditional stage in light of testimony—have an established name? How is it to be distinguished from adjacent phenomena such as deferred judgment, the hermeneutic circle, or what is sometimes called 'aesthetic humility'? And if it lacks a specific designation, what term and boundary conditions would best capture it, particularly so as to distinguish genuinely open-ended temporalization from cases in which the move functions merely as an unfalsifiable defense of the testimony's authority?
