I am preparing a presentation on whether Aldous Huxley’s Brave New World ultimately critiques or accidentally endorses the view that only the absence of craving and suffering matters—what my teacher calls “tranquilism.” The World State eliminates suffering and desire through soma and conditioning, yet Huxley clearly treats this as dystopian; at the same time, John the Savage actively chooses suffering and death over comfort. Is Huxley arguing that human dignity requires the presence of struggle and desire, or is he showing that our attachment to craving is itself a conditioning we mistake for freedom? I want to read the novel as a philosophical test case: does the failure of the World State come from its success at tranquilism—because absence alone cannot ground meaning—or from its failure, since citizens still experience subtle cravings they suppress? I would especially like to understand whether Bernard Marx’s discontent is evidence that tranquilism is psychologically unstable, or whether it proves that partial tranquility is actually worse than full suffering, and where an honest critic must admit that the novel resists being reduced to either side.
