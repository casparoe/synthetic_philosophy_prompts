Here are the cases I keep circling.

One: Kennedy's quarantine in '62 — risking nuclear exchange to force Soviet missiles out of Cuba. I call this justified brinkmanship. The stakes are high, the risk is transparent, and it's the adversary who chooses whether to escalate. Two: The Able Archer '83 episode, where NATO drills nearly triggered Soviet preemption because of ambiguous signaling. I think this wasn't justified — the danger came from misinterpretation and accidental escalation paths, not a deliberate choice. Three: A state builds a hidden automated dead-hand system that guarantees retaliation after decapitation, without revealing it exists. Even though it strengthens second-strike credibility, I think it's wrong; you're hiding the rules and removing human judgment. Five: Keeping deliberate ambiguity about whether tactical nukes would be used to stop a conventional invasion of an ally. Here I say ambiguity is okay, maybe necessary for deterrence. Six: Deliberately leaving ambiguous whether your own command-and-control is fully secure — letting the adversary fear unauthorized or accidental launch. Even though this also deters invasion, I think it's unjustified.
