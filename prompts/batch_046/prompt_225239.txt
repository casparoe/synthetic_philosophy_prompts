I teach a graduate seminar in philosophy of language and cognitive science, and I am designing a ninety-minute workshop session for twelve participants—advanced undergraduates and first-year graduate students—on conceptual slippage within microdomains, specifically the phenomenon of copycat or fluid concepts that reproduce structural features across contexts while quietly altering their conditions of application. Rather than lecture on this, I want the participants to experience the pull of the slippage themselves through a structured, rule-governed activity with small but real stakes.

Please design a complete classroom activity, including rules, roles, and payoffs, plus a debrief protocol. The setting is a seminar room arranged for three groups of four. The activity should center on an invented, niche microdomain—classifying instances of interference across acoustic signal processing, conversational turn-taking, and adaptive immune response—so that participants must construct and apply a shared definitional criterion without consulting external sources. The rules should require each group to commit to written classifications and to wager a small pool of points, redeemable for a minor collective reward such as selecting the session's closing music, before they learn that the same criterion produces contradictory results across domains. The participants should recognize that their concept has migrated and mutated only after they have already defended their judgments under pressure.

In your response, specify: the exact sequence of steps and time allocations; how the stakes and roles are arranged so that the conceptual shift emerges from the participants' own commitments rather than from facilitator instruction; what will predictably go wrong—for example, one group attempting to save its criterion by ad hoc restriction, another denying cross-domain applicability altogether; and how the facilitator should handle each failure mode without resolving the tension prematurely. Then provide the debrief: map each critical moment of the activity onto a feature of copycat conceptual fluidity; state clearly which aspect—perhaps the absence of a governing meta-criterion or the invisibility of the shift to the user—the rules alone can reproduce, and which aspect must be supplied by the facilitator in the closing discussion because the game mechanics cannot generate it. Do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations. The design should be accessible to a model without web access, yet specific enough to avoid generic repetition, and should be written in a formal, polished register appropriate for pedagogical commissioning.
