Read Steven Spielberg's Schindler's List (1993) through the philosophical problem of the paradox of tragedy — specifically, the question of why viewers actively seek out and value artistic representations of extreme suffering. Rather than treating the film as a simple case study for Aristotelian catharsis or Hume's conversion of painful emotion into aesthetic pleasure, examine whether it produces what might be called moral-aesthetic dissonance: moments in which formal beauty and narrative structure do not resolve the horror they depict but hold it in unresolved tension. Focus on the cinematographic treatment of the girl in the red coat during the Kraków ghetto liquidation, and on Schindler's breakdown in the final sequence in which he laments that he could have saved more people. For each scene, ask whether the viewer is invited to take a kind of pleasure in witnessing moral agency or formal mastery amid atrocity, or whether the film actively frustrates that pleasure. Then consider the execution of Amon Göth: does the refusal to grant the audience a cathartic release there support the idea that the value of tragic art lies in sustained cognitive dissonance rather than emotional release? Conclude by saying what Schindler's List adds to the philosophical debate about the paradox that arguments alone miss — particularly regarding the ethics of aestheticizing real historical suffering.
