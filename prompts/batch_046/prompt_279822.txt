I run a small community-owned repair collective—eight people fixing electronics and furniture for neighbors in our district. We are now deciding which tasks to hand over to automated diagnostic tools and robotic assembly aids, and I have been thinking, without any formal training, about why repair work feels meaningful rather than merely useful. I have settled on an idea I call attentional continuity: the value lies in the worker’s sustained, unbroken focus that links the broken object back to the specific person who brought it in and to the story of how it broke. I want a reusable decision procedure—something my team can actually run in our monthly two-hour meeting, with me signing off as coordinator but everyone voting—that turns whatever philosophy actually knows about work, automation, and human meaning into a practical checklist. It should present questions in a fixed order, state what counts as a sufficient answer for each, mark the hard stop points where automation is ruled out or permitted only under direct human oversight, and flag explicitly where the procedure hides a value judgment that cannot be eliminated by further fact-gathering. Apply it to three cases: using an automated circuit-tester on vintage radios; using a robotic arm for repetitive soldering; and replacing our intake conversation—where a resident explains what broke and why it matters to them—with an online form. Include at least one case where the procedure gives an answer I will not like. Finally, say clearly what the procedure cannot settle, and identify the kinds of proposals where treating it as purely technical will cause it to misfire.
