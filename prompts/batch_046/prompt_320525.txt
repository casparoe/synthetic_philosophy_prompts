I'm preparing a conceptual profile on embedded agency for an interdisciplinary discussion group, and I want to verify that my commitments hang together before I present them. Here is my current package: (1) I take embedded agency literally—my reasoning is a physical process occurring inside the world it models, not a detached Cartesian ego standing outside causation. (2) I think Newcomb's problem shows that rational choice can depend on evidential or logical correlations that outrun direct causal influence, so I one-box. (3) I reject logical omniscience as a rational ideal for embedded agents: I should not be required to know every logical consequence of my beliefs, especially long-range predictions about my own future behavior. (4) I accept a form of reflective equilibrium tailored to embedded reasoning—if I endorse decision principle P after deliberation, then my self-model should register that I implement P, and I should adjust my beliefs about my own future actions accordingly. (5) I hold that all self-modeling is perspectival and partial: because there is no fully objective, detached standpoint from which an agent can observe its own reasoning, any model I construct of myself is situated, fallible, and indexically loaded.

I can see some potential fractures but I'm unsure which are real. For instance, my defense of one-boxing seems to presuppose that I can treat my own decision procedure as stable and transparent enough for the predictor's correlation to hold, yet my rejection of logical omniscience together with my perspectivalism appears to deny exactly the kind of self-transparency that would license that correlation. At the same time, reflective equilibrium looks like it demands a global consistency between my endorsed principles and my self-model that may be incompatible with both limited logical access and the absence of an objective standpoint. Meanwhile, my physicalism might seem to push me toward causal decision theory and away from one-boxing, though I actually think physicalism supports treating logical correlations inside a system as genuinely real. Please audit this set: which pairs are genuinely in tension and which only appear to be? Rank the real tensions by severity, and for each specify which commitment would have to be revised and what that revision costs. Identify the single cheapest revision that cleans up the most problems, and flag any hidden contradiction I have not noticed—particularly whether any combination of (2) through (5) forces a verdict on a case I have not explicitly addressed, such as how I should reason in a self-referential loop where my belief about my own failure becomes part of the failure conditions.
