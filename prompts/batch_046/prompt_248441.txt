At a seminar, Elena and Marcus debated whether an atrocity should be called genocide when a state systematically destroys a group’s cultural institutions and forcibly relocates its members, yet does not organize mass killing. Elena argued that the UN Genocide Convention requires specific intent—dolus specialis—to physically or biologically destroy the group, so without proof of that intent the charge is legally unfounded; applying the word anyway is moral overreach that dilutes the category and undermines the legal mechanisms tied to it. Marcus replied that Raphael Lemkin’s original concept included cultural destruction—sometimes called ethnocide—and that the Convention’s narrowing was a political compromise rather than a conceptual correction; he said that insisting on the narrow legal frame lets states destroy group life through cultural means with impunity. Elena countered that law and morality serve different functions: the criminal category sets a high bar precisely because international obligations of prevention, intervention, and prosecution attach to it, and collapsing the distinction turns a precise legal trigger into rhetorical inflation. Marcus responded that this creates strategic emptiness: states learn to avoid explicit intent statements and shift to cultural destruction, so legal precision actually shields perpetrators rather than protecting victims. Elena said that problem lies with enforcement gaps, not with the definition itself, and that activists who apply the moral label regardless of legal conditions risk making the term politically weaponized and cynically ignored. Marcus concluded that the moral weight of the term is exactly why it should not be surrendered to a politically gutted legal text; reclaiming Lemkin’s broader vision is necessary for honest moral description, even if prosecution remains confined to the narrower standard. I listened as a bystander and genuinely could not tell who was winning.
