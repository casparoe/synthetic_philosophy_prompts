I'm not a student of philosophy — I just find it interesting — but my older brother studied some cognitive science and we've been classifying things together. So far we've agreed on four judgments. First, bacteria swimming toward sugar are Darwinian: they don't learn individually, they just execute genetic programming shaped by natural selection. Second, a rat in a Skinner box pressing a lever for food pellets is Skinnerian: it adjusts behavior through direct experience of reward, with no apparent inner model of the mechanism. Third, a crow that stacks boxes to reach food after apparently picturing the steps first is Popperian: it seems to let wrong hypotheses die in its mind before trying them in reality. Fourth, a lawyer writing briefs and citing precedent is Gregorian: she relies on language, shared cultural knowledge, and written tools to do reasoning she couldn't do alone.

Now we're stuck on a new case, and I want our next judgment to be consistent with these four. The algorithm simulated different shapes and behaviors, discarding ones that failed and keeping ones that moved efficiently. Once the winning design was built in a petri dish, the cluster could navigate toward a target, avoid obstacles, and even repair itself if torn. It has no nervous system, feels no pleasure or pain, doesn't imagine alternatives before acting, and doesn't use symbols or tools. Once in the dish, it does adjust its path based on physical contact with barriers, but there is no reinforcement signal like food or shock — just physical dynamics.
