In our philosophy-of-history seminar, the dispute over whether large-scale human societies move through genuinely cyclical patterns or along an essentially linear trajectory has stalled for years despite broad agreement on the underlying record. The cyclical side—working with Polybius’s anacyclosis, Ibn Khaldun’s dynamics of group cohesion, Spengler’s morphological cultures, and Toynbee’s challenge-and-response model—insists that civilizations pass through comparable phases of growth, maturity, and disintegration, and that modern societies are not exempt from this morphology. The linear side—drawing on Enlightenment and post-Enlightenment narratives—treats these patterns as selection bias and retrospective taxonomy: we remember collapses, forget continuities, and have no causal mechanism that forces repetition. Both sides accept that Rome, the Abbasid caliphate, and the Ming all declined; both accept that technological modernity is anomalous; both reject supernatural teleology. Yet positions have not shifted.

My working hypothesis is that this is not a hidden empirical disagreement—no archive will settle it—but a disagreement about what counts as an explanation in macrohistory. The cyclical camp treats morphological resemblance across societies as itself explanatory, while the linear camp demands a counterfactual or mechanistic account of why a given society had to fall exactly when it did. But I also wonder whether the sides are using “civilization” differently—once as an organic unit with a lifespan, once as a contingent institutional arrangement—or whether they are answering different questions under one heading: one offering a descriptive taxonomy of patterns, the other asking whether cumulative moral or technical progress is possible.

Diagnose the impasse using what each side actually says about evidence, comparison, and explanation. Is there a verbal disagreement about “cycle” versus “trend”? A hidden formal question about whether macrohistorical dynamics can be modeled without teleology? Or are the sides anchored in bedrock intuitions—perhaps about whether decline is a natural phenomenon like aging—that neither can argue for? And given your diagnosis, what would count as progress: a sharper distinction between morphological homology and functional analogy, a quantitative macrohistorical dataset that tests predictive power, a formal model of societal dynamics, or a reconception of the question itself? Or is the stall a sign that “cyclical versus linear” is ill-posed, and if so, what should replace it? I want the diagnosis supported by the actual commitments of each camp, not a survey of Spengler or Toynbee, together with an honest verdict on whether anything could actually end the disagreement or whether no progress should be expected and why.
