I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy background, but a conflict on my team led me to read P.F. Strawson's essay on freedom and resentment. Last week I listened to two friends — Jordan, who works in organizational design, and Maya, who advises on workplace ethics — argue about whether we should hold a failing colleague accountable. I was a bystander and couldn't decide who was right; please adjudicate their actual exchange point by point, not just give me general advice.

Jordan began by applying Strawson's distinction between reactive attitudes and the objective attitude: reactive attitudes like resentment presuppose an expectation of goodwill that the other can meet, and when a senior engineer repeatedly introduces critical bugs despite support and accommodations, continuing to hold reactive attitudes is irrational. We should instead adopt the objective attitude — treating the situation as a system-management problem involving reassignment, process redesign, or separation — rather than framing it as a moral failure. Maya objected that Strawson reserves the objective attitude for extreme cases such as mental illness, severe immaturity, or coercion, not for competent adults in professional roles. Shifting to an objective stance here is not rational management but a withdrawal of moral recognition that damages the relationship.

Jordan then proposed what he called a procedural attitude — neither fully reactive nor fully objective. In organizational contexts, he said, structures like code reviews, performance improvement plans, and role restructuring express expectations of competence without requiring personal emotional heat. Maya answered that this is not a distinct category but bureaucratized blame: Strawson distinguishes reactive attitudes held personally from those held vicariously on behalf of a group, and institutional processes are merely the latter channeled through bureaucracy. Jordan countered that Maya was conflating justification with function: Strawson's compatibilism holds that reactive attitudes are justified by their role in regulating behavior, not by libertarian freedom, so the procedural attitude serves this regulatory purpose without requiring metaphysical freedom or emotional heat. Maya replied that Strawson's point is not instrumental — reactive attitudes are constitutive of our form of life, not merely useful tools — so suppressing emotional engagement through engineered processes risks reducing the colleague to a node in a system, violating the recognition required for genuine moral responsibility rather than just its emotional coloring.

Please evaluate each round: is Jordan correct that Strawson allows modified or suspended reactive attitudes in organizational settings? Is Maya right that institutional processes are vicarious reactive attitudes rather than a new category? Does Jordan's procedural attitude legitimately extend Strawson's framework, or does it collapse into one of the two existing stances? And who is more correct overall about whether reactive attitudes should be suspended in this workplace case — and does that depend on determinism? Also say what both miss, particularly about how Strawson treats partial or diminished capacity versus full competence, and whether there is room for a genuinely intermediate stance in cases where capacity is uncertain but not clearly absent.
