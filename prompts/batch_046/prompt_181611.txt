Compose a short story—approximately 700 to 900 words—that renders Blaise Pascal's analysis of justice and force in dramatic form. In the Pensées, Pascal argues that justice without force is powerless, that force without justice is tyrannical, and that human societies therefore stabilize authority by cloaking coercion in customary legitimacy, since no natural justice exists to ground it independently. Your narrative should take place in an imaginary island polity called the Concord, governed by a ritualized cycle: every seventh year the citizenry affirms the enforcers' mandate, yet the voting urns stand permanently inside the enforcers' barracks, and the result is invariably unanimous. The protagonist, an archivist named Elias, discovers a suppressed charter revealing that the Concord's founding was an act of unmediated violence, and that the seven-year ritual was devised afterward to generate the appearance of consent. Through Elias's attempt to expose this origin, the story should illustrate not that a purer justice awaits beneath the fiction, but that without the legitimating fiction force collapses into mere brutality—while with it, justice remains indistinguishable from the power that sustains it. The story should avoid explicit philosophical commentary, anachronistic democratic vocabulary, and direct quotation of Pascal's fragments; let the internal logic of the archipelago embody the conceptual structure. Focus particularly on the moment when Elias realizes that revealing the truth does not liberate the polity but threatens to dissolve the only order it knows.
