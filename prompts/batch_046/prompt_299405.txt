I am preparing for a graduate seminar on democratic theory and have become stuck on the conceptual architecture behind populist appeals to “the will of the people.” Rather than surveying existing literature, I want to construct an evaluation framework from scratch. Could you write out the full list of desiderata that any adequate normative theory of the popular will must satisfy if it is to serve as a legitimate ground for overriding institutional checks—such as constitutional courts, independent central banks, or expert regulatory bodies—in a modern pluralist democracy?

For each requirement, please include the motivating case or distinction that makes it necessary, and indicate whether you take it to be non-negotiable or merely a preference of a particular camp (for instance, liberal constitutionalists, deliberative democrats, or aggregative majoritarians). The theory should be able to distinguish an empirical aggregation of preferences from a normatively authoritative collective decision; it must preserve the difference between a people having a will and that will being morally right; and it should not collapse the distinction between temporary majorities and enduring democratic commitments. It also needs to remain consistent with the formal results of social-choice theory—particularly Arrow’s impossibility conditions and the Gibbard-Satterthwaite result—without treating those results as mere technical obstacles to be ignored. I am especially interested in whether there is a jointly unsatisfiable subset of these demands: for example, can a theory simultaneously require direct, unmediated expression of the popular will, guarantee transitive collective preferences over more than two alternatives, and deny any individual or institution veto power?

In addition, please address a niche but important dimension that standard accounts rarely formalize: what I will call the performative-constructive aspect of popular will, whereby the very act of a leader or movement claiming to embody the people’s will can partially constitute the collective identity it purports merely to represent. A satisfactory theory must either accommodate or explicitly reject this performative loop, and say which institutional designs—referenda, sortition, plebiscitary leadership—are compatible with each stance. Where possible, note briefly how the main existing approaches (aggregative polling models, deliberative-transformation accounts, and exceptionist sovereignty views) fare against your list, but keep the weight on the criteria themselves rather than on the scorecard, since I will apply that evaluation in class.
