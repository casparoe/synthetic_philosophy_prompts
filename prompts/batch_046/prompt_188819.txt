I sit on the Common Spaces and Cultural Policy Board of a large public university. We must issue a formal written opinion before next Thursday’s meeting recommending a concrete course of action with a specific time frame; I will circulate your memo to all board members for a vote.

The case: A student collective, the Intercultural Embodiment Forum, has applied for permanent designation of a 400-square-foot lounge in the student union as a “Reflective Practice Space.” The proposal is not a prayer room: it contains no religious iconography, is open to all students regardless of belief, and features rotating secular philosophical texts about bodily discipline, communal ritual, and temporal experience. However, the space is explicitly designed around the rhythms of Islamic Ramadan and Jewish Yom Kippur fasting—quiet hours calibrated to those calendars, shared tables for breaking fast, and guided discussion prompts drawn from both traditions—while also welcoming secular students who wish to observe silent reflection during those same periods. The university’s current policy guarantees “equal access to cultural expression facilities” but also requires that “designated common spaces maintain symbolic neutrality with respect to religious and comprehensive doctrines.”

The board is divided. One faction argues that accommodating the calendrical and ritual structure of specific religious practices violates symbolic neutrality: the lounge is not generically reflective but architecturally and temporally organized around particular traditions, which risks making students outside those traditions feel like guests in a religiously marked environment. They warn of “creeping confessionalization” of shared university space. The opposing faction argues that Anglo-American multiculturalism demands not merely negative liberty—absence of coercion—but positive recognition: denying the application treats secular neutrality as a mask for dominant cultural norms (the standard academic calendar, conventional meal schedules, and unmarked secular time), and effectively forces religious students into invisibility.

We need a clear recommendation—approve unconditionally, approve with conditions, deny, or approve a modified version (for example, temporary designation, removal of calendar-specific structural elements, or relocation to a non-permanent booking system)—together with reasoning that can guide implementation.
