question: Explain Kuhn's notion of incommensurability between scientific paradigms. Does it entail that rival scientists literally live in different worlds? Illustrate with one historical example.

student answer (~350 words):

Kuhn sees a paradigm as a disciplinary matrix that includes symbolic generalizations, models, shared values, and concrete exemplars. Incommensurability happens when two paradigms embed key terms in different lexical taxonomies, so direct translation fails. Take mass: in Newtonian mechanics it is conserved, but in Einsteinian physics it interconverts with energy, so the concept shifts. This is not just linguistic; it changes what counts as a valid problem.

Kuhn also said that after a revolution scientists work in different worlds. I take this literally: the ontology actually changes. In the chemical revolution, phlogiston theorists and Lavoisier did not just disagree about combustion; they inhabited different chemical realities. For the phlogistonists, combustion released a real substance with negative weight, whereas for Lavoisier that substance was nonexistent. This is taxanomic incommensurability — the kind-terms cannot be mapped across the divide.

That said, Kuhn later restricted incommensurability to local clusters of interdefinded terms, not whole languages. Scientists still share values like accuracy and consistency, so rational comparison remains possible. But since those shared values sit inside each paradigm separately, there is no neutral framework for choice. So picking a paradigm is partly a conversion experience, not a purely logical decision.
