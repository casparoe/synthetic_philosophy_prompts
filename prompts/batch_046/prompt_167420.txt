Audience and scale: Philosophy-literate general readers; exactly five branch nodes; six leaves; and exactly two answer-combinations the tree permits but no named position occupies, each diagnosed briefly as incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored.

The six leaves must include: (i) an actual-infinity denial, holding that an infinite sequence of discrete toggles is metaphysically or physically impossible to complete; (ii) a potentialist or non-completion view, holding that the sequence can proceed without end but never reaches a finished state, so asking about the lamp at the deadline is a category error; (iii) a hyperreal or non-standard determinacy view, holding that the limit state is well-defined within an extended number system; (iv) a bivalence-failure view, holding that at the limit the lamp is genuinely both on and off, or neither, revealing that classical logic breaks down there; (v) a deflationary or semantic dissolution, holding that the puzzle vanishes once we see that the predicate "is on at the limit" does not apply to this system; and (vi) a continuity or external-rule resolution, holding that the state is fixed by a physical or conventional boundary condition — energy conservation, mechanical inertia, or an assigned value — that is independent of the toggle history itself.

The five branch nodes must be phrased as concrete cases or claims, not as theoretical labels:

1. A physical-engineering framing: "Suppose engineers build a toggle mechanism that halves its interval each cycle, reaching a two-minute deadline. Could it complete infinitely many toggles without thermal dissipation or quantum decoherence destroying the switch?" This separates physical-impossibility views from logical-possibility views.

2. "If the mechanism does reach the deadline intact, is there a fact of the matter about whether the light is on or off at exactly t = 2 minutes?" This separates determinacy from indeterminacy or non-completion.

3. "If you think there is a fact of the matter, is it fixed solely by the infinite sequence of prior toggles, or does it require an extra principle — continuity, an energy-conservation constraint, or an assigned boundary value — not contained in the toggle rules themselves?" Flag explicitly whether this node can genuinely separate the hyperreal determinist from the continuity-rule advocate; if they share answers here, say so and explain where they diverge in reasoning rather than in verdict.

4. "If the toggle sequence alone fixes nothing, do you treat that gap as showing that classical bivalence fails for limit states, or do you treat it as showing that the predicate 'is on at the limit' simply has no application here?" This separates logical-paradox views from semantic-deflationary views.

5. "Regardless of the logical outcome, do you regard the scenario as revealing a genuine feature of infinite processes, or as an artifact of idealizing continuous time together with discrete states in a way physical reality never permits?" This separates those who think the puzzle teaches us about logic or physics from those who think it teaches us only about the limits of idealization.
