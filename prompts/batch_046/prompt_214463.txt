I am trying to formalize my intuitions about the relation between justice and force, partly in dialogue with Pascal's fragments on the subject. Below are my verdicts on six concrete cases; one pulls in opposite directions and I keep reversing myself on it.

Case 1. A purely conventional rule—say, driving on the right—backed by state sanctions but lacking any intrinsic moral weight. I think it is genuinely law, and in a derivative sense just, solely because the enforcement prevents harm and secures coordination; the particular content is arbitrary.

Case 2. A tyrant's decree enforced by violence but with no moral merit whatsoever (e.g., compulsory tribute to fund personal luxury). I think it functions as law in practice—institutions apply it, people obey out of fear—but it possesses zero justice.

Case 3. A moral principle that is clearly correct yet entirely unenforceable in any existing political order (e.g., that moral culpability is never transferable to innocent relatives). I think it retains full normative authority, but it has no institutional reality; here justice is real and force is absent.

Case 4. A community abandons enforcement of an otherwise just prohibition—say, against fraud—because policing costs exceed the harm prevented. I think the norm does not become unjust; rather, justice persists while law, understood as an effective institution, dissolves.

Case 5. A long-standing property distribution sustained only by the state's continuous willingness to punish trespass. I think the distribution's claim to justice is entirely parasitic on the stability the enforcement creates; there is no independent moral fact that makes this particular arrangement correct.

Case 6. The case I cannot settle: using organized coercive power to overturn an unjust distribution and establish a more equitable one—say, land reform enforced by state violence. One part of me says this is justice at last acquiring the force it lacked; another part says that if justice must be born in violence, it was never justice but merely a competing power wearing justice's mask.

Is there a single principle—perhaps distinguishing normative justice from institutional law, or deriving legitimacy from stabilizing function rather than origin—that delivers verdicts 1 through 5? What does it predict about case 6, and about a further case in which an automated system enforces rules with perfect consistency but no human judgment? If no unified principle can accommodate all six, which verdict should be overturned, and what does that reveal about whether justice can ever justify the use of force?
