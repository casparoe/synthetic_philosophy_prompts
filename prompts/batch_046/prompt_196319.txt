I'm preparing a one-semester dissertation prospectus—roughly 8,000 words, conceptual philosophy of mind—on a question that sits in the weeds of the Malcolm–Dennett debate about whether dreams are experiences. The standard framing pits Malcolm's skepticism, grounded in the absence of concurrent introspective access during sleep, against Dennett's suggestion that dream reports involve post-hoc "loading" or confabulatory reconstruction. I want the prospectus to ask whether this binary is false: specifically, whether there is a coherent "fragmented experience" model according to which dreams involve genuinely occurring but discontinuous, non-integrated experiential fragments that lack the sustained introspective availability both Malcolm and Dennett implicitly treat as necessary for "being an experience," yet are not merely fabricated upon waking. The central research question should be precisely stated: can such a model be articulated so that it is genuinely distinct from both Malcolm's denial of concurrent dream phenomenology and Dennett's loading/confabulation account, and does it survive comparison with analogous "fragmented" waking states—such as microsleeps, subliminal perceptions, or brief attentional lapses—without collapsing into empirical psychology?

The proposal should explain why the existing literature leaves this option underexplored: both sides share an unstated commitment to a unitary, stream-like model of experience that makes discontinuity either impossible (for Malcolm) or evidence of reconstruction (for Dennett). The method should be laid out month by month for one semester—beginning with a conceptual analysis of the conditions on first-person authority and introspective access, then developing the fragmented-experience model, then testing it against the waking analogues and against objections drawn from both standard positions. It should specify what a successful result looks like (a stable conceptual model with clear conditions that distinguish it from both rivals and that does not depend on controversial empirical claims about sleep architecture) and what counts as failure (the model collapsing into one of the two standard positions, or requiring commitments about consciousness—e.g., a radical disunity of the self across sleep and wake—that are independently implausible). Finally, it should include an honest scope assessment: in particular, whether comparing waking analogues risks turning the project into empirical cognitive science rather than philosophy, and what safeguards would keep it conceptual.

Write the proposal in American English. Do not include a bibliography, reading list, or requests for citations; refer to positions and arguments generally, and note that specific references will be added separately. Avoid framing the deliverable as a literature review by citation—this is a plan for original conceptual work.
