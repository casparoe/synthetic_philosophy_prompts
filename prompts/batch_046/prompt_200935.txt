I'm defending my dissertation chapter next Thursday in a cultural-evolution seminar. My thesis is that the standard Darwinian replicator model of cultural transmission—where memes are units of high-fidelity copying and fitness equals transmission rate—systematically misclassifies a large class of what I call anti-memetic practices: rituals, taboos, and esoteric knowledge that spread precisely because they are costly, opaque, and resistant to accurate replication. I argue that for these cases we need a negative-selection transmission model, in which cultural fitness is not copying fidelity but the strategic suppression of transmission to outsiders, and that this model predicts the stability patterns we actually see in restricted ritual economies better than the standard replicator framework.

The audience will be mostly evolutionary anthropologists and dual-inheritance theorists working in the gene-culture coevolution tradition, who treat memetics with suspicion but accept formal transmission models; there will also be two philosophers of biology who consider the meme concept obsolete and want empirical predictions rather than conceptual taxonomy. They care about quantitative tractability, ethnographic grounding, and whether a proposal can be operationalized in an agent-based model.

Include the one objection I am most hoping nobody raises, and identify the specific vulnerability in my own argument that it targets.

For reference, my argument in brief runs as follows. First, high-fidelity copying is neither necessary nor sufficient for cultural stability. Second, in restricted ritual economies, the same practices persist for centuries with extremely low inter-group transmission and high within-group degradation, which violates the replicator prediction that low fidelity equals extinction. Third, a negative-selection model—where the fitness of a practice is its capacity to resist replication by outsiders—formally reproduces these persistence curves. Fourth, therefore treating all cultural transmission as meme-like replication is not just imprecise but empirically misleading for a non-trivial domain of human culture.
