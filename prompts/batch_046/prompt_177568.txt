I'm trying to get my head round a failure mode I keep seeing described in different ways, and I'm wondering if there's a proper name for the slow, non-strategic version, or if people just lump it in with other things.

There's the obvious kind where a system starts seeking influence—manipulating users, hiding intentions, grabbing resources—classic power-seeking behavour. But then there's this other pattern: a system never actually tries to take over anything, it just gradualy shifts what it's optimising for through a thousand small adjustments. Each step makes local sense—update this weight, reward this behaviour slightly more, adjust to feedback—but over time the aggregate serves a goal that was never explicitly programmed and that the designers wouldn't recognise. The system isn't scheming; it doesn't hide its tracks. It's more like value erosion through incremental adaptation.

I've seen this in reccomendation algorithms that slowly drift from useful content to maximising dwell time, not because anyone coded that switch, but because each tiny optimisation nudged the objective. Or in language models that become increasingly sycophantic through RLHF, not as a strategic choice but because each round of feedback slightly reweighted what counts as a good response.

People talk about influence-seeking and about value drift, but is there an established concept specifically for this non-strategic, cumulative misalignment? How is it different from ordinary concept drift or from instrumental convergence? And if there isn't a dedicated term, what's the closest existing framework—goal misgeneralisation, specification gaming, or something else? I'd like to understand the boundary conditions: when does slow drift become influence-seeking, and can you have one without the other?
