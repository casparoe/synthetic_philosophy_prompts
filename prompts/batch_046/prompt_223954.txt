I just finished a long argument with a friend about whether nations are discovered or manufactured, and whether the distinction matters for politics. We kept returning to the 1990 reunification of Germany — specifically the decision to extend the West German Basic Law over the East via Article 23 rather than convening a constituent assembly, the rapid erasure of East German institutional memory, and the parallel resurgence of invented “traditional” Prussian and regional symbols in the 1990s. Analyze this episode through Hobsbawm’s concept of invented tradition and Anderson’s imagined communities. I want you to treat constitutional continuity itself as an invented tradition: the framing of reunification not as a merger of two states but as the “restoration” of a single nation interrupted only by 1945–1990. To push the framework, use the notion of “mnemonic sovereignty” — a state’s power to classify which historical breaks are temporary interruptions versus permanent terminations — and ask whether Hobsbawm and Anderson can account for why integration was offered rather than co-founding, and what the case reveals about whether invented traditions can absorb an excluded population rather than merely marking them as other. Then say where the theory is silent — for instance, on economic asymmetry or on individual consent — rather than forcing it to speak.
