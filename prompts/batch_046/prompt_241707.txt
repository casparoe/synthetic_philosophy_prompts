I am preparing a long-form feature on what philosophers have begun calling the "double inversion"—Darwin's demonstration that biological design can arise without a designer's comprehension, and Turing's demonstration that intelligent behavior can be engineered without first resolving the nature of mind. In my background reading I came across the following summary in an unnamed recent essay by a science writer with philosophical training; I want to verify its reliability before I rely on it in print. The summary reads as follows.

Darwin's theory of natural selection proves that complex biological structures can arise through a process that is entirely blind to purpose: random mutation supplies variation, and environmental retention selects for survival value, so that over deep time the result is an organism exquisitely fitted to its niche despite the complete absence of foresight or comprehension in the mechanism itself. This is competence without comprehension at the cosmic scale. Turing inverted the same logic for artificial intelligence: rather than asking whether a computer possesses consciousness or understanding, he proposed the imitation game, which tests only whether an observer can distinguish machine responses from human ones. By making behavioral indistinguishability the sole criterion, Turing showed that engineering need not solve the mystery of mind in order to produce intelligent performance. Taken together, these two inversions establish that comprehension is neither necessary for the production of design nor for the exhibition of intelligence—nature and technology both achieve sophisticated ends through mechanisms that understand nothing of what they do.

Please audit this summary with the discipline of a fact-checker who also knows the philosophy. I do not want a simple true-or-false verdict; I want a section-by-section assessment of what is accurate, what is a defensible simplification, and what subtly misrepresents either thinker. Specifically: does Darwin's mechanism actually warrant being called "competent" in any non-metaphorical sense, or is that an anachronistic projection from later philosophy of biology? Does Turing's imitation game genuinely dismiss the question of internal understanding, or does it reframe it in a way the summary obscures? Are there points where the summary conflates evolutionary selection with behavioral testing—mechanisms with entirely different structures—into a single "inversion" that neither Darwin nor Turing would recognize? And does the final conclusion, that comprehension is unnecessary for both design and intelligence, actually follow from the two premises, or does it depend on smuggling in a particular philosophy of mind that both thinkers might reject? I also want your judgment on whether framing these two arguments as a unified "double inversion" is a legitimate interpretive lens or an artificial pairing invented by popular writers to make history neater than it is. Please distinguish standard scholarly interpretations from contested ones, flag any claim that is simply wrong, and note where the summary overstates what either figure actually argued.
