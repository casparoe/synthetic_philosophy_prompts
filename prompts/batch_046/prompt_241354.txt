In a study group working through problems of moral luck, a fellow student kept invoking the maxim, "If it wasn't in your control, it can't be in your blame," using it to dismiss both outcome luck — being praised or blamed for results that depend on unforeseeable circumstances — and what she called "structural luck," by which she meant the fortune of being formed within social and psychological conditions that determine which choices are even available before one acts. She insisted that both violate one and the same principle of responsibility, but when I pressed her on what "control" actually requires, her answers seemed to shift between direct volitional command, causal influence, epistemic foresight, and counterfactual ability. I want to understand exactly what principle she might be defending.

Could you lay out the distinct precise theses that slogan could express — specifying, for each, what kind of "control" is at stake, whose control is being assessed, and at what temporal stage (the formation of character, the selection among available options, or the execution of a particular act)? For each reading, indicate whether it is trivially true, substantively defensible, plainly false, or so broad that it eliminates responsibility altogether; and say which interpretations would actually allow her to reject moral luck in both its outcome and structural varieties without collapsing into a general determinism. In particular, I want to know whether any version of the principle can coherently distinguish structural luck from outcome luck, or whether treating them identically forces her to abandon responsibility for actions that are clearly within an agent's power. Please present these as a menu of interpretations rather than settling on one, and indicate which ones genuinely support the dismissal she was trying to make.
