Rather than explaining the theory itself, please design the pedagogical plan I should follow: specify the precise order in which the conceptual components must be introduced, with explicit justification of their dependencies—must the sensitivity and adherence conditions be laid out before closure is mentioned at all, or should closure be presented first as the desideratum that the theory ultimately sacrifices? Identify the single carrying example that should anchor the session; I want something drawn from scientific practice rather than the familiar zoo or brain-in-vat scenarios—perhaps a research team confirming an anomalous atmospheric measurement through redundant but independently calibrated sensors—so that it clearly illustrates counterfactual dependence without inviting confusion with mere statistical reliability. State exactly what logical properties this example must possess (for instance, a nearby, genuinely possible error-state that is not sceptically remote) and what distracting features it must lack (for instance, explicit modal language that makes the counterfactual feel like science fiction). Include one diagnostic question for the end that reveals whether they grasp why closure fails structurally under tracking, rather than whether they can merely recall a counter-example. Finally, state explicitly what technical material should be omitted at this level—such as formal modal semantics for the sensitivity condition, method-relative tracking, or recent attempts to restore closure within a tracking framework—and present the entire plan in British English.
