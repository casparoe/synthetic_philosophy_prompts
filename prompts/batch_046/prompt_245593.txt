Draft the joint statement that two opposing camps in the philosophy of politics could both sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration on whether "Western values" form a coherent category. Party A maintains that the phrase tracks a genuinely unified, family-resemblance cluster of normative and institutional commitments—including individual rights, secular public reason, democratic accountability, and specific conceptions of rule of law—that can be contrasted with other civilizational frameworks without reducing them all to "modernity" or "liberalism." Party B maintains that the phrase is essentially a rhetorical instrument for legitimating geopolitical dominance; that every attempt to formalize its content either collapses into tautology or smuggles in contingent, post-Enlightenment European preferences; and that the category's apparent coherence is an artifact of selective historiography rather than institutional reality.

The statement should be structured as follows. First, a numbered section of agreed claims: what both sides accept about the phrase's actual usage in contemporary argument (e.g., its deployment in immigration policy, international human-rights discourse, and cultural-war rhetoric); about its historical genealogy from the Cold War through post-9/11 discourse; and about the minimal conditions any value-cluster must satisfy to count as descriptively "Western" rather than merely liberal-democratic or modern. Second, the precise formulations of residual disagreement, each phrased in wording its own party would endorse: in particular, address whether a negative-definition strategy—specifying the cluster by institutional exclusions it enforces (e.g., rejection of hereditary caste hierarchy, rejection of theocratic sovereignty, rejection of collective guilt mechanisms in criminal law) rather than by positive doctrinal content—can rescue descriptive coherence, and whether such a strategy avoids circularity. Third, identify the cruxes: the sub-questions such that resolving them in one direction would move at least one party. I take the central cruxes to be (i) whether the institutional similarities across societies conventionally included are structurally deep enough to support normative generalization, and (ii) whether the phrase's demonstrable rhetorical deployment necessarily undermines every descriptive or normative function it might serve. Fourth, for each crux specify one concrete comparative institutional case or body of constitutional evidence that both parties agree is relevant—such as the treatment of religious dissent and blasphemy in specific post-colonial constitutional orders that self-identify with the category—and record in advance each party's prediction about what that case demonstrates.

The document should be neutral in tone, should not adjudicate the dispute, and should make the remaining disagreement exact rather than verbal. Do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; reason from the conceptual structure and the agreed comparative evidence alone.
