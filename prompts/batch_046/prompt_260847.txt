I have come to think that nudging should not be analyzed as libertarian paternalism at all, but as what might be called recursive preference construction: the choice architect does not override an antecedent preference ordering, but participates in a feedback loop that generates the very preferences the architecture then claims to accommodate. On this view, the standard charge of paternalism collapses, since there is no prior autonomous self being violated; the self is co-constituted by the architecture itself. What would genuinely count against this position? I am not asking for the familiar objections about manipulation or hidden coercion. Rather, I want to know which empirical findings about preference stability, which conceptual distinctions between preference formation and preference distortion, and which results in the philosophy of personal identity it stands or falls with. Which defeaters would a committed defender concede as damaging, and which could be absorbed by redrawing the boundary between architecture and agent? Please rank them from fatal to merely awkward, and assess whether the view has been qualified into unfalsifiability — and, if so, whether that insulation is a theoretical defect for an account of this kind.
