Analyze the 1956 Oxford University decision to award Harry S. Truman an honorary doctorate — and G.E.M. Anscombe's protest that honoring him endorsed the deliberate killing of non-combatants at Hiroshima and Nagasaki — through the lens of Anscombe's doctrine of double effect and her distinction between direct and indirect intention. First, apply the framework precisely: how does it classify the atomic bombings, and does it generate any judgment about the moral status of conferring an institutional honor on the decision-maker, or does it remain silent on ceremonial and symbolic endorsement? Then examine whether Anscombe's own protest is consistent with that framework, especially if one considers that public moral condemnation can itself become a form of shared association with the wrong being denounced. Finally, say what the episode reveals about the reach and limits of double-effect reasoning when applied not to an individual battlefield choice but to collective, retrospective institutional judgment: does it help explain why honoring Truman struck her as a communal moral failure, or does it expose a gap in her view regarding the ethics of symbolic recognition?
