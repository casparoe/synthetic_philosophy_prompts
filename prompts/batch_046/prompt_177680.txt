I have been tracking a recurring argumentative loop in debates over grand narratives—particularly in the critical literature responding to framing like Harari’s portrayal of the Agricultural Revolution as an irreversible cognitive and ecological trap, Diamond’s energy-capture thesis linking domestication to state formation, and Graeber and Wengrow’s counter-narrative of seasonal, non-hierarchical societies that allegedly moved in and out of cultivation without locking themselves in. Here is the loop in detail.

A narrative proposes a single macro-driver—say, a shift in information-processing capacity or in energy extraction—that is meant to explain a near-universal transition across independent societies. Critics then produce granular archaeological and ethnographic cases: societies that cultivated grains but retained mobility; communities that built monumental architecture without permanent hierarchy; or writing systems that existed in non-state ritual contexts rather than bureaucratic ones. Rather than treating these as falsifying instances, defenders absorb them by reclassifying the driver as a “structural tendency” or “species-level pressure” that operates “above” local variation. Once that move is made, any local deviation becomes “noise” or “path dependency,” and the macro-driver is protected: if the transition happened everywhere, the driver caused it; if it failed somewhere, local conditions merely masked the underlying pressure. Critics then press the obvious question: under what local conditions could the driver be genuinely absent rather than masked? At this point, defenders often introduce a further distinction—between “structural potential” and “historical actualization”—that allows the driver to remain causally active even where its effects are invisible. The result is a pattern in which the narrative starts as an empirical generalization, survives counterexample by ascending to a structural claim, and ends with a conceptual distinction that makes the original driver empirically inert in any particular case, yet still indispensable for explaining the global pattern.

I have seen versions of this in discussions of whether writing was invented for state control, in debates over whether the Neolithic package was adopted as a unit or assembled piecemeal, and in responses to evidence that some societies intensified cultivation and then deliberately abandoned it.
