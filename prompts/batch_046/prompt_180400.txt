Read Ridley Scott's Blade Runner (1982) through the lens of higher-order theories of consciousness—specifically Rosenthal's higher-order thought account and Block's access/phenomenal distinction. The replicants carry implanted memories and, in Rachael's case, false beliefs about her own past; the Voight-Kampff test probes emotional response rather than reflective self-monitoring. Does the film treat consciousness as requiring an accurate higher-order representation of one's mental states, so that Rachael's discovery that her memories are implanted undermines her claim to genuine phenomenal consciousness? Or does it implicitly separate first-order affect from higher-order awareness, suggesting the replicants remain phenomenally conscious regardless of their meta-cognitive errors? I want a reading faithful in both directions: where the test scene illuminates or misrepresents what a HOT account actually demands; where the narrative of manufactured identity resists being reduced to an argument about self-knowledge; and whether an honest critic must concede that the film ultimately dramatizes a problem—perhaps the social attribution of consciousness rather than its internal architecture—that the access/phenomenal debate tends to overlook. I'm preparing this for a philosophy of mind seminar, so please say what would change your verdict: which interpretation of the replicants' mental life, or which disanalogy between cinematic identification and philosophical self-reference, would lead you to reject the reading?
