I have been noticing a recurring structural tension in debates about decentralized institutional design that seems to sit near, but not squarely inside, the classic social-choice conflict involving minimal liberal rights and collective welfare—usually introduced with the case of two people disagreeing over a single book. In the configurations I keep encountering, however, there is no single shared option being contested; instead, authority is distributed across disjoint domains, and the global outcome is the conjunction of locally sovereign choices.

Consider two concrete instances. First, in a university with strong departmental autonomy, each graduate program chair sets admission standards for her own unit—say, requiring deep specialization in a particular methodology. Every chair’s local rule is defensible, even optimal for her disciplinary coherence, yet the aggregate profile of heterogeneous requirements becomes so fragmented that interdisciplinary collaboration collapses. Research productivity and student placement suffer across all departments. A uniform, slightly relaxed package applied to everyone might leave every chair better off, but no chair can unilaterally relax her own standards without violating the norm that she is decisive within her domain. Second, in a federation of municipal districts, each council holds veto power over zoning within its own boundaries. Every council rejects high-density development to protect local amenities, but the resulting city-wide housing shortage depresses labor markets and tax revenues in every district. A coordinated agreement permitting moderate density everywhere might improve conditions in each district, yet no council can be compelled to accept density locally.

In both cases, decision-makers are sovereign within non-overlapping jurisdictions; the collective result is the product of these separate choices; and that product is worse for every party than an available alternative that requires at least one party to accept an outcome they reject within their own domain. I am unsure whether this distributed-jurisdiction pattern is treated as a direct extension of the liberal-paradox framework, whether the separation of domains introduces a genuinely different logical structure—perhaps closer to a multi-dimensional collective-action failure with protected spheres—or whether it belongs to a separate literature on federalism or mechanism design. Does this configuration have an established name? If so, how precisely does it differ from the original two-agent, single-option case? And if not, what exactly are its boundaries: must the domains be strictly disjoint, can preferences be interdependent across domains, and does the paradox require that the globally superior alternative be available through unanimous consent rather than through centralized imposition? I would value not merely a label, but a careful carving of what the phenomenon is, what it is not, and where its edges lie.
