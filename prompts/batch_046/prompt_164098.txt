Question: To what extent, if any, does the second law of thermodynamics explain the psychological arrow of time—that is, our capacity to remember the past but not the future? In your answer, discuss the role of the so-called past hypothesis, and consider whether it resolves the explanatory problem or merely displaces it onto an unexplained boundary condition.

Student's answer:

The second law states that entropy in an isolated system tends to increase with time. Because human memory is a physical process that requires the formation of ordered traces—neural patterns, written records, and the like—such traces can only be laid down in the direction in which entropy is rising. Hence we remember the past, when the universe was in a state of lower entropy, and we have no corresponding memories of the future, where entropy will be higher. This establishes that the psychological arrow is nothing over and above the causal arrow, which in turn is nothing over and above the statistical arrow of entropy.

The past hypothesis, first articulated by Ludwig Boltzmann in 1895 as part of his statistical defence of the H-theorem, posits that the universe began in an extraordinarily low-entropy macrostate near the Big Bang. Once this boundary condition is imposed, the statistical mechanics of branch systems implies that entropy will tend to increase away from that point in both temporal directions. Nevertheless, because we experience increase only in one direction, the past hypothesis demonstrates that the distinction between past and future is ultimately conventional: the boundary condition fixes a reference point, but there is no intrinsic physical difference between the two directions of time. Reichenbach's principle of the common cause then accounts for the clustering of records near the low-entropy boundary, though this is merely a statistical correlation without deeper metaphysical weight.

Consequently, the conjunction of the second law with the past hypothesis provides a complete and sufficient explanation of why we remember the past and not the future, and no further asymmetry needs to be postulated.
