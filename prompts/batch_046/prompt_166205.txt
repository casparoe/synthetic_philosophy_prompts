I am trying to understand the debate Dennett frames in *Consciousness Explained* (1991) about the color-phi phenomenon and the “timing” of conscious experience—specifically his distinction between an Orwellian revision, in which a subject’s memory of an earlier stimulus is rewritten after a later one arrives, and a Stalinesque revision, in which the brain briefly constructs an erroneous intermediate representation and then suppresses it without leaving a memorial trace. I recently encountered a related illusion, reverse-phi, in which a dot that physically displaces rightward while changing hue is perceived as moving leftward, apparently because the visual system binds motion to the color transition rather than to spatial position. From this I have formed the view that reverse-phi demonstrates the brain can actively represent motion contrary to the physical intermediate state, which means there never was a constructed but then airbrushed intermediate frame; the mistake can only be a retrospective reinterpretation of the first frame in light of the second—making Stalinesque revision impossible and leaving Orwellian revision as the only viable mechanism. Is this reasoning sound, or does it depend on a misunderstanding of what the two revision models are actually committed to regarding the construction, suppression, and memorial fate of intermediate percepts?
