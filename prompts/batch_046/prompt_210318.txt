I am preparing a lecture on Dennett’s taxonomy of minds—Darwinian, Skinnerian, Popperian, and Gregorian—and have constructed what I call the mirror-closure argument, which if sound seems to collapse Gregorian cognition back into the Popperian category. Each premise looks defensible in isolation, yet the conclusion strikes me as intolerable, since it implies that no finite brain can achieve the kind of second-order, culturally mediated representation that language and self-correction appear to require. I want to know exactly where the slide occurs and what price I pay for blocking it.

(1) A Darwinian creature behaves according to evolutionary hard-wiring; a Skinnerian creature modifies behavior through reinforcement without internal modeling; a Popperian creature can generate and internally test hypotheses about its environment; and a Gregorian creature can deploy cultural tools—especially language—to form representations about its own representations and about those of others.

(2) To form a representation about one of my representations—for instance, to judge that my belief that P is unreliable—I must not only hold the first-order belief but also represent the relation between that belief and some standard of reliability.

(3) Representing that reliability-relation is itself a representational state, call it R2, and if that evaluation is to be cognitively accessible rather than merely causal, R2 must itself be anchored.

(4) By the principle I will call recursive representational anchoring, any representational state that serves as an evaluative anchor for another state must itself be anchored by a further state if the creature is genuinely to access the evaluation.

(5) So Gregorian meta-cognition initiates an infinite ascending chain: R1 anchored by R2, R2 anchored by R3, and so on without termination.

(6) No finite biological brain can instantiate an actually infinite chain of distinct representational states.

(7) Therefore, no creature can genuinely achieve Gregorian second-order cognition; the taxonomy collapses, and all minds are at best Popperian.

(8) But this is absurd: we routinely correct our own beliefs, understand false belief in others, and negotiate norms through language, all of which appear to be distinctively Gregorian achievements.

If I must preserve (1) and (6)—the taxonomy and the finitude constraint—where should the argument be broken? Is the error in (4), the recursive anchoring principle I have introduced? Or does (3) smuggle in an illegitimate demand that every meta-representation must itself be evaluated? I am inclined to defend (2) as phenomenologically accurate, but I want to know precisely what I am giving up if I reject (4) rather than (5), and whether there is a more subtle equivocation between representing a representation and evaluating an evaluation that I have missed.
