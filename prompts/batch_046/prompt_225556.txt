Please describe the most important implications of treating consciousness as a user-illusion—specifically, the view that conscious experience functions as a streamlined, action-oriented interface model for behavioral coordination rather than as direct revelation of underlying neural or mental reality—for the coherence of first-person authority and for the attribution of mental states across discontinuous personal identities. Focus your analysis on clinical presentations of dissociative identity disorder, in which competing alters issue contradictory first-person reports about present experience and autobiographical memory despite controlling a single body. Rather than surveying all possible interpretations, take a definite position on whether this framework necessarily dissolves the special evidential status of introspection, or whether it can support a reconstructed, non-privileged form of self-knowledge that remains consistent with fallibilism; defend that position with explicit reasoning rather than leaving the options open. In addition, identify which feature of dissociative identity—discontinuous autobiographical memory or simultaneous competing claims to present consciousness—presents the sharper conceptual challenge to your view, and explain why.
