Hey, I'm writing a piece on privacy and tech for a UK science mag and I keep hitting this weird tension in how some data co-ops actually operate. There's this practice — fedarated learning — where your phone keeps all the raw data and only sends back model updates to a central server. The collective then publishes detailed tranparency reports showing exactly what statistical patterns the global model has learned, and they claim this is privacy-preserving because your personal bits never leave the device. At the same time they treat those extracted patterns as a commons, open for anyone to audit or reuse.

What philosophy of privacy does that setup actually presuppose? Is it just a control-based view — privacy means you retain possession of the raw bits? Or does it assume an epistemic view, that privacy is only violated when specific identifiable knowledge about you is acquired, not when aggregate patterns are extracted and shared? Could it also be read through the lens of contextual integrity — that flows are okay if they stay within the right context — given that the raw context (your phone) is preserved but the derived patterns circulate everywhere?

Reconstruct the implicit theory behind the practice. Say whether it's genuinely committed to one of these, or whether it's philosophically underdetermined so that several rival accounts could license the same design. Then tell me: if we replaced their implicit view with a straightforward non-interference account of privacy, what concrete changes would be needed in the transparency reports or the update protocols?

Write it in British English, please — I'm editing for London. (Sorry about any typos — writing this on my phone between intervews!)
