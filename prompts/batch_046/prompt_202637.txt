In my current novel, a ministerial statistician discovers that her department has quietly altered the sampling frame for a national employment survey—not fabricating numbers, but redefining “active labor force” so that long-term unemployed citizens vanish from the aggregate without any individual record being falsified. This led me to an Arendtian puzzle that seems to prove too much. (1) Arendt holds that politics depends on a shared factual reality composed of contingent, witnessed events; systematic denial of these particular facts destroys the common world that makes collective action possible. (2) She distinguishes these factual truths from rational truths, which are coercive and independent of testimony. (3) The statistical manipulation I describe does not deny any particular witnessed fact—each survey response remains accurate—nor does it alter a rational truth; it only reconfigures the aggregate frame. (4) Therefore, by Arendt’s own criteria, this is not a destructive lie but a permissible methodological revision. (5) Yet the practical effect is identical to reality-destruction: citizens and policymakers inhabit incommensurable worlds, and democratic deliberation collapses. (6) To save Arendt’s diagnostic power, one must extend her critique to cover frame-level manipulation; but then any routine revision of statistical methodology becomes a potential assault on shared reality. (7) Hence her account either misses the most dangerous modern form of political deception, or it proves too much by condemning ordinary administrative adjustment. I believe something slips between (4) and (5)—perhaps an unexamined assumption about the scale at which factual reality operates—but I cannot name it. Which step should be rejected, and what does rejecting it cost? Please indicate what argument or consideration would change your verdict, and note where your reasoning is most likely to fail.
