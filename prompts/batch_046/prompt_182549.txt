Student answer:
Carroll's dialogue demonstrates that if every rule of inference must be turned into an explicit premise, then deriving Z from A, B, and the hypothetical that links them requires a further premise authorizing that very derivation, producing an infinite regress. The structural-rules response, developed within Gentzen-style sequent calculus, solves this by distinguishing logical rules, which introduce logical constants, from structural rules such as weakening and contraction, which shape the derivation environment. In LK, modus ponens operates as a logical rule within the object-language framework, so its authority comes from being constitutive of the proof system rather than from being derived inside it. Therefore Achilles needs no infinite chain: the rule itself performs the linking that the Tortoise demands. This is essentially the same move made in Frege's Grundgesetze, where basic laws of logic are taken as self-evident and require no further proof. A critic might reply that Carroll's challenge applies just as well to structural rules, since we still apply them, but this misses the point because structural rules are not propositions we assert but conditions on the space of assertions, making the demand for a premise a category mistake. Hence the regress is genuinely blocked, and logical justification is shown to be system-relative rather than linearly infinite.
