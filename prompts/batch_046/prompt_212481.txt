Over the past eighteen months I have watched my father’s Alzheimer’s disease progressively dissolve the narrative continuity that made him recognizably himself—first his memory for recent events, then his recognition of family, and eventually the coherent first-person perspective that seemed to anchor his consciousness. This experience has given Schrödinger’s well-known Vedanta-inspired reflections on the unity of consciousness an urgency they never had for me before, but it has also made the standard popular interpretation of those reflections—namely, that individual minds are merely temporary manifestations of a single universal mind, so that death is an illusion of separation—feel both psychologically inadequate and philosophically thin. I want you to argue for a more rigorous reconstruction of Schrödinger’s monism: specifically, that consciousness should be regarded as ontologically singular in precisely the way that a quantum field is singular, and that what we experience as distinct personal minds are not parts or modes of that field in any substantive sense, but rather local indexical frames—functionally analogous to coordinate systems or perspectival origins—through which the singular field acquires structured, first-person accessibility.

On this view, the disappearance of my father’s personality is neither the destruction of an independent mental substance, as dualism would have it, nor a mystical reunion with the universal One, as sentimental Vedanta suggests, but the relaxation of an indexical constraint that was always derivative rather than ontologically basic. Please construct a detailed philosophical argument for this position, showing how it honors Schrödinger’s reluctance to postulate metaphysical entities beyond what physical theory requires, and explaining why it handles the asymmetry of first-person access and the problem of other minds more coherently than either Cartesian pluralism or the naive “all-is-one” reading. You should treat “indexical frame” as a defined technical notion internal to your argument rather than an appeal to an established specialized literature, and you should build the case from conceptual resources available in general philosophy of mind and Schrödinger’s own physicalist commitments, without relying on bibliographic references, citation lists, or texts that would require external verification.

In developing this argument, I am especially interested in whether this reconstructed monism can account for the phenomenological specificity of loss—why the erosion of one indexical frame feels like a tragedy rather than a neutral redistribution—without reintroducing the very substantial selves the view denies. If the position succeeds, it should clarify not only what it means for consciousness to be unified, but also what, if anything, survives the dissolution of the frames through which it was locally manifest.
