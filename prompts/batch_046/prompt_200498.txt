I have been trying, without much formal training, to understand whether "Western values" names anything coherent, or whether it is mainly a rhetorical device that collapses under scrutiny. The phrase is everywhere in debates about immigration, foreign intervention, and domestic culture, yet when I try to pin it down, the lists vary wildly: some speakers emphasize liberal democracy and individual rights; others stress Christian heritage, Enlightenment rationalism, or even specific gender norms; and still others treat it as synonymous with "modernity" or "the developed world." Many of these values are actively contested within Western societies—free speech means one thing in American constitutional law and another in European hate-speech regulation—while others, such as constitutional government or scientific inquiry, are hardly unique to the West.

Write an essay that takes up this puzzle. Examine whether "Western values" can be reconstructed as a meaningful category—perhaps through family resemblance, a shared historical genealogy, or a functional role in political argument—or whether the phrase is better understood as a concept whose power comes precisely from its vagueness and strategic flexibility. In particular, consider whether the category works descriptively, normatively, or primarily as an in-group boundary marker, and whether its internal contradictions—such as invoking universal human rights while using them to exclude non-Western claimants—render it useless or merely expose its strategic purpose. Do not treat the question as purely semantic: address whether recognizing the phrase's incoherence should change how we evaluate arguments that depend on it. Reach a clear verdict—either defend a reconstructed, defensible version of the category, or argue that it should be dropped from serious political and philosophical discourse—and justify that conclusion with specific reasoning.
