Compare Michael Walzer’s account of dirty hands in political life with Bernard Williams’s idea of moral remainder, focusing on a single institutional scenario: a democratically accountable official authorizes a genuinely necessary but rights-violating security measure and is subsequently exonerated by formal oversight mechanisms. For each view, determine whether the official retains a personal moral debt after exoneration, whether democratic accountability absorbs that debt or only displaces it onto the collective, and which account better explains why such a debt might persist despite public justification. Identify the structural feature of representative democracy that drives the divergence between the two frameworks.
