I am preparing the final session for a graduate seminar on the politics of collective memory, and I want to stage an adversarial collaboration between two interlocutors who have argued for months without ever producing a document they could both sign. One interlocutor defends a Margalit-inspired position: societies bear a non-optional duty to remember grave moral wrongs, not merely as historical record but as an ongoing ethical relation to victims, and institutional forgetting—whether through amnesty without acknowledgment, the erosion of archives, or the deliberate retirement of monuments—constitutes a second harm that severs solidarity. The other interlocutor defends a Rieff-inspired counter-position: collective memory is frequently weaponized, there is a genuine moral achievement in letting a society move past grievance, and an unchecked culture of remembrance risks freezing conflicts in permanent resentment rather than allowing the kind of forgetting that makes reconciliation and future cooperation possible.

Please draft the joint statement these two could both endorse after a good-faith adversarial collaboration. Structure it in four parts. First, a numbered section of agreed claims about the nature of collective memory, the conditions under which remembrance or forgetting actually occur, the distinction between individual and institutional obligations, and the limits of what psychological or technological intervention can reliably achieve. Second, a disagreements table in which each side’s view is phrased in wording its own proponents would accept, with precise formulations of where they part ways; include an explicit judgment about which disagreements are substantive and which dissolve as merely verbal once the terms are fixed—particularly around whether “forgetting” necessarily implies denial or can be compatible with prior acknowledgment. Third, identify the cruxes: for instance, whether the value of remembrance is intrinsic or instrumental; whether a society can have a duty to remember while lacking any corresponding right to enforce that duty through state power; and how to weigh the risk of cyclical violence against the risk of moral erasure. To avoid generic slogans, frame these cruxes specifically through the lens of mnemonic governance—the institutional and technological architecture that determines what a society remembers, including questions of whether mnemonic infrastructure should be state-centralized, distributed across civil-society bodies, or left to private and algorithmic actors—so that the debate is not only about whether to remember, but about who controls the conditions of remembrance. Fourth, for each crux, present a concrete institutional design or case study (such as a post-conflict state choosing between a permanently maintained, publicly accessible digital archive with generational replication mandates versus a sunset clause that removes public monuments after fifty years and restricts access to sealed records) that both sides agree would bear on the question, and record each side’s explicit prediction about what that design or case would demonstrate. Keep the formulations neutral enough that neither camp can dismiss the other’s wording as a straw man, and do not include bibliographies, citation lists, or requests for further reading.
