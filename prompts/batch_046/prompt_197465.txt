Write the opening remarks (approximately six minutes) for an interdisciplinary workshop titled Partial Truths and Disciplinary Perspectives. The audience is split: analytic philosophers who treat contradiction as a fatal flaw, and scholars of Jain philosophy who know that anekāntavāda is neither relativism nor contradiction-embracing mysticism, but a disciplined doctrine of many-sidedness. Your speech must welcome both groups without flattening their differences, and the philosophical framework should genuinely constrain what you can and cannot say. Structure the address through nayavāda—treating each disciplinary lens as a partial viewpoint (naya)—and syādvāda, using its conditional qualification to speak of other positions only in a qualified respect. Apply the seven-fold predication (saptabhaṅgī) not to metaphysical objects but to the workshop itself: each discipline’s account may be affirmed in one respect, denied in another, and left inexpressible from yet another standpoint, with no single account being exhaustive. This should prevent you from falling into the analytic caricature that Jain thought licenses contradiction, and also prevent the popular misconception that anekāntavāda means all views are equally true. The tone should be intellectually serious and welcoming, establishing a space where disagreement is precisely located rather than dissolved. Do not cite texts by page number, provide a bibliography, or name specific living scholars; let the conceptual architecture carry the argument.
