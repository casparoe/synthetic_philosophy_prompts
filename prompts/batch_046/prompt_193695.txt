Two colleagues are debating a proposal in their city to convert a preserved site of past mass violence into an ordinary public space after seventy-five years of mandatory memorialization. One argues that maintaining the site as an unaltered archive of suffering is the only ethically adequate response: gradual forgetting constitutes a second erasure of the victims, and future generations must encounter the past in its raw particularity rather than through sanitized abstraction. The other argues that perpetual preservation risks conscripting the dead into permanent service as moral exhibits, and that ritualized, collective forgetting—structured and acknowledged rather than denied—is a form of respect that returns dignity to victims rather than extending their suffering into eternity. The first replies that without direct archival presence, moral knowledge collapses into comfortable abstraction and the present is relieved of genuine burden. The second counters that the "duty to remember" often becomes a performative obligation on the living that substitutes vicarious suffering for present justice, and that maintaining perfect preservation consumes institutional resources that could address ongoing harm. They ask you to referee. Please go through their exchange step by step, rating each move from clearly mistaken to clearly right, identify who is more right overall, and note what both sides miss about the relationship between institutional memory and moral agency.
