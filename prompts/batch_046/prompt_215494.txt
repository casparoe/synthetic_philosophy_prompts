I am drafting a literary novel about the boundary between individual mind and shared experience, and I need a self-contained chapter that takes the form of a short story. The philosophical background is Erwin Schrödinger's Vedanta-influenced conviction that consciousness is not a multiplicity of private streams but one continuous awareness differentiated only by the limiting structures of the brain—what he articulated, in broad terms, as the illusion of plurality sustained by biological filtering rather than by any real ontological division.

To avoid a didactic allegory, I want the story to turn on a speculative, somewhat niche premise: during a clinical study of synchronized neural oscillation, a concert pianist improvises a melody that, within hours, surfaces unaltered in the hypnagogic visions of unrelated subjects across the same city. The narrative should treat this not as telepathy but as evidence that the melody was never composed in the private sense—rather, it was filtered through one nervous system and then, because the filter was temporarily transparent, received again by others. The central tension should be ethical and metaphysical: does the pianist own the melody, does she bear an obligation to suppress it if its recurrence causes distress, and does the concept of plagiarism still apply when all mental content originates in the same universal substrate?

Please write the story in a restrained, precise register—more Chekhov than cyberpunk—keeping the philosophy implicit in the characters' disorientation rather than explicit in dialogue. Avoid framing consciousness as information processing, computation, or quantum mechanics in the popular-science sense; present it instead as an undivided substance that brains sample, not generate. The story should end without resolving whether the shared melody is a discovery or an intrusion, leaving the reader with the same uncertainty the characters face about where one mind ends and another begins.
