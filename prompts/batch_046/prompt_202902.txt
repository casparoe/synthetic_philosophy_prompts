I'm writing about judicial review and democratic legitimacy, and a seminar argument has left me suspicious. The speaker introduced what she called the Transparency Condition: democratic legitimacy requires that citizens can recognize themselves as the authors of any binding decision that affects them. (1) Judicial review violates this, because citizens do not author a court's decision to invalidate a statute. (2) But the same holds for independent central banks, public-health agencies, and election-certification boards: citizens do not author their specific binding rules either. (3) So if the Transparency Condition is correct, democratic governance must either collapse into direct democracy on every issue or forfeit legitimacy entirely—an absurd result. (4) Therefore the condition is false, or it applies selectively. (5) Yet if it applies selectively, we need a non-arbitrary reason why courts violate it while expert agencies do not; appeals to rights-protection are substantive, not democratic. (6) So either democratic sovereignty routinely tolerates counter-majoritarian institutions, or the objection to judicial review must rest on something other than democratic authorship. Where does the reasoning fail? I'd rather keep (1)—there is a real democratic tension in letting unelected judges overrule legislatures—without being forced into (3).
