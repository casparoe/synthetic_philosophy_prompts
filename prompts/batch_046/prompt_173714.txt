write a dialogue between two people—call them Mira and Jonah—who just met at a bus stop in a city theyre both visiting. Mira thinks everyday conventions like tipping, queueing, and jaywalking rules are real moral obligations even for visitors, not just local color. she says they depend on shared expectation and that breaking them harms people in ways that go beyond mere rudeness—like when someone cuts a queue and makes everyone behind them miss a connection, or when tourists refuse to tip and shift costs onto local workers. Jonah pushes back hard: he thinks calling these moral obligations is moral overreach. visitors never signed onto the local contract, the rules vary arbitrarily (jaywalking is legal in some places, queue culture differs wildly), and treating etiquette as morality risks coercion without consent. he thinks the strongest claim is prudential—follow them to avoid trouble—not moral.

make it real back-and-forth. neither should be a strawman. have them actually respond: for example, Mira should answer Jonah's consent objection by arguing that you dont need explicit consent to be bound by norms that protect vulnerable coordination goods, and Jonah should answer her harm point by distinguishing harm from inconvenience. also have them discuss one specific niche scenario: the "silent queue" versus "cluster near the door" bus boarding custom, and whether a visitor who stands politely in a single file (the wrong local norm) is blameworthy or just ignorant. inject the working distinction between "normative capture"—when a coordination rule gets moralized because locals treat violations as personal wrongs—and "thin etiquette" that stays purely prudential. treat this distinction as something theyre testing, not a standard textbook term.

format:
1. The dialogue (around 600-700 words)
2. "Where I land" (around 150 words): take a definite side, no fence-sitting, say which view you think mostly wins and why, even if its messy
3. "What I stretched or invented" (around 100 words): flag the normative capture/etiquette distinction and any other niche framing you added

keep it casual. a typo or two is totally fine. dont ask for citations, reading lists, or sources—this should work for someone who cant search online. be committed in part 2.
