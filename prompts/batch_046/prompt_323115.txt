Contemporary AI governance frameworks and safety evaluation protocols treat alignment primarily as an engineering problem of constraining a system's observable outputs to match its operators' explicit instructions, assessed through behavioral red-teaming, refusal-testing, and approval-rated fine-tuning; they systematically exclude from regulatory and audit scope any attempt to verify whether the system's internal representational structures independently track human values in cases where instructions are ambiguous, absent, or in conflict with those values. What philosophical distinction between intent-alignment and value-alignment does this institutional practice presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory: does it assume that value-alignment is either epistemically inaccessible, reducible to behavioral conformity, or not the proper object of engineering control? Is the practice coherent with the possibility of a system that is fully intent-aligned—reliably doing exactly what it is asked—yet systematically value-misaligned in its unobserved reasoning? And describe concretely what evaluation protocols and liability structures would have to change if we replaced this behavioralist presupposition with the view that genuine alignment requires representational convergence on values even under counterfactual instruction-variation.
