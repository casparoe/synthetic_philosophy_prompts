I was discussing this with my grad students after seminar last week and we got stuck on something that feels like it should be obvious. There's this well-known legal doctrine — the "heat of passion" reduction in homicide law — where killing someone in sudden anger can drop the charge from murder to voluntary manslaughter, but only if the provocation was enough to make a "reasonable person" lose self-control. What's puzzling is that the law treats the emotional state as both a failure of rational agency that deserves some mitigation, and yet also as something you can be evaluated for not controlling properly, almost like a voluntary omission. Meanwhile, in negligence cases the same system says we punish the risk-creating behavior, not the internal feeling that accompanied it, which seems to pull in a completely different direction about whether emotions are part of moral agency or just background noise.

Then there's this newer institutional practice that's been spreading through corporate HR and some public-sector workplaces — they run periodic "emotional climate" audits where employees rate their affective states on standardized scales, and management uses the aggregate numbers to adjust workflows, meeting loads, even physical office conditions. The practice treats emotions as quantifiable environmental conditions, like temperature or noise, that can be optimized without reference to what particular people are actually thinking about.

So here's my actual question: what philosophical commitments about the nature of emotion do these two practices share, if they share any? Does the heat-of-passion doctrine presuppose that emotions are cognitive judgments with intentional content — like seeing an insult as warranting rage — or does it treat them as non-cognitive bodily disruptions that just happen to overwhelm us? Does the corporate audit presuppose a feeling-theory where emotions are private internal states, or something more like a social-performance view? Are the practices consistent with each other, or do they quietly depend on incompatible theories — maybe one cognitivist and one purely physiological? And if we replaced the implicit view with a constructivist account that treats emotions as socially performed roles rather than internal mental states, what exactly would have to change in both courtroom jury instructions and HR analytics protocols? I want you to reconstruct the implicit epistemology, tell me where it's genuinely underdetermined so that several theories could license the same practice, and say whether these two institutions are philosophically coherent or just borrowing language from seperate traditions without noticing the occurence of contradiction.
