Design a 90-minute structured simulation for an upper-level undergraduate philosophy seminar of about fifteen students, intended to make them feel the dynamics in Blaise Pascal’s treatment of justice and force—specifically, that justice without power is ineffectual, that power without justice is tyrannical, and that because justice is inherently disputable while force is unmistakable, people tend to treat enforced custom as if it were justice itself, yielding what Pascal describes as the mystical foundation of authority. Do not provide an opening lecture on Pascal; the idea must emerge from what the participants do.

Divide the group into twelve citizens, three rotating magistrates, and one chronicler. Give each citizen a private, irreconcilable conception of fair distribution (e.g., strict equality, need-based, merit-based, lottery-based), revealed only to that student. The magistrates alone may enforce allocations; citizens may propose distributive rules, but a rule becomes binding only by near-unanimous consent. Over three rounds—abundance, scarcity, crisis—symbolic payoffs accumulate: legitimacy points when enforced rules match a citizen’s private view, stability points whenever order is maintained regardless of content, and revolt points that a citizen can spend to coordinate refusal of a magistrate’s order. The hidden structural feature is that unanimous agreement becomes impossible after round one, because the private conceptions diverge irreducibly. Magistrates therefore always eventually govern by imposition. In round three, introduce a rule-change that benefits the magistrate’s allies; citizens will likely begin retroactively describing the imposed order as “fair” or “natural” in order to avoid the instability of revolt, even though their private conceptions have not changed.

Specify the materials (role cards, point-tracking sheets, a visible “enforcement token” only magistrates may move), the exact payoff matrix for each round, and the rotation schedule for magistrates. Include a 30-minute debrief that maps round one onto the impotence of pure justice, round two onto naked force, and round three onto the epistemic slide where enforced custom is mistaken for justice. Identify what will predictably go wrong—likely an early, uncoordinated rebellion in round two that collapses because citizens cannot see one another’s revolt points, followed by denial in round three that anyone retroactively justified the rules—and say exactly how the facilitator should respond. Conclude with an honest admission of what the simulation cannot reproduce: Pascal’s theological claim that only an indisputable divine justice can supply a ground for authority that is not merely enforced, and instruct the facilitator to supply that limit explicitly in the final five minutes. Do not ask for bibliographies, citation lists, or specific fragment numbers; the prompt should depend only on the conceptual structure described above.
