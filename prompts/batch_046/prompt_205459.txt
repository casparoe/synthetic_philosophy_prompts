After a long argument last night with a friend about whether the axiom of choice is a harmless bookkeeping convention or a genuinely non-constructive leap, I want my philosophy-of-mathematics seminar to feel that tension in their hands rather than hear me assert it. The setting is twelve graduate students, one ninety-minute session, with no lecture beforehand. Design a runnable in-class game — with explicit rules, physical materials, and a point-based payoff scheme requiring no real money — that forces participants to experience the gap between performing a selection locally and being unable to state the global rule that governs it.

Structure the activity in three phases they move through without knowing the formal target. Phase One, Local Comfort: pairs of envelopes containing distinguishable cards; selectors earn points only if they can articulate in writing the rule they used to pick exactly one card from each pair. Phase Two, Scaling with Loss of Orientation: the same number of pairs, but now every card is blank and structurally identical, and the physical orientation within each envelope is randomized so no invariant property distinguishes the members; participants must still physically place one card from each pair onto a central board, but they will find they can no longer write a non-arbitrary selection rule. Phase Three, The Contract: the facilitator announces that the task has been scaled to a hypothetical infinite family of such pairs; each participant silently records whether they believe a complete global selection exists despite the absence of any articulable uniform rule. Those who answer yes sign a collective contract and retain all points earned in Phases One and Two; those who answer no forfeit half their points but receive a skepticism token. Only after the contract is signed do you name the idea: the axiom asserts precisely this kind of existence without a definable selector.

Then provide the debrief: map Phase One to constructive choice functions with explicit rules, Phase Two to the failure of selection invariants across equivalence classes, and Phase Three to the non-constructive existence claim. Specify the payoff scheme in detail — for example, ten points for an articulated rule in Phase One, five points for successful physical selection in Phase Two, and the doubling or halving in Phase Three based on the vote. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — for instance, a student may propose using the envelope's physical position as a hidden ordering, or the group may fracture between pragmatists who say we can simply perform the selection and skeptics who deny the infinite extension before they have fully experienced Phase Two — and exactly how to handle each failure without breaking the experiential arc. Finally, be explicit about which feature of actual infinite choice the physical game necessarily cannot reproduce — specifically, the literal uncountable infinity and the dependency on well-ordering for non-countable cases — and state precisely what I must supply verbally in the debrief to cover that gap. Keep the idea embedded in the rules, roles, and stakes; I should not lecture or name the axiom until after the contract is signed.
