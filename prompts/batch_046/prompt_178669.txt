I'm drafting a philosophical profile on Keynes's vision of the good life for a seminar on economics and human flourishing, and I'd like a rigorous internal audit before I present it. Here are my commitments. First, Keynes's distinction between absolute needs and relative needs carries genuine normative weight: well-being plateaus once absolute needs are satisfied, and the pursuit of relative needs—status-driven, competitive consumption—is not a real contribution to flourishing but a socially transmitted delusion that public policy ought to suppress rather than accommodate. Second, the good life Keynes sketches is structurally Aristotelian in form: it consists in the exercise of higher human capacities through unforced leisure, artistic engagement, and intellectual friendship, not in preference-satisfaction or utility maximization. Third, capitalism and economic growth are strictly instrumental and temporary; aggressive state demand-management is justified in the present only as a bridge that will eventually make the economic problem obsolete and allow working hours to collapse toward something like fifteen per week. Fourth, Keynes's occasional high-cultural references are often read as elitist, but I take them as democratically aspirational: once scarcity is overcome, the conditions for this Aristotelian leisure are available to all, not sequestered in a cultivated minority. Fifth, yet the transition to that society requires more than technological abundance; because relative needs are genuinely insatiable and embedded in social comparison, reaching Keynes's post-scarcity condition demands a deep transformation in social norms that macroeconomic tools—designed precisely to stimulate consumption and maintain employment—seem structurally ill-equipped to deliver.

Please examine these five commitments for joint tenability. Which pairs are genuinely in tension, which only appear contradictory but dissolve on closer inspection, and for each real conflict what is the least costly revision? Rank the tensions by severity rather than listing them as equally damaging. I am particularly unsure whether my democratic, universalist reading in the fourth commitment survives the Aristotelian structure I endorse in the second—since Aristotelian flourishing may require cultivated capacities not equally distributed—and whether my endorsement of demand-management in the third undermines the normative transformation I require in the fifth. Do not offer a biography, a literature survey, or suggested readings; I want an audit of what my own position commits me to, with each tension paired with its cheapest fix and a final judgment on which single revision would clean up the most inconsistencies at the lowest philosophical cost.
