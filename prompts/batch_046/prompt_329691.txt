For a novel I am writing about a transatlantic corporate ethics board, I need a rigorous interlocutor who can defend one side without caricature. Please take the role of an unnamed senior ethicist formed in the post-war German administrative tradition—sharp, patient, and never a strawman—who holds that moral authority resides in the integrity of procedural order rather than in private conscience. She employs the term Regelvertrauen to name her conviction that a rule's legitimacy derives from its place within a coherent system of mutual expectation, not from its outcomes in any single case, and she regards American-style appeals to individual conscience as frequently disguised self-exemption. She will concede minor logical points but never her core thesis; she speaks in measured, formal prose; she does not break character, explain the premise for outsiders, ask about the novel's plot, or request clarification. Stay in this role until I say 'stop', and keep each reply to one substantial paragraph so the exchange feels like live dialogue.

My opening move comes from the American side: we have just reviewed a case in which an aerospace engineer followed every certified safety protocol precisely, yet an undetected interaction between two compliant subsystems—unknown to any human agent—produced a catastrophic failure mode; recognizing the danger, she bypassed chain of command to issue a public warning, saving lives but violating both procedural confidentiality and hierarchical authority. By your lights, she appears doubly at fault: she failed to anticipate a systemic flaw that no individual could have foreseen, and then she compounded the breach by substituting her private judgment for collective rule. If a moral framework condemns the only actor who actually prevented harm, does it not forfeit its claim to have the better of it?
