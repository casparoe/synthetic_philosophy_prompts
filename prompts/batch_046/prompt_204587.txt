I am drafting a short essay for my philosophy of mind seminar on what I call the asymmetry of phenomenal tracking within epiphenomenalism. The familiar paradox holds that if phenomenal properties are causally inert, then judgments about them are produced only by neural correlates, which threatens to make such judgments inexplicable or unreliable. Most treatments collapse first-person and third-person cases, but I want to drive a wedge between them. In third-person attribution—say, judging that another subject feels pain—the physical cause of my judgment is indeed only a state in my own brain, and the subject’s phenomenal state plays no causal role; this is consistent with epiphenomenalism and not especially puzzling, since I lack direct access in any case. But in first-person judgment—my belief that I am now experiencing redness—the physical cause is, ex hypothesi, not the phenomenal redness itself, which is causally inert. So some neural state N causes my belief, yet N is distinct from the redness. For the judgment to be non-accidental, N must be systematically correlated with the redness; but this correlation is causal in neither direction, since redness does not cause N and N does not cause redness (given the causal closure of the physical). The result is a brute coincidence, or an external explanation that epiphenomenalism cannot supply without abandoning closure. I therefore think the first-person case generates a distinct problem—call it correlation-without-causation—that is more severe than the third-person version. I am unsure whether this is merely the zombie argument in different clothing, and I worry I may be implicitly assuming some form of direct acquaintance that an epiphenomenalist must reject. Could you comment on whether the asymmetry holds, whether the correlation problem is genuinely separate from the general causal-exclusion worry, and whether there is an obvious physicalist reply I have missed? I would especially value feedback on whether my framing accidentally smuggles in an epistemic commitment that undermines the very position I am trying to assess.
