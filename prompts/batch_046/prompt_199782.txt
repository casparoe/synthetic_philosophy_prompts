At our school’s philosophy club meeting last Thursday, two seniors—I'll call them Maya and Leo—got into a sustained disagreement about whether cross-cultural differences in folk morality undermine moral objectivity. I was only listening and did not take a side. I would like a point-by-point adjudication of their exchange.

Here is the back-and-forth as I recall it.

Maya began by observing that research comparing WEIRD populations with small-scale societies reveals systematic divergence in moral emphasis: Western subjects tend to center harm and fairness, whereas many other groups treat loyalty, authority, and purity violations as equally fundamental. She argued that if moral truths existed independently of human psychology, we should expect broader convergence, not patterns so clearly shaped by ecology and social structure. She concluded that cultural learning explains the data far better than discovery of pre-existing moral facts.

Leo replied that divergence in which norms are emphasized need not imply divergence in underlying moral structure. He proposed what he called “moral parameterization”: human moral cognition may share a common structural framework—something like a capacity to weigh competing considerations against one another—but cultures set different parameter weights based on institutional needs. On this view, a society that emphasizes purity is not denying harm-based reasons; it is assigning different weights within the same framework. Therefore, the variation is superficial rather than deep.

Maya pushed back that this move saves moral realism only by rendering it unfalsifiable. If any observed cross-cultural difference can be redescribed as parameter adjustment, then no pattern of data could ever genuinely threaten realism. She asked Leo what specific finding would actually count against his view, suggesting that without a clear answer the parameterization strategy looks ad hoc.

Leo then shifted ground. He noted that in some non-WEIRD communities, moral obligations extend to ancestors, spirits, or ecosystems in ways that WEIRD subjects often find conceptually alien—not merely weighted differently, but structured around a different ontology of who or what counts as a moral patient. He argued that this is not parameter variation within a shared framework, but divergence in the basic metaphysics of moral status. If moral realism requires agreement about the domain of moral patients, then such ontological divergence genuinely threatens it.

Maya responded that even ontology divergence can be parameterized: beliefs about ancestors or ecosystems depend on metaphysical commitments about personhood and agency, not on independent moral facts. Therefore, moral realism can be preserved for a thinner structural core—perhaps the idea that moral reasoning involves giving and asking for justifications—while allowing thick moral content to vary with metaphysical context. But she added that this makes realism so thin it loses practical normative force.

Leo answered that Maya’s “thin core” is either trivial or secretly substantive. If the core is just “giving reasons,” then every culture qualifies by definition, and realism becomes empty. If the core includes specific structural constraints—say, that reasons must be universalizable—then those constraints themselves are contested across traditions, and we are back to divergence.

They asked me to referee. Please adjudicate the exchange move by move: rate each of Maya’s and Leo’s arguments from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with brief justification for each rating. Then say who is more right overall, and identify what both parties are missing—perhaps a distinction, an objection, or a middle position that neither raised. Do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; I need reasoning that stands on its own. Finally, please say explicitly what would change your mind: which specific move in this exchange is most vulnerable to being overturned, and what argument or empirical finding would lead you to reverse your assessment of that move.
