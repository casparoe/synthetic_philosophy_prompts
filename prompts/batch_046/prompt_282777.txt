Having recently retired from experimental physics and begun reading in political philosophy, I am struck by an institutional practice in international humanitarian reporting. When mass starvation is officially recorded—whether in Bengal in 1943, during the Great Leap Forward, or in contemporary crises—the event is classified either as a "natural" famine (triggered by drought, flood, or crop failure) or as a "man-made" or "political" famine (triggered by direct government policy, war, or deliberate blockade). This binary governs which aid protocols are activated and, more importantly, whether the event is framed as a tragedy warranting charitable relief or as a crime warranting accountability.

What philosophical commitments about causation, agency, and moral responsibility does this classificatory practice presuppose? Does it rest on a Humean view that separates natural and human causal streams, or could a counterfactual, structural account of causation preserve the distinction? Given Amartya Sen's argument that the Bengal famine, the Irish famine, and the Great Leap Forward all occurred despite adequate aggregate food availability—through collapses in entitlement and distribution—does the practice implicitly reject an entitlement-based theory of famine, or does it apply a different, perhaps intentionalist, criterion for assigning moral agency?

Specifically, does the practice assume that harm amounts to a political crime only when a specific, intentional agent directly produces it, thereby excluding structural negligence, institutional failure, or distributed collective action? And is that assumption consistent with the same institutions' willingness to assign collective responsibility for environmental degradation or economic inequality?

Please reconstruct the implicit philosophical theory that renders this classification coherent, distinguish what it strictly requires from what merely accompanies it, and indicate what would have to change in the classification system if its underlying theory of causation and responsibility were replaced by a structural or entitlement-based framework.
