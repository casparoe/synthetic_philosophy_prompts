I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy background, and after a recent discussion about reward functions in reinforcement learning I started thinking about hedonism—the view that pleasure is the only intrinsic good. I keep hitting what philosophers call the heterogeneity problem: the pleasures I experience don't seem to share any obvious common quality, yet I still group them together. I want to know whether there's a coherent principle behind my judgments, or whether I have to drop some.

Here are my verdicts. Eating an excellent mango and finally resolving a stubborn race condition after days of debugging both feel like unmistakable pleasures, but they share no sensory texture—one is sweet and cool, the other is a mental relief or sudden clarity. So I reject the idea that pleasure is just one specific feeling. The intense rush from a well-designed video-game reward loop—bright feedback, a chime, a level-up—is clearly a pleasure, but it feels thinner and more like a hijacked signal compared to the calm satisfaction of slowly reading a poem. Still, I don't want to call the game rush fake; it's real but different. Schadenfreude also counts: when a competitor's deployment fails publicly I feel a warm satisfaction. That makes me doubt pleasure can be defined purely by a functional role like "reward that reinforces behavior," since this reinforces something ugly. The flow state—writing code for hours without self-consciousness—feels good, yet it lacks any bright phenomenal spike; it's more like the absence of friction. I still think it's pleasure, which puts pressure on any view that requires a positive sensation. Anticipating a vacation feels pleasant in a diffuse, future-oriented way, while being on vacation produces sharper moments; they seem related but not identical. Finally, there's the quiet cognitive pleasure of seeing elegant architecture, whether a bridge or a well-structured API—calm, aesthetic, unlike bodily enjoyment, but I resist reducing it to mere appreciation.

My tentative pattern is that pleasures are genuinely heterogeneous in quality and intensity; some are morally contaminated but still pleasures; flow counts despite lacking intense phenomenal character; anticipation and consumption are both pleasures but not the same kind. Is there one account—family resemblance, a functional theory with normative constraints, or something about desire-satisfaction—that saves all six? Or must I overturn one? I'm especially torn about whether the game-rush and flow-state can both be captured if pleasure is just "positive reward signal," since that seems to erase the moral and qualitative distinctions I care about.

Please state the principle you think best captures my verdicts, explain which case motivates each clause, and tell me what it predicts for a case I haven't mentioned—say, the pleasure of solving a purely abstract puzzle with no practical value. Most importantly, be explicit about where your reasoning is most likely to fail and what specific argument or evidence would change your mind.
