Design a 45-minute final-exam essay prompt for an upper-level philosophy-of-mind seminar, together with a model answer and a marking scheme, on Dennett’s framework of the self as a center of narrative gravity. Do not ask for bibliographies, citations, or reading lists. To prevent a mere summary, embed this concrete hypothetical: a patient with dense anterograde amnesia writes a brief first-person journal entry after every waking episode; over decades the entries cohere into a single autobiography, yet she retains no episodic memory of writing them and reports no phenomenological continuity with the prior author, while caregivers treat the accumulated text as authoritative for her values and preferences. The essay should ask students to do three things: (a) apply the center-of-narrative-gravity metaphor to determine whether the case contains one self, several selves, or no self, and whether phenomenological continuity is necessary for the center to persist if textual coherence can substitute for memory; (b) contrast this narrative-construct view with a Cartesian-theater view, specifying exactly where the two diverge on the ontological status of the journal-author; and (c) evaluate whether telling the patient “you are the thread in the journal” gives her a genuine identity or only a useful fiction, and what that distinction implies for moral responsibility if she later acts against an entry she does not remember writing. Provide a model answer that makes the required moves—treating the self as an abstractum analogous to a center of mass, allowing narrative coherence to sustain identity without episodic memory, distinguishing explanatory utility from ontological reality, and showing why the Cartesian theater requires a single continuous observer that the case undermines—plus a marking scheme that assigns weights (for example 30% for part a, 40% for part b, 30% for part c) and lists specific mistakes that should cost marks, such as conflating narrative coherence with episodic memory, reading Dennett as eliminativist about all selves rather than about the Cartesian self, or ignoring the moral-responsibility implication in part c. In your response, lead with the exam question itself in the first sentence, then present the model answer and marking scheme immediately after; include no preamble, no restatement of this assignment, and no commentary outside the three parts.
