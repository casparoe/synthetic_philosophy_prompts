As a journalist who covers decision theory and its intersection with public policy, I've been observing a persistent stalemate in how economists, philosophers, and forecasting practitioners apply the agreement result associated with Robert Aumann to real-world expert disagreement—particularly in domains like climate risk and long-term technological forecasting. For years, one camp has maintained that persistent divergence among apparently rational experts demonstrates a failure of common priors, or hidden biases that prevent belief convergence; the opposing camp insists the formal result is largely irrelevant outside its axioms, because common knowledge is institutionally impossible in adversarial or fragmented epistemic environments. Both sides command the same mathematics, agree that rational agents with genuinely shared starting points should eventually converge, and even share normative commitments to epistemic humility. Yet the debate has not advanced in any discernible direction, and attempts to resolve it through better data or clearer modeling have consistently failed.

My working hypothesis is that this is not a dispute about formal results at all, but a layered disagreement masked by shared vocabulary: one faction treats common prior as an elicitable psychological state—something that can be reconstructed through betting mechanisms or revealed preference—while the other treats it as a structural feature of shared institutional framing that collapses once you admit ontological divergence about what the predictive models are actually tracking. In climate forecasting, for instance, the disagreement may not be about probabilities assigned to tipping points, but about whether such points exist as discrete causal nodes or are merely continuous statistical artifacts—an ontological prior that never surfaces in the formalism because both sides assume they are arguing over the same world-model. Is this the correct diagnosis? Does the impasse reflect a hidden empirical question about human cognition and belief-elicitation, a verbal disagreement about the scope of prior and common knowledge, a difference about what counts as a moral or methodological reason to converge, or a bedrock conflict about whether rationality requires shared ontology? And given your diagnosis, what would actually move the debate—an extension of the agreement result to heterogeneous ontologies, an empirical study of expert reasoning under adversarial conditions, or a reconceptualization of the question that abandons belief convergence as the right normative target altogether?
