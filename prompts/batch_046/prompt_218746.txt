Having recently left a career in experimental physics to study philosophy in earnest, I find Plato’s normative argument in the Laws—that the legislator can treat regulated drinking as a calibrated exercise for the soul, using controlled inebriation to reveal hidden temperaments and train virtue—strangely reminiscent of an empirical hypothesis dressed in ethical language. Write a deadpan satirical piece of roughly 600 words, formatted as a mock modern institutional review board protocol for a proposed multi-site study entitled Controlled Symposia as Character Gymnastics. The voice should be that of a dry, bureaucratic science agency: risk matrices, inclusion and exclusion criteria, adverse-event reporting thresholds, and dose-escalation tables, all treating the Athenian Stranger’s claim that wine functions as a remedy and poison for the soul and that unsupervised drinking is mere disorder as if it were a testable intervention. The satire should exaggerate the actual features of Plato’s position—his comparison of alcohol consumption to physical training, his confidence that dosage can be precisely legislated, and his view that moral failure under influence is a predictable, character-revealing variable—without attributing to him claims he never makes. Invent for the protocol a quantitative metric, such as an epistemic disinhibition coefficient, that researchers propose to measure, and include a sidebar on informed consent that frames temporary loss of rational control as a necessary diagnostic exposure. The target is Plato’s optimistic regulatory science of alcohol, not any living individual. Because this parody borrows from an ancient text, please append a brief explicit note identifying where the piece takes liberties with Plato’s arguments or depends on a contested interpretation of the Laws. Finally, state plainly what empirical finding about alcohol’s effects on moral reasoning, impulse control, or cognitive consistency would cause the analogy between drinking and physical training to collapse, and thereby change your assessment of whether the satire is fair rather than a misreading.
