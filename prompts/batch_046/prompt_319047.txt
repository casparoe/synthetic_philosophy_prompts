I have been trying to formalise an ethical objection to Russellian panpsychism that turns on what I call the decoherence-suffering thesis. The argument runs as follows.

(1) Russellian panpsychism identifies the intrinsic nature of fundamental physical properties with phenomenal properties.
(2) If phenomenal properties are intrinsic to quantum systems, then any interaction that alters the quantum state—such as decoherence—alters the phenomenal state of the system involved.
(3) Not all phenomenal alterations are neutral: valence panpsychism holds that fundamental phenomenal states include minimal positive or negative affect, so decoherence can constitute a minimal suffering.
(4) The principle of causal transmission of moral responsibility applies across physical scales: if an agent's action initiates a causal chain that results in suffering, and no intervening agent breaks the chain, the initiator bears proportional responsibility.
(5) Human behaviour constantly initiates causal chains that eventually involve quantum-level decoherence (e.g., through thermal radiation, measurement apparatus, or biological metabolism interacting with the environment).
(6) Therefore, every human action initiates an astronomical number of minimal sufferings at the fundamental level, and the agent bears responsibility for them.
(7) If (6) is true, then almost no action is morally permissible, because the cumulative harm dwarfs any local benefit.
(8) But it is false that almost no action is permissible.
(9) Hence, Russellian panpsychism, combined with valence panpsychism and the transmission principle, is false.

This seems to prove far more than I want: it threatens not just panpsychism but any view that grants fundamental phenomenal properties, and it seems to derive moral paralysis from physics. I do not want to reject (8). I am also reluctant to reject (4), since it looks like a standard extension of moral responsibility. Which premise should I abandon? Is the flaw an equivocation between 'phenomenal alteration' and 'suffering of a subject' in step (3), a modal or scope error in applying responsibility across ontological levels in (4), or does the argument collapse because quantum decoherence is not the right kind of interaction to alter intrinsic phenomenal nature? I want you to diagnose the weakest link, explain why it looked plausible, and then say what each of the three main responses—giving up fundamental valence, rejecting cross-scale moral transmission, or denying that decoherence alters intrinsic phenomenal properties—costs philosophically. Do not give me a bibliography; just analyse the argument and, if you can, say whether this kind of ethical reductio is something the panpsychist can answer by appealing to the combination problem, or whether that reply misses the point.
