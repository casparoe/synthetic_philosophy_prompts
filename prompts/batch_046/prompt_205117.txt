I'm taking a graduate seminar on moral change and have been struggling to adjudicate between Bernard Williams's claim that norms like slavery and dueling collapse when the social conditions sustaining their rationality—especially honor-based status economies—disintegrate, and Kwame Anthony Appiah's competing emphasis on moral learning, collective reasoning, and the internal critique of practices. For my final paper, I need an essay that weighs whether moral revolutions are best explained by shifts in material interests, by the dissolution of honor codes, or by genuinely persuasive moral arguments, reaching and defending a specific conclusion rather than ending on an agnostic or purely summary note.

To prevent the analysis from becoming a generic recitation of familiar historical narratives, the essay should treat both the standard cases—slavery and dueling—and a constructed analog designed to test the mechanisms with precision: imagine a nineteenth-century scientific or craft guild in which every member is obligated to answer any public insult to their professional competence with a formal, ritualized rebuttal, where refusal means irreversible social death, but where the sudden introduction of anonymous, printed peer evaluation makes public honor-defense appear not merely risky but conceptually incoherent. Apply the Williams and Appiah frameworks to this analog, and evaluate whether there is a distinct third mechanism—reflexive honor collapse, in which a practice dies not because honor itself vanishes, but because practitioners develop a second-order awareness that honoring the practice undermines the very status it was meant to secure. Please write the essay in American English, arguing for one of the three explanations (interests, honor-dissolution, or moral argument) or for a hybrid account, with particular attention to whether the guild analog reveals a gap in both Williams and Appiah; the conclusion should be explicit, substantive, and justified.
