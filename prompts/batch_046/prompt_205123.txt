Please provide critical comments on the following essay fragment.

The fragment argues that standard Western definitions of corruption—those that treat any exchange of favors for private gain within public office as illegitimate—presume a normative separation between personal loyalty and institutional duty that is not universal. Drawing on the distinction between clientelism and gift-giving, the author proposes that corruption should be analyzed along a reciprocity gradient: at one pole, transparent, immediate exchange with explicit quid pro quo (instrumental corruption); at the other, diffuse, long-term obligations that bind individuals into networks of mutual aid without explicit contractual terms (relational obligation). The claim is that many practices condemned as corruption in Western audit frameworks—such as a civil servant prioritizing a cousin's application or a politician distributing resources to kin networks—sit closer to the relational-obligation pole, functioning as mechanisms of social insurance in contexts where formal welfare institutions are weak or absent.

The author then introduces a hypothetical case: in a municipal administration where salaries are irregular and state pensions unreliable, an official who directs a public contract toward a family member is not necessarily extracting private rent; she may be fulfilling a culturally enforced duty of redistribution that, if violated, would expose her family to destitution. The argument concludes that labeling such acts corruption without attending to the reciprocity gradient risks imposing a universalist ethics that mistakes institutional failure for moral failure.

Please comment on the following: (1) Is the reciprocity-gradient distinction conceptually coherent, or does it collapse into a relativist excuse for nepotism? (2) Are there well-documented anthropological or sociological considerations that would challenge the author's framing of clientelism as social insurance rather than extraction? (3) Does the argument implicitly rely on a functionalist assumption—that practices serving social cohesion are thereby justified—that needs defense? I am particularly interested in whether the distinction between relational obligation and instrumental corruption can be maintained without smuggling in Western assumptions about the proper boundary between public and private spheres.
