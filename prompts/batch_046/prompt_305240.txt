I have drafted notes for an essay that extends Kierkegaard’s critique of “the public” and the press—developed primarily in his 1846 review A Literary Review—into a diagnosis of contemporary algorithmic media. The first part reconstructs Kierkegaard’s claim that the press generates a phantom collective, the public, which holds opinions without owning them: numerical aggregation replaces individual risk with anonymous participation, producing what he calls leveling. The second part proposes an analogy to engagement-optimized recommendation systems. I argue that these systems do not merely create echo chambers but instantiate a modern form of leveling: they construct an abstract, statistically inferred audience—the feed—whose inferred preferences are then presented back to users as normative public opinion. The third part introduces a distinction I call upward versus downward leveling. For Kierkegaard, the press leveled upward by allowing every reader to imagine themselves a participant in universal discourse; algorithmic leveling, I suggest, levels downward by fragmenting that universal into personalized niches that still feel publicly authoritative because they are quantified, visible, and continuously updated. From this I derive a problem for Kierkegaard’s remedy: if becoming an individual requires passionate, risk-laden commitment to a particular vocation, then the downward-leveled environment does not tempt users toward false universality but rather dissolves the very concept of a shared normative space in which such commitment could be recognized. I ask whether this undermines his remedy or transforms its conditions of application.

Please comment on these notes with particular attention to the following. First, is my reading of Kierkegaard’s concepts of the public and leveling accurate, or do I conflate them with his separate critique of the crowd? Second, does the analogy to algorithmic recommendation hold, or does the personalization mechanism actually break the parallel rather than merely modifying it? Third, is the distinction between upward and downward leveling conceptually substantial, or is it only a rhetorical device? Fourth, have I missed a more interesting implication—especially in relation to Kierkegaard’s observation that the press makes everything interesting while draining it of importance, and whether infinite-scroll interfaces reproduce this dynamic in a structurally different way? I am looking for conceptual criticism and suggestions for sharpening the argument; I do not want bibliographic references, reading lists, or citations.
