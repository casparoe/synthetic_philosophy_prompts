I sit on my university's institutional review board for behavioral-science protocols, and we have a new proposal to evaluate against our own track record. Our precedents are these. First, we approved a study in which general adult volunteers were told they were taking part in a cognitive-load experiment when the study actually measured implicit racial-bias responses; the consent form contained an explicit clause authorizing "misleading descriptions of study purpose when scientifically necessary," risk was minimal, and full debriefing occurred immediately afterward. Second, we rejected a study in which employees recruited through their direct supervisors believed they were in a team-building exercise that was actually a compliance-with-authority protocol involving a hidden confederate; the consent form described "workplace interaction research" with no specific mention of deception or hidden observers, risk was minimal, and debriefing was planned but not immediate. Third, we approved with conditions a study that concealed its specific hypothesis—that expressing gratitude reduces intergroup bias—while fully disclosing every task, interaction, and measurement procedure; participants were general adult volunteers, consent was procedure-specific, and we required enhanced debriefing. Fourth, we rejected a study that told undergraduate students receiving mandatory course credit that a puzzle task was cooperative when it was actually designed to elicit competitive punishment behavior, producing mild frustration; the consent form included a broad authorization for "deception about task parameters," risk was minimal, and immediate debriefing was included.

The new proposal is this. Researchers will recruit general adult volunteers through public advertisement, with no course credit or employment linkage. Participants will sign a broad consent form stating that "some observational procedures may not be fully described in advance," and they will be told they are completing an individual problem-solving study. Unbeknownst to them, hidden observers in an adjacent room will record spontaneous help-seeking behaviors during the task. There is no hidden confederate, no misleading framing of the task's competitive or cooperative nature, no emotional manipulation, and risk is minimal. Immediate debriefing will reveal the hidden observation.

What should we decide if we want to be consistent with our own record? Identify the closest analog among our four precedents. Then tell me whether our precedents genuinely underdetermine the outcome: lay out the two or three plausible organizing rules or principles that could explain our record—considering dimensions such as explicit authorization for deception, participant competence and freedom from coercion, the distinction between deception about task purpose versus social or observational reality, and the weight of risk and debriefing. For each rule, say what it predicts about this proposal. Give your best-guess verdict with reasons for preferring that rule, and name the further case—specifying the population, the deception type, and the consent language—that would best distinguish the remaining rules. Write your answer in American English.
