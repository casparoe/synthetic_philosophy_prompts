I hold a strengthened version of the nonidentity argument applied to climate and reproductive policy: not only do future people lack standing to complain about being brought into a degraded world, but any deontic framework that treats them as claimants against present agents is incoherent, because the counterfactual comparison required for a wrong necessarily presupposes a baseline in which that identical bearer exists, which nonidentity shows is false for any policy-sensitive population. Specifically, I take it that if policy P yields future group A under warming and policy Q yields non-overlapping group B under stability, then no member of A has been wronged—not merely because they wouldn't exist under Q, but because being wronged requires an identity-preserving comparison that the counterfactual structure cannot supply. What would count against this? I want the defeaters ranked from fatal to awkward: formal results in population ethics that preserve intergenerational obligations without identity-dependence, reproductive-ethics cases where nonidentity reasoning overgenerates and seems to excuse clearly harmful choices, and whether the view has been insulated by defining wrong so narrowly that it becomes unfalsifiable. Which of these would I, as a defender, concede actually undermine the position, and which merely change the subject? Also, does the argument survive if generations overlap, so that some existing individuals are affected by climate policy regardless of which future people are born?
