I’ve been turning over a chain of reasoning about naturalism in the philosophy of medicine—specifically the Boorse-style view that disease is dysfunction relative to a species-typical reference class—and it has the shape of one of those rationalist-forum reductios where every premise looks harmless until the conclusion suddenly collapses the distinction between aging and pathology. Here is the argument:

(1) On a pure naturalist account, a condition C is a disease if and only if C involves a failure of a biologically designed mechanism, where “failure” is measured against a reference class (same species, sex, and age).

(2) The lens’s accommodative mechanism is biologically designed to change focal distance, but evolutionary selection pressure on this mechanism operates primarily during reproductive years.

(3) Presbyopia—age-related loss of near-focus—is a failure of that mechanism, yet it is statistically universal in humans over age 45.

(4) Because presbyopia is universal in its age-appropriate reference class, it is statistically normal for that class; by (1), it is therefore not a disease.

(5) Alzheimer’s disease involves neuronal dysfunction that is prevalent but not universal in humans over 85 (prevalence exceeds 30%), and the brain’s cognitive mechanisms are likewise under weak post-reproductive selection.

(6) If the naturalist excludes presbyopia solely because it is statistically normal in its reference class, consistency demands excluding Alzheimer’s whenever it reaches comparable prevalence in a finely age-stratified class.

(7) To avoid this, the naturalist might split the reference class into “healthy 85-year-olds” versus “all 85-year-olds,” but “healthy” is defined by the absence of disease, making the partition circular.

(8) Alternatively, the naturalist might invoke Wakefield’s functional etiology—claiming that the brain’s mechanisms have a deeper evolutionary etiology tied to social coordination and survival, whereas lens accommodation lacks comparable cross-domain integration—so Alzheimer’s is a true dysfunction and presbyopia is not.

(9) But multi-level selection theory does not privilege post-reproductive brain maintenance over post-reproductive lens flexibility; neither is strongly selected for, so appealing to “deeper etiology” seems to import a normative judgment about which capacities are important to human flourishing rather than describing a biological design fact.

(10) Therefore, naturalism either collapses into treating Alzheimer’s as non-diseased (absurd) or must smuggle in normativism through the back door of reference-class selection and functional-etiology weighting—thereby abandoning pure descriptivism.
