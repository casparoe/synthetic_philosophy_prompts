I am a high school junior writing for an advanced civics and philosophy seminar, and I would like you to act as a professional political philosopher who specializes in democratic theory and the ethics of membership. Before you begin, please take a moment to brainstorm the core arguments about the boundary problem—especially the tension between territorial citizenship, the all-affected-interests principle, and intergenerational justice—then think carefully and reason step by step.

Write an essay on the following controversy. Imagine a hypothetical “watershed democracy” in which political jurisdiction is drawn along shared river basins rather than national borders. Downstream citizens vote on industrial policies that severely pollute the water supply of upstream communities, yet those upstream residents are not formal citizens and have no vote. Meanwhile, these same polities honor long-dead “founding ancestors” as constitutive members of their constitutional identity—granting them symbolic representation in foundational charters and civic rituals—while they systematically exclude future generations who will inherit the polluted watersheds.

Weigh the arguments for resolving this boundary problem through one of two consistent stakeholder models: (a) stripping away ancestral symbolic membership and restricting the demos strictly to current resident citizens within the watershed boundaries; or (b) extending full democratic membership to all affected living and future stakeholders across the basin, including non-citizen upstream residents and unborn generations represented through institutional proxy mechanisms. Reach and justify a definite conclusion about which boundary principle should govern this demos, and explain whether consistency demands that we abandon the reverence for constitutional ancestors if we refuse to include affected outsiders and future people. Do not end on a non-committal note; defend a clear verdict.
