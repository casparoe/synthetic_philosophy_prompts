As part of my coursework in political philosophy, I have been examining the institutional architecture of contemporary workfare regimes, specifically the simultaneous deployment of two mechanisms within the same administrative system. The first is behavioral conditionality: claimants must sign contractual commitments, submit documented proof of job applications, attend mandatory skills workshops, and accept any “reasonable” offer of work, with benefits reduced or suspended for non-compliance; this frames unemployment as a remediable deficit of human capital or will. The second is predictive fraud surveillance: agencies deploy algorithmic risk-scoring models that flag claimants for investigation and sanction on the basis of demographic correlations, transactional anomalies, or statistical deviations from expected claim patterns, often treating such flags as evidence of moral dishonesty rather than of structural disadvantage or clerical error.

What philosophical commitments about agency, desert, and the moral ontology of poverty does this dual architecture presuppose? Does the conditionality mechanism implicitly depend on a voluntarist or character-building view of unemployment—treating joblessness as primarily a failure of individual effort that disciplined pedagogy can correct—while the predictive mechanism depends on a determinist or essentialist view that treats certain recipients as inherently prone to deception? Can a single coherent theory of welfare accommodate both the “deserving poor,” who are presumed capable of self-correction and therefore subjected to disciplinary improvement, and the “undeserving poor,” who are presumed untrustworthy and therefore subjected to exclusionary surveillance? Or does the practice only remain stable by implicitly partitioning the claimant population into two moral categories with incompatible metaphysical presuppositions?

Furthermore, I want to know whether this institutional design is philosophically underdetermined. Could a purely structuralist account of unemployment—which denies that individuals are morally responsible for macroeconomic job scarcity—nonetheless justify both mechanisms on purely instrumental, paternalistic, or efficiency-based grounds? And if we replaced the implicit theory with a consistently probabilistic or structuralist view of both unemployment and fraud, what exactly in courtroom-style administrative practice, sanctioning procedure, or public justification would have to change? Please reconstruct the implicit theory of welfare, moral responsibility, and evidentiary reasoning that makes this dual practice intelligible, distinguish what it strictly requires from what it merely accommodates, and indicate where the practice becomes incoherent when its parts are pressed into consistency.
