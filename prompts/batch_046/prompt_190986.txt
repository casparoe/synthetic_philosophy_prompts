I serve on the General Education Curriculum Committee at a research university, and we face a concrete vote at our March meeting: whether to retain our required first-year history sequence—three discrete survey courses titled "The Medieval World," "The Renaissance and the Birth of Modernity," and "The Scientific Revolution to the Enlightenment"—or to dissolve those boundaries in favor of thematically organized tracks on, for example, "Global Exchange Networks," "Institutional Power and Resistance," and "Knowledge Practices Across Cultures." The dispute among faculty is not merely pedagogical; it turns on whether "the Middle Ages," "the Renaissance," and "the Scientific Revolution" name real discontinuities in human affairs—what some historians call genuine "historical joints"—or whether they are retrospective "epistemic scaffolding," convenient fictions invented by later scholars to impose order on continuous change. In a recent workshop, one colleague proposed that we think of these labels on the model of institutional kind terms: categories like "adolescence" that lack sharp biological boundaries yet become genuinely real through sustained institutional use, legal recognition, and collective self-understanding. I want the committee to take that analogy seriously rather than dismiss it as jargon, because it suggests that even invented periodizations might acquire normative weight once curricula, archives, and public memory are built around them.

Please draft the short written opinion I will circulate before Thursday's meeting. It should recommend one of the two structural options—retaining the tripartite sequence or adopting the thematic replacement—with a clear implementation time frame (for instance, a phased transition beginning in the 2026–2027 catalog cycle, or an immediate retention with a review clause in three years). The reasoning must engage the philosophical issue directly: treat the strongest argument for the view you reject—that is, if you recommend dissolution, explain why the institutional-kind analogy fails to justify preserving boundaries that misrepresent historical continuity; if you recommend retention, explain why abandoning these categories risks erasing real shifts in mentalité, institutional form, and self-conception even if the boundaries are somewhat fuzzy. Do not treat this as a purely administrative matter; the memo should show that you understand the dispute is about the ontology of macro-historical phases, not just course logistics.

The document should be formal, addressed to the committee, and should avoid bibliographies, reading lists, or citations of specific scholars—our model without web access should not need them. Instead, ground your analysis in the conceptual distinction between historical joints and epistemic scaffolding, apply the institutional-kind analogy concretely to course design, and conclude with an actionable bottom line that the committee can adopt or amend by vote. If your recommendation overturns one of the two positions sharply, state explicitly what practical concession the losing side should receive—perhaps a pilot thematic module within the retained sequence, or a mandatory historiography unit within the new thematic track—so that the memo functions as a genuine compromise proposal rather than a winner-take-all decree.
