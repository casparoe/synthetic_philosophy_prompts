I've been in this philosophy of value reading group for like two years now and we keep circling back to the wrong-kind-reasons problem — you know, that thing where there can be reasons to want something or believe something that dont actually make the thing valuable or true. At first it seemed like everyone was just sorting examples: the gunman case for belief, the devil's bargain for desire, whatever. But lately the argument has totally stalled in this weird way. One side says the whole thing collapses into a confusion between motivating reasons and normative reasons, and if you just keep those straight there's no special wrong kind left over. The other side says no, there's a genuine structural feature of value — that value-based reasons have to be merit-responsive in a way that purely instrumental reasons aren't — and that this explains why the gunman reason feels like it misses the point. They both cite the same thought experiments but come to opposite conclusions about whether WKR is one unified phenomenon or just a grab-bag of unrelated puzzles.

My working hunch is that they're not even disagreeing about values; they're answering different questions under the same heading. One group is doing meta-ethics about what makes a reason count as value-conferring, the other is doing philosophy of mind about how reasons figure in practical reasoning. But I'm not sure if that's right, or if there's a hidden empirical assumption about human psychology doing the work. So here's what I actually want: a diagnosis of why this debate hasn't moved. Is it verbal? Is it that 'reason' is doing double duty? Are they using different standards for what counts as explaining a normative failure? And if your diagnosis is right, what would actually break the impasse — a new conceptual distinction, a formal model of merit-responsiveness, or should we just accept that the question is ill-posed and split it into two?
