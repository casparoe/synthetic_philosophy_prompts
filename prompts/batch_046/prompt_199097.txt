I recently learned that a close friend and I share a clinical diagnosis for which our doctors cite the same objective statistic: a 30% five-year recurrence rate. This made the Principal Principle suddenly personal—should our credences align with that chance, or may they diverge given our private, non-screening health indicators? Write a formal dialogue between two equally competent philosophers, A and B, facing precisely this situation. A defends strict deference: rational credence should match the known objective chance unless one holds admissible evidence that screens it off. B grants the principle but introduces a centered-chance challenge: clinical frequencies are defined over reference classes that may be heterogeneous with respect to indexical position, so the relevant objective probability might be conditional not only on shared evidence but on the agent's own location within the class, and private knowledge of that location may not count as screening evidence.

The dialogue must contain genuine back-and-forth—A should answer B's indexical objection directly, B should reply to that answer, and neither should be a strawman. They should ask whether the dispute is substantive (about the boundary between "part of the chance-setup" and "evidence about it") or merely verbal. Keep the exchange focused, do not ask for citations or bibliographies, and ensure the argument could be evaluated without web access.
