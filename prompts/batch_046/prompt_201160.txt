I'm trying to determine what I actually believe about pacifism by testing my intuitions against concrete scenarios rather than by appealing to any received doctrine. Here are my judgments, mostly stable but with one tension I cannot resolve.

Case 1: A pacifist bystander strikes an armed attacker with a heavy object, unintentionally killing him, in order to save a stranger's life. Verdict: permissible; the death was foreseen but not intended, and the intervention was immediate, bodily, and unmediated by any institutional authorization of lethal force.

Case 2: The same person, now an urban planner, approves a building layout she knows includes architectural chokepoints where police are specifically trained to deploy lethal force against active shooters. She makes no change. Verdict: not permissible; she has structurally embedded a specific lethal authorization into an environment she directly controls.

Case 3: She refuses to pay the portion of her income tax allocated to military expenditure, accepting the legal penalties. Verdict: morally required; this is a direct refusal to fund institutional lethal capacity.

Case 4: She teaches non-violent de-escalation techniques to security staff, knowing that statistically about ten percent of trainees will still resort to lethal force in crises despite her instruction. Verdict: permissible; the risk is diffuse, not specifically directed by her, and not embedded in a particular pathway she authorizes.

Case 5: She serves on a jury and votes to acquit a defendant she believes will likely kill again upon release, on the ground that she must not participate in state punishment. Verdict: not permissible; through a direct institutional decision she has enabled a specific, predictable lethal outcome.

The tension: Case 1 involves a lethal outcome that she directly caused with her own body, yet I excuse it; Case 2 involves no direct bodily harm and only a remote architectural contribution, yet I condemn it. Meanwhile, Case 4 involves a statistical risk of lethal outcomes she facilitates, yet feels acceptable, while Case 5 involves a single predictable outcome facilitated by her vote, and feels wrong.

My tentative idea is that pacifist responsibility tracks something like "the specificity with which a lethal pathway is embedded within one's direct sphere of control" — an authorization-gradient principle — but this feels ad hoc. It saves Case 2 and Case 5 by appealing to specificity, yet struggles with Case 1, where the lethal pathway was specific and within her direct control but I still excuse it because it was defensive and unmediated by an institution.

The case I keep going back and forth on is this: a pacifist physician is asked to prescribe a sedative to a terminally ill patient who requests it specifically to hasten death by suppressing respiration; the drug carries a precisely one-percent risk of fatal respiratory failure even at standard therapeutic doses, and the patient understands this. Should she prescribe? My intuition oscillates: it resembles Case 4 in being statistical and therapeutic, yet resembles Case 2 in being a specific, foreseeable lethal pathway embedded in a professional act she authorizes.

Can you articulate a principle — or a small competing family of principles — that captures as many of these verdicts as possible? State each principle precisely, identifying the clause that addresses each case, and give its verdict on the physician case and on one further scenario I have not considered: a pacifist parent who enrolls her child in a school protected by an armed guard, knowing that the guard's presence statistically reduces overall risk but also introduces a specific, institutionally authorized lethal capacity in immediate proximity to her child. For each candidate, be explicit about which of my original five verdicts it must overturn to maintain consistency, and whether overturning any of them is less costly than accepting a more complex principle.
