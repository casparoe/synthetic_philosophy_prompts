At a seminar on historical explanation, two colleagues disagreed about how to account for the fall of the Western Roman Empire. Please adjudicate their exchange.

Marcus argued that the collapse was a clear case of causal overdetermination. He claimed that the fiscal crisis of the third century and the sustained barbarian pressure along the Rhine-Danube frontier were each, counterfactually, sufficient to bring about imperial disintegration within roughly the same timeframe. Because both conditions satisfy the counterfactual test of sufficiency, he said, including both in an explanation is redundant in exactly the way that explaining a broken window by citing both the thrown stone and the glass's fragility is redundant when the stone's impact was already decisive.

Elena replied that Marcus was conflating modal sufficiency with explanatory relevance. She said that in the actual historical sequence, the fiscal crisis degraded the military recruitment and logistics that made frontier defence possible, while the migrations exploited that degraded capacity. Neither factor operated in isolation; they were mechanistically integrated. She added that if Marcus's counterfactual criterion were correct, then every multi-causal explanation in history would collapse into overdetermination, since historians routinely identify several conditions that, had they occurred alone, might still have produced the outcome. The real task, she insisted, is not to eliminate redundancy but to show how distinct causal strands were woven into a single process.

Marcus countered that Elena's "mechanistic integration" simply redescribes the interaction without answering whether the interaction itself was necessary. He proposed a test: if we hold the fiscal crisis constant at its actual severity but imaginatively remove the barbarian migrations, does the empire still fall? If yes, then the migrations were not a necessary part of the causal chain, and Elena's integration story is just a narrative gloss on overdetermined causes. He claimed that historians who reject overdetermination are usually smuggling in a normative preference for "rich" narratives rather than facing the modal structure of the past.

Elena responded that Marcus's counterfactual test assumes a kind of causal atomism inappropriate to historical systems. She pointed out that the empire's fiscal and military institutions were not separable modules; altering one alters the conditions under which the other operates. Therefore, the counterfactual "hold fiscal crisis constant, remove migrations" describes an impossible or radically different system, not a clean test of redundancy. She concluded that overdetermination in history is rarely demonstrable, not because historians dislike simple explanations, but because the counterfactual isolation Marcus requires violates the systemic interdependence that makes historical causation distinctive.

Marcus then said that Elena was protecting her view by making historical causation unfalsifiable: if any attempt to isolate causes is rejected on grounds of systemic interdependence, then multi-causal explanation becomes an unfalsifiable framework that can absorb any counterexample. He asked whether she could name a single historical case where she would genuinely accept that two causes were redundant rather than integrated.

Please go through the exchange move by move and say who makes the stronger point at each stage, who is more right overall, and what important consideration both are neglecting. In particular, address whether Marcus's counterfactual test can be applied to complex institutional systems without distortion, and whether Elena has provided a principled way to distinguish integrated multi-causality from genuine overdetermination, or merely asserted that history is always integrated.

Please also state how confident you are in your adjudication overall, and identify the specific point about which you are least certain.
