I've been thinking about American exceptionalism not as a single slogan but as three different things running together: a historical claim that the American experience is genuinely unlike Europe's, a normative claim that the United States ought to avoid old-world entanglements, and an ideology that can dress up ordinary political interests in the language of special destiny. I'd like to apply all three to the real episode of the U.S. Senate's rejection of the Treaty of Versailles and refusal to enter the League of Nations in 1919 and 1920. Through each lens, what exactly were the opponents of Wilson appealing to—was it a historical argument about American development, a moral argument about entangling alliances, or mainly rhetorical cover for partisan and domestic concerns? Please map this carefully onto the known features of the debate, including the objections to collective security and the Lodge Reservations, and say where exceptionalism actually explains the voting pattern and where it is silent—say, on Wilson's stroke or the simple partisan split. Then tell me what the episode does back to the theory: does it confirm that America was genuinely different in its refusal, undermine that by showing the country was already deeply entangled economically and militarily, or show that exceptionalism functioned mainly as ideology? I want the analysis disciplined, not forced—if one of the three versions doesn't fit, say so plainly.
