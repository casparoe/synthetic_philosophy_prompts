As a novelist working on multigenerational family sagas, I keep returning to a figure I cannot quite classify: the family member, often deceased or estranged, who never receives an interior point of view, whose motives remain mutually contradictory across every account, and yet who functions as the fixed coordinate without which no other character's arc can be narrated. In fiction, she is the absent matriarch whose unexplained departure is simultaneously cited as the cause of every subsequent rupture and reconciliation; in lived experience, he is the relative about whom siblings tell entirely incompatible stories—ruthless versus saintly, absent versus suffocating—while agreeing unanimously that the family turned on his presence. This figure is not a conventional mystery, not a foil, and not a protagonist in hiding. Rather, she behaves like a singularity in narrative space: dense enough to bend every surrounding biography into orbit, yet apparently hollow at its center, sustained not by any coherent self-story but by the convergent gravitational pull she exerts on others. I am aware of philosophical descriptions of the self as a center of narrative gravity, but those seem to concern the fiction an individual spins about herself. The phenomenon I observe is the inverse—an intersubjective center that exists almost entirely in other people's narratives, often with little corresponding internal narrative of its own. Does this configuration have an established name in narrative theory, philosophy of mind, or social ontology? If there is no precise term, how should one distinguish it from an empty signifier, from a Lacanian Other, and from a mere absence? I am not seeking a bare label; I want to know exactly what this is, what it is not, and where its boundaries lie—particularly whether any internal coherence is required, or whether the phenomenon can be maintained purely by the structural necessity it imposes on surrounding accounts.
