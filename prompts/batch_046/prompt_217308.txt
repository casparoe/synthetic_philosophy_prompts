I have recently found myself preoccupied with a moral question that arose during a long conversation with a friend who is considering whether to have children in an era of accelerating climate instability. We were attempting to apply prioritarian reasoning—giving greater moral weight to benefits that accrue to those who are worse off—to intergenerational choices, and we kept running into the leveling-down objection: the worry that if what matters is the absolute improvement of the worst-off, then one might be required to diminish the well-being of the better-off merely to achieve equality, even when no one is thereby made better off. Rather than asking for a bibliography, a reading list, or a survey of named authors, I want to establish the conceptual ground rules myself.

Write the desiderata that any adequate revision of prioritarianism must meet if it is genuinely to dissolve the leveling-down objection without either abandoning the core prioritarian intuition or collapsing into a form of strict egalitarianism that treats equality itself as the sole value. For each desideratum, provide the motivating case or conceptual pressure behind it, with particular attention to intergenerational scenarios in which the relevant parties are successive cohorts rather than contemporaneous individuals, and in which welfare is assessed not by simple utility but by integrated capability bundles—that is, structured sets of functioning that include environmental security, bodily health, and practical reason. Indicate which requirements you take to be non-negotiable and which are merely negotiable preferences of particular theoretical camps, and identify whether any subset of them appears jointly unsatisfiable given standard constraints on aggregation and the non-identity problem. I am far less interested in a scorecard of existing positions than in seeing whether the list itself reveals a tension that any viable view must navigate.
