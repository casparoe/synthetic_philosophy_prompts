I was recently examining how several major archives and critical editions organize Borges's complete works, and I noticed a consistent curatorial choice that puzzles me: rather than arranging essays and fictions chronologically, they cluster texts thematically and annotate them with cross-references that treat works separated by decades as addressing the same conceptual problem without temporal priority. For instance, editions often place the 1940s fiction involving infinite or circular structures in direct conversation with essays on eternity written thirty years later, using reference apparatus that allows a reader to enter the corpus at any point without being told that one text "precedes" another in any philosophically significant way. Meanwhile, these same institutions maintain strictly chronological catalogs for Borges's correspondence and for the works of authors who are not Borges.

What philosophical commitments about time does this practice presuppose? Must it be adopting something like Borges's own denial of linear progression—his suggestion that all moments are simultaneously present in a kind of eternal or labyrinthine structure—or can it be defended on purely pedagogical or aesthetic grounds that carry no metaphysical weight? And is the practice internally consistent with the chronological treatment of Borges's letters: does it presuppose that fictional texts inhabit a different temporal ontology from historical documents, or does it quietly commit to a view about literary objects (that they exist in a logical rather than temporal space)? I want to distinguish what the curatorial method strictly requires from what it merely accommodates, and to see whether a linear, historicist theory of literature could license the same arrangement without contradiction.
