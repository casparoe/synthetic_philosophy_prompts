This is not merely ignorance but active concealment, and it requires a genuine partition inside one person, almost as if there were two subjects.

Sartre's notion of bad faith addresses the same phenomenon but preserves the Freudian architecture. In bad faith, an individual lies about their freedom or facticity, and this succeeds because one sector of the self acts as liar and another as dupe. Sartre criticizes Freud's language of mechanisms and the unconscious, yet he retains the essential idea that self-deception demands a divided agent. Consequently, both accounts agree that the unity of consciousness must be sacrificed if self-deception is real.

More recently, philosophers of cognitive science have proposed that self-deception can be explained by a dual-process architecture: one subsystem generates a belief through motivated reasoning, and a separate monitoring subsystem blocks its revision without ever representing it explicitly. This model explains the data without invoking a Freudian unconscious or a Sartrean split in being."
