I sit on my university’s faculty senate curriculum committee. For decades, every undergraduate in our college has been required to complete a year-long, in-person common-text seminar taught by tenure-line faculty in small groups, regardless of major. A working group now proposes replacing it with a modular, asynchronous, competency-based online sequence, arguing that the old format is an arbitrary gatekeeping mechanism with no measurable learning outcome and that it imposes uneven burdens on working students and commuters. Opponents respond in Burkean terms: the seminar is an evolved institution whose value may lie in hidden functions—shared intellectual reference points, cross-disciplinary relationships, and a common rhythm of argument—that standardized assessments do not capture; they invoke Chesterton’s image of the fence, warning that tearing down a long-standing structure because its original purpose is forgotten risks destroying goods we do not yet know we depend on. The senate must vote next Thursday on whether to sunset the requirement over a defined transition period or preserve it with only cosmetic adjustments. Please draft the short written opinion I will circulate before the meeting: recommend one of these two concrete courses with an explicit time frame, and treat the Burkean-Chestertonian objection as a serious cost rather than dismissing it as mere nostalgia.
