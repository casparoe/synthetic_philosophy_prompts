Two graduate students in my philosophy of science reading group have been deadlocked over a specific case that they think exposes a fault line between counterfactual and interventionist theories of causation. They are analyzing the causal claim that the introduction of a specific feedback loop in the 1970s caused the stability of modern aircraft autopilot systems, but they disagree about what makes this claim true or false. The first student, who favors a Lewis-style counterfactual analysis, argues that the claim is true because in the nearest possible worlds where the feedback loop was never introduced, autopilot systems remain unstable, and these worlds are closer to actuality than worlds where the loop exists but other design parameters change. The second student, who favors a Pearl-style interventionist account, objects that nearest possible world is undefined for complex engineered systems with thousands of interdependent variables, and that the only legitimate causal judgment comes from an actual structural causal model in which we can perform a well-defined do-intervention on the feedback-loop variable while holding the rest of the architecture constant. The first student replies that interventionism secretly smuggles in modal assumptions about which variables are held constant, and that without a similarity metric for worlds, the interventionist cannot explain why we reject bizarre interventions — for instance, holding constant the laws of physics but changing the feedback loop. The second student counters that counterfactualism cannot distinguish genuine causation from mere correlation in engineered systems where designers intentionally build redundancy, because redundancy makes the counterfactual dependence disappear even when the component is genuinely causally responsible. Neither will concede that the other framework has resources to resolve this.

Can you adjudicate their exchange move by move: assess whether the counterfactualist's appeal to nearest worlds is genuinely ill-defined for engineered systems, evaluate whether the interventionist's reliance on do-operators avoids or merely relocates the modal problem, and determine whether either framework can adequately capture causation in intentionally redundant systems. Finally, identify what both are missing — particularly regarding whether historical explanations of technological development require a hybrid account that neither pure counterfactualism nor pure interventionism can provide on its own.
