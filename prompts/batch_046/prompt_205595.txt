First, lay out the four positions as they apply to this hypothesis. Second, explain how Nāgārjuna's rejection of all four relates to the doctrine that emptiness (śūnyatā) is identical to dependent origination (pratītyasamutpāda). In answering, distinguish Nāgārjuna's own dialectical method from the later Prāsaṅgika-Madhyamaka interpretation advanced by Candrakīrti in the Prasannapadā, particularly regarding whether the assertion of emptiness functions as a propositional claim or as a non-assertoric, reductio-based negation. Finally, evaluate whether Madhyamaka should be read as a quietist therapeutic practice—one that eliminates metaphysical commitments altogether—or as a revisionary ontology that denies inherent existence while retaining conventional reality.

Student answer: The catuṣkoṭi examines four possibilities for svabhāva: entities exist with it, they lack it entirely, they both have and lack it, or they neither have nor lack it. Nāgārjuna rejects all four in the Mūlamadhyamakakārikā because each assumes a fixed nature that contradicts dependent origination. Emptiness therefore means that things arise dependently and have no intrinsic essence, but they still exist conventionally. Because emptiness is itself empty, Nāgārjuna avoids self-refutation: he is not advancing a metaphysical thesis but removing an erroneous one. Candrakīrti in the Prasannapadā clarifies this by treating emptiness as a non-assertoric negation that operates through reductio ad absurdum rather than through direct propositional assertion, which keeps it outside the four corners. However, since Nāgārjuna denies that emptiness applies to itself in the same reflexive manner, he implicitly relies on a conventional framework that functions like the Yogācāra mind-only thesis: conventional reality requires a grounding structure that Madhyamaka itself cannot provide. Consequently, there is no performative contradiction, and the view is best understood as purely therapeutic—eliminating all metaphysical commitments without leaving any ontological residue—rather than as a revisionary ontology.
