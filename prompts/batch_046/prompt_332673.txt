After a long argument with a friend about Daniel Dennett's compatibilist defense of moral responsibility versus Gregg Caruso's hard incompatibilist rejection of just deserts, we tried to determine her actual position by testing her intuitions on concrete punishment cases. She rendered the following verdicts, using "deserved punishment" in the retributive sense unless otherwise noted. First, a driver who suffers an undiagnosed epileptic seizure and kills a pedestrian: she said no retributive punishment is deserved, though medical containment is permissible. Second, a driver with the same condition who ignored explicit medical advice not to drive, then seized and killed: she said full retributive punishment is deserved for the reckless choice. Third, a long-term opioid addict who steals to avoid withdrawal, where initial use was voluntary but dependence is now severe: she said desert is significantly diminished, and the response should be primarily rehabilitative with only minimal censure. Fourth, a fully rational corporate executive who knowingly calculates that polluting will save costs and harm residents: she said full retributive desert applies. Fifth, a person who commits theft while acting under direct post-hypnotic suggestion with no subsequent memory: she said zero desert, and only protective measures are appropriate.

We now face a new case. A research neuroscientist participates in a clinical trial of an "executive-function enhancer" that suppresses emotional aversion to risk and dampens empathic response without impairing logical reasoning, working memory, or knowledge of moral norms. During the trial, she calculates that embezzling grant funds to continue a research program she genuinely values—and valued before the trial—is worth the risk. She knows the act is illegal and harmful to colleagues, but the usual emotional brakes are pharmacologically suppressed. After the drug clears, she remembers everything, understands she acted wrongly, regrets the harm, but maintains that her reasoning was sound and that she still endorses the values that motivated the act.

Predict what my friend will say about whether she deserves retributive punishment, identify the closest analog among her earlier cases, and explain whether her verdict is genuinely underdetermined by her track record. Then lay out at least two competing rules that could jointly explain her first five verdicts—one closer to Dennett's capacity-based compatibilism, one closer to Caruso's skepticism about deep desert or to an authenticity-based revision—stating what each rule predicts for this new case and which prior case it treats as the decisive analog. Give your best guess about which rule she is actually running, with reasons, and specify one further hypothetical case that would decisively distinguish the two strongest rival rules. Please write your answer in American English, and do not include bibliographies, citations, or reading lists.
