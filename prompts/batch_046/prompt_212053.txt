I am a novelist auditing a graduate seminar in philosophy of mind, largely to borrow conceptual vocabulary for a work in progress about a blind cartographer who navigates dense urban space through refined echolocation. During a recent session on embodied cognition, a fellow student invoked the familiar slogan knowing how is not knowing that to dismiss my question about whether her navigational expertise could be rendered in narrative form—say, through internal monologue describing acoustic reflection—without necessarily betraying its essential character. The phrase seemed to be doing contradictory argumentative labor: at moments it was treated as an ontological claim that practical skills are non-propositional entities altogether; at others as an epistemic claim that they cannot be transmitted through testimony or written instruction; and at still others as a merely grammatical observation that the verb phrases differ in logical form.

Before I return to office hours, I would like a precise menu of the distinct theses this slogan could actually be expressing, ordered from the trivially true to the radically anti-intellectualist. For each reading—whether psychological, ontological, epistemic, or logical—please indicate whether it genuinely supports the dismissal of my narrative-translation question, and distinguish in particular the view that propositional knowledge is necessary but insufficient for skill, the claim that skills are stored without belief-like structure, and the intellectualist counter-position that knowing how is simply knowing that under a practical mode of presentation. I also want to know which of these readings a philosopher of mind would regard as defensible, which are false, and which are merely linguistic, so that I can identify which version my interlocutor likely meant when they implied that any written account of the cartographer's method would be a fundamental misrepresentation of her craft.
