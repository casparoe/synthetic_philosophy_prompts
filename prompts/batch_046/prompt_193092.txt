I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy training, and I'm organizing a 90-minute workshop for about 14 participants—mostly other engineers and graduate students—on the physical cost of computation. I want a runnable game or structured simulation that makes them feel, not just hear, why Maxwell's demon cannot violate the second law once information is treated as a physical resource.

Design an activity in which pairs of participants act as demons controlling a gate between two bins of tokens representing fast and slow particles. By observing tokens and sorting them, the pairs build a temperature differential and extract work in the form of energy tokens. The rules must require them to maintain a physical logbook—actual paper pages—recording every observation and gate decision. Between rounds, they must erase those logs to reuse the notebooks, paying exactly one energy token per erased entry according to a fixed exchange rate that embodies Landauer's principle. The payoff structure should allow them to extract net energy in early rounds so they initially believe they have beaten the second law, only to have the erasure bill cancel or reverse those gains.

Inject a niche twist: allow one pair to adopt a reversible-computation protocol. Instead of erasing, they uncompute their log steps backward using a secondary buffer notebook, preserving every intermediate state intact. They should discover that they can defer the erasure cost across multiple rounds, accumulating hidden debt inside the buffer, but that fully collapsing the buffer to close the cycle imposes the entire deferred cost at once.

Specify the full rules, token economy, physical materials (cards, two bins, primary and secondary notebooks), rotation schedule, and time breakdown—about 60 minutes of running rounds plus 30 minutes of debrief. The debrief should map each phase onto the corresponding physical concept—sorting, work extraction, log storage, erasure, and reversible uncomputation—without naming those concepts until participants have described in their own words what happened. Predict the likely failure modes: participants hiding logs to avoid payment, misinterpreting the exchange rate as arbitrary, or assuming quantum coherence can rescue them. Be explicit about what the simulation cannot reproduce—actual thermal fluctuations at the kT scale and the quantum coherence required for true reversible gates—so I know exactly what I must supply verbally.

The central tension should emerge from the rules and payoffs, not from any upfront lecture. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; the design should stand on its own without requiring anyone to look up sources.
