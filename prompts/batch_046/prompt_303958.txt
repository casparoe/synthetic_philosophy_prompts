Consider the case of Mara, a full-time caregiver for an adult with progressive cognitive decline. Without consulting the recipient, Mara switches the recipient's diet to an exclusively plant-based regimen. She offers three reasons: first, that the recipient once expressed vague concerns about animal welfare; second, that Mara anticipates her recipient's fully rational future self would endorse this choice; and third, that initiating discussion now would cause unnecessary distress given the recipient's anxiety about change. Analyze this case through the lens of care ethics. Specifically, address whether Mara's action exemplifies attentive care or violates relational autonomy; distinguish caring-for from caring-about; and evaluate whether anticipatory reasoning of the sort in her second reason can be justified within a framework that prioritizes responsive presence over substitutive judgment.

The student wrote:

Care ethics, especially in formulations associated with Nel Noddings, replaces the liberal ideal of autonomy as self-legislation with relational autonomy: the self is constituted through its connections rather than prior to them. Applying this to Mara, we should first distinguish caring-for, which involves direct, receptive engagement with a particular other, from caring-about, which denotes a more generalized concern that can motivate justice but does not require immediate presence. Mara is clearly caring-for given her daily involvement, yet her decision also reflects caring-about animal welfare.

The central question is whether Mara violates responsive presence by substituting her judgment for the recipient's will. I argue she does not. Care ethics permits limited substitutive judgment when a caregiver possesses deep relational knowledge and acts with emotional attunement. Mara's anticipatory reasoning—that the recipient's fully rational future self would endorse a plant-based diet—is not an external imposition but an interpretation of values already latent in prior remarks about animal welfare. Because this interpretation is anchored in existing relational knowledge, it functions as an extension of responsive presence rather than its negation.

Moreover, care ethics does not treat autonomy as requiring explicit consent in every decision; that is precisely the procedural liberalism the framework critiques. The presence of a distant moral concern—animal welfare—alongside direct care enriches the relational context, showing that caring-about can inform caring-for without corrupting it. Thus Mara's action represents a complex exercise of relational autonomy. The appropriate response is not to condemn the lack of consultation but to ensure Mara remains open to revising her interpretation as conditions change.
