After a long argument last night with a friend about updateless decision theory, I'm trying to understand why the central disagreement never actually moves. We both accept the Newcomb payoff matrix, both grant that a perfect predictor makes one-boxing dominant, and both can write down the same formal comparison of expected utility if we just go through the steps. The stall begins when we ask what UDT's logical counterfactual actually achieves that evidential decision theory does not. My friend argues that conditioning on the logical output of one's own decision algorithm is structurally identical to EDT's conditioning on the predictor's belief: in both cases you are updating on a correlation rather than tracking a causal pathway, and calling the dependency "logical" rather than "evidential" is a notational shift that hides the same screening-off problem. I respond that logical counterfactuals are not empirical correlations but internal dependencies within a single computation—altering the output of a deterministic algorithm does not require a causal mechanism distributed in physical time—so UDT avoids EDT's error of treating the predictor's belief as an external cause rather than as a logical consequence of shared source code. Once we reach this point, each of us can formalize our claim, but then we simply restate it. Is this disagreement substantive—about the ontology of causation in non-physical dependencies—or is it verbal, turning on whether the word "cause" applies outside physical processes? Are we in fact answering different questions, with one of us concerned with rational choice under logical uncertainty and the other with descriptive prediction? More specifically, does the impasse depend on a hidden formal issue, such as whether parametric logical counterfactuals—varying an algorithm's output while holding its structure fixed—can be coherently separated from structural counterfactuals—varying the algorithm itself—without collapsing back into causal or evidential reasoning? Or is the deadlock ultimately bedrock intuition about what it means for an agent to "choose" within its own computation? I want a diagnosis grounded in what each side actually says, not a survey of interpretations, together with an honest assessment of what—if anything—would count as progress: a formal result about logical uncertainty, a conceptual distinction that genuinely separates the frameworks, or an argument that the question is ill-posed and that no further resolution should be expected.
