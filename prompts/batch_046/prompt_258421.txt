hey, im trying to work out an argument about moral worth and acting for the right reasons but it feels like it collapses into absurdity. my friend wrote it out like this:

(1) For an act to have full moral worth, the agent has to do it because it's right — not just in line with rightness, but motivated by the rightness itself.
(2) That means the agent's motivating reason must be the very same thing as the normative reason that makes the act correct.
(3) Normative reasons are facts in the world (someone's in pain, a promise was made), but motivating reasons are mental states (beliefs, intentions).
(4) A world-fact cant literally be identical to a mental state.
(5) So for every act, the motivating reason is never identical to the normative reason.
(6) Therefore no act ever has full moral worth.
(7) But thats crazy — we clearly think some people act with moral worth.
(8) So (1) must be false, and moral worth doesnt require being motivated by rightness itself.

i want to keep (1) if i can, and i definately dont want to end up saying moral worth is a myth. my friend says reject (4) by saying representation collapses the gap: the mental state represents the fact, so they can be 'the same reason under different modes'. but then i worry about this: if representation is enough, then a person who correctly represents that suffering is bad but is totally emotionally dead inside still counts as acting for the right reason, which feels wrong. so maybe the problem is in (2) — there's an ambiguity between 'same reason' and 'same reason represented'? or is the slide somewhere between (4) and (5)? if i reject (4) what exactly am i committing to? does it mean mental states literally contain world-facts, or just that we should stop demanding literal identity?

where does this actually break? and if the answer is that (2) is too strong, what does weakening it do to the concept of moral worth?
