Write a polished, formal dialogue between two philosophers—one defending an origin-dependent account of the value of original works, according to which the causal history linking an artifact to its creator is partly constitutive of its worth, and one defending an origin-independent, functionalist account, according to which value supervenes solely on intrinsic and interpretive properties accessible to an audience. Their exchange should center on a carefully specified hypothetical: an undetectable, perfect forgery of a lost preliminary draft of a canonical philosophical text is recovered; the forged draft contains a rigorous, previously unknown argument that resolves a longstanding problem in the field, yet it was composed centuries after the author’s death by an unknown forger. Each interlocutor should respond directly to the other’s specific points about whether the argument’s philosophical merit is undermined by its fraudulent provenance, whether an audience’s justified belief in authenticity plays an essential role in evaluation, and whether there can be wrong-kind reasons for appreciating the argument if one knows it is forged. Avoid straw-manning either position, keep the back-and-forth substantive rather than rhetorical, and do not invoke bibliographic references, citation lists, reading recommendations, or appeals to specific scholars by name.
