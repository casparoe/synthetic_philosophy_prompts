Design a structured 75-minute seminar activity for about 15 advanced students in history of science or philosophy that makes them feel the philosophical instability of contrastive historical explanation, centered on the framing "why Britain and not China?" associated with the Great Divergence debate.

Split the group into five triads. Provide all teams with an identical packet listing structural conditions present in both 18th-century Britain and Qing China—coal geography, market integration, agricultural yields, bureaucratic capacity. Secretly assign each triad a different unstated counterfactual baseline: (A) a unified Qing empire preserving Song-era technological leadership; (B) a fragmented Qing landscape where one region achieves coal-based mechanization; (C) Britain failing to industrialize while a specific Chinese province succeeds; (D) both regions stagnating with Britain escaping by contingent accident; (E) no counterfactual at all—explain Britain alone.

Each team has 12 minutes to build a three-step causal explanation for the divergence using only the shared packet, then delivers a 4-minute presentation. Only after all five presentations does the facilitator reveal the hidden baselines. The group then re-ranks the explanations and discusses why the "best" answer changed.

Specify: exact roles and rules (including a "devil's advocate" in each triad who must defend the hidden baseline without naming it); materials needed; a timeline broken into preparation, presentation, reveal, and deliberation phases; a reputation scheme that rewards predictive consistency rather than rhetorical skill (e.g., each student privately writes which explanation they endorsed pre- and post-reveal, with recognition for those who update); and a debrief that maps the experience onto the philosophical problem—namely, that selecting the comparison class determines causal salience, so "why Britain not China?" may presuppose a unitary "China" that never existed as a single trajectory.

Include: a predictable failure mode (teams will unconsciously embed their hidden baseline into their reading of the "shared" conditions) and how the facilitator should intervene; and an explicit admission of which feature of the actual debate—such as the empirical heterogeneity of Qing regional economies that makes "China" an unstable comparative unit—the activity necessarily flattens and that the facilitator must supply verbally.

The force of the idea should come from the hidden-information structure and the voting reversal, not from an introductory lecture. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citation requests.
