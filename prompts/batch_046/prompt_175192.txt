I sit on the classification board of a national museum of material culture, and we are constantly asked to rule whether a restored, reconstructed, or materially altered artifact still counts as the original object for acquisition, insurance, and exhibition purposes. At present we argue case by case, but I want a reusable decision procedure grounded in the logical architecture of the White Horse Dialogue (Bai Ma Fei Ma) and the School of Names—specifically the distinction between properties that constitute membership in a kind and properties that merely differentiate an instance within that kind—translated into curatorial practice rather than metaphysical debate. The procedure should be something my board can run in sequence: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a definitive no (the object has left the category), and where it must hand the decision to a person because the issue is one of institutional values rather than classification logic. It must survive three hard cases: when the defining property is functional rather than material, when an accidental property is structurally load-bearing but invisible, and when the provenance chain is intact but the material substrate has been mostly replaced.

Please construct the procedure, marking each step as either a factual check, a logical inference drawn from the School of Names framework, or an explicit value judgment that the procedure cannot eliminate. Then apply it to three proposals: first, a seventeenth-century brass astronomical instrument whose internal gears have been replaced with identically dimensioned titanium replicas produced by additive manufacturing, leaving the original housing and engraved face intact; second, an ancient textile whose faded dyes have been chemically restored using synthetically reproduced but historically accurate pigments, with no change to the weave structure; and third, a wooden ship's hull preserved by impregnation with modern synthetic resin so that the original timber now constitutes less than thirty percent of the total mass, though the form, provenance documentation, and navigational history remain unbroken. Include at least one case where the procedure yields an answer that a board concerned with authenticity will find unwelcome, and state clearly what the procedure cannot do—whether it fails to settle hybrid cases, whether it presupposes a distinction between essential and accidental properties that modern material science undermines, or whether it silently imports normative commitments about authenticity.
