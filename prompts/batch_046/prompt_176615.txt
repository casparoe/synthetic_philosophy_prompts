After retiring from a career in atmospheric physics, I have spent the last several years reading philosophy of science and applied ethics with the patience I once reserved for calibrating instruments. I now serve, somewhat to my surprise, on the technical review panel of a regional watershed authority that must evaluate preemptive ecological interventions—proposals to release engineered biocontrol agents, alter seasonal flow regimes, or cull populations before predicted collapse—under conditions where probabilities are genuinely uncertain, outcomes may be irreversible, and the board includes engineers, ecologists, and one appointed ethicist who rarely agrees with anyone. Decisions are rendered monthly, within a forty-eight-hour review window, by a three-person subcommittee with no formal philosophical training, and my signature is required on every recommendation, though I am the only member who has read anything about decision theory.

I want you to translate risk-weighted expected utility—not in its standard form, but in a version I have been trying to formulate—into an actual procedure my subcommittee can run without consulting a textbook. The version must include two modifications that matter to me: first, an irreversibility premium, meaning that if any outcome branch involves damage that cannot be remediated within a human generation, its disutility is weighted by a factor that increases with the confidence interval of that branch rather than decreasing; second, a moral-uncertainty suspension rule, meaning that if the proposal forces a choice between utility frameworks that disagree about which entities count (for instance, present human users versus future ecological states versus the affected non-human populations), the procedure must halt and flag the disagreement rather than averaging across frameworks. The deliverable should be a step-by-step protocol—ordered questions, what counts as a sufficient answer at each step, explicit stop points where the answer is no, explicit hand-off points where the question is one of values and not of fact, and the conditions under which I, rather than the procedure, must sign. Include the hard cases the protocol is designed to mishandle, and be explicit about where judgment cannot be eliminated.

Then apply the protocol to three proposals: first, releasing a genetically modified fungal pathogen to suppress an invasive aquatic plant, where ecological recovery is possible but not guaranteed and the affected river communities include both present users and uncounted future generations; second, authorizing a preemptive winter cull of an overpopulated deer herd to prevent mass starvation, where the procedure may well recommend action but where I personally find the reasoning repellent and want to see whether the protocol forces me to endorse it; and third, delaying reinforcement of a flood-barrier system because climate-model variance makes the expected damage calculation ambiguous, where I expect the protocol to break down and require direct human judgment. For each case, show the step where the procedure applies, where it stops or hands over, and what it can and cannot tell us. Finally, tell me plainly what this procedure is incapable of doing—where it will systematically misfire, which value conflicts it merely postpones, and whether using it is better than relying on unstructured deliberation given our time and expertise constraints.
