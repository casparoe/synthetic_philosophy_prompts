Lena, a public historian, says the museum must go with the archival record. she argues collective memory is just a narrative people build to process trauma, and if you let it overrule documented fact you get mnemonic relativism -- every group gets its own private history and shared truth collapses. she admits reviving the ritual might help social cohesion but thats a seperate issue; you can honor culture without falsifying meteorological record.

Marcus, a cultural anthropologist, pushed back. he said calling the oral account false already assumes archive supremacy -- treating bureaucratic records as neutral while treating lived experience as myth. he said for villagers the storm wasnt a weather event but a relational rupture between community and sea, a kind of ecological grief that official logs cant register because they dont measure that. he thinks the museum should present both frames but make the community version primary, with meteorological data in a footnote, otherwise youre just reproducing colonial logic that erased indigenous knowledge.

Lena came back saying Marcus is smuggling in a metaphysical claim -- that relational rupture is a real kind of event -- without defending it. she said if the museum lists storm yes per community and storm no per NOAA side by side, visitors will just be confused about whether a physical storm actually happened.

Marcus replied that confusion is exactly the point. history should be messy, and forcing everything into a single what really happened frame is itself an ideological move that hides power.
