I have been reflecting on the long-running philosophical deadlock over Glaucon's challenge in Plato's Republic—the thought experiment in which the just agent, given Gyges' ring of invisibility and thus perfect immunity from social sanction, is asked whether she still possesses any reason to refrain from injustice. In contemporary moral philosophy, this has crystallized into a recurring standoff between two orientations. On one side stand those I will call the internalists, who maintain that moral reasons are embedded in the structure of practical rationality itself, so that even the fully invisible agent fails rationally if she acts unjustly, regardless of whether anyone can observe her failure. On the other side stand the instrumentalists, who treat justice as a cooperative scheme valuable only under conditions of mutual visibility, and who therefore conclude that the ring-bearer, facing no reputational cost and no legal risk, has no remaining reason to be just except perhaps a contingent psychological aversion. The literature on both sides is now extensive, yet the exchange rarely advances: internalists invoke practical identity and the transparency of reason to itself, while instrumentalists reply that such transparency is precisely what radical invisibility undermines, and the cycle repeats without resolution. I want you to diagnose this stall rather than adjudicate it. Is the disagreement substantive—about the metaphysics of reasons, about the conditions under which they bind an agent, or about whether practical rationality can be separated from social rationality? Or is it partly verbal, turning on whether having a reason requires that the reason be accessible to the agent's deliberative perspective, a condition the internalist asserts and the instrumentalist denies without independent argument? Do the two camps share the same explanandum—what the invisible just person ought to do, what she will do, or why morality retains authority in anonymity—or are they answering different questions, one normative and one predictive-psychological, under the single heading of why be moral? Is there a hidden formal question about rational choice under conditions of perfect opacity and zero observability that neither side has made explicit? Or is the impasse ultimately a collision of bedrock intuitions about whether morality survives the removal of all social scaffolding? Please support your diagnosis by attending closely to what each orientation actually claims about the ring-bearer and about the relation between invisibility to others and invisibility to one's own commitments. And tell me what, if anything, would constitute genuine progress: a conceptual distinction separating practical reason from cooperative advantage, an empirical result about moral motivation in anonymous conditions, a formal model of decision-making under unobservable action, or an argument that the disagreement is ill-posed because Glaucon's scenario conflates social invisibility with the dissolution of normative authority.
