I'm preparing the final examination for an upper-level seminar on philosophy of logic and philosophy of mind, and I want one essay question that forces students to move beyond the standard textbook summary of the Lucas-Penrose anti-mechanist argument. The question should center on the following tension: Gödel's first incompleteness theorem applies only to consistent formal systems that are sufficiently strong, yet the Lucas-Penrose argument asks us to imagine that the human mind is modeled by such a system F, and then observes that humans can apparently recognize truths—specifically, the Gödel sentence constructed for F—that F itself cannot prove. The crucial difficulty is that if F is inconsistent, the theorem does not apply at all, and there is no independent, non-circular proof that human cognition is globally consistent. Moreover, Penrose's development of the argument appeals not merely to logical incompleteness but to the claim that human mathematical understanding involves a capacity that is not algorithmic, and he sometimes suggests that this might depend on physically non-computable processes. For the exam, I want students to evaluate whether this stronger claim can be sustained without committing to a specific ontology of mind—such as quantum-gravitational mechanisms—or whether it collapses into a more modest epistemological point about the limits of formal axiomatization.

To keep the question from being a pure historical reconstruction, inject the following contemporary framing: ask students to consider a hypothetical machine capable of recursive self-modification—continuously revising its own axiomatic base to preserve consistency whenever a contradiction is detected. Would such a system escape the Lucas-Penrose objection, or would it merely become a different formal system F' for which a new Gödel sentence can be constructed? The essay should require students to navigate this problem while maintaining the distinction between consistency and soundness, and while assessing whether human recognition of Gödel sentences genuinely places human cognition "outside" every formal system it can survey.

Please write the essay question itself, a model answer of approximately 650 words, and a marking scheme calibrated for a 90-minute exam in which this question carries 40 percent of the total grade. The marking scheme should separate an A answer from a B answer by naming the specific argumentative moves each must contain: a correct statement of Gödel's first theorem with the consistency condition made explicit; a precise explanation of why recognizing the Gödel sentence for F does not entail that F can derive it; an evaluation of the consistency objection and whether it undermines the anti-mechanist conclusion; an analysis of Penrose's move from an epistemic "standing outside" to a claim about non-computational ontology; and an assessment of the recursive self-modification escape route, specifically addressing whether it solves or merely relocates the Gödelian limitation. The scheme should also flag specific misconceptions that should cost marks—such as conflating the first and second incompleteness theorems, treating human mathematical intuition as infallible, or assuming without argument that recursive revision eliminates rather than shifts the target system. Do not include a bibliography, reading list, or citation requests; the deliverable should be the question, the model answer, and the rubric alone.
