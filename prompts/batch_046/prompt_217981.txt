I have been working through Benacerraf's access problem, and I want to check whether my understanding of its scope is correct. The standard presentation treats it as a challenge that arises specifically within a causal theory of knowledge: if mathematical objects are abstract and therefore causally inert, then on a causal account of knowing we should be unable to know them. It strikes me that if one rejects the causal theory—say, in favor of a reliabilist or virtue-theoretic account of mathematical cognition—the epistemological difficulty dissolves, because our cognitive faculties can be responsive to structural relations without being causally triggered by the relata themselves. But I wonder whether this dissolution comes at a hidden metaphysical price. Once we explain mathematical knowledge entirely in terms of internal cognitive dynamics—evolutionary selection for approximate number sense, representational constraints, inferential coherence—have we not made mathematical objects explanatorily redundant? And if so, does this generate a second-order version of Benacerraf's dilemma, not about whether we can know abstract objects, but about whether we have any reason to posit them at all? I am not asking about Field's separate argument against mathematical realism, but about whether Benacerraf's own reasoning, once its epistemological premise is removed, rebounds as an ontological objection. In short: is there a principled distinction between solving Benacerraf's problem by abandoning causal epistemology and solving it only to find that the objects one wished to preserve have become causally and explanatorily invisible? Or have I simply collapsed two different anti-platonist strategies into one?
