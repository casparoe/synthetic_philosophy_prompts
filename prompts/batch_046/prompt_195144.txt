In philosophy of biology and astrobiology, debate over whether organizational closure alone—autopoiesis without heredity or evolutionary potential—is sufficient for life has stalled despite agreement on the underlying biochemistry. Take as the focal case a synthetic lipid vesicle maintained in a flow chamber: it continuously regenerates its own boundary through chemical feedback loops, responds homeostatically to pH shifts, contains no nucleic acids or ribozymes, cannot replicate its internal organization, and has no evolutionary lineage. Proponents of the organizational view classify it as alive; critics treat it as a sophisticated chemical steady state, arguing that evolutionary potential is what makes "life" explanatory rather than merely descriptive. Both sides accept every measurement of the vesicle's behavior. Is the impasse a hidden empirical disagreement about how such systems will respond to novel stressors, a conceptual disagreement about whether "life" should serve a classificatory or explanatory function, a substantive metaphysical claim that evolutionary capacity is a necessary condition, or a verbal dispute in which the parties agree on the phenomena but weight the term differently? Diagnose the structure of the disagreement using what each side actually treats as decisive, and say what—an experimental result, a distinction between organizational and evolutionary closure, a demonstration that the concept does no independent work, or an argument that the question is ill-posed—would count as genuine progress rather than further restatement.
