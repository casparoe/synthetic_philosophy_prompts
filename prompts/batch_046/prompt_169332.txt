I've been thinking lately about that bidding game economists talk about—where two people compete for a dollar bill, except both the winner and the runner-up have to pay whatever they bid, so you can end up paying two dollars to win one, and people keep raising the stakes just to avoid being the one who loses money for nothing. From what I understand, this is called the dollar auction, and psychologists use it to show escalation of commitment: once you're in, the cost of quitting feels worse than the cost of continuing, even when continuing is clearly irrational. I'm not a student of economics or philosophy—just someone who noticed the same pattern when my housemates argued over who should replace the shared refrigerator filter, each refusing to back down because "I've already complained three times," until the emotional cost far exceeded the price of a filter.

Could you write a joke that actually builds on the specific mechanics of this auction—meaning the highest and second-highest bidders both pay, the item is worth less than what's being risked, and participants escalate specifically to avoid the worst outcome rather than to win the prize? I'd like the joke to be set in an unconventional context—perhaps a philosophy reading group bidding on who has to summarize a tedious chapter, or a couple deciding who will apologize first in a circular argument—rather than a generic classroom demonstration. Most importantly, inject a small twist about self-awareness: the characters should know, in advance, that they are prone to escalation, and they should try to use that knowledge to protect themselves, yet the protection mechanism itself becomes the reason they escalate further. The joke should make coherent sense to someone who understands escalation of commitment; it should not merely use the phrase as a punchline, but should let the listener recognize the structural trap in the dialogue or situation.
