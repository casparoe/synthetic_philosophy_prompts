I'm moderating a Q&A session with a philosopher who has recently defended an unnamed but specific position on moral conflict: she holds that what we call moral dilemmas are never cases in which two genuinely binding obligations remain in force after deliberation, but rather symptoms of what she terms structural ambiguity in the practical-reasoning framework that generates them. On her view, obligations are always indexed to a fully specified decision procedure or institutional role; apparent conflicts arise only when that index is left implicit, and once it is made explicit, one track of reasoning dominates without leaving the kind of moral residue associated with Bernard Williams. She denies that regret after hard choices is ever appropriate as residual moral cost, treating it instead as a heuristic signal that the framework needs further parameterization.

Write me a sequence of six to eight interview questions that press her on the content of this view rather than asking her to restate it. I want at least one question that forces her to distinguish her position from standard anti-dilemma accounts—such as those that appeal to consistency conditions on practical reason or to the hierarchy of prima facie duties found in W.D. Ross—and a follow-up keyed to the reply she is most likely to give. I also want one question that addresses the phenomenology of being torn: if the conflict is only structural ambiguity, why does the agent experience genuine anguish rather than mere confusion about which procedure applies? Include a follow-up that anticipates her answer, likely that the anguish tracks the cost of parameterization rather than an unresolved normative clash. Finally, end with a question about what kind of evidence or case could actually falsify her view—specifically, whether there is a conceivable scenario in which no further parameterization of the reasoning framework resolves the conflict without arbitrary stipulation, and what she would say about such a case.
