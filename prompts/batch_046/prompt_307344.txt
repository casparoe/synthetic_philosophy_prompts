I'm a software engineer without any formal philosophy training, and I've been trying to think through an argument about environmental history that I sketched out after reading about how volcanoes and epidemics shaped societies. I want to know exactly where the reasoning breaks, because right now it seems to show that either every physical constant is a "historical actor" or we have to accept environmental determinism, and I don't like either horn.

Here is the chain: (1) If we cite a non-human cause—say, the 1783 Laki eruption or the 1918 influenza mutation—to explain a social crisis, we face a choice: either that cause is merely a background condition, which makes human institutions the only real actors, or it is itself an actor interacting with them. (2) But if it is only a background condition, then whenever its effects are severe enough to constrain human responses tightly, our explanation treats those human responses as epiphenomenal—that is environmental determinism. (3) So to preserve human agency while still granting causal weight to germs or climate, we should adopt an asymmetrical interaction model: the non-human force acts by presenting a constrained range of options, and human institutions act by selecting within those constraints. (4) Under this model, Laki is an actor because it presented Icelandic authorities with a range of possible relief and migration policies, all within a famine-induced constraint. (5) By the same logic, the presence of atmospheric oxygen presented evolutionary processes with a range of possible metabolic adaptations, selecting within biochemical constraints. (6) Therefore oxygen, like Laki, is a historical actor in the asymmetrical interaction model. (7) Generalizing, every necessary physical condition—gravity, solar radiation, tectonic stability—presents evolutionary or social processes with constrained ranges of response. (8) So either all of these are historical actors, which collapses the actor/background distinction entirely, or we reject (3). (9) But rejecting (3) seems to force us back into (2): if we deny the interaction model, then citing severe climate or disease events in historical explanation does erase human agency.

I feel like there is a subtle slide between (4) and (5), or perhaps between (5) and (6), but I can't name it—maybe an equivocation about what "presenting options" means across biological and political contexts? I would really like to keep (3), because it feels like the only way to talk about pandemics or eruptions as genuinely shaping history without making people into puppets. Which step should I actually reject, and what does giving it up cost me? If the argument is valid and the problem is just that (3) is too expensive, I want to know that too; but my intuition is that something equivocal is happening in how "constrained range" gets applied across evolutionary and institutional settings.
