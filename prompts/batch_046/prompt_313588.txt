Consider Avicenna's flying-man argument, reconstructed from his writings on psychology: imagine a person created fully formed, suspended in empty space, blindfolded and with limbs outstretched so that he touches nothing — not even himself. Despite this complete sensory deprivation, Avicenna claims he would still affirm his own existence. I am struggling to see whether this establishes a genuinely non-bodily self-awareness, or whether it tacitly presupposes a minimal awareness of one's body as an oriented, bounded presence in space. Could you help me understand how his claim relates to the contemporary distinction between minimal selfhood (a pre-reflective, first-personal givenness) and narrative selfhood (which requires autobiographical memory and conceptual self-attribution)? In particular, does the flying man's immediate affirmation of his own existence amount only to minimal self-awareness, or does it already involve the objectifying, conceptual grasp characteristic of narrative identity? And if the latter, does that undermine his conclusion that the soul is substantially independent of the body?
