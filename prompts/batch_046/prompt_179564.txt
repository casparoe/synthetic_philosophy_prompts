Question: Consider Dennett’s thought experiment in “Where Am I?”, in which his brain—named Yorick—is removed and sustained in a vat, while an exact duplicate brain is implanted in his body and directs its behaviour. Some commentators have argued that the case tacitly assumes what might be called a single-stream model of narrative integration: that personal identity requires one unified autobiographical thread, with no genuinely distributed or partial centres of narrative gravity. Write an answer of approximately 500 words addressing the following: Does Dennett’s argument that he is “where Yorick is” depend on this single-stream assumption? In particular, assess whether the duplication of the brain undermines his conclusion because both candidates possess equal psychological continuity, or whether Dennett can consistently claim that spatio-temporal continuity of the original brain provides the decisive factor. Your answer should explain the role of the narrative centre of gravity and address whether a distributed-narrative objection—according to which both the vat-brain and the duplicate-body could maintain non-conflicting, partial autobiographical threads—succeeds in dissolving Dennett’s intuition that there is a unique answer to “where am I?”.

Student answer:

Dennett’s scenario asks us to imagine that his brain, which he calls Yorick, has been extracted and kept alive in a vat, while an exact duplicate brain has been placed in his skull and now controls his body. Both entities claim to be Dennett, so the puzzle is to determine where the self actually resides.

Dennett’s response appeals to the narrative centre of gravity. In Consciousness Explained he develops this idea to show that the self is not a Cartesian soul but an abstract centre around which experiences and memories are organised. Applying it here, he argues that he is where Yorick is, because Yorick is the original brain that preserves the spatio-temporal continuity of his mental life. The embodied duplicate, despite behaving identically, lacks this direct causal link to his prior states and is therefore an impostor.

The duplication problem is nevertheless serious. If identity depends on psychological continuity, then both candidates share it equally. Dennett anticipates this distributed-narrative objection—he considers whether both entities could maintain partial, non-conflicting autobiographical threads—and rejects it by insisting that narrative integration requires a single stream. There cannot be two centres of gravity for one life-story; hence the symmetry must be broken in favour of the original brain.

This shows that the self is not identical with the physical body, since the body can be occupied by a different brain. At the same time, the argument does not reduce identity purely to psychology, because Dennett still relies on the physical continuity of Yorick to break the tie. The intuition that he is “where Yorick is” therefore combines narrative continuity with a bodily anchor. Without that anchor, the single-stream assumption collapses, and we face a genuinely distributed self. So Dennett’s conclusion holds, but only if we accept both the narrative model and the priority of original spatio-temporal continuity.
