I'm presenting a thesis at an interdisciplinary philosophy-of-mind workshop: half cognitive neuroscientists who work on predictive processing and temporal binding, half philosophers of perception skeptical of representationalism, plus a philosopher of science who specializes in replication issues in small-N psychophysics. I defend the view that the color phi phenomenon—specifically its reverse-phi and ghost-trajectory variants—exemplifies an Orwellian rather than Stalinesque revision of consciousness. On my account, the brain does not preserve an intact underlying representation that a later cognitive layer merely falsifies; instead, it updates the experiential record of the immediate past itself, altering what it was like to have just seen. To make this precise, I introduce a distinction between experiential memory (the retention of the just-past moment within the live temporal window of working consciousness) and archival memory (the storage available for verbal report and later reflection), and I argue that phi targets the former. This explains why subjects sincerely report continuous motion through a gap without access to a "corrected" original trace. The neuroscientists will likely say I'm ignoring standard predictive-coding accounts of postdictive construction; the phenomenologists will worry I'm smuggling representationalism back in via talk of "experiential records"; the replication skeptic will question whether ghost-trajectory data are robust enough to bear this metaphysical weight.
