The debate between illusionists about phenomenal consciousness—roughly the Dennett tradition that qualitative properties are theoretical posits dissolving under representational analysis—and phenomenal realists has persisted for decades despite broad agreement on the underlying neuroscience, particularly predictive-processing and global-workspace architectures. My working theory is that the stall is not empirical, since both camps predict the same interoceptive and attentional dynamics, but conceptual: they diverge over whether representational transparency entails the absence of qualitative character, and over whether higher-order seeming-to-seem states can be fully explained without invoking phenomenal properties. Is that diagnosis correct, or is there a disguised formal disagreement—perhaps about explanatory adequacy, or about which neural patterns count as data—doing the actual work? I want an analysis of whether this is verbal, about standards of explanation, or rooted in bedrock intuitions neither side can argue for; and given that diagnosis, whether any conceptual distinction, formal result, or carefully reframed thought experiment could actually move either party, or whether we should expect a permanent stalemate. Please treat this as a structural diagnosis of the disagreement itself, not a survey of interpretations or a verdict on who is right.
