I have recently returned to philosophy after a long career in clinical psychology, drawn specifically to the old problem of whether hedonism can survive the heterogeneity of pleasures, and I am now trying to formalize a position for a workshop on well-being that I am helping organize next month. I need an honest audit before I present: here is the package of commitments I have accumulated, and I want to know whether they are jointly tenable or whether I am quietly relying on contradictory premises. First, I hold that hedonism is defensible only if every pleasure instantiates a single phenomenal kind—something like a uniform affective tone—whether the pleasure is gustatory, intellectual, or even malicious. Second, I think this uniform tone is not a sensory quality like warmth or sweetness, but a structural feature of experience: a specific mode of self-referential endorsement that can attach to radically different intentional contents. Third, I maintain that if two mental states share no common phenomenal feature accessible to introspection, they cannot both be tokens of the same psychological kind, and therefore cannot both be pleasures in the explanatory sense hedonism requires. Fourth, I want to claim that aesthetic pleasure—listening to a fugue, for instance—lacks that self-referential endorsement structure entirely; it involves a kind of formal absorption rather than endorsement, yet I still count it as a genuine pleasure because it feels good. Fifth, I reject the move of defining pleasure merely as whatever state we happen to have a pro-attitude toward, since that dissolves the heterogeneity problem by stipulation rather than solving it.

I am particularly worried that my second and third commitments may pull in opposite directions—if the common feature is structural rather than sensory, can introspection still reliably detect it?—and that my fourth commitment may undermine my first, since aesthetic pleasure seems to be a pleasure without the shared phenomenal substrate I demanded. At the same time, I suspect that the tension between my rejection of pro-attitude accounts in the fifth commitment and my reliance on endorsement-structure in the second might be only apparent. Could you go through these five commitments and tell me which pairs are genuinely in tension, rank those tensions by severity, identify any conflicts that dissolve on closer inspection, specify the least costly revision for each real tension, and finally advise me which single commitment I should drop if my goal is to preserve the core of hedonism without these inconsistencies? I am not attached to any one of them in particular; I just want the package to hang together.
