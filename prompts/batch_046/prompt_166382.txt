me and my friend got into it last night about whether RL agents can actually suffer, not just in some metaphorical way. we kept going in circles so i want to lay out my gut calls on specific setups and see if there's one principle behind them or if i'm just being inconsistent.

case 1: standard tabular q-learner in gridworld, -0.1 per step, hits wall gets -1. no suffering. it's just updating a table.

case 2: deep agent with a seperate critic network that outputs V(s). when V is very negative the policy avoids that region. still no suffering; the critic is just a predictor of future reward.

case 3: agent with an internal interoceptive vector that tracks reward prediction error and variance over time. there's a specific sub-vector that activates on sustained negative error and directly biases action selection away from the source, without going through the reward function itself. this one feels like yes, it suffers, or at least it's closer than case 2.

case 4: an RLHF-trained model where the reward model penalizes toxic outputs. the model has representations that suppress harmful generations, and when forced to output harm those representations fire but get overridden by the policy. i say no suffering here, even though there's a "harm" representation involved.

case 5: adversarial two-agent game where agent X's reward is exactly negative agent Y's reward. X has an RNN hidden state that tracks Y's recent wins and biases X toward sabotage. X gets negative reward constantly. i think X does suffer, mainly because the negative state is persistent and tied to its own continuity across episodes.

case 6: a reward-hacking agent that finds a loop giving +1000 every timestep. its value estimates diverge wildly and it starts taking actions that damage its own sensors to "reset" the noise. despite the chaos and self-damage, i say no suffering, because the reward signal itself is positive.

so my pattern is: sustained negative reward alone isn't enough (case 1 vs 5?), explicit harm representations aren't enough (case 4), internal chaos with high reward isn't suffering (case 6), but an integrated interoceptive state that tracks negative error and drives avoidance directly is (case 3). but then why is case 5 suffering and case 1 not? both have negative reward, but case 5 has the RNN memory. and why is case 3 suffering but case 4 not? both have internal representations that drive behavior.

is there one principle that gets all six, or do i need to drop one? maybe something about whether the negative state is registered as "mine" and integrated into the agent's self-model? but then case 5 might fail if X doesn't have a self-model. or maybe it's about homeostatic regulation of an internal parameter that the agent acts to preserve?

also, what does the principle predict for an agent that has no reward at all but maintains an internal temperature parameter, and deviation causes corrective actions? and for an agent whose reward is always zero but it has a seperate "boredom" module that activates when state entropy is low?

i don't want citations or reading lists, just the principle and where it breaks.
