I am writing a historical novel about an Athenian logographer in the final years of the Peloponnesian War, and I am consulting on the final exam for an upper-level seminar in democratic deliberation. I need a 90-minute essay question that treats the Mytilene debate, the decision to launch the Sicilian expedition, and the trial of Socrates not as separate moral failures but as instances of what I call the rhetoric of irreversible commitment: the tendency of democratic bodies, once they have visibly expended blood or treasure, to treat any reversal as a moral stain on collective identity rather than as practical correction. The prompt should ask students to evaluate whether this framing explains the refusal to moderate in all three cases better than the standard accounts—demagogic manipulation for the first two, personal malice for the trial—and to identify where the framing breaks down. Please provide the exam question itself, a model answer of roughly 600 words written in American English, and a marking scheme totaling 25 points built around five must-hit analytical moves, specifying the points each is worth, the precise conceptual error that should cost marks, and the common misconception students typically display. Do not ask for bibliographies, citations, or references to modern scholarship; the analysis should rest on the events and speeches as known from the classical record.
