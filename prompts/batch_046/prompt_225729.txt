I'm writing a feature on an underreported fracture in contemporary metaphysics: whether social institutions—parole boards, regulatory agencies, licensing committees—can bear genuine dispositional properties that are systematically masked or finked by their own internal procedures. On one side stand the Institutional Realists, who maintain that such bodies possess real capacities—being disposed to grant release or approve applications—even when hierarchical veto rules, algorithmic overrides, or procedural delays consistently intercept manifestation before it occurs; the interference is an external mask, not a denial of the underlying modal property. Opposing them are the Procedural Reductionists, who contend that in institutional contexts the distinction between a masked disposition and its absence collapses: if a body's decision-protocols are structured so that a putative capacity is always countermanded by an internal rule prior to expression, then attributing that capacity is either false or reducible without remainder to the categorical properties of the rules themselves.

Draft the joint statement these two camps should produce after a sustained adversarial collaboration. Begin with a numbered list of agreed claims—covering the logic of finkish interference in standard cases (the poison-antidote structure and its analogues), the conditions under which disposition-attribution is warranted versus merely projected, and the empirical or conceptual criteria both sides accept for testing specific institutional scenarios. Follow this with a disagreements table in which each divergence is phrased so that its own camp would accept the wording: one column for the Institutional Realists, one for the Procedural Reductionists. Identify at least two cruxes—sub-questions such that resolving them would shift at least one party—including whether institutional roles can have dispositional essences independent of their procedural implementation, and whether there exists a principled boundary between a genuinely masked manifestation and a structurally impossible one. For each crux, present a concrete institutional case or structured thought-experiment that both camps agree bears on it—such as a parole board with an unwritten but consistently followed norm of leniency that is invariably overridden by a mandatory risk-assessment algorithm—and record in advance what each side predicts or requires about that case.
