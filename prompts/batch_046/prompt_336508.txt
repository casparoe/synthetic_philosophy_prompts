As a journalist writing a feature on the philosophy of mind for an audience that follows cognitive science but does not specialize in metaphysics, I am focusing on Jaegwon Kim's causal exclusion argument and the broader crisis of mental causation. Rather than a historical survey, I need a strategic assessment: if a small interdisciplinary team—philosophers of science working with cognitive neuroscientists—had three years and modest resources, which open questions or lines of work in this area should they prioritize, in what order, and on what grounds?

To keep the inquiry from collapsing into well-trodden rebuttals, I am introducing a distinction that has not been fully mapped onto Kim's framework: token-level exclusion, in which a specific mental event is causally preempted by its physical realizer, versus type-level exclusion, in which mental categories are rendered redundant as predictive or explanatory tools across populations of mechanisms. Some work in predictive processing and neural dynamics suggests that mental types might survive token exclusion if they capture stable macro-regularities that purely physical descriptions fail to encode economically—but whether this is a genuine resolution or a category mistake depends on resolving deeper upstream issues about causal relevance, supervenience bases, and whether closure applies to mechanisms or only to descriptions.

Given that framing, please provide a defended agenda. First, identify the two or three open questions that should receive the bulk of attention—for instance, whether the causal closure principle survives counterexamples from complex biological overdetermination; whether supervenience can be reconceived to allow mental efficacy without token-identity; how mental-level descriptions function in predictive models versus mechanistic explanations; and what this implies for clinical neuroscience, where mental-level interventions appear causally effective. Rank them and explain the dependency structure: which answers must come first, which downstream debates they unlock or foreclose, and which are tractable for philosophers without direct laboratory access.

Second, state the criteria governing your ranking—such as theoretical importance, tractability, neglectedness, and how many other problems depend on the resolution—and defend the trade-offs explicitly: why prioritize one upstream problem over another that might seem more urgent?

Third, specify which prominent lines of inquiry you would actively deprioritize and why, particularly those that generate extensive literature without altering practice in neuroscience, clinical psychology, or artificial intelligence.

Fourth, indicate what conceptual or empirical evidence over the next few years would cause you to revise the ranking—for example, formal results on causal relevance in multilevel models, or findings about downward causation in distributed neural networks.

Please avoid bibliographies, citation lists, or appeals to specific papers and page numbers; the reasoning should stand independently. I want an agenda that tells my readers not just what is unsettled, but where the field should actually place its bets.
