I am preparing the final exam for an upper-level undergraduate course on Kant’s practical philosophy. Write one essay question that a student can answer in about 60 minutes (target length 800–900 words). The question must center on Kant’s absolute prohibition against lying, particularly as developed in his 1797 essay responding to the murderer-at-the-door case, but it must not be answerable by summarizing that standard debate. Instead, frame the problem around this constructed scenario: a smart-home security system is programmed to broadcast an automated audio message—“this house is empty”—whenever the doorbell is pressed after midnight, regardless of whether the visitor is an intruder or a lost traveler. Ask students whether this algorithmic output counts as a “lie” in the precise sense Kant condemns, and whether the absence of a human speaker’s intentional assertion alters the moral evaluation. The prompt should require them to distinguish (i) the perfect duty of truthfulness from (ii) the imperfect duty of beneficence, and to explain why Kant holds that even a well-intentioned falsehood corrupts the speaker’s rational nature rather than merely harming the listener. Provide a model answer of approximately 600 words that makes these analytical moves without relying on summary commentary, together with a marking scheme built around five must-hit points (total 20 marks, 4 marks each). For each point, include a one-line note on the typical error that should cost marks—such as conflating the duty not to lie with a general rule of prudence, treating the algorithm as a moral agent, or reducing Kant’s argument to consequentialist harm. Keep the prompt formal and do not ask for bibliographies, citations, or reading lists.
