I host a philosophy podcast, and next week's guest is an unnamed philosopher who defends a view he calls inferential contextualism about cross-cultural variation in philosophical intuitions. He maintains that divergent responses to standard thought experiments — including Gettier-style epistemic cases, trolley-style moral dilemmas, and puzzles about diachronic personal identity — do not simply represent disagreements in verdict over a shared question. Rather, he claims they arise from culturally parameterized differences in the structure of analogical reasoning, in norms governing deference to testimony, and in the relative weight assigned to first-person versus third-person descriptions. On his account, Western analytic philosophy systematically mistakes its own locally calibrated intuition pumps for universal cognitive machinery, thereby generating false confidence in the cross-cultural validity of conceptual analysis.

Draft eight to ten interview questions that go well beyond having him restate this position. I want at least one question on each of the following three topics, with a follow-up for each that is keyed to the answer he is most likely to give:

First, on how his contextualism interprets empirical findings from cross-cultural experimental philosophy: specifically, whether divergence in reported intuitions is best explained by parameter differences in reasoning pathways rather than by differences in belief content, and what normative status those empirical findings should then have for philosophical practice.

Second, on how he accommodates invariant structural features he concedes exist — for instance, widespread resistance to explicit contradiction or basic patterns of causal reasoning — without allowing those invariants to collapse his contextualism back into universalism.

Third, on what his view implies for the methodology of conceptual analysis, particularly when an analyst embedded in Western academic norms uses her own parameterized intuitions to fix the extension of concepts such as knowledge or moral responsibility.

Beyond these three, include at least one question that presses the boundary between his contextualism and full-blown conceptual relativism, asking specifically whether he can consistently allow that some philosophical questions have genuinely culture-independent answers. The remaining questions should anticipate standard objections: that his view renders philosophical disagreement intractable; that it undermines the authority of expert intuition; and that it lacks a principled way to distinguish benign parameter variation from straightforward reasoning error.

Make the questions turn on the actual content of his position, pressing where the literature on cross-cultural philosophy of mind and experimental philosophy presses, and frame each follow-up so that it responds to his most probable reply — for instance, that empirical surveys only track belief reports and not underlying reasoning structures; that logical invariants operate at a formal level while parameterization operates at a semantic or pragmatic level; and that conceptual analysis should be reconceived as explicit mapping of parameter spaces rather than as a search for necessary and sufficient conditions.
