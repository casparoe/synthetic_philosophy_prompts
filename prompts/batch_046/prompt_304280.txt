I will defend, in a seminar for philosophers of gender and social scientists, the claim that the gender-equality paradox—greater occupational gender segregation in more egalitarian societies—neither confirms innate sex differences nor reduces to structural barriers, but instead illustrates what I term autonomy-driven sorting: the removal of economic coercion allows culturally gendered identity-affinity preferences to become the primary mechanism of career clustering. The room will include feminist philosophers of science wary of evolutionary explanations, evolutionary psychologists inclined to read the paradox as biological confirmation, and political philosophers focused on distributive access rather than preference formation. Draft my Q&A prep sheet: order the objections by how damaging they are and by the order in which they are likely to arise; phrase each in the vocabulary its proponents would use; for each, indicate what I should concede up front, provide the concise reply I can deliver in under two minutes, and flag the weakest point in that reply; and include the one objection I most hope is not raised—namely, that if these preferences are culturally formed, my account collapses into standard social constructionism and forfeits its explanatory purchase.
