I serve as a labor-policy analyst for a municipal economic-development office, and every quarter I must recommend whether to approve automation subsidies for local manufacturing firms. I need a reusable decision procedure—usable within a single 90-minute review session with staff who have no philosophy training, yielding a written recommendation I can defend to the city council and to union representatives—that applies a philosophical account of meaningful work to determine whether a proposed automation plan will preserve, destroy, or restructure the meaningfulness of the jobs affected. Ground the procedure in this specific two-factor framework: “craft integrity,” defined as the worker being able to trace a direct, intelligible causal path from their particular action to the finished product; and “social visibility,” defined as the worker’s contribution being named, recognized, and valued by the end beneficiary rather than being fully absorbed into an opaque system. Deliver an ordered sequence of tests or checks with explicit decision criteria for each; a rule for how to weight craft integrity against social visibility when they pull in opposite directions; clear flags marking the steps that are unavoidable judgment calls that I must personally sign off on; three worked applications—one clear approval, one clear denial despite strong economic benefits, and one that forces a recommendation I intuitively dislike; a precise statement of what kinds of questions the procedure is not designed to answer and therefore excludes; and a brief documentation protocol for recording the judgment-call steps so they can survive audit. Lead with the bottom-line structure of the recommendation in the first sentence, then present the reasoning and procedure, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
