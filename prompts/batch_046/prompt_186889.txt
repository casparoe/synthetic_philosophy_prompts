I spent last evening in a long and somewhat frustrating argument with a close friend who insisted that Machiavelli is ultimately just a cynical manual for power, and that his pairing of fortune and virtù reduces to the banal advice that skill beats luck. I want to correct that reduction by designing a single fifty-minute session for philosophy undergraduates who have read Plato's Republic and Aristotle's Nicomachean Ethics but have never encountered Renaissance political thought. The idea I want them to grasp is not definitional but structural: virtù is not a static excellence but a specific mode of temporal responsiveness, and fortuna is not random misfortune but a field of contingency that can be shaped only if one first recognizes its gendered, almost physical resistance. I need a teaching plan that respects the conceptual dependencies—what must be in place before fortuna can be distinguished from Aristotelian tuche, what must precede the claim that virtù includes the willingness to violate conventional morality, and how the river-and-dikes analogy functions as more than illustration—rather than delivering the explanation itself.

Please lay out the sequence in which these components must be introduced, with the dependency relations made explicit: for instance, whether the students must first reject the equation of virtù with moral virtue before they can understand why the gendered metaphor of beating fortune is conceptually load-bearing rather than decorative. I am inclined to carry the session with the river-and-dikes analogy from The Prince rather than with the biography of Cesare Borgia; specify what properties this example must possess and what it must lack—perhaps it needs to show active construction rather than passive endurance, but it risks reducing fortuna to mere hydrology—so that it sustains the full structural point. Map the misconceptions you expect them to form in the order they are likely to arise: that fortune is external randomness, that virtù is just effectiveness, that the violent gendering of fortune is rhetorical ornament, and that Machiavelli's point is merely prudential preparation. For each, indicate the precise point in the sequence where it should be anticipated and defused, not merely corrected after the fact.

Finally, tell me what I should deliberately leave out at this level—likely the broader humanist debate on fortuna in Petrarch and Boccaccio, the specific historiographical context of the Italian Wars, and the differences between the treatment in The Prince and in the Discourses—and how to signal that omission honestly without making the framework seem artificially neat. Give me one diagnostic question I can ask at the end that reveals whether they have understood the dependency between fortune and virtù—whether they see that virtù is partly defined by its object—rather than whether they can repeat the river image. And please do not suggest readings, bibliographies, or citations; I want this session to stand on its own conceptual architecture.
