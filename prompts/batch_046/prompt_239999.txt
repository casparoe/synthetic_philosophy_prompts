I'm a software engineer without formal philosophy training, and I've been trying to understand Miranda Fricker's framework of epistemic injustice—testimonial and hermeneutical injustice—because it keeps surfacing in discussions of algorithmic decision systems, particularly health diagnostics and content moderation. I've noticed what looks like a long stall in how the concept is applied there. One camp frames the harm as a credibility deficit: a user's report or self-description receives systematically reduced weight relative to the system's output. The other camp frames it as a hermeneutical gap: the system's categorical taxonomy makes certain harms literally unrepresentable before any credibility judgment can occur. Both sides invoke the same scenarios—patients whose symptom clusters fall outside training ontologies, or users describing harassment that doesn't match the moderation taxonomy—but they don't seem to move each other, and the literature feels like it's repeating the same exchange.

Is this disagreement substantive, verbal, or are they answering different questions—perhaps one about post-deployment auditing and the other about interface taxonomy design—under the single heading "epistemic injustice in AI"? I want a diagnosis rather than a survey: what must each framing assume about how machine-learning pipelines structure knowledge; is there a hidden empirical or formal question about whether categorization necessarily precedes credibility assignment; and are the two mechanisms genuinely distinct, or just two descriptions of the same pipeline failure?

In particular, I've been wondering whether a distinction like "pre-emptive hermeneutical closure" versus "retrospective testimonial deflation" actually separates the mechanisms, or merely relabels them. If the stall is real, what conceptual distinction would genuinely divide the camps rather than redescribe them? And given that diagnosis, what would count as progress—a formal result about information flow in classification architectures, an empirical study of user-report patterns, or a philosophical clarification that shows the two sides are not competing explanations at all? Also, what is the objection I'm most likely missing because I'm approaching this from engineering rather than philosophy?
