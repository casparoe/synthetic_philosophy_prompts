Write the full desiderata for an adequate theory of cross-cultural divergence and convergence in philosophical intuitions—one that explains why practitioners in different linguistic and educational traditions report different verdicts on canonical cases (trolley-style tradeoffs, Gettier-style knowledge attributions, personal-identity fission scenarios, and modal essentialist judgments) without collapsing the phenomenon into mere translation error or mere ignorance.

Give me the requirements list: for each, state the specific case or empirical pattern that motivates it, the distinction the theory must preserve (for instance, between an intuition that differs because the underlying concept differs versus one that differs despite conceptual equivalence, or between pre-reflective judgment and post-training calibration), and any result from anthropology, psycholinguistics, or comparative philosophy it must remain consistent with. Include at least these injected constraints: the theory must handle the translation-equivalence problem for modal thought experiments (where rendering a case into a language with different epistemic-verb morphology or different deontic framing may structurally alter the judgment being measured rather than merely expressing it); it must account for the training-homogenization effect (whether formal philosophical education makes intuitions converge across cultures or instead amplifies local divergence by teaching different reflexive-calibration norms); and it must respect the distinction between cultural variation in first-order intuition and cultural variation in meta-cognitive standards for when an intuition counts as data.

Flag any pair of desiderata you think cannot be jointly satisfied, and identify the single requirement that, if taken seriously, eliminates the largest number of existing approaches (whether universalist rationalism, strong cultural constructivism, or dual-process hybrid models). Then score those three contenders briefly against the full list.

Take a committed position: defend which of these requirements are genuinely non-negotiable versus which ones are prejudices of Anglophone analytic philosophy, and state clearly whether you think a unified theory is still possible or whether the desiderata themselves force a partitioned account. Keep the weight on the criteria and their motivating cases rather than on the scoring. A table is fine. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations.
