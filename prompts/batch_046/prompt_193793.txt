Daniel Dennett and Gregg Caruso have argued for years over whether retributive punishment requires basic desert and whether determinism undermines it. Dennett, working within a compatibilist framework, maintains that moral responsibility tracks capacities for rational self-control and that punishment can be justified instrumentally—through deterrence, rehabilitation, and the maintenance of social cooperation—without requiring that offenders metaphysically deserve suffering in some deep, non-derivative sense. Caruso, advancing a free-will skeptical position, contends that because agents do not ultimately cause themselves—their characters and actions are the product of factors beyond their control—no one ever truly deserves punishment; consequentialist and restorative aims may remain, but retribution is always unjustified. Despite extensive exchange, neither camp has conceded ground: Dennett charges that Caruso collapses causal determination into moral excuse, while Caruso replies that Dennett’s notion of freedom offers only a hollow, socially constructed substitute for genuine desert. I suspect the impasse is not about hidden empirical data or formal errors but about the architecture of justification itself—specifically, whether a theory of punishment must render suffering morally fitting to the offender or merely license it for broader social ends, and whether desert names a primitive moral relation or a derivative institutional practice. Is that diagnosis correct? Or does a deeper disagreement—about the ontology of agency, about the evidential authority of folk-psychological intuitions, or about where the burden of proof lies for claims of moral responsibility—actually sustain the stalemate?
