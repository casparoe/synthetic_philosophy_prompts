Assume Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz is presented with the core framework of Integrated Information Theory (IIT) as articulated by Giulio Tononi—specifically, the identification of consciousness with a maximal value of integrated information (Φ) within a causal complex, together with the exclusion postulate that, within any region, only the complex possessing the highest Φ counts as a conscious subject, while overlapping subsets with lower Φ are mere aggregates lacking phenomenal existence. Drawing strictly on Leibniz’s own metaphysical commitments—his doctrine of monads, the hierarchical distinction between dominant and subordinate monads within organic bodies, the principle of continuity (that nature makes no leaps), and his denial of causal interaction between substances—predict in detail how Leibniz would evaluate IIT’s localization of consciousness to a unique maximal complex. Would he regard the exclusion postulate as a mathematical formalization of the dominance of one monad over its organic parts, or would he reject it as illegitimately suppressing the genuine, if confused, perceptions of subordinate monads? Furthermore, address the specific hypothetical case of a bifurcated neural grid—analogous to a split-brain architecture—in which two spatially disjoint subsets of elements each generate an identical local maximum of Φ. Would Leibniz allow for two co-dominant souls, invoke pre-established harmony to preserve the unity of apperception across both, or argue that Φ fails to track substantial unity altogether because true monadic unity is primitive and not reducible to causal integration? In constructing your answer, explain whether Leibniz’s commitment to the principle of continuity can be reconciled with IIT’s sharp, binary threshold between conscious complexes and non-conscious aggregates.
