I am drafting a philosophical novel in which a central character, the decision theorist Dr. Elena Voss, has staked out public positions on several paradoxical choice scenarios. Her verdicts, delivered across earlier chapters, are these. First, on the canonical Newcomb problem—an almost infallible predictor, Box A containing a transparent ten thousand dollars, Box B containing either nothing or one million depending on the prediction—she argues unequivocally for taking only Box B, on the ground that rational choice should honor evidential correlation even when causal influence is absent. Second, on a medical analog in which a patient’s genetic profile predicts spontaneous remission with high reliability but administering a placebo does not alter the underlying biology, she urges taking the placebo, treating predictive correlation as practically authoritative regardless of causal mechanism. Third, on a low-stakes variant in which the predictor is only fifty-five percent accurate and the disparity between boxes is negligible, she advises taking both boxes without reservation. Fourth, on a transparency variant in which the predictor does not observe the final action but scans the intermediate reasoning steps that produce it, she maintains that rational deliberation should be randomized precisely to defeat such predictive access, even at the cost of coherence.

In the scene I am now composing, Voss confronts a case I will call the degradation-cost scenario. The predictor remains ninety-eight percent accurate; Box A holds ten thousand dollars; Box B holds either nothing or one million according to the prediction. However, before the agent selects any box, she may expend one thousand dollars—deductible from Box A’s contents, leaving nine thousand—to activate a cognitive blocker that causally degrades the predictor’s accuracy to fifty percent. The blocker is undetectable to the predictor once engaged, operates purely causally, and does not alter the physical contents of the boxes. The agent must therefore decide, in sequence, whether to pay the degradation cost, and then whether to open Box A, Box B, or both.

What verdict should Voss render—pay and open both, pay and open only B, decline the blocker and open both, or decline and open only B—if she wishes to preserve consistency with her four earlier judgments? Identify the closest analog among her prior cases, and state honestly whether her track record genuinely underdetermines the answer. If the precedents do underdetermine it, specify the principle she would be adopting with each available choice. Further, propose the single hidden rule—whether evidential decision theory, causal decision theory, a reliability-threshold hybrid, or some other framework—that best unifies her earlier verdicts, and name the one further case that would most sharply distinguish that rule from its nearest competitor.
