I've been thinking about the frame problem—how we know what hasn't changed when one thing does. Here are my judgments, and I want the principle behind them, if there is one.

If a program learns "the ceramic cup was moved from the table to the sink," I think it should update location and that the table is now empty, but it should not check whether the room's temperature changed. If it learns "the cup was dropped and shattered," it should update that the cup is now fragments and is no longer intact; again, temperature feels outside the frame. If it learns "the cup was sold to a museum," I think ownership and location update, but I do not think its listing in the original owner's home-insurance policy updates—that feels as remote as temperature.

But here is the tension. If the program learns "the cup's owner got married," I suddenly feel her tax-filing status should be updated, even though marriage is legally distant and physically irrelevant to the cup. Meanwhile, if it learns "the owner bought a new hat," I do not think any legal document about the cup should change, which is consistent with the insurance case. So physical distance is not the rule, yet legal distance sometimes matters and sometimes does not.

Is there a coherent principle—perhaps about the kind of property being tracked, or about which causal pathways are "live"—that keeps temperature and insurance outside the frame while pulling tax status inside when marriage occurs? Or must I abandon one of these verdicts? Please state the principle precisely, say what it predicts for melting the cup into glass for a window—does atomic composition stay in the frame?—and tell me honestly which of my judgments it cannot accommodate.
