I have been trying to determine exactly what inferential license the Cartesian cogito grants, and my judgments about specific cases feel partly ordered but partly in tension. Here are my verdicts. First, when Descartes reasons from the fact that he is thinking to the conclusion that he exists, I take this to establish the existence of a thinking thing, but not yet that this thing is a substance with any particular nature beyond thinking. Second, if someone in a vivid dream asserts that because they are thinking they exist, I think they genuinely establish their own existence at that moment, even though the content of their thoughts is largely false and they are not awake; the proof survives false premises about the external world. Third, if an artificial system outputs the claim that because it processes information it exists, I do not think it has performed an equivalent proof of its own existence, even though the logical form is parallel; something about first-person access seems missing, yet I cannot say it is merely biological embodiment, since I would grant the proof to a disembodied Cartesian soul. Fourth, if a person suffering from severe amnesia asserts that because they are aware they exist, I think they prove their present existence but not that they are the same persisting self who performed actions yesterday; continuity is not secured. Fifth, if a philosopher argues that the premise I think already presupposes an I and therefore the reasoning is circular, I reject the circularity charge, but I also do not think the reasoning proves that the I is a unified ego rather than a bundle of perceptions; the inference to unity feels like an overreach. Sixth, however, I feel uneasy about my own treatment of the artificial system: if first-person access is the key, then a system with perfect introspective access to its own states should count, yet my intuition resists this. Is there a single principle—perhaps concerning the mode of access, the immunity to error through misidentification, or the performative structure of the assertion—that captures all six verdicts? If so, state it precisely, show which clause handles each case, predict the verdict for a sleepwalking agent who sincerely asserts that they are thinking and therefore exist, and identify which of my judgments must be abandoned if simplicity demands it.
