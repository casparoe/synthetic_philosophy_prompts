I retired from an evolutionary-biology lab about five years ago and since then I've been trying to make sense of philosophy—mostly Dennett's claim that the self is just a center of narrative gravity, with no hidden Cartesian ego behind it. I was arguing with my daughter (she's in law, so she cares about harm and obligations) and I think I pushed the idea into a reductio that proves way too much, but I can't see where the wheels come off.

Here is the chain, numbered so I don't loose my place:
(1) If personal identity is nothing but narrative gravity, then there is no substrate—no biological continuity, no soul—that makes you you apart from the coherance and gravitational pull of your autobiographical thread.
(2) Now imagine two people, M and N, whose lives have become so entangled—shared diaries, identical emotional responses, co-authored memories—that their narrative centers have merged into one gravitational well with two residual nodes.
(3) If the merged well is stable and neither node adds unique coherance, then deleting one node doesn't damage the story; it's like trimming a redundant loop in software.
(4) But if there's no damage to the narrative and no hidden substrate to harm, then killing one residual node isn't harming a person—it's just editing a redundant subplot.
(5) Clearly, killing someone in that situation is still horrific.
(6) So narrative gravity can't be the whole story; there must be some non-narrative fact that makes each node a seperate moral subject.

I feel like step (3) or (4) sneaks in an equivocation, maybe between narrative coherence and moral status, but I can't name it. Is the argument actually valid, and I'm just resistent because I'm attached to old humanist instincts? If I have to choose, I'd rather keep (1) than (5)—I like the idea that we're stories—but I want to know what keeping (1) costs me. Where exactly does this go wrong?
