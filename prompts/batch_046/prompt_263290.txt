The debate over evolutionary adaptationism and the Gould-Lewontin spandrels critique has persisted since the late 1970s without meaningful convergence. Adaptationists tend to treat natural selection as the default explanatory framework for widespread, complex organismal traits: if a feature is common and intricate, the burden falls on the skeptic to demonstrate that it is not an adaptation, and functional narratives are constructed to show how selection could have shaped it. Critics, following Gould and Lewontin, argue that many features are spandrels—structural byproducts of other adaptive changes or of architectural constraints—and that adaptationists systematically conflate correlation with selection for proof of design, substituting plausible-sounding functional stories for direct evidence of selection history. Both camps accept the same basic evolutionary mechanisms and routinely invoke the same canonical illustrations, including the panda's radial sesamoid and debates over the human chin. Yet after decades of exchange in evolutionary biology and philosophy of science, neither side has substantially revised its core methodological stance.

My working hypothesis is that the impasse is not primarily empirical—successive waves of genomic and developmental data have not settled it—but structural: the two sides may be answering different questions under the single heading How should we explain biological form? One side seeks the best predictive or engineering explanation of a trait; the other demands a historically adequate causal narrative with stricter evidential thresholds. Still, I am unsure whether the stall instead reflects a hidden normative disagreement—perhaps about whether biology ought to emulate the explanatory form of physics—or bedrock intuitions about the prevalence of contingency in nature.

Please diagnose the disagreement. Is it verbal, turning on competing definitions of adaptation or explanation? Is a concealed empirical or formal question actually driving it—for instance, the statistical base rate of spandrels relative to adaptations, or formal limits on selectionist modeling? Are the parties pursuing distinct explanatory projects—functional analysis versus historical reconstruction—that should be separated rather than reconciled? Or is the question ill-posed because adaptationism denotes a family of methodological attitudes rather than a single thesis? Given your diagnosis, what would count as progress: a quantitative meta-analysis of trait explanations, a formal model that distinguishes selectable structures from architectural byproducts, a clarification of explanatory norms in evolutionary biology, or an argument that resolution is impossible and why?
