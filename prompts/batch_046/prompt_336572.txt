I have been talking with a senior colleague in philosophy of biology about how she draws the line between species, and I have collected a small set of her judgments on contested cases. She treats the ring-arranged greenish warbler populations—adjacent groups around the Tibetan plateau interbreed, but the two ends where they overlap do not—as a single species. She considers two orchid populations separated by an ocean for millions of years, morphologically identical and using the same pollinators but with no possible gene flow, to be one species. She classifies two bird populations that inhabit the same forest but breed six months apart—morphologically nearly identical and fertile when crossed in captivity, yet never encountering each other naturally—as two distinct species. She holds that Neanderthals and anatomically modern humans are separate species, despite well-documented interbreeding and close genetic relatedness, because of sustained morphological, cognitive, and behavioral divergence over hundreds of thousands of years. Finally, she judges that a bacterial lineage which acquired a plasmid allowing it to metabolize an industrial toxin, now restricted to contaminated soil but still able to exchange genes horizontally with its ancestral population, remains part of the original species rather than constituting a new one.

Now I want to know what she would say about a newly reported case: a microbial population discovered in an isolated deep aquifer is metabolically and morphologically indistinguishable from a widespread surface bacterium, but genomic analysis indicates divergence for approximately twenty million years with no natural mechanism for gene flow; in the laboratory, however, researchers have demonstrated that the two populations can exchange genetic material through a specific plasmid pathway. Would she classify the aquifer population as the same species or a different one? In your answer, please identify at least two competing principles—such as reproductive community, ecological coherence, or phylogenetic separation—that could consistently explain her earlier verdicts, and show how each principle predicts a different outcome for the aquifer case. Give your best-guess prediction, explain which of her prior judgments is the closest analog and why, and be explicit about whether her track record genuinely underdetermines the verdict and, if so, what conceptual commitment she would be making with either choice. If you believe the rules can be distinguished, propose one further hypothetical case that would settle the matter. Please respond in American English, using a formal and polished register.
