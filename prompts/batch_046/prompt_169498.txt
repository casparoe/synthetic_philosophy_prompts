I've been working through what I take to be a consistent package across moral and epistemic normativity, but I'm unsure whether the pieces actually fit. Here is where I've landed:

(1) Moral rightness is indirect: an act is right just in case it is what a fully informed, coherent, unbiased deliberator—who does not take moral principles as direct premises—would choose, given the structural features of the situation.

(2) This rules out one-boxing in Newcomb's problem. Once the predictor has fixed the contents of the boxes, treating one's present mental state as evidence about that fixed state is not world-directed practical reasoning; an ideal deliberator should not let evidential correlations override causal structure.

(3) At the same time, I think you should pay the driver in Parfit's hitchhiker. The ride is already given, but an ideal agent values the coherence of her temporal commitments; violating the ex-ante disposition to pay undermines the rational architecture that made the agreement possible, with no compensating gain.

(4) I extend the same indirect structure to epistemic norms. Rational belief is not direct real-time evidence-tracking but what survives idealized reflection on explanatory coherence, predictive scope, and simplicity—much as solving a Bongard-style induction task requires selecting the concept that organizes sparse examples rather than the feature set with the highest local statistical fit.

(5) I also hold a strict separation thesis: actual agents should never try to run the ideal-deliberation algorithm locally. Doing so collapses indirect normativity into direct rule-following and invites motivated reasoning.

(6) So in practice, actual agents should stick to direct, non-derived heuristics: honor your debts, avoid evidential reasoning about causally fixed past events, and rely on trained conceptual judgments rather than attempting on-the-spot theoretical reconstruction.

My question is whether these six commitments hang together. Specifically: (2) and (3) both concern reasoning about states that are, in some sense, already fixed—the boxes are filled, the ride is complete—yet I reject the reasoning in one case and endorse the action in the other. Is there a principled distinction that an ideal deliberator can draw, or am I helping myself to an asymmetry? Second, (4) and (5)-(6) seem to pull in opposite directions: if rational belief is what survives ideal reflection, but actual agents are barred from engaging in that reflection and must use direct heuristics, in what sense does the indirect norm actually govern actual belief formation—say, when someone is confronting a genuinely novel Bongard-like classification task? Finally, is there an apparent tension I'm missing that actually dissolves on closer inspection—perhaps involving the status of coherence or simplicity as premises within (1)?

Please audit the package. Order the tensions by severity, specify for each what would have to be modified or abandoned to restore consistency, and say what the cheapest repair costs in terms of the other commitments. Don't lecture me on indirect normativity in general; I want to know whether my particular configuration is stable.
