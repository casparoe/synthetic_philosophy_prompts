I have been trying to derive the proper scope of citizen concealment from the state by synthesizing three regulatory ideals—German informationelle Selbstbestimmung, American transparency-of-government norms, and the Chinese social-credit framework—into a single normative argument. The result appears to collapse democratic citizenship into an intolerable choice between total opacity and total transparency. I cannot locate the precise failure.

(1) A democratic state's legitimacy rests on mutual accountability: citizens are answerable for actions that affect the public order, and the state is answerable for its exercise of power.

(2) Under German constitutional doctrine, informationelle Selbstbestimmung protects not mere privacy but the informational conditions of autonomous personhood; data that constitute one's social identity may be withheld because disclosure is not a precondition for democratic co-authorship but an intrusion upon it.

(3) American openness norms invert the visibility relation: the primary danger to democracy is state secrecy, not citizen secrecy; citizens therefore bear no general duty to reveal themselves, only a duty not to obstruct legitimate public inquiry.

(4) The Chinese social-credit model treats citizen transparency as infrastructural: predictive governance requires continuous behavioral data, so concealment is not a private choice but a withdrawal from the social coordination mechanism.

(5) These three frameworks share a hidden premise: that the boundary between hideable and revealable information is stable across institutional contexts.

(6) But modern democratic states possess the administrative and technological capacity to shift between the American and Chinese models without formal constitutional rupture—democratic institutions can be repurposed for predictive governance without altering their democratic form.

(7) Consequently, any information disclosed under the protective assumptions of (2) or (3) can be retrospectively integrated into a (4)-style predictive system, turning voluntary disclosure into involuntary exposure.

(8) By a precautionary argument: if rational agents face a non-zero risk that disclosure in context C₁ will be exploited in context C₂, and if the cost of exploitation in C₂ exceeds the benefit of disclosure in C₁, then agents ought to withhold all information that is technically transferable between contexts.

(9) Given that all personal data are technically transferable, the precautionary principle entails total citizen opacity.

(10) Yet total opacity makes democratic accountability in (1) impossible, and therefore undermines the very legitimacy that justified the inquiry in the first place.

Which step should I reject? I am particularly reluctant to abandon (2)—that informational self-determination is a condition of autonomy—and (5)—that institutional contexts are unstable—because both seem descriptively accurate of how data regimes actually function. If I must preserve the intuition that democratic citizenship requires some shared informational space, I do not see how to do so without denying either the possibility of institutional repurposing or the rationality of precautionary concealment. Where does the argument go wrong, and what does rejecting that step commit me to?
