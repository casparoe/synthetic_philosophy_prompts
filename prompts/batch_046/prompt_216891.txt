I have been working with a specific metaphysical interpretation of Jain anekāntavāda—particularly the syādvāda doctrine articulated through the saptabhaṅgī (sevenfold predication)—that reads it not as an expression of epistemic caution but as a substantive ontology of aspect-relative properties. On this view, contradictory predicates applied to one substance (for instance, that an object is both permanent in its substantial nature and impermanent in its modal modifications) do not conflict because they are indexed to distinct, equally real ontological dimensions called nayas: the dravya-naya captures the enduring substance, the paryāya-naya captures its changing modes, and so forth. The syāt qualifier—often rendered as "perhaps" or "in some respect"—functions here not as a modal hedge or admission of ignorance, but as an irreducible indexical marker indicating that the predicate holds with respect to that specific aspect-structure without claiming exclusivity or exhaustion. If this reading holds, then anekāntavāda offers a structured middle path between classical bivalence and modern dialetheism: contradiction is never instantiated within a single aspect, yet no aspect is metaphysically privileged, and the substance genuinely comprises all of them.

This interpretation seems to shield Jain philosophy from the standard charge of logical incoherence, and it also distinguishes anekāntavāda sharply from contemporary perspectivism or contextualism, since the nayas are treated as objective features of reality rather than mental or linguistic frames. Yet I am trying to determine what would genuinely count against it. Which interpretive or philosophical considerations would force us to read the syāt operator epistemically or modally rather than as an ontological index? Are there internal tensions—perhaps in how spurious or partial standpoints are identified, or in the criteria for distinguishing genuine from false aspects—that undermine treating all seven predications as ontologically on par? Could it turn out that the saptabhaṅgī is best understood as a dialectical or pedagogical taxonomy rather than a metaphysical classification of property-types? More broadly, if defenders respond to every counterexample by insisting that the critic is confined to a single naya, does this render the view unfalsifiable in a way that should worry us? I want an honest map of the view's exposure: which objections would be decisive, which merely awkward, whether committed defenders should concede any of them, and whether the insulation of the position is a virtue or a vice.
