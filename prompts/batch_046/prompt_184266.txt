As a software engineer with no formal philosophy background, I have been trying to think about consciousness through the lens of interface design: perhaps it is not a direct representation of underlying computation but a user-level abstraction, possibly illusory in the way a graphical desktop hides machine code. I have formed verdicts about several concrete scenarios, but they feel difficult to reconcile into one principle, and I want to know whether I should revise one or accept a more complex account.

Case 1: A robotics system running on distributed servers includes a "self-monitoring" module that outputs first-person status reports for maintenance engineers—statements like "I am experiencing latency in my left actuator." There is no biological substrate, no affect, and the module is purely instrumental. My verdict: there is no consciousness here. The "I" is a grammatical convenience for human operators, comparable to a car navigation system saying "I am turning left."

Case 2: A human patient with blindsight can navigate obstacles and discriminate shapes in a "blind" visual field while sincerely reporting no visual experience whatsoever. My verdict: the processing is genuinely unconscious. There is no hidden inner screen or private qualitative display that the patient is failing to access; the absence of report tracks the absence of conscious vision.

Case 3: In a split-brain patient with a severed corpus callosum, the left hemisphere generates elaborate confabulations to explain actions that were actually initiated by the right hemisphere. I feel strongly that there is still only one conscious person, not two separate minds. At the same time, I think this shows consciousness is a post-hoc narrative interface—one module constructing a unified story for distributed processes. The tension is that if consciousness is this kind of unified user interface, I should perhaps expect two interfaces here, yet I do not.

Case 4: A user in high-fidelity virtual reality stands near a virtual cliff. She knows intellectually that the display is pixels and that she is physically safe, yet she feels genuine fear and a compelling sense of "presence"—of actually being there. My verdict: the fear and physiological arousal are real mental states, but the presence—the feeling of being genuinely located in that space—is an illusion produced by predictive processing, like a well-rendered UI that obscures the underlying hardware.

Case 5: A future brain-computer interface allows direct implantation of procedural skills. After receiving a skill, the recipient reports, "This feels like something I have always been able to do," with no episodic memory of acquisition and no sense of an external source. My verdict: this undermines the idea that consciousness requires a continuous autobiographical narrative, but I still think there is a single unified "user" experiencing the skill, not a fragmented or absent subject.

These cases seem to point toward consciousness as a functional user illusion—real in its causal and emotional effects, but not a direct window onto underlying neural processes. Yet there is a hard conflict: in Case 1 I deny consciousness to a system that also produces unified first-person reports, while in Case 3 I affirm a single consciousness despite clear fragmentation of the underlying "system." In Case 4 I separate real affect from illusory presence, but I do not have a clear rule for when an interface-component should be treated as real versus illusory.

Is there one precise principle—perhaps involving integration, embodiment, predictive modeling, or functional role—that delivers all five verdicts? If not, which of my verdicts should I overturn, and what does the simpler principle predict? Please state the principle exactly, identify which case motivates each clause, apply it to a case I have not listed (specifically, a hive-mind collective with shared sensory input but distributed, non-overlapping decision-making nodes), and be explicit about which of my intuitions the principle cannot accommodate.

Please also say what would change your mind: which specific argument, empirical finding, or conceptual distinction would lead you to a different principle or cause you to overturn one of my verdicts, and identify where in this reasoning you are most likely to be wrong.
