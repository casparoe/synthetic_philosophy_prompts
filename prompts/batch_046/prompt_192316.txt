I'm a doctoral student in comparative moral philosophy preparing a workshop contribution on whether Western liberal individualism or East Asian relational philosophy provides the more accurate account of how persons and communities are related. Rather than writing a standard comparative analysis, I want to produce an adversarial-collaboration document that a committed defender of "bounded-agency" liberalism—who holds that the moral person is ontologically prior to social attachments and that obligations arise mainly from consent or universal reason—and a committed defender of "role-constituted" relationalism—who holds that the self is constituted through reciprocal social roles and that obligations are often indexed to particular relationships rather than universal rules—could both sign after sustained good-faith disagreement.

Please draft this joint statement in American English. First, provide a numbered section of agreed claims about the ontology of the self, the nature of moral obligation, and the empirical reality of cross-cultural variation in self-construal, without citing specific studies or texts. Second, present a disagreements table that states each camp's view in wording its own proponents would accept, avoiding straw-man formulations. Third, identify at least three cruxes—sub-questions such that resolving them would move at least one side—including whether moral obligations can be genuinely inherited through social roles without explicit consent, whether relational identity is compatible with robust personal autonomy, and whether universal rights can accommodate role-specific duties without collapsing into relativism.

For each crux, specify a concrete case that both sides accept as relevant and not question-begging: imagine a family-owned stakeholder corporation that collapses due to fraud by its deceased founder; the founder's adult grandchildren, who never held shares or management positions but were raised within the family ritual system, face demands from creditors and family elders to contribute personal assets to restore solvency and honor. Both sides should record in advance what they predict about the moral status of these obligations—whether they are genuine, whether they are transferable, and whether the state should enforce or merely recognize them—so that predictions can be tested against shared intuitions. Finally, note whether any portion of the dispute is merely verbal (for instance, if "autonomy" functions in incommensurable ways across traditions) and, if so, which portion. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; the document should stand without web access. Maintain a formal tone appropriate for a philosophy workshop.
