hey, I'm working on an essay for my science-and-ideas column and I wanted to run the outline by you before I pitch it to my editor. The basic argument is that "collapse" is mostly a retrospective narrative we slap on complex societies that changed shape, not actually failed. I'm looking at three cases people always cite: the Late Bronze Age, the Classic Maya, and Easter Island. For the Bronze Age I'm saying the palace economies around 1200 BCE didn't suffer some universal civilizational heart attack—what happened was a network failure where specialized redistribution broke down, but local agriculture and literacy (like Linear B) just got simpler. With the Maya I'm pushing back on the idea that they "disappeared" in the 9th century—really the southern lowlands depopulated and political power shifted north and to the coast, so its more of a decentralization than a collapse. Then Easter Island: the moai-building halt and deforestation gets taught as ecological suicide, but I'm framing it as "complexity shedding"—like a society molting—where ritual investment pivoted away from massive statuary because the social payoff changed, not because everyone died off. Its definately not the same as societal death.

I'm wondering if you can comment on a few things. First, am I overstating the continuity in the Bronze Age—did the shift away from palace economies actually preserve local wellbeing, or am I romanticizing it? Second, does the "complexity shedding" idea for Easter Island sound like a useful reframing, or just jargon that obscures real suffering? And third, I keep seeing people conflate the Maya decentralization with the Bronze Age fragmentation—are these comparable in any meaningful way, or am I forcing a comparison just because both get mislread as collapse? I dont need a bibliography or anything, just honest feedback on whether the argument holds water and where it might fall apart.
