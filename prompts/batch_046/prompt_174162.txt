As a software engineer without formal training in philosophy, I have been trying to understand whether consequentialist moral reasoning collapses under scale in ways analogous to system-resource exhaustion. I understand the classic demandingness objection to act-utilitarianism—that it appears to require surrendering nearly all disposable income to maximally effective causes until marginal utility equalizes—but I want to compare its severity against four related pressure points. These are: (1) the integrity objection, that relentless utility-maximization crowds out the long-term personal projects that constitute a coherent identity; (2) a cognitive-overhead framing I am importing from software architecture, in which continuous moral optimization operates like an unoptimized garbage-collection cycle, depleting the mental bandwidth required for creativity, deep relationships, or strategic planning; (3) the perverse-incentive objection, that anticipating extreme demands causes agents to reduce baseline moral engagement defensively; and (4) the reciprocity objection, that demandingness violates a tacit contractualist expectation that obligations are bounded by what others can reasonably be expected to bear. Please rank all five—classic demandingness, integrity, cognitive-overhead, perverse-incentive, and reciprocity—by how severely each undermines act-utilitarianism specifically (not rule-utilitarianism or other consequentialist variants), assigning each a score from 1 to 10. Provide a one-sentence justification for each score. Then identify the two that are closest in severity but diverge on whether the core flaw is quantitative overload versus a qualitative mismatch between moral demands and human psychology, and state in one sentence what empirical observation would distinguish them.
