As a novelist constructing a long-form narrative about characters who undergo discontinuous embodiment, I find myself returning to the same cluster of judgments about personal survival, yet I cannot tell whether they cohere. I want the principle behind them—stated precisely, with the motivating case for each clause, and an honest account of which of my verdicts must be sacrificed if the principle is to remain simple.

Here are my verdicts on concrete scenarios.

First, the standard teletransportation case: my body is destroyed on Earth, the structural and psychological information is transmitted to Mars, and a physically continuous replica is assembled there with my complete memory and character intact. My judgment is that I survive; the replica is me, and the discontinuity in physical substrate does not break survival.

Second, gradual synthetic replacement: over ten years, every neuron is substituted by a functionally identical synthetic equivalent, with all causal connections preserved. During this process, I write an autobiographical novel that profoundly alters my values and self-conception. At the end, my psychology differs markedly from the original in affective orientation, yet I judge that I have survived; the change was continuous and self-authored.

Third, non-destructive scanning: the same transmission occurs, but my original body remains intact on Earth. A perfect replica exists on Mars. Here I judge that the replica is not me; I remain the Earth-bound original.

Fourth, symmetric duplication: my brain is divided, and both hemispheres are placed into new bodies, each receiving a complete psychological profile. Both bodies wake with my memories and personality. I judge that I do not survive as either; duplication seems incompatible with being the single continuer.

Fifth, asymmetric resolution of that same duplication: immediately after activation, one of the two bodies is destroyed, leaving only one with the complete psychological profile. In this case, I judge that I do survive as the remaining one.

Sixth, reconstructed alteration: my body is destroyed, the information is transmitted correctly, but the reconstruction process introduces a systematic, unintended shift in emotional temperament—say, all fear responses are dampened and risk-assessment patterns are altered—while leaving memory and narrative self-understanding intact. I judge that this is not me; survival requires not merely psychological similarity but preservation of the specific functional character that constituted my psychology.

These judgments seem to pull in different directions. In the second case, I permit substantial psychological change through gradual, internal transformation; in the sixth, I deny survival for a smaller, externally caused alteration. In the fourth and fifth, my survival appears to depend on whether a competitor exists, which feels metaphysically suspicious.

Is there a single principle—whether psychological continuity with uniqueness, a causal-chain condition, a narrative-identity constraint, or something else—that captures all six verdicts? If so, state it precisely, indicate which clause addresses which case, and predict its verdict on a case I have not listed: continuous streaming of psychological states to a digital substrate over years, with the biological body gradually atrophying until only the digital version remains, with no discrete moment of destruction or replication.

If no single principle accommodates all six, which verdict should I overturn, and what is the simplest coherent account that results? I am especially interested in whether the tension between the second and sixth cases can be resolved without collapsing into either pure psychological similarity or pure physical continuity, and whether the asymmetry between the fourth and fifth cases reveals that uniqueness is genuinely constitutive of survival or merely an epistemic artifact.
