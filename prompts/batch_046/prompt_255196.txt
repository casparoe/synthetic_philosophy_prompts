I am trying to establish whether there is an established term for a particular reversal of explanatory direction that I keep encountering in debates about narratives that straddle the boundary between legend and recorded history — notably in discussions of the Trojan War, the Arthurian cycle, and the Exodus. The pattern unfolds as follows. Initially, the narrative is regarded as largely fictional or at least historically unverifiable, though it may contain scattered echoes of real events. Then an ambiguous archaeological or textual discovery emerges: it could account for a small kernel of the story, or it could be an unrelated occurrence that later tradition borrowed and magnified. At that point, the burden of argument appears to invert. Rather than asking how a real event might have generated the larger narrative, scholars and popular writers begin asking how the real event can be accommodated within the narrative they already possess. The legend thereby becomes the framework through which the evidence is interpreted, rather than the phenomenon requiring explanation. Consequently, the story acquires a kind of default historical standing, and sceptics are expected to disprove the larger narrative rather than proponents being required to demonstrate its development from the kernel.

I have observed this with the archaeological work at Hisarlik, with various sub-Roman finds associated with Arthur, and with Levantine and Egyptian discoveries relating to the Exodus. I am not referring to euhemerism, since that proceeds by reducing myth to underlying historical fact; here the movement is opposite, with the myth structuring the reading of the fact. Nor am I describing ordinary confirmation bias, because the shift involves a change in which entity is taken as explanans and which as explanandum. Does this reversal have a specific name in historiography or philosophy of history? If not, how might one define its boundaries precisely — in particular, the point at which a legend ceases to be the object of explanation and becomes the explanatory framework — and how does it differ from treating the narrative merely as a working historical hypothesis? Please answer in British English, and please do not provide bibliographies or reading lists.
