Question: Hume contends that money is not itself a form of national wealth but rather an instrument facilitating exchange. Drawing on his essays on money and trade, explain how this premise, together with the price-specie-flow mechanism, undermines the mercantilist programme of maintaining a permanently favourable balance of trade.

Student answer: Hume regards money as a token representing labour and commodities, not as wealth in its own right, so amassing bullion cannot enrich a nation. Mercantilists wrongly suppose that a continuous trade surplus draws in gold and thereby strengthens the state. Hume shows that this is impossible: an inflow of specie raises domestic prices, exports become uncompetitive, imports are encouraged, and gold flows back out until equilibrium is restored. Hence any policy designed to preserve a surplus—such as high tariffs—is self-defeating. Hume also adheres to a strict quantity theory of money, according to which the value of money is always inversely proportional to its quantity; consequently, an increase in the money supply can only raise prices in exact proportion and can never produce a temporary stimulus to employment or output. Lastly, Hume defends free trade on Ricardian grounds: countries should specialise according to comparative advantage, so restricting imports harms both trading partners.
