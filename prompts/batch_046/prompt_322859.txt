Write a proposal for a one-semester graduate research paper (approximately 6,000 words) in environmental and political philosophy addressing the following problem: conventional ethical frameworks for space colonization treat planetary environments either as resources to be preserved intact or as raw material to be engineered for human use. But suppose a settlement is built upon a synthetic ecology—every organism engineered for interdependence in a closed-loop system in which no species can survive independently—creating a functional whole that is neither natural wilderness nor a conventional human artifact. Does this generate obligations that are irreducible to either conservationist duties toward nature or contractual duties toward colonists? Frame the central question precisely: is the synthetic interdependent system itself a locus of moral consideration, or does consideration remain distributed only among its constituent organisms and the human populations that depend upon it? Explain why standard answers—pure holistic environmental ethics, pure contractarian obligations to future persons, or pure aggregative welfare calculations—leave this case unresolved. Describe your analytical approach: map the logical space of collective agency and distributed welfare theories onto engineered ecologies, then test whether any existing normative framework can accommodate obligations that seem to attach to the system's functional integrity without collapsing into anthropomorphism or mysticism. Specify what a positive result would look like (for instance, a defensible criterion for non-reductive moral status in synthetic interdependent systems) and what would count as failure (for instance, a demonstration that all apparent obligations to the system reduce without remainder to interests of colonists or individual organisms). Conclude with an honest assessment of scope: which part of the argument—conceptual analysis of collective agency applied to synthetic ecology, or normative evaluation of resulting obligations—is tractable within one semester, and which portion risks overreach given the metaphysical questions involved. Do not include a bibliography, reading list, or citation requests; you may refer to general philosophical positions only by their standard descriptive labels.
