Having retired from experimental physics and recently turned to aesthetics, I find myself struck by the analogy philosophers repeatedly invoke: art, they say, is like a game—specifically like chess or football—in that it lacks any essential defining feature and instead exhibits only a network of family resemblances among its instances. Thinkers such as Morris Weitz use this comparison to argue that "art" is an open concept, resistant to necessary and sufficient conditions, just as "game" resists definition beyond Wittgensteinian overlap. I want to audit this analogy with the rigor one applies to a physical model. First, lay out the explicit correspondence: what in the domain of art maps onto rules, players, objectives, and the boundary between playing and not playing? Second, identify precisely where the mapping fractures—for instance, games are constituted by their rules in a way art may not be, and a move that violates chess rules is not chess, whereas an object that violates institutional conditions might still be art, or might not. Third, examine what the source domain quietly imports: does treating art as game-like smuggle in the idea that art is voluntary, competitive, or governed by explicit norms that participants must accept? Fourth, assess whether Weitz's conclusion—that art is therefore indefinable—survives once these disanalogies are weighted and separated into those that undermine the argument and those that are merely decorative. If the analogy fails to carry the anti-essentialist conclusion, is there a more honest comparison—perhaps art as a functional kind like a tool, or as a social kind like currency—that captures the institutional theorists' intuition without importing game-specific baggage? Please state which verdict on definability, if any, the audited analogy can actually support, and what a repaired principle would predict for a borderline case such as a found object placed in a gallery without the artist's authorization.
