In dementia care we treat someone as the exact same legal person even after they've lost nearly every narrative thread—no memories, no future plans, no coherent story about who they are—so thier will, property, and medical rights all stay intact. But then if that same person seems content eating pudding in a nursing home, ethicists often argue over whether to honor an old advance directive that said don't keep me alive if I don't recognize my family. What theory of the self does that whole practice presuppose? Does it need something like Dennett's center of narrative gravity to explain why rights stick around, or can pure biological continuity do all the work? And if narrative coherence is really what grounds identity, why do we still assign the same legal standing to the pudding-eating patient instead of treating them as a new narrative center that just inherited an estate?
