Having spent a career in condensed-matter physics and only recently turned to moral philosophy with the same appetite for logical structure, I want a taxonomy—structured as a decision tree with concrete branching questions rather than a list of named camps—that classifies actual positions on whether a present choice that determines who will exist can be morally wrong. Begin with one practical case: Society must choose between Path X (moderate climate mitigation, yielding a future population of ten billion people with high welfare) and Path Y (delayed mitigation, yielding climate stress followed by recovery and a disjoint future population of ten billion people with moderately lower welfare). The first node must ask whether Path Y wrongs any particular individual who lives only under Path Y, phrased so that a respondent need not know Parfit’s name or any technical label to answer; include branches for “yes—through an existence-comparative harm account,” “no,” and “the framing is malformed.” From the “no” and “malformed” branches, proceed to whether the respondent accepts reasons that are not tied to particular individuals (impersonal reasons); from the “yes” branch, proceed to whether that harm can be weighed against aggregate benefits. For those reaching the impersonal-reasons stage, split on whether welfare is aggregated as a total sum, as an average, or against a critical-level threshold. Then split on the mere-addition judgment: whether bringing into existence a life with welfare just above zero is always good, good only if it does not lower average welfare, or not an unconditional good—thereby linking the tree to the population-paradox literature. Each leaf should carry a precise label (e.g., “person-affecting restriction,” “impersonal totalism,” “critical-level sufficientarianism,” “deflationary no-harm view,” or “unnamed” if unoccupied) and exactly one sentence stating its most awkward commitment, such as being forced to deny that a reckless reproductive choice producing a child with a barely worth-living life is wrong. Please flag any pair of leaves your tree genuinely cannot separate—especially any ambiguity between strict deontological restrictions and the person-affecting stance—and briefly diagnose every unoccupied combination of answers (whether the gap is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored). Finally, clearly separate what is settled in the specialist literature (that the problem turns on the non-identity of future people and that standard harm-based morality struggles with it) from what remains genuinely contested (whether impersonal reasons resolve it, which aggregation rule is correct, and whether climate policy is genuinely analogous to individual reproductive choice), and do not present any contested resolution as the consensus among those who work on the topic.
