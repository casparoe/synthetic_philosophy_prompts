Student anwer (Jordan):

The sphex wasp does a check-then-drag loop. First-order sphexishness is just that: it runs the same steps no matter what happens outside. Second-order sphexishness is when there's a meta-rule that corrects errors — like "if the prey is displaced, re-check the burrow" — but the meta-rule itself can't be updated. So it looks smart but it's still rigid.

Philosophers like Hofstadter and Dennett use this to argue about free will. Hofstadter says consciousness is just stacks of these loops, and second-order ones create the illusion that we're free because they model interruptions. Dennett adds that determinism is actually false if you have true second-order loops, since flexibility requires indeterminacy at the meta-level. So if human habits involve second-order correction, they aren't deterministic, and that saves free will.

But I think this misses the point. Human habits aren't sphexish because we can always reflect and stop a habit. Even second-order loops in people are open-ended, not mechanically fixed, so sphexishness doesn't apply to us. Therefore the argument that habits undermine free will fails, since humans clearly have more flexibility than wasps.
