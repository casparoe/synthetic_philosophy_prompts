I am in my final year of secondary school, and for an interdisciplinary project I have been comparing the methods used in strategic forecasting with the historical philosophy we study in my advanced history seminar. Recently I read about how intelligence communities apply reference-class forecasting — the outside view — to estimate risks to nation-states, often drawing on the lifespan distributions of past empires, dynasties, and great powers to generate base rates for regime collapse or geopolitical transition. What struck me was that the same agencies apply these statistical analogies almost exclusively to sovereign states recognized by international law, while treating multinational technology platforms and decentralized financial networks as mere market actors or infrastructure, never as entities that might belong in the same reference class despite their territorial reach, coercive capacities, and institutional longevity.

This institutional pattern seems to presuppose a particular ontology: that only Westphalian-style states possess the kind of historical agency or structural identity that makes them comparable across time, whereas corporate or digital entities are ontologically different in a way that renders empire-style base rates inapplicable. But I am unsure whether that commitment is genuinely philosophical — perhaps a tacit Hegelian or realist view of sovereignty — or whether it is merely a tractability heuristic disguised as metaphysics. Could one consistently maintain a purely functionalist or institutionalist theory of political entities and still exclude these non-state actors from the reference class? Or does the practice quietly depend on a substantive theory of value that assigns historical significance only to entities with a monopoly on legitimate violence?

If we replaced that implicit ontology with one that treated territorial control, resource extraction, and administrative hierarchy — rather than juridical sovereignty — as the criteria for membership in the reference class, what would have to change in forecasting methodology? Would we need new base-rate distributions drawn from the lifespans of the East India Company, the Hanseatic League, or modern cloud-computing consortia? And does the current practice's exclusion of these analogues reveal an unexamined commitment about which forms of power are historically consequential?
