I'm a phil PhD student thinking about practical rationality, and I sketched this argument for a seminar but it feels suspcious because the conclusion is too broad. (1) Rational choice should maximize expected utility from now forward. (2) Sunk costs don't affect future utility, so they should be ignored. (3) But when I honor a commitment — finishing a dissertation chapter in a dead-end framework, staying in a collaboration that's turned bad — I'm not just wasting resources; I'm preserving the diachronic credibility of my past self, the idea that my earlier choices meant something. (4) If that credibility is a real component of my well-being, then honoring sunk costs can actually maximize future utility. (5) But by that logic, every past investment creates a reason to continue, no matter how awful the present situation is, as long as quitting would feel like betraying my earlier self. (6) That clearly proves too much — we all agree there's a point where you should just walk away. So where's the slip? Is (4) overstating the value of diachronic credibility, or is there a hidden boundary condition I'm missing? I'd prefer to keep (2) if I can, but I want to know what it costs.
