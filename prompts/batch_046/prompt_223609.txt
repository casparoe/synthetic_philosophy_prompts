In my grad seminar on historiography students keep throwing Ranke's "how it actually was" around — sometimes to defend naive realism, sometimes to dismiss Carr or Hayden White as just playing language games. Seperate the precise claims hiding in that slogan: is it about source proximity, about suppressing presentist judgement, about the historian's duty, or just disciplinary branding? Which of these are platitudes, which are false once you take Carr's selection problem and White's emplotment seriously, and which reading is actually being smuggled in when someone uses it to shut down a debate about whether economic history can be objective? I'm especially curious if there's a defensible middle claim — maybe about the normative pull of primary documents — that survives the sceptical hits but isn't just a tautology.
