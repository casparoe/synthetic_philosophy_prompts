I'm preparing a fifty-minute seminar for third-year philosophy undergraduates who have just finished a unit on Rawls's original position and are now encountering Scanlon's contractualism for the first time. I want the session to focus specifically on the aggregation problem — why contractualism resists summing minor harms across many individuals to outweigh a catastrophic burden on one, yet still permits saving five lives rather than one without collapsing into a simple head-count. Do not explain the doctrine itself; instead, plan the pedagogical arc.

Specify the dependency order: what must be established about reasonable rejection before the aggregation puzzle can be posed, especially the point that rejection is assessed pairwise rather than collectively, and why the carrying example must involve genuinely incommensurable burdens rather than merely scaling up bodies. Identify the single concrete scenario that should anchor the explanation, noting what properties it needs and must lack to prevent students from treating it as a disguised utilitarian calculus. Map the misconceptions in the sequence they are likely to arise — I anticipate they will first interpret reasonable rejectability as a democratic vote, then slide into treating it as a harm-benefit aggregation rule with a veto, and finally conclude that contractualism arbitrarily forbids counting altogether — and for each indicate the precise point in the arc where it should be intercepted. Propose one diagnostic question near the end that distinguishes whether a student grasps the pairwise comparison of reasons for rejection or is only repeating that numbers do not matter. Finally, name one technical distinction — such as that between personal and impersonal reasons for rejection — that should be acknowledged but deliberately left unexplained at this level, with a brief justification for setting it aside.
