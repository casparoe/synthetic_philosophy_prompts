As a novelist, I have come to regard the Gettier problem not as a mere technical gap in the analysis of knowledge, but as a failure of narrative closure. I hold that a belief counts as knowledge only when its truth is sustained by the same epistemic arc that justifies it—when the subject’s reasoning and the world’s cooperation form a single, coherent plot rather than an accident. In standard Gettier cases—the stopped clock, the barn facades, the forged provenance—the belief is true, but only because an external contingency rewrites the ending behind the subject’s back, like a deus ex machina that rescues a misconception without validating the reasoning that produced it. Thus I propose that knowledge requires narrative coherence: the truth-maker must lie within the explanatory reach of the justification, not outside it.

What would have to be true for this view to be wrong? I want an honest map of its exposure. Which specific Gettier-style intuitions—perhaps cases where narrative coherence is present yet knowledge is intuitively absent, or absent yet present—would count against the narrative-closure condition? Which formal results in epistemology, say about the value of knowledge over true belief or about the structure of justification, does this view predict differently from its rivals, and where might it collapse into them? Would defenders of a standard anti-luck epistemology concede that narrative coherence captures something real, or would they dismiss it as a metaphor imported from fiction? Rank the potential defeaters from fatal to merely awkward. Finally, assess whether the view has been insulated by qualifications—if every counterexample can be redescribed as a hidden narrative rupture—such that it risks nothing. If it is well insulated, say so plainly, and tell me whether that insulation is a virtue of conceptual stability or a vice that renders the view unfalsifiable.
