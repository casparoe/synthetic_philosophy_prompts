In our philosophy of mind seminar this term we have been working through the debate over whether knowing-how reduces to knowing-that, with particular attention to Ryle’s anti-intellectualist framing and the intellectualist responses developed by philosophers such as Stanley and Williamson. Our professor has rendered consistent verdicts on the sequence of cases we have discussed. She holds that an experienced surgeon who operates with exceptional precision yet cannot articulate the anatomical rationale for her incisions genuinely knows how to operate. She denies that same knowledge to a medical student who has memorized every surgical protocol but has never held an instrument and cannot execute under pressure. She judges that a fluent native speaker who produces grammatically correct utterances while sincerely endorsing false explicit syntactic theories nevertheless knows her language, and she gives the same verdict for a musician who plays complex pieces entirely by ear while explicitly rejecting any music-theoretic description of what she is doing. By contrast, she maintains that someone who has studied swimming manuals exhaustively but panics and sinks in open water does not know how to swim.

Next week we will consider a practitioner of microsurgical suturing who performs exclusively through a direct neural-interface system that maps an expert surgeon’s motor patterns onto her own musculature in real time. While the interface is active she executes flawless repairs; once it is deactivated she is unable to perform even basic stitches, she falsely believes she possesses independent skill, and she cannot describe the procedures she has just completed. Given my professor’s prior rulings, I want to predict her verdict on this practitioner, but I am more interested in identifying the rule she is implicitly applying. Is she running an internal-capacity criterion that requires genuinely internalized skill, an extended-process reliability criterion that permits technologically mediated correct performance, or a condition that rules out knowledge whenever the agent holds false intermediate beliefs about her own agency? For each of these candidate rules, what is the predicted verdict on the neural-interface case? Which rule best coheres with her treatment of the surgeon, the manual-reader, and the fluent speaker? And if these rules genuinely underdetermine her response, what further case—perhaps an artificial system trained on expert motor data that produces correct sutures without any biological agent, or a patient with preserved procedural memory but no conscious access—would most sharply discriminate between them?
