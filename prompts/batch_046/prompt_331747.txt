Consider a scholar applying Aristotelian political theory—drawing on Politics I and the Nicomachean Ethics—to classify relationships of natural rule and natural slavery within a polis. The scholar has rendered the following verdicts.

1. A captured foreign soldier who understands commands and learns crafts, but who, in the scholar’s judgment, entirely lacks the deliberative part of the soul (to bouleutikon) required for citizenship: classified as a natural slave.
2. A free Greek citizen forced into manual labor through debt-bondage, who retains full rational and deliberative capacity: classified as not a natural slave; the condition is conventional rather than natural.
3. A working ox that serves human needs, feels pain, and possesses a sensitive soul, but altogether lacks logos: classified not as a slave (since slavery applies only to beings with souls capable of being ruled for their own good), but as a naturally subordinate instrument.
4. A freeborn woman who exercises practical wisdom in household management, but whose deliberative faculty is present without authority (akuron): classified as naturally subject to rule in the household, yet not as a natural slave.
5. A child with undeveloped reason who will eventually mature into full citizenship: classified as temporarily under rule, not naturally enslaved.

Now consider a new case: a human being, hypothetically modified through irreversible genetic and neural intervention, who possesses robust practical intelligence, emotional attachment, and the capacity to execute complex labor, but whose deliberative faculty regarding justice, the common good, and universal principles has been permanently excised with no potential for development. This being lives within a polis and performs necessary social labor.

Predict the scholar’s classification—natural slave, naturally subordinate instrument like the ox, temporary subject, or a distinct category. Identify at least two consistent interpretive rules that explain the five verdicts above (for instance, rules centered on total absence of deliberation, on presence-without-authority versus absence, or on conventional versus natural origin of the condition), state which rule best predicts the verdict on the new case and why, identify the closest analog among cases 1–5, and specify one further hypothetical case that would distinguish the competing rules.
