I was recently appointed to an interdisciplinary regulatory advisory panel tasked with drafting boundary conditions for “synthetic moral agency”—a predicate that must apply to certain advanced language-model outputs in some institutional contexts but not in others, and whose precise extension is currently contested in both law and philosophy. In our deliberations, a persistent divide has emerged between panel members who treat vagueness about terms such as “agency,” “deception,” or “moral patient” as an epistemic limitation—holding that there is an objective fact of the matter about every case, but that the sharp cutoff lies permanently beyond our cognitive reach (the epistemicist position)—and members who treat the same vagueness as semantic: there is no hidden boundary, only admissible precisifications, and borderline assertions are neither determinately true nor determinately false (the supervaluationist position). Rather than submit another round of opposing briefs, both camps have agreed to a formal adversarial collaboration, and I need the joint statement they should produce.

The document must contain four parts, formatted exactly so that either side could sign it. First, a numbered section of agreed claims about the formal behavior of vague predicates—specifically, how each framework resolves the sorites paradox, what logical role is played by “definitely” and “indefinitely” operators, whether there exist sharp but unknowable cutoffs in the extension of normative terms, and whether linguistic imprecision reflects ignorance about the world, indeterminacy in meaning, or some combination. Second, a disagreements table in which each camp’s view is stated in wording its own proponents would accept, including precisely whether supervaluationism’s rejection of bivalence for borderline cases commits it to metaphysical vagueness or can remain purely semantic, and whether epistemicism’s posited unknown boundaries are compatible with ordinary speakers’ tolerance for borderline applications without collapsing into a form of precisification. Third, identification of the cruxes—particularly whether there is a mind-independent fact of the matter about borderline status independent of our stipulative practices, and whether actual legal and engineering practices of drawing regulatory lines presuppose one metaphysics of vagueness over the other. Fourth, for each crux, a concrete case or argumentative scenario both sides agree is probative—such as a statutory definition of “manipulation” applied to a synthetic system that falls at the statistical median of every behavioral metric used in our framework—together with each side’s explicit prediction about how that case should be resolved and what specific empirical or conceptual finding would cause them to revise their position. The aim is not adjudication but a publishable neutral product that separates genuine disagreement from verbal confusion, with an honest treatment of the strongest consideration on the losing side of each crux.
