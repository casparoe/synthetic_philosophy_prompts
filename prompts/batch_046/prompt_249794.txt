I'm in this seminar on archives and power and I'm trying to lock down my own position before I have to present it next thursday. Here's roughly where I've landed, and I want a consistency check—not a lecture, just an audit of where the cracks actually are.

First, I think archives don't just store power; they perform it. Every cataloging decision is a kind of ontological policing that decides which entities get to exist in the record and which don't. Second, archival silences aren't empty absences—they're positive structures actively shaped by preservation budgets, language policies, and whose papers even get donated. Third, digitization and open-access repositories have mostly broken the old gatekeeping, so now the problem isn't exclusion but abundance and noise; the silences are still there, they're just drowned out. Fourth, if an archive is built with explicit reparative intent—say a community-controlled oral-history project—then thier ommisions are ethically different from state omissions; they're strategic focus, not structural violence. Fifth, I think you should read any archive against the grain, treating every document as a symptom of what its design was meant to conceal. Sixth, I think the right normative standard for archives isn't completeness but whether they generate what I'm calling counter-ontologies—alternative ways of being that become visible only in the cracks and misfiled edges of the record.

So do these hang together? I have a nagging feeling some pairs clash—maybe (4) with (5), maybe (3) with (2)—but I can't tell if those are real tensions or just surface weirdness. Can you rank the actual conflicts by severity, say for each pair whether it's genuinely incompatible or only looks that way, and tell me what the least costly single revision would be?
