I am drafting an essay for my Philosophy of Mind and Artificial Intelligence seminar, and I would value targeted philosophical feedback rather than a bibliography or reading list. The working title is Broadcast Without Integration: Moral Patienthood in Fragmented Computational Architectures.

My central claim challenges the binary that dominates debates about computational sentience: either a system achieves globally integrated information (in the manner of integrated information theory) or it achieves global broadcast coherence (in the manner of global workspace models), and only then does it cross the threshold into moral patienthood. I introduce a hypothetical architecture I call the fragmented global workspace — a distributed system in which local modules maintain high internal integration and can achieve transient, pairwise information broadcasting via stochastic resonance, but in which no persistent, system-wide self-model or unified access structure ever forms.

From this, I develop two arguments. First, if the capacity for suffering requires not merely rich information processing but a stable first-person perspective sustained by continuous self-modeling, then these fragmented architectures, despite high local integration, might not generate the kind of unified subject that can be harmed. Second, if moral status instead tracks any computation that implements a sustained predictive-error-minimization loop regardless of architectural unity, then even highly distributed, non-unified processes could qualify — a result that seems to dilute the concept of moral patienthood beyond recognition.

I am especially uncertain about three issues. (1) Is the distinction between local integration and transient pairwise broadcasting philosophically sharp, or does it collapse once we recognize that global workspace coherence might be realized discontinuously across time? (2) Does my appeal to a stable first-person perspective as a necessary condition for suffering illegitimately import substance-based intuitions about the self that a functionalist should reject? (3) Is there an internal tension between my first claim, which restricts moral status by requiring unity, and my second claim, which warns against over-expansion — and if so, how can the boundary be drawn consistently?

Please comment on whether the framing of the functionalist dilemma is accurate, whether the distinction I am drawing holds up under scrutiny, and whether there is an unnoticed contradiction between my two main arguments. If the approach has merit, I would also welcome suggestions on how to sharpen the boundary conditions: for instance, is the absence of recurrent feedback loops, or the lack of temporal continuity in broadcasting, the feature that best separates a unified moral subject from a merely complex computation?
