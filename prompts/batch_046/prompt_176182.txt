Specify the desiderata that any adequate theory of individual moral responsibility in collective-harm contexts must meet, with particular attention to cases in which a single agent's contribution is causally redundant (the harm proceeds regardless of that act) yet structurally embedded within an institution or practice that produces the harm at scale. Use climate-related emissions, industrial animal agriculture, and democratic voting as your reference cases, but formulate the requirements generally so they apply to any overdetermined collective harm.

For each desideratum:
1. State the phenomenon, distinction, or case it must accommodate, with the conceptual motivation behind it.
2. Mark whether it is non-negotiable for adequacy in this domain, or whether it reflects the preference of a particular normative camp (e.g., consequentialist, deontological, virtue-theoretic, or relational).
3. If any subset of your desiderata appears jointly unsatisfiable—whether due to logical tension, empirical constraint, or competing normative commitments—identify the conflict explicitly and explain why it arises.

Be sure to include a requirement addressing what I call the dilution-gradient problem: the observation that, as the number of contributors to a collective harm grows, the intuitive moral significance of an individual contribution does not diminish linearly but instead follows a non-linear trajectory (possibly asymptotic, possibly discontinuous at thresholds of collective efficacy). Your theory must either account for the shape of this gradient or provide a principled reason for rejecting it as a criterion of moral assessment.

Finally, briefly evaluate three broad theoretical approaches against your list: a pure consequentialist analysis focused on expected marginal impact; a universalizability-based deontological account adapted for collective action; and a relational or participatory view that treats institutional complicity rather than causal contribution as the primary moral category. Keep the emphasis on the criteria and their motivating conflicts rather than on exhaustive exposition of each view. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; reason from the internal structure of the positions.
