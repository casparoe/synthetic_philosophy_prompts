After thirty years in molecular biology I have turned, in retirement, to philosophy of action, and I keep returning to the digger wasp—Sphex ichneumoneus—whose burrow-checking ritual is often cited as the paradigm of mechanical rigidity. In a reading group of fellow retirees I tried to formalize an argument that now strikes me as swallowing far more than I intended, and I cannot locate the precise leak. The argument runs as follows. (1) If an agent's behavior is governed by a procedure that repeats identically regardless of relevant environmental change, then that agent exercises no free will in that domain. (2) The wasp's ritual is precisely such a procedure: if the cricket is displaced during inspection, the wasp repeats the check rather than adapting its sequence. (3) Human cognition, at its neural implementation, is governed by synaptic dynamics that are deterministic or stochastically fixed given identical inputs. (4) Therefore human behavior is governed by fixed procedures at the implementation level. (5) So, by (1), humans lack free will in every domain—indeed, in all of them. Here is where my interlocutor pushed the reasoning further. (6) One might object that humans display flexibility—revising intentions, abandoning plans—whereas the wasp does not. (7) But this flexibility is itself implemented by higher-order neural mechanisms with their own fixed transition rules. (8) Hence flexibility is not an escape from sphexishness but nested sphexishness: rigidity at one descriptive level masked by rigidity at a higher one. (9) Consequently, no conceivable empirical discovery—no demonstration of neural plasticity, no identification of a non-algorithmic event—could falsify the absence of free will, since every observation can be redescribed as layered determinism. (10) Yet if the conclusion is unfalsifiable in principle, then (1) is not an empirical criterion about behavior patterns but a definitional stipulation disguised as an observational claim. (11) But then (5) does not follow from empirical premises at all; it follows by tautology.

The difficulty is that I want to preserve (1) and (2)—they seem to capture a genuine contrast between adaptive choice and mechanical repetition—and I am inclined to reject (9) or (10) rather than abandon the intuition behind them. But I am unsure whether the error lies in (4)'s slide from neural implementation to behavioral procedure, in (7)'s reduction of flexibility to nested rigidity, or in (10)'s inference from unfalsifiability to tautology. If the argument is valid, which premise is least costly to surrender, and what does surrendering it commit me to? Or is there a hidden equivocation—perhaps between "procedure" in (1) and "implementation" in (3)—that makes the chain appear sound only because the terms shift their meaning between steps? I would prefer to keep the core intuition about the wasp intact, even if it costs me the universal negative conclusion about human agency.
