I'm giving the retirement toast for Dr. Mara Chen, a moral philosopher who spent thirty-five years working on supererogation — particularly the claim that there is a structural instability in praising heroic acts, since public recognition tends to convert them into implicit obligations and thereby collapses the supererogatory category into ordinary duty. Roughly half the audience are philosophers who know her work on the accumulation problem in demandingness theory and her argument that a defensible account of going beyond duty requires a non-comparative baseline of well-being rather than a comparative cost-benefit calculus; the rest are family and staff with no philosophy background. Write a five-minute toast that uses the logic of her position to shape what can and cannot be said: I want to honor her unpaid mentoring and institutional labor without framing it as obligations she heroically exceeded, since that framing commits exactly the error her research warns against. The toast should land for both halves of the room — affectionate, not a lecture — and should make the philosophical constraint feel like a feature of the praise rather than an obstacle. Please separate, either woven into the remarks or in brief framing, what is broadly settled among people who work on this (that supererogation is a coherent category) from what remains genuinely contested (whether recognition destroys supererogation, whether a non-comparative baseline holds up, and whether moral holidays are possible), and do not present her more controversial commitments as the consensus view.
