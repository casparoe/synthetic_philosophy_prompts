I've been considering the accreditation criteria used by several national heritage funding bodies, which now require that permanent historical exhibitions include what they term a continuity narrative linking the displayed era to present-day liberal democratic values. For instance, a gallery on seventeenth-century guild regulation is expected to frame those institutions either as proto-labour-rights movements or as barriers eventually overcome on the road to modern economic freedom, whereas exhibitions that treat guild members as motivated chiefly by religious duty or communal honour—without translating those motives into contemporary rights-language—tend to receive lower ratings in curatorial reviews. At the same time, the very same bodies mandate that school programmes tied to these exhibitions teach pupils to analyse primary sources in their own terms and to avoid presentism.

What implicit philosophy of history and historical explanation does this pairing of practices presuppose? Must it commit to a progressive, Whiggish account in which the past derives its significance from its contribution to present moral and political arrangements, or can the continuity-narrative requirement be justified without that metaphysics of progress? Reconstruct the underlying theory of why some historical representations count as legitimate or valuable, determine whether the anti-presentist teaching rule and the continuity-narrative rule actually share the same philosophical commitments or are merely contingently aligned, and say what would have to change in museum design and pedagogy if the implicit view were replaced by one that treats past societies as genuinely discontinuous with our own moral categories.
