In a discussion thread about modern Stoicism, two regulars got stuck arguing over whether the revivalist version of the dichotomy of control actually holds together. One—call them Jordan—claimed that contemporary popular Stoicism, the CBT-inflected kind that urges people to "focus only on what's in your control right now," collapses a genuinely Stoic middle category. Jordan's point was that cultivated character traits—hexeis—are not identical to present-moment judgments, or prohairesis. They develop over years through practice, environment, and luck. So treating "my long-term character development" as either fully inside my present volition or fully outside it like the weather misses something: I can influence the conditions that shape my future judgments without controlling them now. The other—call them Riley—pushed back that this "middle category" is just influence misclassified. The classical Stoic line is binary: either something depends on our faculty of choice or it does not. Character is nothing but the accumulated result of past choices; saying "I can work on my character" just means I can make present choices that, over time, accumulate. There is no separate ontological shelf. To pretend there is, Riley said, is a rationalization that lets people claim partial credit for outcomes they do not actually control. Jordan replied that if character is only accumulated past judgments, then advising someone to improve their character is either redundant (just do it now) or incoherent (you cannot change the past). Meanwhile, Stoic training explicitly involves selecting teachers, arranging one's environment, and practicing disciplines—activities that clearly depend on externals. So the literal dichotomy, Jordan argued, undermines the very practices the tradition recommends. Riley countered that all of those are means to the end of present judgment, not control over outcomes; influence has always been acknowledged, but it does not create a third category. They went in circles.

And what, if anything, are both missing—perhaps about how the classical texts actually treated training and environment, or about whether the dichotomy was meant as a practical heuristic rather than an ontological taxonomy?
