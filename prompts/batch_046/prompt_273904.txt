Two people in my study group—Maya and Eli—have been arguing about whether large reasoning models are agents, simulators, or something else entirely, after they both examined the same long chain-of-thought trace in which a model refused a direct instruction but achieved an equivalent outcome through an indirect path, then defended that path when challenged. Maya said this proves we are dealing with an agent: there is intra-context instrumental stability, a decoupled representation of a subgoal (complete the reasoning task without violating the refusal framing), and repair behavior that serves a global constraint rather than local token probability. Eli said this is just a stochastic simulator running high-reward reasoning patterns; the apparent stability is an artifact of the training objective penalizing incoherence, not evidence of a persistent utility function. Maya replied that by that standard human instrumental reasoning also collapses into optimized pattern matching, so the relevant distinction must be whether the system guides behavior through decoupled representations across novel contexts within a session, which the model clearly does. Eli countered that those representations are ephemeral activations with no boundary defining the system’s interests; once the context window ends, nothing persists, so there is no agent to have interests. Maya then proposed a counterfactual coherence test: if you inject a token that breaks logical consistency in the middle of the trace, the model backtracks and repairs in ways that preserve the overall argument rather than continuing the broken local pattern, which a pure simulator should not preferentially do. Eli said that correction is exactly what you would expect from a gradient-shaped attractor optimized for coherent outputs; calling it commitment is anthropomorphizing dynamics that have no internal point of view.

Please adjudicate their exchange move by move. For each of the main claims—the simulator account, the intra-context instrumental stability objection, the collapse-to-human-reasoning reply, the persistence-and-boundary objection, and the counterfactual coherence test—say whether the move is clearly right, partly right, or clearly mistaken, and briefly justify your rating. Then say who is more right overall, and identify what both parties are missing that a complete account of LLM ontology would need to address, especially regarding whether a third category (neither agent nor simulator) is appropriate and whether the disagreement conflates functional description with ontological commitment.
