I'm preparing a short talk on the philosophy of mind and have been thinking specifically about epiphenomenalism and the paradox of phenomenal judgment—the problem that if conscious experiences are causally inert byproducts of neural activity, then our judgments about those experiences are also physically caused, not caused by the experiences themselves. This seems to generate a peculiar asymmetry: we constantly report our inner lives with apparent sincerity, yet according to the view, those reports are always one causal step removed from the qualia they purport to describe. A colleague and I were discussing how this plays out in therapeutic or confessional contexts, where first-person authority is taken for granted, and I realized that the tension between sincere phenomenal reporting and strict physicalist explanation could be mined for humor that isn't just the tired philosophical-zombie cliché.

I'd like you to write a joke that turns on this specific feature of phenomenal judgment—namely, that attributing a mental state to oneself is never directly caused by that mental state. To keep the framing fresh, set it in a support group for people who genuinely believe they are philosophical zombies, facilitated by a neuroscientist who treats every utterance as predictable brain output. The participants should be earnestly attempting to share their (causally disconnected) inner experiences, while the facilitator calmly explains that the meeting is proceeding exactly as predicted by the closure of the physical domain. The punchline should hinge on the gap between first-person report and third-person explanation—perhaps involving the idea that even the act of complaining about being causally ignored is itself causally ignored—rather than on generic skepticism about other minds. It should be witty enough for an audience that knows the literature, but accessible enough that the conceptual point lands without footnotes.
