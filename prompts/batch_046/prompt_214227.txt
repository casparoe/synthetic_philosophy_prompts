I'm working through Scanlon's contractualism for a paper on the aggregation problem, and I've constructed an argument that seems to show the view requires sacrificing one person to save any arbitrarily large number, which can't be right. Here are the steps:

(1) Contractualism asks whether any affected party could reasonably reject a principle permitting the action in question.
(2) The evaluation is ex ante: each party considers the principle without knowing whether they will be the one bearing the cost or among those receiving the benefit.
(3) If a principle permits saving N lives at cost C to one randomly selected person, then from each person's ex ante perspective, the risk of bearing C is 1/(N+1), while the chance of being saved is N/(N+1).
(4) As N grows, the expected cost approaches zero and the expected benefit approaches the value of one's own life.
(5) Therefore, for any finite C and sufficiently large N, no one can reasonably reject the principle: the ex ante gamble is favorable to everyone.
(6) So contractualism requires imposing cost C on one person to save N lives, for any N, however large.
(7) But Scanlon denies that numbers aggregate in this way: he insists that the one person's burden is not justified merely by benefits distributed across many others.
(8) Thus the argument proves too much: it turns contractualism into a form of utilitarianism about expected burdens, precisely what Scanlon designed the view to avoid.

My instinct is that the error lies in (2) or in the move from (3) to (5), but I'm unsure whether Scanlon's rejection of aggregation blocks the ex ante reasoning altogether or only applies ex post. If (2) is wrong and evaluation is ex post—looking at the actual positions—then (3) collapses, but then I don't see how contractualism can justify saving five over one without smuggling in aggregation. Which step should I reject, and what does each option commit me to about whether contractualism can accommodate numbers at all?
