For an upper-level seminar on rationality and embedded cognition, draft one 75-minute essay exam question on embedded agency. Present a hypothetical decision system, S, whose predictive model of its own imminent action is physically implemented in its working memory with direct causal coupling to its motor outputs: any revision to the model immediately shifts the action probabilities it predicts. Ask students to write about 800 words addressing four things: (i) why this scenario challenges the Cartesian framing of agency—specifically the idea that an agent is ontologically separate from the world it models—paying attention to the causal transparency of S’s reasoning; (ii) whether this is genuinely distinct from Newcomb-style predictor problems and failures of logical omniscience, or just a redescription, with specific reasons; (iii) a defensible criterion for when an embedded agent may legitimately treat part of its own reasoning as genuinely “up to it” without requiring an impossible view from nowhere; and (iv) the weakest point in that criterion, including the specific empirical or conceptual finding that would cause the student to revise or abandon it. Instruct respondents to build arguments directly rather than summarizing named authors, to write in American English, and to include in the answer an explicit statement of what would change its mind and where its reasoning is most likely to fail. Then provide the exam question in clear parts, a model answer of about 600 words, and a 20-point marking scheme divided into five must-hit moves (with point values and a one-line note on the typical error that separates B-level from A-level work). The scheme should reward direct construction of the argument over literature review.
