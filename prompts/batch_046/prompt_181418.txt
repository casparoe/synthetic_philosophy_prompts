I'm drafting a dissertation chapter on the problem of dirty hands in democratic politics, with a specific focus I think remains undertheorized: emergency regimes in which elected leaders delegate lethal or coercive decisions to predictive algorithmic systems, knowing that errors harming innocents are probable but unavoidable if a larger catastrophe is to be averted. Rather than a survey of the literature, I want to decide where limited conceptual research effort should go. Among the open lines of work—whether dirty hands can be institutionalized without corrupting the democratic norms that authorize them; whether the agent's psychological burden is constitutive of the moral problem or merely symptomatic; and whether algorithmic delegation introduces a genuinely novel category of moral taint or only extends classic frameworks—which should be prioritized for someone working on institutional resilience? Rank them and defend the ordering: identify which questions are upstream of the others, which are tractable through conceptual analysis rather than empirical study, and which should be deprioritized because resolving them would not materially alter institutional design. I am especially interested in whether the delegation case requires genuinely new conceptual machinery or can be handled by applying existing accounts of divided agency and moral luck.
