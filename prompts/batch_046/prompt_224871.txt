I work as a software engineer with no formal philosophy training, though I recently audited an online course on practical rationality and have been arguing with friends about whether an autonomous system should ever be permitted to rewrite its own objective function. That debate led me to the idea of transformative experiences—choices that alter not merely what an agent knows but who she is and what she values, in ways she cannot genuinely anticipate from her current perspective. Rather than asking for a literature summary, I want to see the concept stress-tested through an engineered scenario.

Please construct a thought experiment to the following specifications. Design a realistic, near-future decision case—no exotic physics, no perfect predictors, no demons, and no devices that violate ordinary human cognition—in which an agent must choose whether to undergo a single, well-defined transformative experience. The experience must be both epistemically transformative (she cannot know what it will be like prior to undergoing it) and personally transformative (it will systematically restructure her core preference ordering). Engineer the case so that standard subjective expected utility maximization clearly recommends undergoing the experience, while the transformative-experience framework either recommends refusal or licenses only rational agnosticism—without either theory disputing the objective description of outcomes.

Present the scenario in no more than one paragraph. Then provide the verification: state the belief or decision at stake, the evaluation method each framework applies, the relevant nearby worlds or counterfactual conditions, and exactly why expected-utility theory delivers its verdict and why the transformative-experience framework delivers the opposite. Next, isolate the single structural feature that does the work—specifically, the breakdown of first-person preference projection across the transformation boundary—and explain what happens if you remove that feature, for instance by allowing the agent to run a high-fidelity internal simulation of her post-transformation preferences. Finally, include an honest note on how a defender of expected-utility theory would attack the case, perhaps by appealing to pre-commitment value-lock protocols or meta-preference preservation mechanisms, and assess whether introducing such a mechanism collapses the problem back into a standard non-transformative decision problem.

As a niche but intelligible injection, include in the scenario a concrete mechanism—call it a weighted meta-preference anchor—that allows the agent to preserve a fixed subset of her current second-order values through the transformation. Show whether the presence or absence of this anchor changes either theory's verdict, and keep all terminology within the range of standard discussions of rational choice and transformative experience. Do not provide citations, bibliographies, or reading lists; I need an answer that stands on its own reasoning.
