I am currently developing a position for my doctoral work that attempts to hold together two commitments usually treated as incompatible: a recognisably Camus-style acknowledgment of cosmic absurdity, and a contemporary hybrid account of meaning in life in the vein of Susan Wolf’s fittingness-plus-subjective-attraction model, though I do not endorse her formulation exactly. My specific claim is that the absence of any transcendent purpose or essential human telos does not merely leave an empty space that subjective projects happen to occupy; rather, it functions as a negative filter. It eliminates candidates for meaning that depend on metaphysical guarantees—divine vocation, cosmically inscribed value, or an essentialist human nature—and thereby forces meaning onto a narrower base: projects that an agent finds genuinely engaging and that also exhibit what I call counterfactual stability, by which I mean they remain worthy of pursuit across a range of nearby possible worlds in which the agent’s background metaphysical commitments turn out false. On this view, a life is not meaningless because it is small, painful, or ordinary, but because its central projects collapse under modal pressure—if, for instance, one’s dedication to scientific inquiry would evaporate upon learning that the universe possesses no inherent structure, or one’s artistic practice would feel like mere therapy once the notion of human excellence is thoroughly debunked. Meaning is therefore rare and hard-won, yet entirely independent of cosmic endorsement. The risk, however, is that the view may have been qualified into unfalsifiability: any project that survives scrutiny can be declared stable, and any that fails can be retrospectively judged to have lacked the property all along. I want an honest assessment of how exposed this position actually is. What observations, arguments, intuitive cases, or results from adjacent domains—say, psychological research on motivation, modal reasoning in ethics, or theories of personal persistence—would genuinely count against it? Which defeaters would be fatal rather than merely awkward? Which ones would defenders of this hybrid-absurdist view themselves concede as damaging, and which would they dismiss as missing the point? Is the threat of unfalsifiability here a serious methodological flaw, or is it an unavoidable feature of any account that grounds meaning in structural features of agency rather than in external validation? Please rank the considerations by how decisive they ought to be, and be willing to conclude that the position is either well-insulated in a defensible way or hollowed out by its own protective qualifications.
