I'm taking a course on quantitative ethics and political philosophy, and we've reached an impasse in seminar about whether the claim that "the world is becoming less violent" is even conceptually coherent before we ask if it's empirically true. I want to compare two frameworks that seem to be talking past each other, not merely as statistical methods but as competing ontological commitments about what violence is. The first is the per-capita normalization framework: violence is defined primarily as intentional physical harm between individuals, measured relative to population size, so that declines in homicide, battlefield mortality, and interpersonal assault register as genuine moral progress even if absolute casualties remain flat. The second is the absolute institutional framework: violence includes not only direct physical harm but also preventable structural harm—economic exclusion, ecological damage, carceral displacement—measured in absolute terms irrespective of population growth, and it treats the state's monopoly on legitimate force as potentially violent when that monopoly suppresses direct violence only by redirecting harm into bureaucratic or economic systems.

Can you compare these two not just descriptively, but by identifying what each framework must deny about the other in order to remain consistent? Specifically, does the per-capita framework implicitly depend on a form of methodological individualism that makes collective or structural harm invisible by definitional fiat, and does the institutional framework risk a concept-stretching that renders "violence" analytically useless for comparative measurement? To ground this, consider modern state capacity: under the first framework, the consolidation of state power reduces violence; under the second, the same consolidation may increase structural violence by displacing it into prisons, borders, or labor markets rather than eliminating it. Is there a principled, non-circular way to adjudicate between these interpretations—say, by appealing to victim experience, to causal responsibility, or to some independent normative baseline—or is the dispute ultimately framework-dependent in a way that means there is no single fact of the matter about whether violence is declining? I'm especially interested in whether either framework can accommodate the intuition that a society with very low homicide but extreme economic precarity and mass incarceration has genuinely reduced violence, or whether accommodating that intuition requires abandoning the framework altogether. Please avoid summarizing standard authors; I'm trying to see whether the disagreement can be resolved at the level of concept rather than data.
