Here are my judgments about whether particular metaontological disagreements are verbal or substantive, and I want to know whether one principle can capture the pattern. When metaphysicians debate whether tables exist or only particles arranged tablewise, I think the dispute is largely verbal: both sides accept every concrete physical fact and differ only in which descriptions they treat as ontologically serious. When intuitionists and classical mathematicians disagree over whether every real number has a decimal expansion, I think the disagreement is substantive—they genuinely differ on which mathematical objects exist, not merely on labeling conventions. Yet when philosophers ask whether a specific digital artifact—say, a token recorded on a blockchain—persists through a hard fork once its causal-historical chain is broken, I think the dispute is substantive despite the parties accepting exactly the same computer-science facts; they disagree about which relations ground identity, not about words. By contrast, when anthropologists and biologists dispute whether 'race' denotes a natural kind, I think the disagreement is partly verbal—much hinges on whether 'natural kind' requires biological essentialism—but partly substantive, since there really are different causal structures in human populations. Is there one criterion—perhaps about whether the dispute survives ideal translation into a shared formal language, or about whether the parties assign different modal profiles to the same entities—that delivers all four verdicts? If not, which judgment should I revise? And what does your best principle predict for a case I have not listed: a debate over whether the 'boundaries' in a shared virtual environment are real entities or merely useful navigational fictions?
