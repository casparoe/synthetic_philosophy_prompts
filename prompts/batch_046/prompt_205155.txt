I’m on the Joint Ethics and Rules-of-Engagement Panel for the Ministry of Defence — don’t ask, it’s a long story — and we’ve got a concrete case that keeps looping back to the moral equality of combatants in a way that feels off-model from the usual textbooks.

The situation: we’re reviewing Protocol 9-B, which would formally classify civilian contractors operating long-range autonomous strike swarms as 'combatants with limited liability' in conflicts where the state’s casus belli is contested. These operators sit in climate-controlled facilities three time zones away; they bare no physical risk, but they directly authorise lethal force via distributed algorithmic hand-off (the system doesn’t just execute, it re-ranks targets based on real-time civilian proximity data that the operators don’t fully see). The panel is split. One faction says the moral equality of combatants still applies — they’re functioning as soldiers, so enemy forces shouldn’t target them outside direct engagement, and they recieve prisoner-of-war protections if captured. The other faction argues that equality requires reciprocal risk exposure; without it, these operators are more like munitions engineers who happen to pull triggers remotely, so they shouldn’t get combatant immunity, especially since the underlying cause is unjust.

There’s also a weirder angle someone raised: if the swarm’s decision-layer introduces what they’re calling 'vicarious moral exposure' — where risk is technically transferred to the machine’s probabilistic calculus rather than borne by any human — does that break the symmetry condition in a different way? I’m not convinced that’s coherent, but I need the memo to address it seriously rather than dismiss it.

Could you draft the short opinion memo I’ll circulate before Thursday’s meeting? It needs to recommend a concrete course of action — either extend the combatant classification with conditions, deny it outright, or propose a middle-ground status — with a clear time-frame for implementation review. The reasoning should engage the strongest argument on the loosing side honestly. And please don’t just restate Walzer or the Geneva Conventions; treat the equality thesis as live and contested, particularly in this asymmetrical, algorithmically-mediated context. No need for a bibliography — I just need something the panel can actually vote on.
