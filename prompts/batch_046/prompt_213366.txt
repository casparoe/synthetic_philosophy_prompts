For a 75-minute undergraduate philosophy workshop on Ubuntu and relational conceptions of personhood (16 participants, divided into four groups of four), design a structured negotiation game called "The Recognition Protocol" that forces participants to experience personhood as a relational achievement rather than an individual possession before the concept is named. Each participant secretly draws one card: either "node" (a being whose standing depends entirely on being acknowledged by others) or "anchor" (a being whose acknowledgment carries binding weight for others, though the anchor does not initially know this). Each group must collectively allocate twelve "standing tokens" that grant basic procedural voice; the rules are that (i) no participant may allocate a token to themselves, (ii) a token activates for its recipient only if two other group members have first spoken a shared group name for that recipient aloud, and (iii) if an anchor is present in the group but has not been recognized by name by at least one other member, any token directed at a recipient that the anchor silently opposes is voided—though anchors are unaware of this veto power and believe themselves to be ordinary nodes. Run the activity in three phases: 10 minutes of silent card-reading and rule-comprehension, 25 minutes of negotiation with real but small collective stakes (e.g., the group's success determines whether all members receive a shared resource or must complete an extra collective task), and 15 minutes of group reports. Follow with a 20-minute debrief that maps specific negotiation moments onto the relational view—self-allocation failures illustrating that personhood is not self-constituting, the chase for acknowledgment showing that standing is conferred rather than claimed, and the hidden veto revealing asymmetries in recognition authority. Predict that groups will typically freeze into silence or consolidate around false anchors (loud or dominant speakers mistaken for hidden authorities), and advise the facilitator to treat these failures as data. Be explicit about which feature of Ubuntu this design cannot reproduce: the temporal, intergenerational dimension in which personhood is inherited and sustained through narrative and ancestral acknowledgment across generations, which the facilitator must supply verbally rather than through the game mechanics.
