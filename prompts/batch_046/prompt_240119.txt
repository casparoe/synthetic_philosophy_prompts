Assume you are allocating three years of work for a purely theoretical group with no empirical resources, focused on normative social-choice theory. The specific terrain is Sen’s liberal paradox: the tension between a minimal liberal principle—giving each individual decisive authority over some personal sphere—and the Pareto principle, as shown in the classic two-person, one-book case where each prefers the other read it but both prefer any reading over none.

Identify the open conceptual directions that now matter most in this terrain, rank them by priority for this group’s limited effort, and defend the ordering. At a minimum, consider: (a) whether the paradox can be dissolved by domain restrictions on preferences without weakening either principle; (b) whether assigning liberal rights to coalitions or veto groups rather than isolated individuals changes the impossibility result; (c) whether sequential or revisable decision procedures escape the contradiction; (d) whether extending the framework to multi-option, continuous-choice spaces deepens or dissolves the conflict; and (e) whether the paradox exposes a genuine normative incoherence in liberalism or is an artifact of static, binary framing.

For each direction in your ranking, specify: why it is tractable for philosophers and logicians rather than empirical researchers; which other directions depend on its resolution; what kind of result—formal impossibility, domain restriction, or normative reinterpretation—would shift its priority; and whether resolving it would alter institutional design and policy reasoning or only taxonomic neatness. The deliverable is a defended agenda with explicit trade-offs and dependency relations, not a literature survey or reading list.
