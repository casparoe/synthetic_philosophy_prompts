To force engagement with that crux without letting students fall back on memorized case law, build the prompt around the following injected hypothetical: a legally mandated distributed ocean-temperature monitoring network uses twelve redundant sensors, any three of which can trigger an automatic storm alert. On a given night, eleven sensors are independently disabled by random electrical interference; the twelfth is disabled because a maintenance technician, bound by protocol to check it, forgets. The alert never fires, and a vessel is lost. The exam should ask students to determine whether the electrical interference affecting the eleven sensors, the organizational decision not to maintain a non-redundant backup, and the technician's specific failure are all omissions with identical causal status, and—crucially—whether introducing a legal-duty element changes the metaphysical causal structure or merely selects among causally equivalent omissions.

Write the exam question (approximately 600–800 words of prompt text, including explicit instructions about what the essay must address), a model answer of about 650 words, and a marking scheme that distinguishes an A answer from a B answer by naming specific required moves. An A answer must: (i) articulate the asymmetry problem between metaphysical equality of omissions and causal-selection principles; (ii) apply a counterfactual or interventionist analysis to the redundant failures and show why eleven failures do not individually make a difference while the twelfth does; (iii) evaluate whether the legal-duty feature operates as a causal determinant or as a normative filter on causation; and (iv) consider at least one objection that all omissions remain metaphysically on a par despite selection effects. A B answer should be identifiable by treating all omissions uniformly without addressing the redundancy asymmetry, or by conflating normative selection with metaphysical causation without argument. The marking scheme should list specific mistakes that cost marks (e.g., describing the case without analyzing counterfactual dependence, invoking "common sense" causal selection without explaining its metaphysical basis, or introducing bibliographic references instead of structural arguments).
