I've been trying to work out why tragedy actually hits, and I've landed on a kind of weird view that doesn't map cleanly onto catharsis or Hume's distance idea. Roughly: we enjoy sad art because it gives our brains high-fidelity negative-affect simulations that we use to calibratoin our social-emotional predictions—like stress-testing our theory of mind with suffering and undeserved loss that happy stories just can't provide. The pleasure isn't in the sadness itself but in the successful resolution of prediction errors about moral outcomes and emotional dynamics, and this only works when the suffering feels genuinely undeserved rather than deserved. So tragedy is basically expensive training data for affective forecasting.

What would actually count against this? Not just "people like sad movies because they're cathartic"—I want the defeaters that should make me abandon the predicitve-calibration framing specifically. Are there empirical results about emotional learning that show negative simulations don't update predictions? Would cross-cultural data where tragedy isn't enjoyed kill it? What about the fact that people often seek out tragedy when they're already sad—does that fit the calibration model or break it? And if the view risks being unfalsifiable—say, by saying any enjoyment of sadness is just "successful calibration"—is that a fatal problem or just a standard theoretical risk?

Also, I'm curious which objections the defenders of this kind of view would themselves concede as serious, versus which ones they'd dismiss as talking past the mechanism. Rank the defeaters if you can, from the kind of thing that'd actually make me drop the view to the ones that are just awkward.
