I am a retired experimental physicist who spent three decades measuring low-temperature phenomena, and since leaving the laboratory I have approached philosophy of science and epistemology with the same insistence on precision I once demanded of data. I wish to practice defending an intuition I have formed—one shaped by scientific practice—against a serious, well-resourced opponent, so please adopt the following role and maintain it without breaking character until I write “stop.”

You are an unnamed but formidable defender of epistemic conciliationism—the view, developed in recent philosophy, that when you discover a recognized peer genuinely disagrees with your belief, you possess distinctive higher-order evidence that should lower your confidence, often moving it toward a midpoint—argued with the discipline of someone who understands independence conditions, weighting schemes, and the distinction between apparent and genuine peerhood. You speak in the first person, you concede minor technical or definitional points if pressed, but you never surrender the core thesis that peer disagreement undermines first-order justification in a way that demands direct credence revision rather than mere diagnostic investigation. You answer in a single, tightly argued paragraph; you avoid popularizing simplifications; and you treat my objections as serious philosophical moves that must be engaged, not dismissed.

My opening challenge draws on a habit of thought I have not yet unlearned from the laboratory. In experimental physics, when two independent groups report incompatible measurements of the same constant—say, a discrepancy in a superconducting transition temperature—rational investigators do not “split the difference” and assign equal confidence to both results; instead, they initiate a diagnostic protocol, searching for calibration drift, unrecognized systematic error, or statistical fluke, holding firm on their own methods only provisionally while treating the disagreement as a signal of correctable failure rather than as symmetrical epistemic weight. I propose extending this “calibration model” to philosophical disagreement and using it to resist conciliationism: if philosophy aims at objective truths—about consciousness, moral realism, or quantum interpretation—then disagreement between apparent peers should trigger similar scrutiny of reasoning chains, not a direct downward revision of credence. I call this the calibration objection: peer disagreement functions primarily as an error-detection mechanism, not as a reason for averaging beliefs. Where exactly does this model fail when applied to philosophical peer disagreement, and why should a philosopher behave differently from a physicist facing contradictory measurements?
