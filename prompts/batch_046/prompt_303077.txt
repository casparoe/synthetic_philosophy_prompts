I've been working through the logic of cooperation with a friend who studies behavioral economics—though I have no formal academic background myself—and I've settled on a cluster of views that feel right individually but may not hang together. First, in a strictly one-shot prisoner's dilemma with complete anonymity and no future interaction, I think defection is the only rational move, since there is no mechanism for reward or punishment to change the payoff. Second, I believe that most real-world one-shot cooperation happens because human cognition is calibrated for repeated interaction: essentially, we run an iterated strategy like tit-for-tat in an environment where it doesn't actually apply. Third, I hold that tit-for-tat is the best approach in genuinely iterated games because it rewards cooperation and punishes defection immediately, though I think its success specifically requires a fixed, known probability of the game continuing rather than an open-ended or unpredictable horizon. Fourth, I take evolutionary accounts to show that cooperation only emerges when the expected number of future rounds exceeds the ratio of defection's gain to cooperation's reward, which makes cooperation an evolutionary contingency rather than a rational choice in its own right.

Fifth, I want to defend the idea of a "hard cooperator"—someone who chooses cooperation even in a verified one-shot game—on the grounds that such a person derives direct utility from maintaining a cooperative identity, regardless of the material payoff. Sixth, I think this identity-based story conflicts with signaling explanations: if cooperation is cheap, then hard cooperators don't signal anything credible, but if cooperation is costly enough to be credible, then the evolutionary contingency I mentioned in (4) becomes redundant because signaling alone could stabilize the behavior. Are these six positions jointly tenable? Specifically, does my cognitive-misfire explanation in (2) undermine the rationality claim in (5), and does (6) force a false choice between dropping the evolutionary story and dropping the identity story? I'm not asking for citations or a reading list—just an audit of which commitments actually pull against each other, ordered by severity, and what the least costly revision would be to restore consistency.
