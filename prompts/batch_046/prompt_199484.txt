I'm on the ethics committe at Riverside General. We've got this messy case: patient M. was declared brain-dead two weeks ago by neuro criteria, but her three adult children refuse withdrawal of ventilation and feeding. Their argument isn't religious in the usual sense — they claim that after forty years of extreme emotional entanglement (they literally call it 'living with one mind split across four bodies'), M.'s self-pattern isn't locked in her cortex anymore but is genuinely distributed across their own neural architectures, in a Hofstadter kind of way. They say shutting down the body doesn't destroy the 'I' because that pattern persists actively in them, so they have standing not just as next-of-kin but as co-carriers of the same self to demand continued support for what they call the 'host body.'

M. actually left an advance directive asking for withdrawal if permanently unconscious. The kids don't dispute the paper exists; they say it was writen by a localized self that doesn't represent the distributed entity they now share. Our policy allows reasonable accommodation but doesn't define it. We meet Thursday. Please draft the committee's written opinion recommending either honoring the refusal for a defined window — say, seventy-two hours for a ritual closure — or authorizing withdrawal with conditions. Give us a clear bottom line with a timeline, treat the distributed-self claim seriously rather than dismissing it as grief or pseudoscience, and include an honest look at the strongest point on the side you're arguing against. At the end, tell me how confident you are in your recommendation and single out the specific point you're least sure about.
