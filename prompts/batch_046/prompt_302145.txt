In a discussion about when legend becomes history—specifically comparing the Trojan War, the Arthurian cycle, and the Exodus narrative—a colleague shut down skeptical objections by saying, "absence of evidence is not evidence of absence." She meant that the lack of contemporary archaeological or documentary confirmation for all three should not count against their historicity. I'm sure the line is doing ambiguous work, but I can't specify how. Could you disambiguate the precise claims this slogan might stand for—distinguishing, for example, epistemic principles about warranted belief, metaphysical theses about whether events necessarily leave recoverable traces, and historiographical norms about weighing negative findings—and for each say whether it is trivial, defensible, or false, and whether it actually supports her uniform dismissal? In particular, address whether the silence of Hittite archives regarding Troy, the absence of sub-Roman British inscriptions naming Arthur, and the missing Egyptian record of a mass departure should be treated identically, and which reading of the slogan, if any, justifies that uniform treatment.
