I hold the view that societies bear a positive moral duty not merely to preserve memory of historical wrongs, but actively to engineer institutional forgetting once direct survivors have died—designing archives, curricula, and commemorative rituals so that the affective and normative weight of specific traumas is deliberately diluted across generations. The idea, drawing on Margalit's conception of memory as an ethical relation and Rieff's warning that remembrance can become pathological, is that unmodulated collective memory eventually hardens into identity-entrenchment that obstructs reconciliation; forgetting, on this account, is not a failure of duty but a second-order architectural task. What would genuinely count against this? I am asking for a map of its exposure: which empirical observations about post-conflict societies, which conceptual distinctions between engineered forgetting and propaganda or denial, and which psychological findings about intergenerational trauma transmission would be decisive rather than merely awkward; which defeaters would even defenders of the view concede as damaging, and which could be absorbed by qualification? I also want an honest assessment of whether the position risks unfalsifiability—for example, if every reconciliation failure is read as insufficient forgetting and every success as its vindication—and whether that insulation is a defect for a normative claim of this kind. Please say how confident you are in what you claim, and single out the specific point about which you are least certain.
