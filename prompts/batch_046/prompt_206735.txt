I'm trying to determine the principle that distinguishes a life-promoting engagement with history from a life-denying one, working with Nietzsche's categories of monumental, antiquarian, and critical history but finding that my intuitions on concrete cases outrun the triad. Here are my verdicts.

First, a young republic that stages public commemorations of its founding revolution to inspire present citizens to take political risks for liberty: healthy, clearly monumental history in service of life.

Second, an aging society that preserves every folk ritual and blocks institutional reform out of reverence for ancestral ways: unhealthy, antiquarian history suffocating the present.

Third, a movement that systematically dismantles national founding myths to expose hidden violence, leaving its adherents with no shared narrative to organize collective action: necessary but dangerous; if critical history stops at dissolution, it becomes life-denying, yet suppressing it is worse.

Fourth, a corporation that constantly invokes its founder's genius to justify current stagnation and resist innovation: unhealthy, though it wears the costume of monumental veneration.

Fifth, a research collective that reconstructs past ecological collapses in granular detail, not to blame ancestors or preserve culture, but to build predictive models for present policy—then deliberately abandons the historical narratives once the models are operational: healthy, but it seems to use the past without venerating, preserving, or destroying it; call this predictive reconstruction with narrative amnesia.

Sixth, a scholar who spends a lifetime cataloguing every interpretation of a canonical text, contributing no original claim, convinced that exhaustive understanding of the past outweighs present action: unhealthy.

The pair I cannot easily reconcile is the first and fourth: both invoke a heroic past to shape present behavior, yet one promotes life and the other paralyzes it. Another tension is the fifth: it feels right, but neither monumental, antiquarian, nor critical history seems to capture it, and I am unsure whether it is a hidden variant of one or a genuinely fourth mode.

There is also a case I keep reversing: a museum curated specifically to make visitors feel that modern life is shallow compared with a reconstructed past, offering no practical path to recreate that past—only aesthetic contempt for the present. Sometimes I think this is a toxic form of antiquarianism; sometimes I think it is critical history misdirected inward.

What I want is not a summary of Nietzsche but a precise principle, or a small family of competing principles, that captures as many verdicts as possible. For each principle, specify the clause that is motivated by which case, its verdict on the cases I have not mentioned—such as a historian who writes solely to resolve personal trauma, or a culture that applies critical debunking only to foreign myths while shielding domestic ones—and an honest statement of which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate without ad hoc complexity. If the only way to save all six is to multiply distinctions beyond usefulness, say so, and tell me which verdict is the outlier. In particular, does the principle need a fourth category for predictive reconstruction, or can the fifth case be reduced to a properly constrained critical or monumental use?

Please do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations. I am asking for conceptual analysis, not scholarly apparatus.
