Exam question for a philosophy of politics course: Consider a workplace governed by a smart-contract protocol in which managers hold cryptographic authority to restructure teams automatically if productivity metrics fall below a set threshold, yet employees have no veto power or voice in defining those metrics. In practice no restructuring has ever occurred, and the firm offers extensive upskilling modules that expand workers’ external career options. Analyze whether the workers here suffer a deficit of republican liberty, and whether they simultaneously gain positive liberty while retaining negative liberty. Use Berlin’s and Pettit’s distinctions precisely and explain whether the three forms of liberty can genuinely come apart in this case.

Student answer: The scenario clearly separates the three liberties. Negative liberty is intact because the managers never actually interfere: the employees are left alone to do their jobs, which fits Berlin’s definition of freedom as absence of coercion. Positive liberty is enhanced by the upskilling modules, which expand real capabilities and support rational self-direction; Berlin links this form of freedom to social conditions that enable individuals to be their own masters. Republican liberty, as Pettit defines it, is violated because domination requires only that one party hold arbitrary power over another, not that the power be exercised. The managers’ cryptographic authority is arbitrary since it is not constrained by the employees’ interests or democratic input, so the workers are dominated even though left untouched. Nevertheless, because the restructuring mechanism has never been triggered, the domination remains merely potential rather than actual, which means the republican deficit is weaker than in cases of direct interference. The claim is therefore largely correct, though it slightly overstates the independence of positive liberty, since expanded exit options can sometimes mitigate domination by reducing the manager’s effective control.
