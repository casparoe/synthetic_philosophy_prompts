I've been trying to determine whether my actual view on quantum interpretations is psi-ontic or psi-epistemic, not just which framing sounds sharpest in a forum thread, so I catalogued my gut reactions to concrete scenarios — but the pattern is messy. In the classic double-slit experiment with single electrons, I feel the wavefunction must be physically real: there's genuinely something spread across both paths that interferes, not merely my ignorance about which slit was taken. But with an EPR pair exhibiting perfect anti-correlation at spacelike separation, my intuition flips to epistemic: the joint state tracks shared preparation knowledge, and while Bell's theorem blocks local hidden variables, it doesn't force me to treat the quantum state as a literal object in space. I also invented a stress-test case: a purely discrete physical substrate with no spatial degrees of freedom at all — just an internal Hilbert space of dimension 2^10 that evolves unitarily and yields Born-rule outcomes, but describes no particles, fields, or configurations. There, treating the wavefunction as ontic feels like a category mistake; it seems obviously a bookkeeping distribution over hidden internal states.

The tension sharpens with macroscopic coherence: if a superconducting qubit were engineered to maintain a superposition of 10^9 atoms for milliseconds with decoherence suppressed, and we observed interference between the macroscopic branches, I'd lean back toward realism — there'd be a collective physical state that is neither "all atoms in A" nor "all in B." Meanwhile, in Wigner's-friend scenarios where two observers assign incompatible pure states to the same system, I think both assignments are correct relative to their information, which pulls me toward epistemicism, yet that seems hard to square with my double-slit verdict unless I allow the ontology to shift by context. So is there a single principle — perhaps tied to whether observable interference requires a real vector, or whether the Hilbert space embeds in physical space, or to observer-relativity — that accommodates all of these? Which verdict must I drop if I want consistency? And what does the principle predict for a case I haven't considered: a quantum computer running Shor's algorithm over a superposition of 2^2048 computational-basis states, where no localized physical instantiation corresponds to any single term, but the correct result depends on treating the full vector as genuinely interfering? Does that push me back to onticism for computation, or does the absence of spatial embedding keep it epistemic — and which of my earlier judgments does that choice overturn?
