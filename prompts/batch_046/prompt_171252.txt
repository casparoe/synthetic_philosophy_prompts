I have recently begun advising on legislative procedure, and watching long-standing norms dissolve without formal repeal has made me return constantly to analogies with the late Roman Republic—specifically, how senators invoked the senatus consultum ultimum not by altering statute but by declaring an emergency that suspended provocatio ad populum, leaving constitutional forms intact while hollowing out their protective content. That analogical move is now widespread in commentary on democratic backsliding: modern states are said to fall like Rome, not by coup but through the erosion of unwritten conventions. What philosophical commitments does that practice of comparison presuppose? Does it require that democratic institutions possess an essential nature that can be corrupted while remaining formally intact? Does it presuppose an ontology of norms in which unwritten rules are the real constitution and written law is decorative? Or does it depend on a cyclical philosophy of history in which republics inevitably succumb to identical structural pressures? Reconstruct the implicit theory of institutional legitimacy and historical causation that makes the Rome-modern analogy more than rhetorical ornament. Distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what it merely accommodates—could a linear-progressive historian, or a legal positivist who denies unwritten constitutional substance, still deploy the analogy consistently? And identify where it is philosophically underdetermined: if we rejected the presupposed view, would we have to abandon talk of “norm erosion” altogether, or could we retain the diagnosis under a different metaphysics of institutions?
