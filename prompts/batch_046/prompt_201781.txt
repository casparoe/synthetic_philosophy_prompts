Question: A museum displays a bronze statue, but the metal is actually a synthetic alloy whose atoms are replaced automatically every hour; after thirty days none of the original atoms remain, though shape and location never change. Applying Wiggins’s idea of a dominant sortal and Baker’s constitution view, is the statue at day thirty identical to the statue at day zero? Is either identical to the alloy present? Explain whether temporal parts resolve any remaining tension.

Student answer:
Wiggins holds that persistence conditions are fixed by an object’s dominant sortal. A statue falls under “statue,” so it persists as long as artistic form and location are maintained; material turnover is irrelevant. The alloy falls under “mass of matter,” whose conditions allow complete atomic replacement, so the original alloy is destroyed and replaced. Hence the statue survives, but it was never identical to the alloy.

Baker adds that the statue and alloy are spatially coincident yet constitutionally distinct, not identical. They differ modally: the alloy could survive being melted into a cube, while the statue could not. This confirms that successive alloys can constitute one persisting statue, since constitution is not composition.

Still, Baker’s account requires an actual constituter at each moment. When the original alloy is destroyed, the statue is destroyed with it, and the immediate replacement alloy constitutes a numerically new statue. So the day-thirty statue is not identical to the day-zero statue, despite the shared form. A temporal-parts framework fixes this: the statue is a four-dimensional worm whose hourly parts are each constituted by different alloys. The parts sum into one enduring whole, preserving Baker’s relation without multiplying statues. Thus temporal parts reconcile constitution with continuous identity.
