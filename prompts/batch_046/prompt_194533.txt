As a novelist accustomed to treating the self as a narrative that rewrites itself in real time, I want you to write a villanelle—exactly nineteen lines, five tercets followed by one quatrain, ABA rhyme scheme, with two refrains that must remain verbatim throughout. The first refrain (line A1): There is no audience in the darkened hall. The second refrain (line A2): The draft revises the draft that writes it all. The philosophical subject is Daniel Dennett’s rejection of the Cartesian theater—the myth of a central stage in the brain where consciousness is presented to a single observer—and his alternative multiple drafts model, in which parallel streams of information are continuously revised and no canonical version ever reaches a final editor. Structure the poem so that its form enacts this theory: each of the five tercets should present a different draft of the same moment of awareness, for instance the recognition of a red apple, first seen, then smelled, then misremembered from childhood, then doubted as illusion, with sensory details shifting between versions so that no single stanza claims authority. The quatrain must bring both refrains together for a turn in which the speaker, who has assumed the posture of the audience watching the play, realizes they are merely the latest revision achieving temporary prominence. Somewhere in the middle stanzas, render the idea that conscious content does not arrive at a center but achieves influence through widespread distribution—a kind of reputation or echo rather than a spotlight—without falling into the error of denying that experience happens at all. The tone should be one of precise, controlled bewilderment, and the poem should be accurate enough that a philosopher of mind recognizes the anti-Cartesian point: there is no inner eye, no final edited version, yet something is still occurring.
