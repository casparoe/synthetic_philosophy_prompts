You are a philosopher specializing in comparative ethics and normative theory. Before you answer, take a moment to map out the logical relations among the commitments listed below; then proceed with a careful audit rather than a defense of any single view.

This has become personally urgent for me after a recent retreat and some reading in Mahayana ethics: I am trying to hold together the following package, and I need to know whether it is genuinely consistent or whether I am smuggling in incompatible motivations.

My commitments are:
(1) A scalar consequentialist evaluation of acts: rightness is determined entirely by an act’s contribution to reducing dukkha across sentient beings, weighted by intensity and duration.
(2) The Mahayana view that upaya-kausalya (skillful means) permits a bodhisattva to transgress conventional sila (precepts) when doing so prevents far greater harm, but only when the motivation is karuna (compassion) rather than self-interest.
(3) A naturalistic interpretation of karma: not metaphysical retribution, but a psychophysical conditioning mechanism in which harmful cetana (intention) strengthens aversive mental formations that increase the agent’s own future dukkha.
(4) The claim that the intrinsic quality of intention has independent normative weight—an act performed with pure compassion is, other things equal, morally better than an act producing identical welfare outcomes through indifference.
(5) The rejection of any direct moral authority for “naturalness,” tradition, or monastic code adherence.

Here is where I think the tension lies, and I want you to be precise about which parts are real and which dissolve. First, (2) and (4) seem to pull apart: if upaya justifies breaking precepts purely on outcome comparison, but (4) assigns independent weight to intention, then in a case where breaking a precept with compassion produces the same welfare result as keeping it with indifference, (4) favors transgression for its mental quality while (2) seems to require evaluation by outcome alone. Second, (3) introduces a hidden agent-cost—breaking precepts harms the breaker’s mental conditioning—which either collapses into (1) as just another entry in the welfare calculus (making (3) redundant) or functions as a deontological side-constraint (undermining (1)). Third, if I try to resolve this by saying upaya applies only when the agent’s conditioning will not be corrupted, (2) looks circular: I can break the rule only when breaking it does not harm me in the way that makes breaking it wrong.

Please rank these conflicts by severity, state for each whether it is genuine or merely apparent, and specify exactly which commitment must be modified or abandoned to restore consistency—without assuming I will keep (1). In particular, address whether (4) forces me into a non-consequentialist axiology that makes (1) unstable, and whether (3) can be interpreted consistently within a purely scalar framework without circularity. Keep your answer focused on the logical structure of these five commitments.
