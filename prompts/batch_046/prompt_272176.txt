A colleague summarized Zygmunt Bauman's thesis in Modernity and the Holocaust, together with its bearing on the Historikerstreit, in the following way. Bauman, she said, argues that the Holocaust was unique among atrocities because it was generated specifically by modern bureaucratic rationality and technological efficiency; consequently, comparing it to premodern massacres or to Stalinist terror is a category error that denies modernity's distinctive causal role. She added that Bauman explains perpetrator behavior through a radical division of labor that produced moral blindness: individual functionaries were so fragmented from the final outcome that they could not cognitively register themselves as participating in killing, acting without moral knowledge of what they did. From these two points she derives a further claim: any historian in the Historikerstreit who insists on comparing the Holocaust with other genocides must therefore be rejecting Bauman's modernity thesis and implicitly aligning with Nolte's relativizing strategy. She takes this derivation to show that Bauman's uniqueness claim is the logical backbone of his resistance to comparison. Is this summary fair? Please distinguish what Bauman actually maintained from what is conflated or overstated, and evaluate whether the uniqueness thesis genuinely entails the comparison-criticism she draws from it, or whether the two positions can come apart.
