I've been thinking on my own about when a society truly "collapses" rather than simply changing form, and I've noticed that a friend who reads widely in history—though she has no formal training—holds very consistent views on the classic cases. She judges that the Late Bronze Age crisis in the Eastern Mediterranean was a collapse: the palace economies unraveled, writing systems such as Linear B vanished, and the old political order did not survive in any direct form. She judges the Classic Maya lowlands around 900 CE to be a collapse as well: royal courts ceased commissioning monuments, the great cities emptied, and the society that replaced them in the north was recognizably different. By contrast, she says Easter Island was not a collapse: though the population fell and moai construction ended, the Rapa Nui remained in place, retained their language, and reorganized rather than disappeared. She also denies that the Western Roman Empire in the fifth century collapsed, arguing that Latin, Roman law, and Christianity persisted, and that the successor kingdoms represent transformation rather than termination.

These judgments seem compatible with more than one underlying rule. One rule might be that collapse requires both severe demographic decline and a rupture in core institutions that is not repaired by direct cultural successors. Another might be that the decisive factor is continuity of collective identity: if the survivors still understand themselves as the same people, it is change; if not, it is collapse.

Here is a new case to test which rule she is actually using. Consider a large river-basin civilization that loses roughly seventy percent of its population over two generations, abandons its capital, and allows its writing system to fall out of use for about four centuries. Nevertheless, survivors in scattered highland villages continue speaking the same language, practice modified forms of the old religion, and eventually establish smaller kingdoms that claim direct dynastic descent from the former rulers. They even revive a simplified version of the old script for royal inscriptions.

Would my friend classify this as collapse or as transformation? Which of her earlier cases is the closest analog? If her past judgments genuinely underdetermine the answer, what principle would she be adopting with each possible choice? And what single further case—whether drawn from history or imagined—would best reveal whether her criterion turns on demographic-institutional rupture or on the continuity of cultural identity?
