I am a retired field ecologist who has recently begun reading philosophy of value and environmental ethics, and I find myself with a cluster of intuitive verdicts about ecological disturbance and wild-animal welfare that I cannot quite systematize.

Consider the following cases.

First, clear-cutting an old-growth boreal forest and allowing it to regenerate as a sparse, managed conifer plantation eliminates the dense populations of insects, small rodents, and ground-nesting birds that characterized the original ecosystem—populations marked by intense winter starvation and high predation rates. The plantation supports far fewer individuals and far less intense ecological interaction. My verdict: on balance, this reduces aggregate wild suffering; I find the destruction regrettable for biodiversity reasons, yet not wrong within a welfare calculus focused on net suffering.

Second, draining a freshwater peat bog—an environment that sustains enormous densities of amphibians, aquatic insects, and small fish with very high reproductive turnover—into dry meadow supporting sparse, long-lived mammal and bird populations. Again, my verdict is that the reduction in the total number of lives lived in likely net-negative conditions makes this welfare-positive, despite the ecological loss.

Third, fragmenting a continuous prairie with highways and fencing, creating isolated habitat patches. In these patches, prey populations boom in the temporary absence of predators near the barriers, then crash cyclically through starvation and epidemic disease. Here my verdict reverses: the fragmentation is welfare-negative. Even if average biomass across the fragments is lower than in the continuous system, the suffering is concentrated in horrific crash events, and the instability itself seems to generate more suffering per unit time.

Fourth, a prescribed savanna burn conducted every few years to suppress woody encroachment, thereby maintaining open grassland with stable populations of large grazers—animals with low reproductive rates and relatively stable lives—while immediately killing thousands of small vertebrates, reptiles, and insects in the fire. I keep returning to this case. The maintained system resembles cases one and two—low turnover, low suffering—yet the mechanism involves direct, immediate killing rather than indirect reduction of carrying capacity. I cannot settle whether my acceptance of the first two cases commits me to accepting the burn, or whether the directness of the harm breaks the pattern.

Fifth, introducing an efficient non-native predator to an island where an herbivore has overpopulated and is suffering cyclical mass starvation. The predator reduces herbivore numbers through predation that involves fear and painful death, preventing larger starvation events. My verdict is that this is welfare-negative and unacceptable, even if the arithmetic of lives spared from starvation resembles that in case one.

Is there a coherent principle—perhaps concerning the distinction between reducing reproductive potential versus directly inflicting suffering, or between stable low-turnover systems and unstable boom-bust dynamics—that captures cases one through three and five, while explaining why case four remains ambiguous? Or must one of my verdicts be abandoned for the sake of simplicity?

If such a principle exists, what does it imply about a case I have not considered: the gradual warming of a high-latitude tundra that allows shrub encroachment, increasing carrying capacity for small herbivores and their predators, thereby raising total biomass and reproductive turnover, but without any direct human intervention? Would the principle treat this natural transition as welfare-neutral, or would it generate a reason to intervene?

Finally, if no single principle accommodates all five verdicts, which one should I drop—the acceptance of clear-cutting, the rejection of the predator introduction, or my ambivalence about the burn?
