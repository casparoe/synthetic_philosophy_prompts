For a seminar I am preparing on historical explanation, I have tried to set out my own working position so that my students can test its coherence. Here is where I have landed, across six commitments. I want to know whether they form a stable package, or whether some pull against others in ways I have not seen, and if there is conflict, what the cheapest revision would be and what it costs.

(1) I treat hindsight bias not as an epistemic vice in historical explanation but as its constitutive mechanism: the historian selects and weights antecedents precisely because the outcome is known, and any method that attempts to "bracket" that knowledge produces not objectivity but mere chronology.

(2) I reject what I will call the symmetry of ignorance—the methodological prescription that historians should adopt an epistemic stance analogous to that of the agents they study. Privileged retrospective knowledge is not an obstacle to explanation but its enabling condition.

(3) I grant that cognition is subject to the narrative fallacy—the imposition of coherent story-structures on disjoint events—and that knowing the outcome exacerbates this. Nevertheless, I maintain that the fallacy is disciplinable through what I term "counterfactual anchoring": testing whether a purported cause remains relevant across a tightly constrained range of nearby possible worlds, without constructing full alternative narratives.

(4) I hold that counterfactual reasoning in history is legitimate only when it serves this anchoring function. Once it produces "speculative continuities"—fully elaborated narratives of worlds that never occurred—it inherits all the narrative fallacies of actual history, multiplied by the absence of evidential constraint, and should be excluded from explanation.

(5) I distinguish sharply between explanatory hindsight, which uses outcome-knowledge to select causes, and moral hindsight, which judges agents by outcomes they could not have foreseen. I regard only the latter as ethically objectionable in historical writing.

(6) At the same time, I concede that in practice the two are inseparable: explaining an agent's action by reference to its rationality requires comparing that action to the actual outcome, so that explanatory writing unavoidably carries moral coloring, however carefully suppressed.

Please audit this package. Which pairs are genuinely in tension, not merely apparently so? For each genuine tension, specify the severity, which commitment is doing the pulling, and what the cheapest fix would be (including what it costs). Identify also any tension that looks serious but dissolves on inspection. Order the real conflicts by severity, and conclude with which single revision—weakening, qualifying, or abandoning one commitment—would do the most work in restoring coherence. Be precise about the logical or motivational incompatibility; do not lecture me on the general literature.
