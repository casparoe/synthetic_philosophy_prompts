You are an experienced philosophy instructor and exam grader specializing in social ontology and the philosophy of language. Before you evaluate anything, take a moment to recall precisely what David Lewis requires for a convention: a stable regularity in behavior within a group, a coordination problem with genuinely arbitrary alternatives (not merely interchangeable in every practical respect, but such that some other regularity could have solved the problem), a conditional preference for conformity given that others conform, and common knowledge of these conditions. Be especially careful not to confuse Lewis's rational-choice, common-knowledge framework with evolutionary or game-theoretic dynamics, and not to treat arbitrariness as requiring perfect functional equivalence across all contexts.

Consider the following exam prompt that was assigned to an undergraduate seminar:

"David Lewis treats conventions as regularities that solve coordination problems in which each party prefers to conform if others do, and in which the particular regularity adopted is arbitrary—meaning at least one alternative regularity exists that could have served the same coordinating function. Apply Lewis's framework to this case. After a prolonged ferry strike isolates two neighboring districts, residents begin gathering every Sunday morning at an unmarked old coal pier to exchange goods. They could have used the new marina or the town square, but the coal pier is now the stable meeting point. There is common knowledge that traders will be there, and each trader prefers the coal pier provided others go there. More recently, some residents have begun using the coal pier on weekday mornings for casual fishing, which occasionally causes confusion when Sunday traders arrive early and find the space occupied. Is the Sunday exchange meeting a Lewisian convention? In your answer, address (i) whether there is a genuine regularity, (ii) whether the location satisfies Lewis's arbitrariness condition, (iii) whether the common-knowledge and preference conditions hold despite the weekday interference, and (iv) whether that interference undermines the convention's status."

Student answer:

"The Sunday meeting is definitely a convention on Lewis's view. First, there's a clear regularity: people go to the coal pier every Sunday. Second, it solves a coordination problem—everyone needs to trade and must agree on a location. The coal pier is arbitrary because the marina and town square are functionally identical alternatives; they are completely interchangeable in every respect, which is exactly what Lewis means by arbitrariness. Third, common knowledge and conditional preference are satisfied: everyone knows that everyone knows about the pier, and each person prefers going there if the others do. Fourth, Lewis explains the stability of conventions through evolutionary dynamics—over repeated interactions, behaviors that yield higher payoffs are selected for, so the coal pier persists because it maximizes collective fitness. The weekday fishing doesn't destroy the convention, because Lewis requires that a regularity be followed without any exceptions, and since people still mostly come on Sunday, the regularity technically survives. So the Sunday meeting is a full convention, though the weekday use is evolutionary noise that should eventually be corrected."
