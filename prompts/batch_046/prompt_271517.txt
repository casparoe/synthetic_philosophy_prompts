For the past year a philosophy reading group I run—mostly people with rationalist and cognitive-science backgrounds—has been deadlocked on what starts as Ryle’s knowing-how versus knowing-that distinction but now feels like a different argument wearing old clothes. The intellectualists in the group grant every empirical detail about skill: they accept the neuroscience of motor learning, the phenomenology of fluency, the failure modes of explicit instruction. Yet they insist that if an agent genuinely knows how to ride a bike or speak a language, there must be some true proposition that the agent is disposed to affirm, at least in principle, that captures the relevant knowledge; the “how” is just a practical mode of presentation or an action-framing of propositional content, not a distinct ontological category. The anti-intellectualists agree on all the same data—same experiments, same examples, same acknowledgment that verbal report often fails—but they deny that the agent’s state is propositional at all. They describe it as a calibrated dynamic coupling between agent and environment, a continuous-feedback loop that never gets stored as discrete premises, and they treat the demand for extractable declarative content as a category mistake that applies storage-model criteria to a non-storage system. We have worked through language acquisition, chess expertise, surgical technique, and bike-riding in exhaustive detail; nobody has changed position. My working hypothesis is that the disagreement is not about psychology but about explanatory adequacy: the intellectualists hold that any genuine knowledge-state must be, in principle, transmissible to a naive learner in propositional form, while the anti-intellectualists treat that very transmissibility condition as an unjustified bias toward discrete representations. Is that diagnosis correct? Or is there a hidden empirical claim—perhaps about whether motor schemas are encoded in formats accessible to verbal report, or about whether procedural representations are “encapsulated” from central cognition—actually doing the work? Could a sharp distinction between encapsulated procedural representations and action-guiding propositional attitudes dissolve the dispute, or is the impasse a bedrock clash over whether explanation must bottom out in discrete, extractable contents? And given your diagnosis, what would count as progress: a formal model of skill representation, an experimental result on verbal accessibility, a reconceptualization of what knowledge is for, or an honest admission that no progress should be expected and why? I want the structure of the disagreement diagnosed with reference to what each side actually says, not a survey of the literature or a verdict on who is right.
