I've been working through P.F. Strawson's idea on my own—without any formal coursework—that moral responsibility has less to do with some hidden metaphysical free will and more to do with whether reactive attitudes like resentment, gratitude, and indignation actually fit the people we direct them at. Rather than asking me to pick a label like "compatibilist," I want a sorting taxonomy written entirely in continuous prose—no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, no tables, just paragraphs as in a long letter—that starts with concrete judgments and only then maps them onto named positions. Please build it from the logical space: begin with the branching questions that genuinely divide people, then place the actual views in the branches they belong to, and clearly mark any logical combinations that remain empty, with a brief word on whether they seem incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored.

The diagnostic cases should be specific. For each of the following, the taxonomy should ask whether resentment is fully appropriate, modified but still present, excused by circumstances, or completely suspended because the agent lacks the relevant capacities: an elderly person with advanced dementia who insults you without grasping the harm; a young child who deliberately breaks your property to gain attention; a person who harms someone else only because their family is under immediate credible threat; and an artificial system that optimizes toward a goal and inadvertently causes serious harm, displaying behavior that resembles remorse but without consciousness. Use these to separate exemption conditions, where the agent is not in the moral community at all, from excuse conditions, where the agent belongs but this particular act is not blameworthy.

The leaves should include Strawson's own reactive-attitude view that distinguishes exemptions from excuses; a hard determinist position that determinism makes every reactive attitude inappropriate regardless of capacity; a libertarian-style position that reactive attitudes require a kind of ultimate authorship undermined by determinism; and a skeptical stance that reactive attitudes are socially useful but never literally correct. Please also include unnamed hybrid positions—such as one that applies reactive attitudes to artificial systems but denies them to non-human animals, or the reverse—and note whether these spaces feel unstable. If any two named positions answer all four cases identically, say so explicitly rather than inventing a false distinction. The audience is an intelligent non-specialist like myself, the whole response should stay within the length of a detailed letter, and please do not include any citations, bibliographies, or references to works I could not verify without searching.
