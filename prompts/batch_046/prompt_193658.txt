I'm applying for a one-year fellowship and need a tight two-page proposal on presentism in historiogaphy — specifically whether historians can ever fully escape the standards and conceptual frameworks of their own time. The usual debate treats presentism as mostly moral (judging past people by today's ethics), but I want to push a weirder angle: there might be a structural presentism baked into the analytical categories historians use — causation, agency, social structure — that isn't about judgment at all but about the temporal indexing of our tools. I want to ask whether there are distinct "orders" of presentism (first-order using our concepts, second-order recognizing we do, third-order trying to correct) and whether reaching second-order reflexivity actually fixes anything or just relocates the problem. Existing work argues for or against presentism as one phenomenon; the gap is that nobody has mapped whether escaping one order commits you to another, or if some are structurally unavoidable. Approach: close philosophical analysis of methodological texts plus a small formal model of conceptual translation across contexts — not empirical case studies, since a year isn't enough for that and philosophy is what I can actually do. The delvierable should be a framework that distinguishes correctable presentist distortions from structural ones, with one worked example. It fails if the order-distinction collapses into mere taxonomy. Realistic risk: reviewers might say this is just philosophy of history wearing methodology's clothes.

Please write it up with the question precise, why current answers don't settle it, the approach and why not the obvious empirical alternative, what a result looks like and what counts as failure, and honest scope limits. Also separate what's settled from what's genuinely contested among people who actually work on this, and don't present a contested view — like presentism being purely a moral problem — as if it's the consensus.
