I am writing a historical novel about a fictional natural philosopher of the 1640s who is convinced that he can derive a science of sovereignty with the same demonstrative rigor he applies to the quadrature of curved figures, and in preparing the manuscript I have spoken with scholars and read widely in the secondary literature. Along the way I encountered a summary—presented with great confidence by an unnamed essayist in a popular history of science—that strikes me as both illuminating and suspiciously neat. The essayist claims that Hobbes regarded geometry as the sole genuinely scientific discipline precisely because its objects are definitions we construct for ourselves, and that he sought to import this method into politics by adopting a purely synthetic procedure in which one moves uninterrupted from first principles to conclusions without ever resolving the commonwealth back into the motions of its constituent bodies. The essayist further asserts that Hobbes actually executed this program by generating the concept of the state through continuous linear motion, analogously to the way he generated circular area in his quadrature proofs, and that after Wallis exposed the flaw in his quadrature Hobbes tacitly admitted defeat, retreating into political writing and quietly substituting an empirical, observational psychology for his earlier geometric ambitions.

Something about this account feels too tidy for a thinker as combative and methodologically consistent as Hobbes, yet I do not have the technical command to isolate the precise distortions. Would you audit it for fairness? In particular, I would like to know which elements a historian of early modern philosophy would accept without hesitation, which parts conflate Hobbes's actual resolutive-compositive method with a simpler Euclidean synthesis, whether it is accurate to say that Hobbes ever abandoned his conviction that civil philosophy could be demonstrated with geometric certainty, and whether the essayist's implied narrative—that a failure in pure mathematics caused a methodological retreat in politics—rests on any defensible interpretation of Hobbes's later work, or whether it simply inverts the chronological and logical relationship between his mathematical and political writings. Please separate accurate characterizations from common simplifications and from claims that Hobbes or his defenders would explicitly deny, and tell me whether the summary's tidy arc survives once those corrections are made.
