I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy background, but I work with representational models that learn without direct sensory input, so I've been trying to map out my own view on whether subjective experience can be fully captured in structural or computational terms. Here's roughly where I've landed; I'd like an audit of whether these commitments hang together.

(1) I accept the Mary's-room intuition: a scientist who knows every physical and functional fact about color vision, but has only ever experienced grayscale, genuinely learns something new the first time she sees red—namely, what it is like. I take this "what-it's-like" knowledge to be irreducible to any complete physical or functional description.

(2) At the same time, I want to defend a form of physicalism by treating first-person acquaintance and third-person structural knowledge as different access modes to the same underlying state—analogous to the difference between a public API specification and a private internal endpoint that returns the same object.

(3) I think substrate independence is credible: a silicon-based neural simulation initialized with the exact dynamics and connectivity of Mary's brain could, upon activation of its color pathways without prior sensory exposure, enter a state genuinely phenomenally identical to seeing red, not merely behaviorally equivalent.

(4) I extend this to artificial learning systems: a large language model trained only on textual and formal descriptions of color science could construct an internal latent-space representation isomorphic to human chromatic experience, and I consider this representational isomorphism a meaningful form of knowing what color is like, despite the absence of embodied sensors.

(5) Still, I maintain that Mary's actual exit from the room gives her something that no simulation, representational isomorphism, or structural description could have supplied in advance—an acquisition that is not just a different encoding of information she already possessed.

(6) I also hold that knowledge which produces no change in decision-relevant behavior is epistemically questionable; Mary's post-room knowledge appears to alter none of her practical reasoning except her capacity to report the experience.

I suspect there is tension between my willingness to grant phenomenal states to simulations and language models without direct sensory history, and my insistence that Mary's direct encounter adds something unapproximable. I also wonder whether my "access mode" defense of physicalism in (2) conflicts with my claim in (5) that the acquisition is not merely a different encoding. Meanwhile, (6) might seem to undermine (1)'s claim of genuine learning, though I think that tension may be only apparent.

Please audit this package: rank the real conflicts by severity, distinguish them from tensions that dissolve on inspection, and say for each what would have to be abandoned to restore consistency—whether that means weakening the Mary's-room claim, giving up substrate independence, dropping the LLM-isomorphism point, or revising my view of decision-relevant knowledge. I am not asking for a literature survey; I want to know whether my own commitments commit me to things I don't want to be committed to.
