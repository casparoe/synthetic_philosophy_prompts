I'm drafting the final exam for an upper-level seminar on moral psychology in the Scottish Enlightenment, and I need one essay question that forces students to resolve a structural tension in Adam Smith's Theory of Moral Sentiments rather than recite his vocabulary. The tension is this: Smith defines sympathy as an imaginative change of situations that lets one person share another's feelings, yet he also introduces an impartial spectator as an internal judge of one's own conduct—a figure who, by definition, has never lived one's particular life and therefore cannot perform the literal imaginative exchange sympathy seems to require. This generates a dilemma: either self-judgment is not genuinely sympathetic but something like detached calculation, or sympathy collapses into reflexive self-approval that destroys the spectator's critical distance.

Please write the exam prompt, a model answer of roughly 900 words, and a marking scheme totaling 20 points. The exam is a 90-minute in-class essay; student answers should run about 1,000–1,200 words.

The exam question should ask students to do three things. First, reconstruct Smith's resolution of the tension by drawing on his distinction between sympathy proper and the moral approval that follows from it, together with his account of self-deception and the internal observer he describes in Part III. Second, explain why this resolution avoids reducing the spectator to mere social conformity, despite Smith's acknowledgment that we initially learn to judge ourselves by observing others' reactions. Third, apply the reconstructed framework to Smith's claim that human beings naturally admire the rich and powerful more readily than the wise and virtuous, and show whether the spectator can correct this bias or whether the bias is built into sympathy itself.

The marking scheme should name five must-hit analytical moves, assign marks to each (totaling 20), and include a one-line note on how candidates typically miss each one. Separate an A answer from a B answer by specifying which moves must be present and which common errors should cost marks.

The model answer should treat settled interpretive points—such as the basic mechanics of sympathy and the spectator's role in propriety judgments—as settled, while clearly flagging genuinely contested issues, such as whether the spectator is best read as a realist internal voice or as a regulative fiction, rather than presenting one contested reading as consensus. Do not ask for bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; the answer should be fully answerable from course knowledge without external sources. Avoid framing the problem through highly specialized secondary literature or formal models of spectator theory that a well-prepared seminar student would not have encountered.
