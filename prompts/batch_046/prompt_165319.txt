I've been going back and forth with my roommate about whether guilt or shame is actually the "better" moral emotion, and she sent me this summary from an essay she found for her cultural psych course. The author apparently argues that guilt is the superior emotion because it targets specific actions you can correct, whereas shame targets your whole identity and just makes people withdraw. They also claim that guilt is basically universal and hardwired, but shame works more like a social technology—something cultures can install or uninstall depending on their governance needs. The essay points to Japan as an example, saying Japanese culture deliberately selected shame as its dominant moral regulator after teh Meiji restoration, while Western liberal societies gradually phased it out in favor of guilt-based accountability. From all this the author concludes that if a culture actually wants moral progress, it should deliberately engineer a shift toward guilt and suppress shame, maybe through education policy and public norms. This feels way too tidy to me, especially the idea that cultures just "choose" emotional regimes like software updates, and I'm skeptical that guilt is really culture-independent either. Can you go through the summary and tell me what's accurate, what's a common oversimplification, what's flat wrong about how these emotions actually work, and whether that policy conclusion about engineering emotional culture actually follows from anything defensible in the rest of the summary?
