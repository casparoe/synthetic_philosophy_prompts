I'm trying to map out what I actually think about the death penalty, not the polished version I'd defend in class but the gut-level one, and I'm noticing I care about retribution and about not killing the innocent but I don't really buy the deterrence story — the studies always seem muddy.

Here are my verdicts:

1. If someone deliberately murders an innocent person and we are as close to certain as humanly possible — DNA, video, confession with corroboration — then execution isn't wrong. It might even be permitted, maybe required.

2. If the conviction rests mainly on one eyewitness who could be wrong, execution is absolutely out, even if I'm 90% sure he's guilty.

3. There's a system that executes 100 people, all with near-perfect evidence, but the process is flawed in a way that makes it statistically inevitable that 1 in 500 is innocent, and sure enough one innocent person dies. For each of the 99 guilty ones I still feel execution was okay in that specific case, but I also feel the system should be shut down. That feels contradictory but I can't shake it.

4. Executing for drug trafficking or robbery — no. The crime doesn't match the punishment, risk or no risk.

5. I don't think deterrence justifies it on its own — life without parole probably deters just as well — but in a hypothetical world where we have a perfect, error-free method of establishing guilt (say, a direct brain-state readout that can't lie), I think execution should be legal for intentional killing but still not for property crimes.

6. I keep going back and forth on the accomplice case: someone drives the getaway car for a murder, knows the plan, but doesn't kill anyone himself. Convicted of felony murder, facing execution. Sometimes I think if he willingly joined the killing plan he deserves the same fate; other times I think execution should be reserved for the actual killer, and I don't know wheather that's about desert or about risk or something else.

So is there one principel that captures 1–5 without just being an ad-hoc list? Which verdict does it have to drop? And what does it say about case 6 — should he live or die?

Also, what would your principel predict for a case I haven't listed: a person is convicted of murdering a police officer during a robbery, but it's later shown he was severely intellectually disabled and genuinely didn't understand that killing was wrong, though he knew the act was illegal. Is execution wrong because of diminished culpability, or is it wrong mainly because our certainty about his mental state makes the risk profile different?
