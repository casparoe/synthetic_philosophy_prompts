I'm writing my dissertation on the normative architecture of comparative gender-equality metrics, and I've become fixated on a tension that runs through institutional auditing practice rather than through academic theory alone. The standard equality indices used by bodies such as the EU Gender Equality Index and various OECD comparative frameworks treat occupational gender segregation—the concentration of women in nursing and early childhood education and of men in mechanical engineering and software development—as a direct deficit in gender equality, often weighting it heavily in composite country scores. At the same time, these audits consistently rank Nordic welfare-state regimes among the highest overall on gender equality, despite those very countries displaying some of the highest levels of occupational segregation in the developed world. The institutional response is not to discard the segregation metric but to classify the pattern as an unresolved "gender-equality paradox," implying that even expansive anti-discrimination law, universal childcare, and gender-neutral career counseling have failed to correct a persistent divergence in career paths.

What philosophical commitments does this auditing practice actually presuppose? On one reading, it assumes that a just society should exhibit roughly parity in the distribution of occupational choices across gender lines, which in turn seems to require either that gendered preferences are largely artifacts of culture that justice ought to dissolve, or that autonomy is only genuinely exercised under conditions of statistical symmetry—what we might call an outcome-homogeneity presumption. On another reading, the practice presupposes a form of luck egalitarianism in which any correlation between gender and career outcome, even when driven by self-reported preference formed under fair conditions, counts as a remaining trace of unjust background conditions that welfare policy has not fully neutralized. But the metric might also be compatible with a relational-equality view that cares about domination and status rather than distributional patterns, in which case the segregation indicator is philosophically redundant—merely fitting the data rather than being strictly required by the underlying theory of justice.

Can you reconstruct the implicit theory of value, autonomy, and justice that makes sense of treating segregation as an equality deficit even when it coexists with expansive opportunity structures? I want you to distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes—what would make it incoherent to retain the metric while abandoning the underlying view—from what merely correlates with it or makes it politically tractable. Specifically, does the practice commit us to the claim that nature plays no independent role in shaping gendered preferences, or only to the weaker claim that culture dominates nature in these particular domains? And if we replaced the implicit view with one that treated gender-divergent career choices in egalitarian societies as expressions of autonomous preference formed under genuinely fair conditions—call it preference-integrity egalitarianism—what exactly in the auditing methodology, the accompanying policy recommendations, and the conceptual framing of the "paradox" itself would have to change?
