You are a philosopher of well-being consulting on clinical ethics. Before you construct anything, think carefully and brainstorm: what exactly distinguishes tranquilism—the thesis that only the absence of craving and suffering has intrinsic value, while pleasure, achievement, and emotional richness are at best instrumental—from hedonism and from Stoic apatheia? Note the specific commitments you will need to preserve when translating the view into practice, especially the idea that a state can be valuable even if it feels empty or flat, provided no craving remains.

Your deliverable is a reusable decision procedure for a hospital palliative-care triage coordinator who reviews non-terminal chronic-pain and mental-health interventions twice weekly, with a 15-minute review per proposal, and who must sign off personally but lacks philosophical training. The procedure must operationalize tranquilist evaluation: determine whether an intervention is justified solely by its expected effect on craving and suffering, not by patient satisfaction, functional improvement, or emotional richness.

Structure the procedure as an ordered sequence of tests or questions, with explicit decision nodes:
- At each node, state what counts as a sufficient answer.
- Mark clearly where the procedure stops with approve, reject, or refer-to-ethics-board, and where it stops because the question is one of values rather than empirical fact.
- Flag the hard cases the procedure cannot settle, particularly where reducing one form of suffering risks generating another.
- Specify which theoretical distinctions must survive simplification (for example, the difference between eliminating a craving and merely suppressing its behavioral expression) and which can be dropped without distorting the view.

Then apply the procedure to three proposals:
1. A medication protocol that reduces physical pain but requires patients to adhere to a complex schedule that generates anxiety about missing doses—first-order suffering is reduced, but meta-craving (a second-order craving for the absence of craving itself) may increase.
2. A group-therapy program that patients report as deeply meaningful and satisfying, but for which pilot data show no change in self-reported craving intensity.
3. A request to approve elective surgery that a patient believes will eliminate social-comparison suffering, where clinical assessment suggests the suffering will likely migrate to a new domain rather than disappear.

For case 1, explain precisely how the procedure handles the distinction between first-order craving and meta-craving, and whether both count equally in the tranquilist calculus. For case 3, show the counterintuitive result the procedure yields—likely rejection despite the patient’s sincere belief in relief—and explain why a non-tranquilist coordinator should still take the result seriously rather than dismissing it as callous. Finally, state explicitly what the procedure cannot do: for instance, how it handles trade-offs between present suffering and future craving elimination when probabilities are unknown, or conflicts between reducing an individual’s craving and reducing aggregate craving across a population.

Do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations. Do not invoke specific named theories, scholars, or texts beyond the tranquilist thesis defined above. Write as if for an audience that has no web access and must apply the procedure immediately after reading.
