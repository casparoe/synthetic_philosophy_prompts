I'm taking a course on the politics of memory and find the standard opposition between perpetual remembrance and deliberate forgetting too blunt to handle real urban-planning dilemmas. I want you to write an essay that addresses the following controversy: in several post-industrial cities, decommissioned factory complexes—sites that were also spaces of documented labor violence and systematic exploitation—are being debated for either preservation as memorial museums or demolition and replacement with affordable housing and living community infrastructure. Some advocates, drawing on Avishai Margalit's idea that shared memory can constitute an ethical civic obligation, argue that maintaining the physical ruin is necessary so that the structure remains an active moral witness rather than a neutralized artifact. Others, influenced by Philip Rieff's critique of therapeutic culture and the institutionalization of grievance, contend that structured forgetting—replacing the site with infrastructure that serves present lives—can be a morally serious form of remembrance rather than an erasure, provided the replacement is not amnesiac but transformative.

Weigh these positions with philosophical care, but do not end on a non-committal note. Defend a specific verdict: does the duty to remember require preserving the physical site, does it permit demolition and redevelopment under specific conditions, or does it demand some other arrangement entirely? Your answer should be written in American English, should place your bottom-line conclusion in the very first sentence, and should not exceed 600 words.
