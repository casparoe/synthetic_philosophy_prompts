I am preparing an essay for a literary quarterly on the formal architecture of rules that govern their own revision. I take as my point of departure the game Nomic, in which participants may alter the rules of play—including, critically, the rules that govern how alterations are made—while the game is in progress. The puzzle I wish to examine arises when one procedural rule, let us call it Rule 301, stipulates that it may be amended only by unanimous consent, while a second rule, Rule 302, authorizes a simple majority to suspend any procedural safeguard. The interaction between these two provisions seems to generate a self-referential loop: the conditions for revising Rule 301 are themselves subject to revision under conditions that Rule 301 was meant to protect.

My instinct as a novelist is to map this onto narrative structure. I imagine a novel in which a character discovers, embedded in the text, a clause granting her authority to rewrite the author's compositional rules for subsequent chapters. Should she invoke it to change the protocol for future revisions, she has not merely altered the plot; she has altered the jurisdictional conditions under which plot-alterations are authorized. I want to suggest that legal self-amendment and metafictional narrative share a common formal feature: a jurisdictional regress in which the authority to revise governing norms must be grounded in a norm, yet any such norm remains revisable.

However, I am uncertain whether this analogy holds or whether it imports features that obscure more than they reveal. In institutional law, paradoxes of self-reference are typically managed—not resolved—through interpretive conventions, hierarchical courts, and practices of constitutional settlement that possess no obvious counterpart in fiction, where the author retains an extra-diegetic sovereignty that characters can never truly seize. I also worry that my framing of the interaction between Rule 301 and Rule 302 as a jurisdictional regress may conflate administrative complexity with genuine logical contradiction.

Would you comment on the accuracy of my philosophical framing—specifically, whether the loop between Rule 301 and Rule 302 represents a genuine paradox of legal self-reference or merely a practical tension resolvable by institutional design—and on whether the analogy to narrative self-reference illuminates the structure of self-amending rules or distorts it? I am particularly interested in knowing whether my central claim about collapsed jurisdiction survives once the disanalogies between legal procedure and fictional authorship are made explicit, and whether there is a more precise analogy—perhaps drawn from game theory, logic, or another domain—that better captures how a system can contain rules about its own rules without collapsing into contradiction.
