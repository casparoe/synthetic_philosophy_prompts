I have been trying to understand why biologists and philosophers still argue about what a “species” actually is, even though people have been naming plants and animals for centuries. I came across the idea that there is a “species problem”: experts disagree on whether species are real divisions in nature, merely useful labels, or groups defined by shared DNA, the ability to interbreed, or evolutionary history.

That made me think about Carl Linnaeus, who built the classification system we still use. From what I understand, he saw nature as arranged into fixed, permanent kinds that reflected a divine order, and he grouped organisms mainly by visible features—especially the structure of flowers and reproductive parts. He died long before anyone knew about genes, natural selection, or laboratory engineering.

So I want to pose a hypothetical case that seems to break every part of his framework. Imagine a synthetic organism engineered to digest microplastics in ocean water. Its genome is a mosaic: it uses bacterial genes for metabolism, fungal genes for structural proteins, and long stretches of DNA designed from scratch that match no natural sequence. It reproduces, but only inside a sealed tank filled with a chemical nutrient that does not exist outside the lab; in open seawater it dies within a day. It looks nothing like any known plant, animal, or fungus, and it cannot exchange genes with any wild population.

If Linnaeus were presented with this being, what do you think he would conclude? Would he try to squeeze it into one of his categories—perhaps as a strange plant or animal—or would he say it falls outside nature entirely because it lacks the fixed, self-sustaining form he believed every true kind possessed? Given that he linked reproduction to the natural order, would the fact that it only breeds in an artificial environment convince him that it is not a real living kind, but something closer to a machine?

More broadly, I am trying to figure out whether Linnaeus’s taxonomy was only a practical sorting method, or whether it rested on a deeper philosophical claim about what kinds of beings are possible in nature. If his view really was that nature contains only stable, interbreeding kinds arranged by design, then does synthetic life like this show that his framework was always limited, or does it actually confirm his belief that anything outside those bounds is not truly part of nature?
