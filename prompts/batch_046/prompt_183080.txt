I have recently begun working as an archivist of personal cognitive scaffolding—designing external memory systems for clients who require selectively partitioned access to their own histories. Last month, I applied an identical architecture to myself after an incident I could not integrate, and I now find myself genuinely uncertain whether what I have built is self-deception or something structurally different. Write a short story about a professional “mnemonic architect” who constructs algorithmic filters that partition her clients’ autobiographical retrieval. After she deploys the same system on herself to wall off a specific witnessed event, she must confront whether she has genuinely deceived herself about the event’s occurrence, given that she retains full knowledge of the filter’s design and purpose but lacks direct access to the filtered content. The story should dramatize the classic paradox of self-deception—that to deceive oneself seems to require simultaneously knowing and not knowing the same proposition—while exploring whether outsourcing the concealment to an external, designed scaffold resolves, relocates, or intensifies the paradox. Make the philosophical tension emerge through the protagonist’s practical reasoning, her interaction with a client or colleague who challenges her framing, and a specific moment in which the filter either succeeds or fails in a way that illuminates the conceptual problem. The tone should be restrained and introspective; the narrative should leave open whether her condition counts as deception, avoidance, or a distinct third category, and it should not invoke named theories or request any citations.
