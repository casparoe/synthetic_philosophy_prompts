Last night I had a long, exhausting argument with a close friend about the paradox of fiction—specifically, whether emotional engagement with characters we know are not real can ever be fully rational, and whether the classic formulation of the paradox tacitly assumes a kind of narrative closure that many actual fictions do not possess. We kept circling the same moves because she insisted that knowing a character is fictional automatically undermines any grief-like response, while I kept trying to argue that the emotional structure becomes more complicated once you allow for narratives whose canonical events remain unsettled. To see whether our disagreement is genuinely substantive, I want it rendered as a rigorous, formal dialogue rather than a strawman exchange.

Please write a dialogue between two interlocutors—call them A and B—both equally competent in philosophy of mind and aesthetics, engaging in real back-and-forth rather than parallel monologues. The dialogue should center on what I will call open-system fiction: serial, collaboratively authored, or live-updated narratives in which the canonical status of key events—a death, a betrayal, a redemption—remains provisionally unsettled and subject to future authorial or collective revision. A should defend the view that emotional engagement in such cases involves provisional affective commitments: cognitive-affective states that take internal representational content directed at the fictional entity, but are explicitly indexed to an epistemic stance of suspended closure rather than to full belief or to pure make-believe. A should argue that this dissolves the paradox for open systems, because there is no direct contradiction between feeling grief and knowing the target is both non-existent and ontologically unstable.

B should resist by arguing that indexing an emotion to provisional closure does not eliminate its representational content; if the grief is genuinely about the character, it still presupposes an object of concern, and allowing that object to be mutable and non-existent relocates the paradox into a meta-level tension about rational emotional investment in unstable ontological targets. B should press A on whether provisional commitment is merely belief with an epistemic hedge, and if so, why that hedge does not collapse under the weight of emotional investment—particularly when the reader has already seen the author reverse major events in prior installments. B should further challenge whether A's framework can distinguish rational emotional investment in an unstable fiction from irrational investment in a real-world relationship that one knows might be suddenly redefined by new information.

The exchange should be relatively formal and polished, reflecting the intensity of a serious argument between friends who respect each other’s intelligence but genuinely disagree. Each speaker should respond directly to the other’s previous point, not merely restate their own position. The dialogue should avoid requests for citations, bibliographies, or references to specific texts by page or date; the arguments should stand on their conceptual structure alone. Do not open with meta-commentary like “Here is a dialogue”; begin immediately with the interlocutors in mid-conversation, or with a brief, direct setup that plunges straight into the dispute.
