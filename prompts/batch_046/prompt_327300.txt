I have spent the last six months serving on a review panel for research ethics, and a recent deliberation has made me personally preoccupied with the tension between first-order belief and higher-order evidence about one’s own reasoning. Write a short story of approximately 800 to 1000 words about a professional whose work demands precise judgments—perhaps a sleep-study technician interpreting polysomnography traces, an art-authentication expert assessing provenance, or a clinical-trial monitor evaluating adverse-event reports—who receives credible, institutionally validated evidence that her own cognitive processes in that domain are systematically unreliable, such as a statistical audit showing her confidence ratings are miscalibrated or a peer-reviewed study demonstrating that her perceptual judgments correlate poorly with ground truth. The protagonist should continue to hold a first-order belief that feels rational and well-supported by direct experience, while simultaneously accepting the higher-order verdict that she ought not to hold it. Rather than resolving the tension, depict the practical and emotional consequences of this epistemic akrasia as they unfold in a concrete, time-pressured decision she must make. After the story, append a brief author’s note of no more than 150 words identifying where the narrative takes liberties with the philosophical literature on higher-order evidence and epistemic akrasia—distinguishing, for instance, whether it implicitly adopts conciliationism, an equal-weight view, or a more permissive stance—and separating settled points (such as the coherence of describing the phenomenon) from genuinely contested ones (such as whether rationality always requires deference to higher-order evidence).
