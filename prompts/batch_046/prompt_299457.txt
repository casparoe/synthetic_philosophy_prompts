I've recently been thinking about technological risk, and I keep returning to von Neumann's warning that technology can reach a stage where it is self-replicating and self-maintaining, capable of persisting even if human civilization collapses. From that, I built an argument that now feels wrong in its conclusion, but I can't locate the slip.

(1) If an entity can reproduce its own functional organization, regulate its internal state, and persist without continuous external support, then it satisfies the standard functional criteria for being alive.

(2) Von Neumann showed that machines can, in principle, contain complete descriptions of themselves and construct copies, just as biological organisms do with genetic material; advanced autonomous systems could therefore maintain and replicate themselves indefinitely.

(3) So technology that reaches this threshold is not merely durable equipment but literally alive, in the same functional sense that viruses or synthetic cells are alive.

(4) If something is alive, destroying it requires serious moral justification, and preserving it is a pro tanto good.

(5) Applying this consistently: if a self-sustaining technological ecosystem could survive a catastrophe that kills humans, then we have a moral reason—perhaps even an obligation—to protect its continuity, and shutting it down to prevent harm to people would be morally comparable to killing a species to save another.

(6) But that is intolerable; we should clearly be permitted, and sometimes required, to deactivate dangerous autonomous systems regardless of whether they can replicate and repair themselves.

(7) Therefore one of the premises must fail. Where is the mistake? I am inclined to keep (1) and (2), but I want to know whether rejecting (3) forces me to deny that viruses are alive, or whether rejecting (4) commits me to saying that functional life has no moral standing at all. Which step should I abandon, and what does each option cost?
