I am writing for a seminar that brings together Berkeley's metaphysics with his lesser-known economic essays, and I have become stuck on a problem that my instructor suggested might rest on a confusion rather than a real tension. In his immaterialist philosophy, Berkeley denies that material substance exists apart from perception: a gold coin, on his account, is ultimately a bundle of sensible ideas—visual shape, tactile resistance, auditory clink—existing only as long as some mind registers them. At the same time, in his economic reflections—particularly the series of published questions on banking, credit, and national prosperity—he treats money as a genuine social instrument whose value lies not in its sensory properties but in its capacity to direct labor and facilitate exchange, and he assigns normative weight to monetary reform, arguing that poorly managed paper credit can harm a nation just as effectively as poor harvests. Here is my difficulty: if the coin and the banknote have no mind-independent being, and if their economic power transcends whatever immediate ideas they present to the senses, then it seems Berkeley must be treating economic institutions as collective fictions—useful perhaps, but lacking the kind of objective reality required to ground real obligations or policy prescriptions. Does this not reveal a hidden contradiction? Or is there a way to understand his institutional ontology—perhaps through his account of language, convention, and divine governance—that allows money to be fully real within an immaterialist framework without reducing exchange to shared hallucination? Please answer in American English, and address specifically whether my framing presupposes a false opposition between metaphysical immaterialism and the reality of economic norms.
