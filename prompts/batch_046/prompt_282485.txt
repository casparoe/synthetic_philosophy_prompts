I heard a philosophy podcast try to explain "indirect normativity" — the idea that normative force reaches actions only through some idealized filter, not straight from the facts of the case. The host summed it up like this: "The view is that an action is never right or wrong in itself; it is right only if your fully rational, fully informed counterpart, after ideal deliberation, would endorse it. So normativity is always mediated by a hypothetical judgment, never direct. That means every indirect theory is really just hidden direct consequentialism, because once you idealize away all biases, the ideal self just computes which outcome maximizes value, and the indirect rule is merely a heuristic for reaching that computation. So indirect normativity collapses: either the idealization is too thin to do real work, or it is so thick that it just reproduces direct evaluation under a different name, leaving no distinctive space for indirect views." Something about this feels off — I think it conflates a couple of traditions and overstates the collapse — but I cannot pinpoint the errors. Can you audit the summary? Specifically: is it fair to say that all indirect normativity requires endorsement by an idealized self? Does ideal deliberation necessarily collapse into direct consequentialist computation? And does the host's collapse argument survive correction, or does it miss distinctions that defenders of indirect theories — say, Railton-style indirect consequentialists or Scanlon-style contractualists — would insist on? Please separate what is accurate, what is a simplification, and what is wrong, and say whether the host's conclusion actually follows from the accurate parts.
