I'm trying to work out whether there is a principled distinction between well-posed and ill-posed contrastive questions in comparative history—specifically the "why X rather than Y" form that drives debates like the Needham question about the Great Divergence. I started with a tentative rule: a contrastive historical question is well-posed only when the two cases share a common explanatory baseline—similar institutional frameworks, comparable initial conditions, and exposure to the same kind of exogenous pressure—so that the divergence can be traced to a specific locally variable factor rather than to global incommensurability. But my own judgments about particular cases seem to violate this rule, and I want to see whether it can be repaired or must be abandoned.

Here are my verdicts. First, I think the classic Great Divergence framing—"why did Britain experience an industrial breakthrough in the late eighteenth century rather than China, given that both were large agrarian societies with advanced markets, substantial technological capacity, and integrated economies?"—is well-posed, even though Qing China and Britain possessed radically different property regimes, state structures, and ecological constraints, and faced no shared exogenous shock that would make them natural competitors on the same causal playing field. Second, I think the comparison between Japan's Meiji consolidation and Qing China's Self-Strengthening Movement—"why did Japan successfully build a centralized industrial state after 1868 while China's comparable elite-led reforms failed, despite both facing Western naval pressure, importing similar military and industrial technologies, and operating within East Asian diplomatic systems?"—is well-posed and fits my tentative rule rather neatly. Third, I think asking "why did the Western Roman Empire fragment into successor kingdoms after 476 CE rather than being restored like the Han Dynasty after its interregnum" is ill-posed, even though both were expansive agrarian empires facing frontier pressure, fiscal crisis, and internal elite fragmentation—conditions that seem to supply exactly the shared baseline my rule demands. Fourth, I think comparing Britain and the Dutch Republic—"why did Britain achieve mechanized factory production on a national scale in the 1780s rather than the Netherlands, which possessed comparable capital accumulation, access to coal, scientific culture, and proximity to Atlantic trade?"—is poorly framed, despite the two cases being geographically close, contemporaneous, and institutionally similar in many respects; the Dutch trajectory was not a failed version of British industrialization but a different economic specialization altogether, so the question assumes a single teleological path that was never actually shared.

Can you help me identify a principle, or a small family of competing principles, that delivers all four verdicts without ad hoc adjustment? If a single account cannot accommodate both my acceptance of the Britain-China comparison and my rejection of the Rome-Han comparison, please say explicitly which verdict I should overturn and why. I also want the principle tested against two further cases I have not considered: "why did democratic institutions stabilize in post-war West Germany rather than in the Weimar Republic, despite shared legal inheritances, party structures, and cultural contexts?" and "why did the Scientific Revolution take institutional root in seventeenth-century Europe rather than in the Islamic Golden Age, given that both possessed advanced mathematics, observational astronomy, court patronage, and translation movements?" For each clause of your proposed principle, indicate which of my original four cases motivates it, state its verdict on these two new cases, and be honest about whether it forces me to revise any of my initial judgments.
