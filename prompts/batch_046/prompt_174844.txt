A colleague who recently read Douglas Hofstadter's Gödel, Escher, Bach summarized sphexishness and its implications for free will as follows. The digger wasp Sphex ichneumoneus stings its prey, drags it to a burrow, enters to check that the path is clear, then pulls the prey inside; if an experimenter moves the prey during the check, the wasp repeats the entire checking sequence indefinitely, which shows it is not responding to the actual situation but executing a fixed behavioral program. Hofstadter coined sphexishness to name this kind of mechanical loop, and he argued that human cognition is likely composed of similarly nested sphexish routines—complex but ultimately deterministic subprograms that run without genuine self-awareness. From this my colleague concludes that free will is impossible: if our decisions are just more elaborate versions of the wasp's loop, then we never genuinely choose but only execute behavioral scripts. The only escape, he claims, is Hofstadter's strange loop of self-reference, in which a system can represent and modify its own structure; without such a loop any behavior, however flexible it appears, remains sphexish and therefore incompatible with real agency. Is this summary fair? Please audit it point by point: which descriptions of the wasp's behavior and of Hofstadter's terminology are accurate; where does it conflate sphexishness with determinism or with the absence of consciousness; whether Hofstadter actually invoked strange loops to address a free-will problem rather than a problem of self-consciousness and emergent identity; and whether the conclusion that flexibility requires strange loops follows from the premises given. In particular, does the summary overlook an important distinction between algorithmic rigidity—the repetition of a fixed sequence—and representational flexibility—the ability to update one's model of the situation—such that a system could be non-sphexish yet deterministic, or sphexish despite being indeterministic?
