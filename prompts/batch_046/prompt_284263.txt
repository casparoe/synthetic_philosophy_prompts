I have been volunteering twice a week at a neighborhood food pantry for the past six months, and I am genuinely troubled by a question about my own motives. On the one hand, I believe that people in my community should not go hungry, and I want to contribute to fixing that—when I see families leave with groceries, I feel a real sense that something just has been done. On the other hand, I also know that this experience strengthens my application for a public-service fellowship, and I have to admit that the social atmosphere—the regular volunteers have become close friends—makes the shifts something I look forward to. Last week, a fellow volunteer praised me for being “pure-hearted” about it, and I felt uneasy rather than flattered. If I am honest, I probably would still show up even without the fellowship or the friendships, but I cannot be sure; part of me suspects I might drift away if the work felt isolating and unrewarding.

So my practical question is this: should I feel that my help is somehow tainted or less genuinely good because these other benefits are in the mix? More generally, when someone does the right thing but also gets something out of it—enjoyment, reputation, personal growth—does that diminish the value of what they have done, or is there a meaningful distinction between having a good reason present and having it be the reason that actually drives you? I am especially curious whether the answer changes depending on whether the personal benefit is an intended aim or merely an unintended side effect, and on whether the agent would still act in nearby circumstances where the benefit disappeared.

Please treat this as more than a request for reassurance or generic self-help advice. I am asking you to identify the philosophical structure underlying the conflict—particularly the problem of whether the value of an act depends on the purity, dominance, or counterfactual stability of one’s right-making reasons—and apply the relevant theoretical distinctions to my situation. In doing so, please separate what is broadly settled in the philosophical literature from what remains genuinely contested among people who know this topic well, and do not present a disputed claim—such as the view that an act loses all value whenever self-interest is present, or the view that the mere presence of a right reason is always sufficient—as though it were the consensus. I would also appreciate it if you noted where small differences in framing change the assessment, for instance whether the agent’s right-making reason would survive if the self-interested payoffs were removed.
