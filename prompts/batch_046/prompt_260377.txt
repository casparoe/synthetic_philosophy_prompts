I'm currently developing material for a philosophy of mind course that moves beyond the standard conscious or unconscious binary, and I want to explore whether sentience admits of degrees with moral status tracking that gradation rather than flipping on at a single threshold. To ground this concretely, imagine a continuum that begins with an ordinary bimetallic thermostat reacting through pure physical expansion, extends to an adaptive climate system that models occupant discomfort and adjusts heating preemptively, continues through an insect with nociceptive reflexes but minimal central integration, and ends with a mammal capable of full affective suffering. The conceptual problem I keep returning to concerns what happens in those intermediate zones: specifically, whether there is a principled distinction between a system that merely integrates information about damage and one that genuinely registers that damage as bad-for-itself, or whether any functional analogue of pain is sufficient to generate moral urgency.

Can you provide a detailed example of a conceptual issue that arises precisely in this middle range—perhaps involving a hypothetical entity that possesses some but not all of the representational and functional features we associate with pain, yet lacks the intrinsic badness that philosophers typically take to make suffering morally weighty? The example should illuminate why treating sentience as a purely quantitative scale might obscure a qualitative structural break in how we ought to respond to different kinds of beings, and it should turn on distinctions such as that between mere predictive modeling of harm and genuine valenced experience, without relying on unsettled empirical neuroscience or asking me to identify specific named theories.
