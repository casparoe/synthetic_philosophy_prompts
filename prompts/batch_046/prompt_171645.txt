I sit on my university's Committee on Founding Obligations and Institutional Continuity. We are required to issue a written opinion recommending whether to enforce, release, or modify a founding pledge made fifteen years ago by the original members of the Centre for Interpretive Social Science. At its inception, the Centre's founding charter — signed collectively by its then-director and four senior fellows — contained an explicit promise that no synthetic or algorithmically generated qualitative data would ever be admitted to its core archives, in exchange for a permanent endowment from a private donor. Every original signatory has since departed; the current director and research staff contend that the collective moral agent which made the promise no longer exists, and that maintaining the prohibition now obstructs legitimate methodological evolution within the research programme. The donor's trustees, conversely, maintain that the institution's identity persists through its charter and that reliance interests — including those of external scholars who deposited material under the original assurance — continue to generate binding force regardless of personnel turnover. Our committee's regulations permit 'reasonable release from founding commitments' only where enforcement would cause 'institutional harm disproportionate to the reliance interests at stake', but they offer no guidance on whether a promise binds when the promisor-collective has dissolved, nor on whether reliance interests can survive when directed at an abstract institutional identity rather than at identifiable individuals.

Please draft the short written opinion I shall circulate before Thursday's meeting. It should recommend a concrete course of action — enforce the prohibition, release the Centre from it, or release it subject to specified conditions — together with a clear time frame for implementation or for a subsequent review. The reasoning must treat the strongest consideration on the losing side with genuine seriousness: specifically, it must address whether the dissolution of the original collective extinguishes the obligation, and whether reliance interests retain normative force when the promise was made to an enduring institutional role rather than to particular persons. The document should be suitable for circulation to both the donor's representatives and the current Centre staff; its tone should therefore be formal, impartial, and actionable, not a mere restatement of university policy.
