I have been trying to articulate a recurring asymmetry in debates about whether Nazi Germany could have existed without Adolf Hitler, and I am unsure whether historians or philosophers of history have given it a specific name. The pattern runs like this. On one side, structural explanations—Weimar institutional fragility, the Treaty of Versailles, economic crisis—are invoked to show that some authoritarian right-wing regime was highly probable regardless of individual leadership. On the other side, when the question is narrowed to the specific character of the regime—the radical biological antisemitism, the total war of annihilation, the particular fusion of party and state—historians retreat to Hitler's personal ideology, charisma, and decision-making, treating these as indispensable. The asymmetry appears when one applies counterfactual pressure consistently: if structural conditions were sufficient for generic authoritarianism, could they not also have generated a specifically genocidal form under a different radical leader, or with Hitler present but constrained? Conversely, if Hitler's personal traits were necessary for the specific outcome, were they not also necessary for the generic seizure of power, given that no comparable movement existed? Instead of facing these mirrored questions, the argument shifts its explanatory register—structural for possibility, personal for specificity—so that neither claim is exposed to the counterfactual that would test it. I have seen this move in discussions of the Holocaust's origins and in broader great-man versus structuralist historiography, though I do not know whether it is simply overdetermination by another name, a form of modal scope fallacy, or something distinct. Does this configuration have an established term? If so, how exactly does it differ from ordinary overdetermination, from counterfactual dependency arguments, and from the standard great-man thesis? If there is no precise term, I would like a careful definition that marks its boundaries—what makes it different from mere equivocation between two claims, and what conditions must hold for the asymmetry to be present rather than a legitimate division of labor between different causal levels?
