I'm preparing a discussion for a reading group that pairs Kazuo Ishiguro's The Remains of the Day with John Maynard Keynes's well-known essay on economic possibilities for our grandchildren and the problem of the good life. I want a philosophical reading that treats both the novel and Keynes seriously, rather than reducing Stevens's retrospective journey to a simple cautionary tale about workaholism, or stretching Keynes's vision of liberated leisure into a framework the novel does not actually support.

Read Stevens's account of his career—the cult of "dignity," the subordination of personal feeling to service under Lord Darlington, his refusal to acknowledge political and emotional error, and his belated sense that he may have served the wrong cause—through Keynes's claim that once economic scarcity is overcome, humanity's real difficulty will be learning to live well. Where does Keynes illuminate Stevens's choices? In particular, consider Keynes's distinction between absolute needs and relative needs that are insatiable because they depend on comparison and status: is Stevens's devotion to professional dignity an absolute moral vocation, or is it a socially constructed, competitive need that he has mistaken for necessity? And how does his class position—as a servant for whom leisure is structurally impossible—complicate Keynes's assumption that reduced labor will naturally open space for art, friendship, and love?

At the same time, say honestly where the novel resists a Keynesian frame. Keynes imagined a future of shortened working hours in which people could pursue activities valuable for their own sake; Stevens lives in a world where labor is not about to diminish, and where his identity has become so fused with service that he seems unable to conceive of any good life outside it. Does the novel therefore expose Keynes's vision as a class-specific luxury, or does Stevens's tragedy reveal a deeper failure of moral imagination—an inability to treat personal relations as ends in themselves—that Keynes anticipates but does not fully explain? I want an assessment that can guide our discussion: what does Ishiguro's portrayal of institutional loyalty, gendered deference, and retrospective regret add to Keynes's economic argument that abstract reasoning alone misses, and where should a careful critic refuse to force the fit?
