I am drafting an essay on embedded agency that argues the problem is not merely epistemic—an agent being too small to observe its own effects—but structurally causal: any self-model an embedded agent constructs must treat its own reasoning procedure as an unmodeled causal node, producing what I call a "causal shadow" that makes standard Bayesian conditioning inapplicable. I further claim that this shadow carries normative force: assigning utility to world-states that include one's own decision procedure generates a fixed-point paradox structurally analogous to the liar paradox, which pushes embedded agents away from outcome optimization and toward what I term "procedural rationality"—reasoning about the structure of reasoning itself rather than about terminal states.

Please comment on the coherence of this line. Specifically:

1. Is the analogy to the liar paradox defensible, or does it conflate logical self-reference with causal self-reference?
2. Does the inference from an epistemic shadow to a normative fixed-point actually hold, or does it depend on an unstated premise about how utility must be represented?
3. Is "procedural rationality" sufficiently distinct from existing accounts of bounded or reflective rationality to merit the term, or am I renaming something already in the literature?

Also, please flag any points where I treat embeddedness as a quantitative limitation rather than the qualitative structural feature I intend it to be.
