I recently joined an ethics board reviewing long-term reproductive policy for climate adaptation, and we are grappling with embryo-selection protocols that would favor traits suited to a substantially changed environment—say, metabolic adjustments for sustained heat stress. The standard worry is the nonidentity problem: if selection changes who comes into existence, the resulting people cannot claim they were harmed by being born into a hotter world, since they would not have existed otherwise. But our board is now weighing an additional institutional layer: the selected cohort would also be assigned, from birth, specific social roles—stewards of carbon-capture infrastructure—whose obligations and life narratives are fixed regardless of the individuals' particular biological traits. Give an example of the conceptual issue that arises at the intersection of these two frames: a concrete scenario in which biological nonidentity seems to block a harm claim, while institutional continuity seems to preserve a subject who can be wronged, and show precisely where the two frames conflict rather than overlap. Make the example distinct from standard intergenerational climate nonidentity cases, specify the decision point that triggers the tension, and indicate what this reveals about whether wronging requires a stable subject across both biological and institutional identity.
