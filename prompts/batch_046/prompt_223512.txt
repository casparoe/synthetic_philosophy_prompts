I have recently been sitting with a friend through a long and irreversible decline, and an old philosophical question has become urgently personal rather than merely abstract: why do promises bind? Not in any legal or institutional sense, but in the moral sense—why does uttering “I will” generate an obligation that I cannot dissolve by a simple change of mind, and is that obligation owed primarily to my own past self, to the other person who heard me, or to some shared structure that neither of us controls alone?

Write a villanelle—nineteen lines, five tercets followed by a quatrain, with rhyme scheme ABA and with two refrains that must appear exactly and unchanged in lines 1, 6, 9, 12, 15, 18, and 19. Let the first refrain express the self-binding intuition: that a promise ties me to my own prior word, as if my past self has authority over my present one. Let the second refrain express the second-personal intuition: that what binds me is not my own past act but the claim I have granted to the other, who now holds a standing entitlement to hold me to account.

In the opening tercets, establish the moment of promising—the utterance, the shift in expectation, the sense that something in the practical world has been altered. At the volta in the third tercet (around line 9), turn sharply against the self-binding view: show that if only my past self were in charge, I could release myself by revoking my own authority, yet the obligation persists with undiminished force precisely when I most wish to be free of it. In the remaining tercets, develop the idea that the promisee is not merely expecting but is normatively entitled, and that this entitlement is not a transferable debt like a financial obligation but remains anchored in the particular relationship between speaker and hearer.

In the closing quatrain, bring both refrains together without collapsing them into a simple synthesis. The binding force should emerge as something that exists between the two accounts—neither reducible to internal self-constraint nor to an external claim, but as a shared practical reality that neither party can dissolve unilaterally. The formal repetition should itself feel like the persistence of obligation: the returning lines should accumulate weight with each appearance, and the rhyme scheme should carry emotional as well as argumentative force.

The poem should be precise enough that someone familiar with the philosophical debate—between expectation-based, autonomy-based, and second-personal accounts of promissory obligation—recognizes the conceptual architecture rather than seeing mere metaphor. At the same time, it should work as a poem for a reader who has never studied the topic: the argument should be carried by image and rhythm, and the closing couplet should leave the listener with the sense that promises bind not because we are chained, but because we have, by speaking, made another person present in our future.
