I recently received formal commendation for mediating a workplace dispute that I had no genuine power to resolve—my director had already locked the outcome into an automated decision protocol, yet my colleagues insisted my conciliatory remarks reflected a freely chosen moral achievement. Re-examining Frankfurt cases and the principle of alternative possibilities has therefore become personally urgent rather than merely academic. Please write a sustained satirical parody in the form of a corporate "Employee Handbook and Moral Responsibility Disclosure," approximately 600 words, composed as numbered legal-policy clauses in the deadpan voice of an HR compliance team. The corporation should have adopted a Frankfurt-inspired framework for all performance evaluations: praise and blame are assigned solely on the basis of actual mental states and dispositions, regardless of whether the employee possessed any genuinely open alternative pathway at the moment of action. The satire should exaggerate real structural features of Frankfurt's argument—interventionist counterfactual control, the irrelevance of unactualized possibilities, the distinction between mere guidance and coercive interference—pushing them into bureaucratic absurdity so that a specialist in the philosophy of action recognizes the logic being faithfully extended rather than caricatured. Inject one niche conceptual twist: include a clause titled "Black-Box Alternative Pathway Audit" that treats opaque, deterministic predictive models of employee behavior (without citing named researchers or specific technologies) as the functional equivalent of Frankfurt's counterfactual intervener, and require the handbook to address whether an employee can be held blameworthy for a dispositional trait they could not have avoided acquiring under the company's own deterministic onboarding algorithms. The piece must land standard objections—how the corporation defines "could have done otherwise," who controls the audit apparatus, whether moral praise survives the elimination of robust alternative possibilities—without ever stating them didactically; let the contradictions emerge from the legalese itself. Maintain rigorous internal consistency: the handbook's authors must remain committed to the Frankfurt-inspired denial of the principle of alternative possibilities throughout, and any slip toward compatibilism should occur only if it serves as the punchline. The target is the argument and its institutional application, not any individual philosopher.
