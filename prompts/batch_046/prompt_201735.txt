An essay I recently read offers the following summary of philosophical intuition pumps: they function by isolating a single conceptual variable and holding all other conditions constant, which allows unexamined intuition to fill in the remaining structure; this explains their persuasive force even when they smuggle in hidden premises. Applying this framework to the Trolley Problem, the author claims the scenario activates evolved agent-detection mechanisms—specifically a bias against direct bodily intervention—because framing harm as pulling a lever makes it feel like an immediate physical act rather than an abstract policy choice. Consequently, the essay argues, the pump does not test moral theory but rather triggers an evolutionary response, and since every intuition pump is essentially a framing device that embeds such biases, philosophers should abandon them in favor of formal modeling. I find the mechanism described somewhat convincing, but the leap to total abandonment feels abrupt, and I am unsure whether the evolutionary-psychology point is applied correctly here. Is this a fair characterization of how intuition pumps are understood, particularly with respect to Dennett’s original purpose for the concept? Please separate what is accurate about the isolating-variable mechanism, what misrepresents the standard philosophical defense or deployment of intuition pumps, and whether the claim about direct-intervention biases applies specifically to the Trolley Problem in the manner described. Also, assess whether the inference from hidden framing biases to the rejection of all intuition pumps holds up, or whether it overlooks an important distinction that defenders of thought experiments typically invoke.
