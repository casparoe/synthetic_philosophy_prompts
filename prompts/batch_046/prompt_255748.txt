So I'm writing this novel about a young organizer who slowly realizes her political party is eating her ability to think for herself—sort of a modern riff on Simone Weil's argument that parties are basically machines for manufacturing collective passion and crushing individual conscience. I'm taking her position seriously as the backbone of my character's crisis: that joining any party, even for good ends, means surrendering the kind of sustained, solitary moral attenton she kept writing about, and that this isn't just political corruption but a kind of spiritual gravity pulling you away from grace. But I need to know what would actually count against that stance, not just the obvious 'parties are necessary for democracy' reply that every first-year student makes.

I'm thinking of deeper defeaters—like whether individual moral judgement is even possible without some collective framework providing language and values; or whether her distinction between gravity and grace depends on a mystical anthropology that collapses once you drop her religious framework; or whether small grassroots collectives avoid the corruption she saw in large bureaucratic parties. Rank them from fatal to merely awkward, and say which ones Weil herself might have conceded versus dismissed as missing the point. I'm particlarly curious whether her view has been qualified into unfalsifiability—if anyone who thinks well inside a party just gets defined as 'not really under party gravity'—and whether that's actually a problem for a view meant to guide real life rather than just mystical experience.
