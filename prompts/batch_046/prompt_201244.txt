I'm leading a ninety-minute seminar for advanced undergraduates in political theory on whether sustained prosperity and social order can by themselves justify authoritarian governance, with China and Singapore present only as background reference points rather than as empirical case studies to be memorized. The concept I need them to grasp is performance legitimacy, specifically construed not as the causal claim that good outcomes make people obey, but as the normative claim that reliable delivery of prosperity and order can become a standing reason for citizens to accept authority without procedural consent. Before I begin, plan the explanatory arc for me rather than delivering the lecture itself: lay out the conceptual dependency chain—what prior distinction, such as authority versus bare power or legitimacy versus mere stability, must be secured first so that performance legitimacy is intelligible rather than circular; propose the single carrying example—I'd prefer a stylized, fictionalized scenario of a technocratic administration navigating a multi-year public-health and economic crisis, and specify exactly what features this scenario must include and what it must omit in order to isolate the normative structure from empirical contingency; map the misconceptions students will bring and form in sequence—likely that this view is just rule-utilitarianism, that visible order proves tacit consent, and that it necessarily relies on some form of cultural exceptionalism—indicating precisely where in the sequence each should be interrupted; design one closing question that reveals whether they have separated performance-based justification from de facto stability or from democratic output; and state clearly what comparative institutional or historical detail about China and Singapore should be deliberately withheld at this level, with an explicit acknowledgment that those empirical complexities are being set aside. I was discussing this with a colleague who teaches comparative politics and found that students consistently conflate outcome-based authority with democratic performance, so I want the plan to be especially vigilant about that particular confusion.
