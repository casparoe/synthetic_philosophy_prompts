As a retired physicist who has recently begun reading in the philosophy of history and collective memory, I find myself holding firm but apparently inconsistent judgments about when a defeated cause may be transformed into a sustaining national myth and when it must be dismantled. I want to know whether these judgments reflect a single principle, or whether I must abandon some of them.

My verdicts on concrete cases are as follows. First, the British Dunkirk narrative—the framing of the 1940 evacuation as a moral triumph and proof of national resilience, despite being a strategic retreat—strikes me as permissible, even salutary. It does not deny the retreat, and it serves present solidarity without licensing present injustice. Second, the Masada narrative—commemorating the Zealot defenders’ mass suicide around 73 or 74 CE as heroic resistance to imperial domination, despite historical controversy over the event’s details and the rebels’ earlier violence—also strikes me as permissible. The myth honors refusal to submit, and it does not seem to generate actionable hatred toward any present group. Third, the Serbian Kosovo Field myth—interpreting the 1389 defeat as a cosmic martyrdom that legitimizes modern territorial claims—strikes me as impermissible. It suppresses the region’s multi-ethnic reality and licenses present exclusion. Fourth, the American Lost Cause—framing the Confederacy’s defeat as a tragic defense of agrarian virtue rather than a rebellion to preserve chattel slavery—strikes me as plainly impermissible; the historical falsehood actively rehabilitates a regime of racial domination.

So far the pattern might be that defeat-myths are permissible when they do not sanitize an ideology of domination. But then I consider the Warsaw Uprising of 1944: framing a strategically catastrophic rebellion, which led to the city’s destruction and massive civilian death, as a necessary sacrifice for national honor. This seems to me permissible, structurally closer to Masada than to Kosovo, even though it too involves enormous civilian suffering and strategic futility. This creates a tension I cannot resolve. Both Masada and Kosovo involve martyrdom narratives tied to territorial identity and used in modern political conflict; both simplify a complex past. I accept one and reject the other. Is the difference that Masada carries no present territorial claim? That the Zealots were not maintaining an institution of domination? Or is my acceptance of Masada itself the verdict I should overturn?

Then there is the case I keep going back and forth on: the Alamo narrative in Texas public memory—commemorating defenders who fought partly to preserve slavery within Mexico, yet framed as a universal story of liberty against tyranny. I find myself sometimes treating it as a regional variant of Dunkirk, sometimes as a miniature Lost Cause. I cannot settle whether the presence of slavery among the defenders makes the myth impermissible, or whether the narrative’s subsequent separation from racial politics saves it.

Finally, to test any proposed principle, I ask what it predicts for a case I have not listed: the 1975 Black April narrative maintained by parts of the Vietnamese diaspora, which frames the fall of Saigon not as liberation but as a betrayal that sustains irredentist longing for a lost state. Is this closer to Dunkirk or to Kosovo?

Is there a coherent principle—perhaps one that distinguishes retrospective coherence from prospective mobilization, or that appeals to the counterfactual stability of the myth’s social function—that captures all five verdicts? Or must I drop one? In particular, does a simpler principle that overturns either my acceptance of Masada or my rejection of Kosovo do better than a complex principle that saves every verdict at the price of ad hoc distinctions? And what does your best principle say about the Alamo and about Black April?
