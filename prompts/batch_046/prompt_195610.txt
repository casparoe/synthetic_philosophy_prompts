As a novelist constructing a narrative about the architecture of discovery, I find myself returning to the calculus priority dispute not as historical footnote but as a living tension between two metaphysics of invention. Imagine Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz is brought before the modern sociology and philosophy of science, having observed not only his own parallel development with Newton, but also the later cases of Darwin and Wallace, and—more pressingly—the contemporary pattern in machine learning where identical architectures emerge simultaneously across unconnected laboratories. Given his rationalist commitment that mathematical truths are eternal and accessible to any mind that orders its reasoning correctly, would he argue that the differential calculus was bound to be articulated once conceptual prerequisites ripened, rendering attribution a mere historical courtesy? Or, attentive to the particular symbolic machinery he devised—the notation that now dominates analysis—would he maintain that discovery comprises both an eternal form and a contingent expressive vehicle, so that Newton’s fluxions and his own differentials represent genuinely distinct achievements, neither substitutable for the other? I ask you to apply this frame to the broader debate over simultaneous invention: are discoveries inevitable manifestations of rational structure, or do notation, conceptual framing, and institutional path-dependency constitute them? In answering, separate what is historically settled about the Newton-Leibniz correspondence and the mathematical equivalence of their results from what remains genuinely contested among historians of mathematics and philosophers of science—particularly the claim that rationalist metaphysics entails discovery inevitability, or that priority disputes are purely epiphenomenal. Do not present any contested interpretive stance, such as the thesis that all simultaneous inventions are merely one rational form manifesting in different minds, as though it were the consensus among scholars who study scientific discovery; instead, map precisely where the disagreement lives, between those who treat attribution as inessential to the logic of ideas and those who treat the specific vehicle of expression as constitutive of the discovery itself.
