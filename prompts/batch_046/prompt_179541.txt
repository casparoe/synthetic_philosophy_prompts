A recurring move in recent AI governance writing is to compare proposed international oversight regimes for transformative AI to the Baruch Plan of 1946: just as Bernard Baruch proposed an international authority with intrusive inspection powers over all nuclear facilities, punitive sanctions for violations, and an explicit rejection of national sovereignty over fission materials, the argument runs that frontier model training requires an analogously invasive global regime—complete with verification protocols and a collective “alignment tax” analogous to the safety controls that made the plan politically unacceptable to the Soviet Union. Audit this analogy precisely. First, lay out the mapping: what corresponds to the international authority, to the sovereignty concession, to the verification mechanism, to the “safety tax,” and to the single strategic bottleneck (fissile material) that made nuclear control technically conceivable. Second, identify where the mapping fractures—particularly around the observability and portability of algorithmic knowledge versus nuclear material, the absence of a clear military-civilian distinction in general-purpose models, and the fact that AI development is distributed across private firms rather than state laboratories. Third, determine which features of the nuclear case the analogy quietly imports (e.g., the idea of a discrete, countable resource; the assumption that technical safety and geopolitical control are the same problem) that the AI target lacks. Finally, evaluate whether the analogy’s usual conclusion—that such a regime is politically impossible now because it was then—survives once the disanalogies are on the table, or whether the comparison is misleading rather than merely pessimistic. If the nuclear era offers no tighter parallel, say so and explain what kind of historical comparison, if any, could actually carry the governance weight being asked of this one.
