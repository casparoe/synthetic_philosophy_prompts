You are preparing a speaker for the Q&A after a seminar presentation on Berkeley’s master argument. The speaker’s thesis is that the argument in the First Dialogue has been systematically misread because commentators conflate two operative senses of “conceive”: one tied to occurrent mental imagery (the sense Hylas invokes when he claims he can picture a tree in an empty forest) and one tied to propositional grasp or reference-fixing (the sense Philonous exploits when he insists that to think of an object is already to think of it as present to some mind). The speaker contends that once these are disambiguated, the master argument is not psychological—about the limits of imagination—but semantic-transcendental: the conditions for referring to an object as existing independently of perception necessarily embed a perceiving subject, so “unperceived existence” is structurally incoherent rather than merely unimaginable.

The audience is a workshop on conceptual architecture in early modern philosophy. Half are historians specializing in Locke and Hume who know Berkeley mainly through secondary literature and are suspicious of importing analytic philosophy-of-language machinery into eighteenth-century texts. Two are analytic philosophers of language who accept that machinery but doubt Berkeley possessed anything like propositional reference-fixing. One is a direct realist in philosophy of mind who thinks the argument, even on this reading, confuses epistemic access with ontological dependence. The talk is forty-five minutes, followed by thirty minutes of hostile but informed questioning.

Prepare the speaker with a prep sheet, not a defense. List the objections in roughly the order they will come, phrased in the vocabulary and framing each subgroup will actually use. For each objection, indicate whether it strikes the interpretive claim (the two senses of conceive), the philosophical claim (the semantic-transcendental conclusion), or the historical claim (that Berkeley intended this distinction). Note which objections carry real force, what the speaker should concede up front—e.g., that Berkeley never uses the term “reference-fixing”—what they should hold firm on, and where each reply is weakest. Include the one question the speaker is hoping nobody asks: specifically, the one that forces a choice between attributing anachronistic semantic theory to Berkeley and admitting that on a historically faithful reading the master argument collapses back into the very psychologism the speaker rejects. Order everything by likelihood and damage.
