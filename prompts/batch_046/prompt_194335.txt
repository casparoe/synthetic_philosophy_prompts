Compare the Kalai-Smorodinsky and Nash bargaining solutions through the lens of aspiration stability: the idea that an agent’s conception of the best achievable outcome—the utopia point—may shift endogenously as the negotiation reveals which outcomes are genuinely feasible, rather than remaining fixed exogenously. First, clarify how each solution formally incorporates the utopia point and the disagreement point; in particular, explain how Kalai-Smorodinsky uses the utopia point as a direct scaling anchor for proportional gains, whereas Nash incorporates it only implicitly through normalization of surplus relative to the disagreement point. Next, analyze a scenario in which learning that a particular outcome is unattainable causes agents to revise downward not only their expectations but also their ideal point itself. Given that Kalai-Smorodinsky satisfies individual monotonicity while Nash satisfies scale invariance, determine which solution is more robust to strategic or epistemic manipulation of these revised aspirations, and whether one solution’s outcome can shift discontinuously when the utopia point is adjusted. Finally, discuss whether this divergence reveals a deeper normative disagreement—whether a fair bargain should be measured against an absolute ideal or solely against the fallback of non-agreement—and what that implies for applying either framework to domains such as climate treaties or constitutional design, where the “best possible world” is itself politically contested.
