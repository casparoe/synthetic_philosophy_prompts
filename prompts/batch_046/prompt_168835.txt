I just spent the evening in a long, circular argument with a friend about whether casting a ballot for the “lesser evil” is best understood as fulfilling a civic duty, as an expressive act that defines one’s political identity, or as a strictly instrumental calculation of harm reduction — and I realized that in the heat of the disagreement we kept sliding between these three registers without noticing. I am now preparing a single ninety-minute session for a reading group of second-year philosophy students who have read Mill and Rawls but nothing in voting ethics or social-choice theory, and I want a teaching plan rather than a lecture script: the dependency order in which the conceptual pieces must be introduced, the one carrying scenario that makes the distinctions stick, the misconception map, the check for understanding, and what to deliberately omit.

Begin by laying out the sequence: which distinction — for example, between the expressive value of a vote and its causal efficacy — must be firmly in place before the duty-based claim can even be formulated, and why introducing the “lesser evil” framing too early collapses the architecture. Choose one realistic, non-trolley scenario — perhaps a municipal election in which one candidate supports a policy that will cause concrete, foreseeable harm to a specific group, while the other supports a policy that will cause a different but measurably larger harm, and a third, genuinely preferable option has no path to victory. Specify exactly what features this scenario must possess (a quantifiable harm differential, an expressive cost to voting for either major candidate, the absence of a “clean hands” option) and what it must lack (a perfect predictor of the outcome, a candidate who is morally unproblematic, or any appeal to exotic coordination mechanisms). Then, for each of the predictable misconceptions — I expect they will conflate expressive voting with mere virtue signaling, and they will treat instrumental efficacy as both necessary and sufficient for a duty to vote — identify the precise point in your sequence where it will arise and the conceptual adjustment, not just a correction, that heads it off.

Next, design the one end-of-session question I can ask that forces them to keep the three accounts separate rather than merging them back into a single “should I vote for X?” judgment. Finally, state explicitly what should be left out at this level — for instance, formal aggregation paradoxes, specific theories of democratic legitimacy, or the literature on compulsory voting — and end the response with a one-sentence summary of the plan’s core pedagogical move as the very last line.
