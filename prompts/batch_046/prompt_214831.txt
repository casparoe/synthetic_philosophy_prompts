I'm working on a paper about public monuments and I keep seeing this patern. A town takes down a statue of some historical figure because of what they did, and they put it in storage or a museum with a big explanatory plaque. They rename the plaza after someone else. But then people keep using the old name for the plaza in casual conversation, and the museum display actually draws more visitors who take selfies with the statue than ever visited the original site. So the removal does'nt erase the figure; it kind of relocates them into a space where they're talked about more, but now framed as a villian. Meanwhile the new name for the plaza never sticks.

I've seen this happen with a couple instances, not just one. Is there an established term in commemoration studies or ethics for this specific failure mode, where removing a memorial physically does'nt erase the memory but actually creates a kind of shadow monument that's more visible in a different form? I know about damnatio memoriae and the Streisand effect, but those are'nt quite right: damnatio is about intentional erasure, and Streisand is about information suppression backfiring in media. This is about physical relocation and renaming producing an unintended double-presence. If there's no standard name, could you coin one and say exactly where its boundaries are — like, does it apply if the statue is destroyed rather than relocated, or if the new name is adopted quickly?
