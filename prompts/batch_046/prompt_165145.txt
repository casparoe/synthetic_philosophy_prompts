I'm part of an institutional ethics working group that has been deadlocked for months on disaster-triage protocols, specifically on whether the raw number of lives at stake should determine allocation rules. One faction—the Aggregative Pluralists—holds that saving more lives is pro tanto better, but they reject naive utilitarianism: they accept that each person has an independent moral claim, and they think the correct protocol assigns weights that scale with the number of claimants in a non-linear fashion, partly to avoid a replaceability objection. The other faction—the Pairwise Relationalists—adopts a Taurek-inspired view with a twist they think the standard literature misses: they claim that moral comparison between rescue options is legitimate only pairwise, that no transitive "greater number" relation is morally real, and that any protocol must therefore be structured as a sequence of head-to-head comparisons rather than an aggregate score. They have been talking past each other because the Aggregative Pluralists keep framing disagreements in terms of "how much weight" to assign, while the Pairwise Relationalists reject that quantitative framing altogether and insist that the very idea of weighing five claims against one is a category error.

We want to produce a joint adversarial-collaboration document before the next review board meeting. The document should contain: (1) a numbered list of agreed claims—covering our shared commitments to the moral equality of persons, the permissibility of random selection when claims are exactly equal, the empirical limits of current rescue technology, and the inevitability of partial rescue; (2) a disagreements table stating each side's position in wording that both camps would accept as accurate to their own view, with no straw-manning—particularly on whether "weight" is merely metaphorical or literally quantitative, and whether pairwise comparison necessarily rules out all probabilistic allocation mechanisms; (3) the cruxes that would actually move at least one side's opinion, which I suspect include (a) whether moral reasons in rescue contexts are cardinal or merely ordinal, (b) whether a protocol designed in advance—when the exact number of victims may be epistemically indistinguishable between, say, four and five—should specify different rules for "four versus one" and "five versus one," and (c) whether there is a meaningful distinction between "saving the greater number" and "giving every person an equal ex ante chance of being among the saved"; and (4) for each crux, a concrete institutional design choice or hypothetical case that both sides agree is genuinely relevant, together with each camp's explicit advance prediction of what the correct protocol should specify. The document must not cite authorities, invoke bibliographies, or appeal to literature; it should be a constructive text both parties could sign, with the residual disagreement rendered exact enough that an outside reader can locate precisely where the logic forks and what evidence or case would settle it.
