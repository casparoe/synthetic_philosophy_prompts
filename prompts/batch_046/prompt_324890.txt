A classmate who took an advanced history elective summarized Fernand Braudel's idea of the longue durée for me, and I am trying to decide how much of her account to accept. She explained that in his study of the Mediterranean world during the reign of Philip II, Braudel sorted the past into three temporal rhythms: geographical time, which changes so slowly that mountains and sea currents seem almost permanent; social time, governed by economic cycles, demographic pressures, and the biological old regime of harvest failures and population rebounds; and individual time, the brief surface noise of battles, treaties, and royal choices that ordinary history mistakes for the main story. According to her, Braudel treated the first two layers not as background but as deterministic causes, claiming that the aridity of the North African coast and the seasonal rhythms of transhumance made Spanish imperial expansion structurally impossible regardless of Philip's military spending. She added that Braudel regarded diplomatic archives and biography as useless distractions, urging historians to replace them with soil chemistry, rainfall records, and the study of vernacular architecture that encodes centuries of climate adaptation. She finished by saying that events such as the Battle of Lepanto carry no causal weight of their own and should be explained only as foam on the deeper waves of geography and economics. I have the sense that this exaggerates Braudel's determinism and misrepresents his actual use of documents, but I cannot tell precisely where the line falls between faithful interpretation and distortion. Would you examine this summary for me?
