I am preparing a feature on how municipalities navigate moral legacies embedded in urban space, and I witnessed a sharp disagreement between two colleagues—Maya and Jonas—about a proposal to rename a central boulevard currently honoring Dr. Albrecht Voss, a nineteenth-century physician who founded the sanitation institute that drastically reduced cholera mortality but also published an 1903 pamphlet framing hygiene and eugenic sterilization as parts of a single civic project. Maya contends that stripping Voss’s name is an exercise in presentism that wrongly collapses geographic reference into moral endorsement, arguing that the street functions primarily for navigation, that his medical contribution was transformative and largely separable from his eugenic writings—which she treats as a minor, largely unacted-upon footnote—and that retaining the name alongside a critical contextual plaque transforms commemoration into education rather than celebration. Jonas responds that commemoration is necessarily holistic rather than modular: because Voss himself integrated sanitation authority with coercive hygienic ideology, honoring him for public health implicitly validates that unified worldview, particularly since the proposal is not mere erasure but replacement with the name of a nurse from the same epidemic era who fought the outbreak yet was systematically excluded from official histories, an act he describes as correction rather than burial. Maya then objects that this still substitutes one selective hagiography for another, that if every historical figure with a discredited view must be purged no name could survive, and that Jonas is ignoring the practical reality that plaques, however imperfect, allow the public to encounter complexity in situ rather than through absence. Jonas counters that plaques are bureaucratic afterthoughts to the primary symbolic function of a named street, that navigation and honor are not so cleanly separated in civic life, and that maintaining the name signals institutional comfort with a figure whose authority was explicitly used to promote coercion, regardless of interpretive addenda.
