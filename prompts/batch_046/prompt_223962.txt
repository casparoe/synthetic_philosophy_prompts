I have been leading a graduate seminar on Kant's moral philosophy, and my students have become somewhat stuck in the standard debate over whether Kant's indirect-duty view regarding animals is ultimately defensible. Rather than asking them to rehearse the familiar objections—that cruelty to animals hardens our dispositions toward human beings, or that it treats rational nature inconsistently—I want them to construct a more architecturally ambitious defense of direct constraints that remains fully internal to Kant's system.

Please argue for the following position: Kant's framework, properly reconstructed, supports direct moral prohibitions on gratuitous animal suffering, but on grounds that do not depend on ascribing intrinsic moral worth to non-rational beings. Your argument should turn on a distinction I want you to inject and develop—call it the distinction between "dispositional harm" and "practical-structural incoherence." On the standard indirect-duty reading (dispositional harm), cruelty to animals is wrong only because it cultivates habits that may spill over into cruelty toward persons. On your proposed reading (practical-structural incoherence), a rational agent who adopts maxims permitting the systematic, trivial suffering of sentient beings introduces a contradiction not in the logical form of the maxim itself, but in her own practical self-relation: she treats sentience—which she necessarily recognizes in herself and in other rational agents as the substrate of moral agency—as normatively weightless in non-rational beings, thereby undermining the consistency required for the universalization of her maxims of respect.

In developing this argument, explain precisely how this reconstructed view differs both from the standard indirect-duty interpretation and from modern attempts to extend Kantian dignity to animals by direct analogy. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence and then provide the reasoning, with no preamble and without restating the question. Write formally, appropriate for a philosophy seminar, and avoid appeals to specific page citations, bibliographies, or obscure secondary literature that could not be reconstructed from general knowledge of Kant's ethics.
