I am drafting a comparative essay for my constitutional law seminar on what I am tentatively calling "inverted subsidiarity": cases where subnational units actively seek centralization rather than resisting it. Most of our course readings—covering the revival of U.S. federalism, the EU's Article 5 TEU protocol, and Swiss cantonal autonomy—treat subsidiarity as a defensive shield for local decision-making. I want to argue that this framing misses a strategic dimension that becomes visible in environmental and fiscal policy.

My essay focuses on three comparative nodes. First, I examine the EU's early-warning mechanism for national parliaments under the Lisbon Treaty, observing that reasoned opinions have tended to cluster around labor-market and social-policy proposals rather than climate harmonization measures. From this I infer that member-state legislatures sometimes welcome Brussels-level standard-setting when it prevents competitive deregulation among themselves. Second, I contrast this dynamic with Germany's Article 72 of the Basic Law and the role of the Bundesrat. Because the Länder are represented through their state executives rather than through independent legislative chambers, I argue that they face a different calculus: supporting a federal framework law can allow state governments to share fiscal burdens while retaining administrative implementation, which blurs the line between defending and surrendering local competence. Third, I turn to Switzerland, where cantons possess strong constitutional autonomy and direct-democratic veto points—such as the mandatory referendum for constitutional amendments—yet cantonal governments have frequently endorsed stricter federal environmental standards, apparently because uniform rules reduce the transaction costs of cross-cantonal coordination.

I am not asking for a bibliography; rather, I would appreciate targeted comments on whether the concept of "inverted subsidiarity" holds together conceptually, and whether my characterization of the Bundesrat's incentives risks conflating legislative procedure with fiscal federalism. Does it make sense to say that the Länder strategically support concurrent legislation, or am I misreading the institutional logic? I also wonder whether comparing the EU's inter-parliamentary yellow-card procedure with the Bundesrat's executive representation is misleading, since the actors and veto thresholds differ substantially. Any suggestions for sharpening the comparative framework—or for identifying where the essay risks collapsing into a generic account of multilevel governance—would be very helpful.
