At a recent philosophy reading group, two members—Mira and Jonas—debated Anscombe’s ethical critique of Harry Truman’s decision to authorize the atomic bombing of Hiroshima. They asked me to referee because they reached an impasse, and I want an external adjudication of their actual arguments, not a general summary of Anscombe’s view.

Mira began by stating Anscombe’s absolute prohibition: it is never permissible to intend the death of innocent people as either an end or a means. She argued that conventional strategic bombing can sometimes satisfy the principle of double effect—when the direct object is a legitimate military target and civilian deaths are genuinely collateral—because the damage to the enemy is achieved through the destruction of the factory or rail yard itself. But with Hiroshima, Mira claimed, the weapon’s scale and the choice of a densely populated urban center meant that the terror and death of civilians were not side effects but the very causal mechanism by which Japan was to be coerced into surrender. Therefore, she concluded, Truman intended those deaths as a means, making him guilty of murder regardless of the lives saved.

Jonas replied that Mira was assuming the conclusion. He insisted that the primary intention was the destruction of Hiroshima’s military-industrial infrastructure and the demonstration of overwhelming force to Japanese leadership; the psychological shock was a foreseen consequence, not the intended means of achieving surrender. He analogized the case to bombing a munitions plant located in a residential district: the good effect (crippling war production) is achieved through the direct damage to the plant, while the terror among survivors, though predictable, is not the causal pathway to victory. If Mira were correct, Jonas added, then nearly all large-scale strategic bombing in modern warfare would be morally impermissible, which suggests her interpretation renders Anscombe’s view implausibly broad rather than precisely critical.

Mira responded by introducing a distinction she said Jonas was ignoring. She noted that Anscombe, writing within a framework of Catholic moral theology, distinguishes between formal cooperation with evil—where one shares the wrongdoer’s intention—and material cooperation—where one participates without sharing that intention. Mira argued that Jonas’s factory-bombing analogy treats Truman as if he were merely materially cooperating with an evil he did not direct. But Truman was not an incidental surgeon sharing an operating room with an unjust act; he was the principal agent selecting the weapon, the target, and the purpose. Consequently, she said, the cooperation framework actually undermines Jonas’s defense: because Truman directed the act itself, there is no separation between the military damage and the terror; they are structurally fused in the atomic weapon’s deployment, and the civilian deaths are used precisely because they are unavoidable and massive.

Jonas countered that Mira was again begging the question about the “structural fusion” of terror and military damage. He pointed out that target selection committees emphasized Hiroshima’s urban-industrial character—its port facilities, military headquarters, and manufacturing—rather than purely demographic density, which suggests the direct object was genuinely military. He also argued that if Mira’s standard requires that no large-scale unavoidable civilian harm can ever be foreseen in a just war, then her position collapses into a form of pacifism that Anscombe herself never explicitly endorsed. He asked whether Mira was prepared to accept that implication, or whether she had an independent criterion for when civilian harm crosses the line from collateral to instrumental.

They turned to me. Please adjudicate their disagreement point by point. Specifically: Is Jonas correct that the psychological shock can be a foreseen but unintended consequence in atomic bombing, analogous to conventional strategic bombing? Is Mira correct that applying Anscombe’s cooperation distinction shows Truman was a principal agent directing harm rather than a material cooperator, and does that distinction actually defeat Jonas’s double-effect defense? Furthermore, identify what each party is missing—whether there is a hidden assumption about the ontology of “intention” in collective or institutional agency that neither has addressed, and whether their dispute ultimately depends on an empirical claim about target selection that neither can settle without historical investigation. Finally, say who is more right overall, and explain why, without simply restating either side’s conclusion.
