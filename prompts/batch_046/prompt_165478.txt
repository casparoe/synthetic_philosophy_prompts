Write a dialogue between two philosophers of history—Dr. Elena Voss, who defends the contrastive framing of the Great Divergence, and Dr. Marcus Chen, who rejects it—debating whether the question “Why Britain and not China?” is well posed. Voss should argue that the question is legitimate only if it satisfies a criterion of modal robustness: the counterfactual “China industrializes first” must be stable across nearby possible worlds where initial conditions vary within historically plausible bounds, and she should claim that comparative work on state fiscal capacity and coal geography meets this test. Chen should respond not by denying the empirical premises but by attacking the framing itself through the idea of path-dependent singularity: he should contend that Britain’s industrialization was a singular, non-replicable concatenation of contingent events (Atlantic trade circuits, specific parliamentary borrowing mechanisms, localized coal seams) such that there is no coherent counterfactual baseline for “China” to have followed, and that treating China’s non-industrialization as a parallel outcome commits an explanatory asymmetry—explaining a presence and explaining an absence require different logical structures. The dialogue must contain real back-and-forth: Voss should ask whether Chen’s singularity claim collapses into an anti-comparative exceptionalism that makes all macro-history impossible, and Chen should ask whether Voss’s modal-robustness test smuggles in the very teleological trajectory it claims to avoid by treating industrialization as a stable attractor. Neither speaker should be a strawman. Conclude the prompt by asking that the response state how confident it is in its reconstruction of these positions and single out the specific point—whether about modal robustness, path-dependent singularity, or explanatory asymmetry—about which it is least sure.
