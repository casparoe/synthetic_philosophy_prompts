I was present at a discussion between two colleagues—one a clinical psychologist who works with trauma survivors, the other a philosopher who defends a version of tranquilism according to which only the absence of craving and suffering carries value, and nothing positively matters. They were debating whether tranquilism can account for the intuition that a life of achieved mastery followed by serene detachment is better than a life of uninterrupted, passive contentment that never involved struggle or creation.

The psychologist said that if both lives end with the same absence of craving and suffering, tranquilism must judge them equal, which erases the value we place on resilience and on the process of building something. The philosopher replied that this intuition is itself a form of craving—specifically, a narrative craving for significance—and that tranquilism correctly diagnoses it rather than honoring it. The psychologist then proposed a distinction between residual craving, which remains as a scar from past suffering, and structural craving, which is built into an active life of pursuit; she argued that the survivor who has genuinely eliminated both is rare, but that the process of eliminating structural craving through mastery produces a qualitatively different tranquility—deeper, more stable—than the passivity of never having had cravings. The philosopher countered that this smuggles in positive value: if the mastery-process tranquility is deeper, that depth must be cashed out either as a further absence (of instability) or as something positively good. If the former, then the passive life could in principle reach the same stability without the struggle; if the latter, tranquilism is abandoned. The psychologist then raised what she called the authority objection: if all positive evaluation is a craving, then the tranquilist's own preference for absence of craving is itself a craving and has no special authority. The philosopher answered that the authority comes not from the preference's strength but from its self-canceling structure: recognizing craving as suffering undermines the craving itself, whereas recognizing achievement as valuable reinforces further achievement-craving.

Can you go through their exchange round by round and say who is right about each specific point—not just who wins overall? In particular, address whether the psychologist's distinction between residual and structural craving is coherent, whether the philosopher's diagnosis of narrative craving succeeds, and whether the authority objection lands. Also identify what both parties are missing: is there a third option that preserves the core of tranquilism without collapsing into the psychologist's intuition, or that preserves the intuition without abandoning tranquilism?

Please say what would change your mind—specifically, which argument or empirical consideration about the phenomenology of post-traumatic tranquility versus passive contentment would lead you to a different verdict, and where your reasoning is most likely to fail, especially regarding the self-canceling structure claim.
