I am preparing an independent research proposal for my school's advanced philosophy of ethics seminar, and I have become preoccupied with lexical threshold negative utilitarianism—specifically, the view that suffering of sufficient intensity is not merely quantitatively worse but lexically prior to any amount of pleasure—together with the deeper suspicion that pleasure and pain may not be interpersonally, or even intrapersonally, commensurable on a single continuous scale. Rather than asking for a literature survey, I want to understand how to allocate my own limited time: if I had roughly one semester and access to a small advisory group to work only on the conceptual core of this position, which open philosophical questions should I treat as genuinely upstream?

In particular, I have been trying to articulate what I call the "calibration asymmetry" problem: if we deny that pleasure and pain share a common metric, then any attempt to locate a lexical threshold—say, the boundary between tolerable discomfort and catastrophic suffering—seems to smuggle in a cross-dimensional comparison that the incommensurability thesis itself appears to forbid. This leads me to three candidate lines of inquiry, though I am unsure which deserve priority. First, can the commensurability problem be dissolved conceptually—perhaps by treating the threshold as a normative decision boundary rather than a psychometric measurement—without collapsing lexical threshold negative utilitarianism into a simpler hedonistic calculus? Second, is there a defensible, non-circular way to justify the specific location of that threshold, given that its placement seems to presuppose the very weighting of suffering that the view is meant to establish? Third, to what extent does the distinction between "eliminable" suffering, which might be addressed through institutional or technological change, and "structural" suffering, which may be inseparable from the conditions of conscious existence, alter the urgency of refining the lexical ordering?

Could you rank these three lines—along with any others you think I have overlooked—according to which are most tractable for purely philosophical work rather than empirical science, which function as logical prerequisites for the others, and which, if resolved, would most change how one should think about practical ethical priorities? I am less interested in a flat list than in the reasoning behind the ordering: the criteria you use to weigh conceptual purity against practical applicability, the trade-offs between them, and what kind of philosophical progress over the next few years would cause you to reorder them.
