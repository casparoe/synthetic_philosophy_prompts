I'm drafting an essay for a general science-and-ideas audience that tries to move the meat-versus-crops debate away from its usual direct-slaughter arithmetic and toward what I tentatively call trophic-time accounting. The familiar comparison holds that chicken production causes more suffering per calorie than beef because of the sheer number of lives involved, while crop agriculture eliminates direct slaughter but causes field-animal deaths during planting and harvest. I want to examine a less-discussed axis: the difference between the brief, intense suffering of farmed animals and the prolonged, diffuse suffering of wild invertebrates in ecosystems disrupted by different agricultural regimes.

Specifically, I propose comparing beef raised on perennial pasture—which disturbs soil biota only through grazing pressure and occasional renovation—against high-yield monoculture crops that require annual tilling, pesticide application, and the collapse of soil food webs, and against industrial chicken production, which concentrates suffering in short lives but demands large feed-crop footprints. The conceptual question is whether a calculus focused solely on the number of sentient lives affected misses a morally relevant distinction in the temporal structure of suffering: is one year of low-intensity invertebrate distress in tilled soil equivalent to, or less weighty than, a few weeks of high-intensity distress in a broiler house? I also want to explore whether pasture-based beef might, counterintuitively, preserve more wild invertebrate life-years per hectare than the crop systems that replace both beef and chicken in a plant-based diet, and whether that possibility undermines or merely complicates the standard recommendation to minimize meat consumption.

Below are the core conceptual moves rather than a finished draft. First, I distinguish between mortality accounting (counting deaths) and trophic-time accounting (integrating duration and intensity of suffering over ecological time). Second, I suggest that crop cultivation is not ethically neutral with respect to wild suffering because tillage is a recurring, landscape-scale disturbance event, whereas grazing is a localized, continuous pressure that some soil communities adapt to. Third, I ask whether the usual preference for replacing beef with legumes holds if legumes require more intensive soil disturbance per calorie delivered than perennial forage.

Please comment on whether this framing is conceptually coherent, where it risks empirical overreach—particularly in assuming we can compare invertebrate suffering across such different ecological contexts—and whether it offers a genuinely distinct lens or merely rebrands existing wild-animal-suffering arguments. I am not asking for citations or reading suggestions; I want critical engagement with the logic and the conceptual architecture itself.
