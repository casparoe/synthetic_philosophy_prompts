Write a short story, roughly 800 words, that applies the logic of Einstein's hole argument to personal identity rather than to spacetime. In your setting, cognitive scientists have shown that the brain's storage substrate lacks intrinsic identity: only relational patterns of memory and affect are physically real. Consequently, a localized "blank" region—whether from injury or deliberate editing—can be filled with infinitely many equivalent but non-isomorphic configurations of past experience, all consistent with the subject's present mental state. The protagonist should be someone who discovers this about herself: perhaps a memory-restoration therapist, an archivist of consciousness, or a patient recovering from surgery. Show, do not explain in lectures, the specific philosophical pressure: she asks whether a particular childhood trauma occurred to her, and realizes that the present relational structure of her mind does not determine a unique answer, because the underlying "points" (individual storage events) have no individual identity independent of the pattern. Have her face a choice between accepting a purely relational self—where large parts of her biography are genuinely underdetermined—and insisting on a bare substrate that anchors every event to a unique past, knowing that this substrate is now permanently inaccessible inside the blank region. The story should make the technical structure of the hole argument lived and emotional, not metaphorical: the ambiguity must concern the identity of the points themselves, not merely unreliable testimony or missing records. Avoid naming philosophical schools or citing texts; let the concept emerge through the plot and her reasoning.
