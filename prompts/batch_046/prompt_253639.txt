As a novelist constructing a narrative that reverses the protagonist's subjective chronology—her memories accumulate backward, so she "recalls" effects before experiencing their causes—I have observed a consistent reader response that I lack a term for. Even when the text makes clear that her consciousness runs counter to the physical order, readers still assign causal and temporal priority to the entropic direction: smoke rising, bodies aging, heat dissipating. The subjective arrow of her awareness does not override the physical arrow in the audience's interpretation; rather, the audience treats the thermodynamic sequence as the "true" timeline and her reversed cognition as a distortion of it. I have seen the same resistance in workshop responses to circular-time narratives: when a loop closes, readers instinctively preserve a linear, entropy-aligned backbone rather than accepting a genuinely cyclical ontology. Is there an established concept for this specific asymmetry—the dominance of thermodynamic causal authority over subjective or narrative temporal orientation? If there is no precise term, can you define the phenomenon and delimit it from the familiar psychological arrow of time, from narrative anachrony, and from the metaphysical problem of time's direction? I want the carving, not a bare label: what belongs inside the phenomenon, what lies outside, and whether it is a feature of cognition, of narrative convention, or of physical intuition.
