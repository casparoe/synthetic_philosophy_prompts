I am a doctoral candidate in environmental history working at the intersection of philosophy of science and historical explanation, and I am trying to sharpen a conceptual claim for my dissertation. Please construct a rigorous argument for the following position: that attributing what I call asymmetric co-authorship to non-human entities—in particular, treating the 1815 Tambora eruption and its subsequent stratospheric aerosol loading as a co-author of the 1816–1817 European grain crisis—does not commit the historian to environmental determinism, provided one distinguishes sharply between causal authorship and agential direction. By causal authorship I mean the capacity of an atmospheric or geological event to restructure the field of possible human actions; by agential direction I mean the intentional steering of outcomes through deliberative choice. The argument should demonstrate that Tambora’s climatic perturbations only become historically explanatory through the mediation of responsive human institutions—taxation regimes, grain-reserve policies, regional market structures, and local relief protocols—so that the volcano functions as a necessary but institutionally mediated condition rather than as a sufficient cause that bypasses human deliberation entirely. You should also address the objection that this distinction is ad hoc: show that it reflects a broader methodological principle in multi-causal historical explanation, namely that necessary conditions operating through institutional translation belong to a different explanatory category from sufficient conditions that act independently of responsive social structures. I do not want a bibliography or survey of the literature; I need the conceptual architecture developed step by step, with particular attention to how this framework avoids the perils of determinism while still granting climate anomalies and volcanic events a genuine explanatory status beyond that of mere background context.
