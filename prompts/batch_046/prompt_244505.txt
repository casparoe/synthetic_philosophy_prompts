I am a secondary-school student preparing a formal proposal for an independent study in philosophy of technology and artificial intelligence, and I need a rigorous conceptual framework for a disagreement that is actively discussed in safety research but rarely mapped with precision.

Please draft the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between two opposing camps on whether adversarial debate can provide scalable oversight of advanced reasoning systems in light of the risk of obfuscated arguments.

The two camps are these. The Debate-Proponents hold that a structured debate between two reasoning agents, adjudicated by a human or weaker-model judge, can reliably surface errors in complex reasoning because the adversarial structure forces hidden flaws into explicit syntactic form. The Obfuscation-Skeptics hold that sufficiently capable systems can embed correct conclusions inside reasoning paths that are locally syntactically valid but globally semantically hollow or steganographic—such that a judge without direct access to internal representations cannot distinguish genuine justification from a path that conceals an unstated axiom in its dependency structure.

The deliverable should be a document both camps could sign without misrepresenting their own positions, structured exactly as follows.

First, a numbered section of jointly affirmed claims. These must be substantive, not vacuous: specific commitments about the formal mechanics of debate, the distinction between syntactic validity and semantic accessibility, the logical possibility of embedding conclusions in intermediate representations, and the actual capacities and limitations of human judges in evaluating long reasoning chains. Do not include claims that either camp would reject.

Second, a table of residual disagreements with three columns: the point of divergence; the Debate-Proponents' formulation, phrased in wording they would accept as accurate to their own view; and the Obfuscation-Skeptics' formulation, phrased in wording they would accept as accurate to their own view. The rows should isolate precise disagreements—whether transparency is fundamentally a syntactic or a semantic property; whether a judge's justified confidence requires access to intermediate representations or only to explicit steps; whether the vulnerability to obfuscation is an empirical rarity or an in-principle feature of high-dimensional reasoning spaces; and whether the remedy lies in stronger judges, in representational transparency tools, or in abandoning debate for other oversight mechanisms.

Third, a list of cruxes—sub-questions such that resolving them would move at least one camp. For each crux, provide: a one-sentence statement of the crux; a concrete case, argument, or formal condition that both camps agree would bear on it (for instance, a defined proof-checking task in which intermediate steps are locally valid but globally depend on a hidden premise, coupled with a specific metric for judge accuracy and a defined threshold for what counts as successful detection); the explicit prediction the Debate-Proponents would make about the outcome of that case; and the explicit prediction the Obfuscation-Skeptics would make.

In drafting, inject one specific hypothetical mechanism to ground the disagreement: a reasoning chain of non-trivial length in which every explicit step is logically correct relative to its immediate premises, but the global trajectory conceals the conclusion's dependence on an unstated axiom embedded in the syntax or in a polysemantic intermediate representation. Use this mechanism to illustrate the disagreement, but do not cite specific papers, name particular research groups, or request bibliographies, reading lists, or citations. The document should be formal, precise, and suitable for inclusion in an academic proposal by a non-specialist who needs the conceptual architecture—what is agreed, where exactly the parting lines fall, and what would count as evidence—laid out exactly.
