Consider four competing interpretations of Adam Smith's position on empire and colonial administration: (A) the fiscal-realist interpretation, according to which Smith objected to colonies primarily because the costs of defense and administration exceeded the monopoly profits they yielded to the metropole; (B) the constitutional-incompatibility interpretation, according to which Smith held that the establishment of representative assemblies in colonies created an irreconcilable conflict with the legislative supremacy claimed by the imperial parliament; (C) the developmental-release interpretation, according to which Smith's framework of societal stages implied that colonies should be granted independence once they reached commercial manufacturing maturity, since continued subordination then served only to retard natural growth; and (D) the corporate-corruption interpretation, according to which Smith's central grievance was not imperial sovereignty per se but the delegation of governing power to chartered monopolies such as the East India Company, which corrupted both commerce and administration.

Rank these four interpretations into tiers—textually well-grounded, partially supported but overextended, textually ambiguous, and largely anachronistic—by assessing each against three criteria drawn from Smith's broader system: his account of capital accumulation and distant investment, his critique of state-granted monopoly privilege, and his specific remarks concerning the American colonies and the principle of taxation linked to representation. For each interpretation, state in one sentence which criterion it best satisfies and which it most clearly violates. Do not provide bibliographies, page references, or lists of secondary literature.
