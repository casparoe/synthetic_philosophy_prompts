Exam Q: Orwell's essay Politics and the English Language and the Newspeak material in 1984 both treat political language as both symptom and engine of tyranny. But they differ in whether corruption works mainly pragmatically—hiding specific acts behind euphemism—or epistemically—shrinking the conceptual space for dissent. Compare the two texts on this distinction, and evaluate whether an individual writer's commitment to concrete, precise language could interrupt the loop once a system is already epistemically foreclosed.

Student answer:
Orwell definitely sees a loop. In the essay he shows politicians using dead metaphors and passive voice to cover up violence—like calling bombing villagers pacification—which lets people accept policies without facing them. This is pragmatic corruption: reality is still there, just hidden. He thinks this happens partly because writers are lazy and imitate bad phrases, but also because it serves power. Then in 1984 Newspeak goes deeper. The Party deletes words like freedom, so citizens literally can't form rebellious thoughts. Syme notes they're reducing vocabulary with each new dictionary edition. This is epistemic foreclosure: not hiding reality but removing the mental tools to grasp it.

The link is that both protect power, but the essay focuses on bottom-up writing habits while 1984 shows top-down engineering. Orwell's remedy—concrete images, avoid cliches, prefer Saxon roots—works for pragmatic corruption because clear writing exposes hidden acts. But once epistemic foreclosure sets in, individual clarity is useless since the state controls the dictionary. So reform can break the loop early, but not in Oceania. Orwell is pessimistic about the latter but still thinks writers should resist by being precise.
