I have been observing a recurring argumentative pattern within a narrowly regulated microdomain—the classification of small-batch ceramic glazes that contain trace minerals structurally identical to compounds used in industrial coatings. In this context, the term "naturally derived pigment" is anchored to two distinct operational definitions. Under the safety-certification register, it denotes extraction from raw mineral sources without synthetic molecular restructuring, which permits exemption from certain industrial chemical registrations. Under the consumer-marketing register, it denotes production through processes that do not involve petrochemical synthesis, a broader claim that supports premium artisanal branding. The phenomenon I wish to identify is not simple equivocation. Rather, it is a specific defensive architecture: when regulators challenge the marketing claim by showing that the extraction process involves chemical restructuring, proponents retreat to the technical register and accuse critics of conflating derivation with synthesis. Once the regulatory threat has passed, they revert to the broader consumer-facing claim without specifying which register governs which assertion. The two definitions thus form a protective loop—each shields the other from refutation, yet neither is held consistently accountable across contexts.

I am familiar with philosophical discussions of conceptual fluidity, family resemblance, and strategic ambiguity in institutional language, as well as with the idea that technical terms can migrate into folk usage. I am also aware that philosophers of science have described protective-belt adjustments around core theories. My question is whether this particular configuration—where a domain-specific concept is tied to two precisely defined operational criteria, and where defenders systematically oscillate between them in a loop that prevents either from being independently falsified—has an established name in philosophy, sociology of knowledge, or regulatory studies. Does it fall under existing categories such as operational pluralism, motte-and-bailey reasoning, or what is sometimes called register shifting? Or does it represent a distinct failure mode of conceptual governance within tightly bounded technical niches? If no standard term exists, I would welcome a precise coinage together with clear boundary conditions, particularly distinguishing it from cases where a concept genuinely evolves over time rather than being strategically anchored to two static definitions.
