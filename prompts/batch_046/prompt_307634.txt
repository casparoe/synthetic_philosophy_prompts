I have recently become concerned with how my own beliefs are justified, and I keep encountering the regress problem across very different domains. Here are my verdicts on five cases; I want to know whether one principle captures all of them, or which ones must be surrendered. First, a scientific explanation that eventually bottoms out in basic physical laws feels properly justified. Second, a conspiracy theory supported only by other conspiracy theories, with no independent evidential check, feels unjustified even though it is internally coherent. Third, legal reasoning that traces authority back through centuries of precedent—without any single founding statute that resolves the present case—strikes me as legitimate despite lacking a foundation. Fourth, a philosopher's claim that justification can take the form of an infinite, non-circular chain of reasons, each link genuinely distinct, strikes me as suspicious but not clearly wrong. Fifth, an AI system that explains its outputs by referencing weights optimized against outputs of other models, producing a loop with no human-grounded training data at the base, seems to me to lack justification entirely, even though the loop is vast and consistent. Is there a single principle—perhaps about the kind of support each link must provide, or about when termination is genuinely unnecessary—that explains why I accept the first and third but reject the second and fifth, and that determines whether I should accept the fourth? Please state the principle precisely, show which of my verdicts it saves and which it forces me to drop, and apply it to a case I have not listed: moral justification in which every normative principle is defended solely by appeal to further normative principles with no non-normative foundation.
