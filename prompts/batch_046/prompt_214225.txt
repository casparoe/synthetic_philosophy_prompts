I've been arguing with a friend—she works in urban planning, I'm finishing coursework in moral philosophy—about whether working-class value outlooks are genuinely defensible on philosophical grounds or merely adaptive responses to economic constraint. The debate quickly moved past sociology into conceptual territory: does the spatial and occupational structure of one's life generate distinct moral vocabularies, or do rural and urban, working-class and professional-class outlooks simply apply shared norms under different conditions?

I'm writing a long-form piece, and I want to avoid the familiar political framing of "coastal elites versus heartland values." Instead, I'm trying to identify which open questions in this space are actually upstream of any rigorous comparison. There is the problem of whether values formed under conditions of scarcity and interdependence should be evaluated by the same standards as those formed under abundance and institutional mobility. There is the question of whether "defensibility" requires universalizability or can accommodate what I will call moral ecology—the idea that ethical frameworks are partially constituted by their material and geographic conditions rather than merely caused by them. And there is the underexplored issue of whether professional-class cosmopolitanism and working-class localism represent genuinely incommensurable goods or merely competing weightings of loyalty, fairness, and autonomy.

Given roughly one semester to develop a coherent argument, I need a prioritized agenda. Which two or three of these open questions should I tackle first? I want the ordering defended by explicit criteria: dependency (which answers unlock others), tractability with conceptual rather than empirical tools, and whether resolving a question actually shifts the evaluation of the outlooks or just generates philosophical noise. I am particularly interested in whether there is an upstream crux—perhaps around the evaluation of adaptive preferences or the possibility of context-neutral moral assessment—that renders other debates redundant. Please treat this as a request for a defended research agenda, not a survey of rural sociology or class studies, and do not provide a bibliography or reading list. I need to know where to spend limited time, which questions are downstream of others, and which apparent disagreements are overrated because settling them would not change the core philosophical comparison.
