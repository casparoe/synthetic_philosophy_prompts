My roommate studies path dependence in tech and has made these calls: QWERTY on mechanical typewriters is pure lock-in, same layout wouldn't have arrived, technically worse than Dvorak; VHS over Betamax is pure lock-in, network effects, technically worse image quality; the 4ft 8.5in railway gauge is pure lock-in, traces back to Roman cart ruts and mining tracks, technically suboptimal for high-speed rail; the 60Hz vs 50Hz split is pure lock-in, generator design choices got reinforced by grid equipment, 60Hz slightly less efficient for transmission; but the internal combustion engine beating electric in 1900 is NOT lock-in, energy density made it inevitable; and the metric system in science is NOT lock-in, functional convergence.

Next case: in the late 1970s Intel designed the 8086 with Little Endian byte ordering, mostly because of how the address bus was wired. Today every x86 chip and almost all consumer software uses it, even though network protocols use Big Endian and byte-swapping is a constant annoyance. The technical arguments are split — Little Endian is actually faster for some arithmetic, but it's clearly an accident of one chip design. Predict whether she'll call this locked in or not, say which of her earlier cases is the closest analog, tell me which rule she's implicitly running — the network-effects-plus-technical-inferiority one, the arbitrary-origin-plus-sunk-cost one, or the convergence-with-noise one — and name the one further case, maybe Roman numerals on analog clock faces where there's no interop at all just manufactoring tradition, that'd actually split those rules apart.
