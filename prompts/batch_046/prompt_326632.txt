I just finished a long and somewhat frustrating conversation with a friend about whether vagueness is a defect in our representations or a real feature of the world, and we kept circling the same two positions—epistemicism, according to which there are perfectly sharp boundaries that we are constitutionally unable to know, and supervaluationism, according to which there is no single correct precisification but rather a range of admissible ones—without ever clarifying what either view is actually obligated to deliver. Before I read another defense of either camp, I want you to write out the full specification of adequacy: what must any theory of vagueness actually get right? Please present, in continuous prose as in a long letter or email with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, the complete list of desiderata—the specific phenomena it must explain, the distinctions it must preserve, the logical and linguistic results it must respect, and the puzzles it must dissolve—together with the motivating case or consideration behind each one. I want you to distinguish the genuinely non-negotiable requirements from those that are merely preferences of particular camps, and I want you to include the familiar demands: resolving the sorites paradox without positing arbitrary sharp cutoffs, accommodating the intuition of tolerance, handling higher-order vagueness without generating a revenge problem in the theory’s own theoretical vocabulary, and remaining consistent with classical logic in domains where precision is appropriate.

Alongside those standard constraints, I want you to inject and defend one further requirement that rarely receives explicit treatment: the theory must account for stake-sensitive boundary shifts, that is, cases in which an agent’s practical stakes alter which vague predicates they are willing to apply—such as being more cautious about calling a heap “large” when the stakes are high—without any corresponding change in their underlying beliefs about the world, and it must do so without collapsing into full-blown contextualism or a mere speaker-error theory. Then, still in unbroken prose, tell me whether any subset of these desiderata is jointly unsatisfiable—if, for example, maintaining unrestricted bivalence, denying that there are unknown sharp boundaries, and retaining full classical logic for all statements cannot all be held together—and identify precisely which combination creates the tension. Finally, briefly assess how epistemicism and supervaluationism each fare against this entire specification, noting for each view which desiderata it satisfies cleanly and where it struggles, especially with the stake-sensitive shift condition. Do not provide a bibliography, a reading list, or citations of any kind; I need the reasoning itself, not directions to further texts.
