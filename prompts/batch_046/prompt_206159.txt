Draft the joint statement that two opposing interpretive camps—the Rational-Autonomy Democrats and the Sovereign-Multitude Collectivists—could both sign after an adversarial collaboration on Spinoza’s theory of democracy. The Rational-Autonomy Democrats maintain that Spinoza’s democratic state derives its legitimacy from enhancing each citizen’s rational capacity, leaving a protected sphere where adequate ideas resist collective error; the Sovereign-Multitude Collectivists maintain that democracy is for Spinoza the absolute form of government precisely because individual will is entirely dissolved into the collective power of the multitudo, leaving no rights or rational capacities independent of the sovereign decision.

The document must contain:

A numbered section of agreed claims. These should cover Spinoza’s characterization of democracy in relation to other regimes (monarchy and aristocracy), his exclusion of certain classes (women, dependents, foreigners) from the democratic assembly, his view that democracy is the form of government closest to the state of nature, and the role of common notions in political reasoning. Formulate these without quotation marks and without citing chapter numbers, editions, or translators; paraphrase all textual points.

A disagreements table with four columns: (1) the precise formulation of disagreement, written so that each camp accepts the wording describing its own position; (2) the crux—the specific sub-question whose resolution would move at least one camp; (3) the case, argument, or conceptual test both sides agree is relevant; and (4) each side’s explicit prediction of what that test will show.

Include at least three cruxes. One must concern whether Spinoza’s metaphysics of adequate and inadequate ideas permits democratic citizens to hold rationally justified dissent against a majority decision, or whether dissent is necessarily a form of inadequate imagination that undermines sovereignty. Another must concern whether the exclusions from the democratic assembly are contingent historical limitations or structurally necessary conditions for the unity of the democratic will. A third should address whether the democratic state’s stability depends on citizens sharing a homogeneous rational nature, or whether Spinoza allows for pluralistic common notions that do not collapse into a single collective mind.

Throughout, avoid all bibliographic references, reading lists, and citations to secondary literature—particularly anything published after 1980. Do not invoke obscure manuscript variants, translation disputes, or specialized terminology unknown to a reader familiar with Spinoza’s Ethics and his political treatise. The statement should read as a formal, polished collaborative document, not a dialogue, and should separate genuine philosophical disagreements from disagreements that are merely verbal.
