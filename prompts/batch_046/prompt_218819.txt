I'm preparing a contribution for a graduate seminar on the conceptual foundations of statistical mechanics, and I've become stuck on a comparison that the literature treats separately without confronting them head-on. On one side stands the Landauer-Bennett resolution of Maxwell's demon: the demon may reduce the entropy of a gas, but only by accumulating information in its discrete memory, and the second law is preserved because eventually that memory must be erased, dissipating at least kT ln 2 per bit. On the other side stands the Szilard-style information-to-energy equivalence, which treats the act of measurement itself as the physical acquisition of energy-information, and which tends to incorporate the demon's internal state into the thermodynamic system from the outset rather than treating it as an external controller.

What I need is a direct conceptual comparison of these two frameworks, focused on a specific variant proposed by my instructor: a continuous-field demon that does not record discrete bit-values for individual molecules, but instead maintains a smooth potential barrier whose shape is continuously updated by a coarse-grained density measurement. In this variant there is no discrete register to erase, yet there remains an information-bearing physical structure—the barrier's configuration—that correlates with the gas macrostate. How does the Landauer-Bennett account, which appears to depend on discrete erasure events, apply here? Does the continuous-field case push us inevitably toward the Szilard equivalence, or does it instead show that Landauer's principle requires a generalized field-theoretic formulation? I am especially interested in whether the epistemic status of the demon's correlation differs between the two accounts—whether in one framework it is merely representational knowledge about the system, and in the other it is constitutively part of the physical state that defines entropy.
