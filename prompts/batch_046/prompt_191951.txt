I spent three decades in condensed-matter physics before retiring, and over the past year I have been working through philosophy of science—particularly the debate surrounding Milton Friedman’s claim that unrealistic assumptions are harmless provided predictions hold, and the various criticisms that have come to be called the F-twist. Rather than merely summarizing positions, I have tried to apply them to modeling practices I know firsthand or have studied closely, and I have formed judgments about specific cases. I want to test whether those judgments cohere around a single principle or whether they underdetermine my view.

Here are my verdicts so far.

First, I regard the Ising model in statistical mechanics—discrete spins on a perfect lattice with only nearest-neighbor interactions—as fully legitimate despite having no literal physical counterpart, because its unrealistic structure isolates the mechanism of symmetry-breaking phase transitions without distorting the causal role that interaction plays.

Second, I regard the classic Lotka-Volterra predator-prey equations—continuous populations, no spatial structure, no genetic variation, deterministic dynamics—as legitimate predictive instruments, but I deny that they genuinely explain the dynamics of any particular ecosystem, since their unrealistic homogeneity erases the very heterogeneities that cause local collapse or recovery.

Third, I once reviewed a hydrological model that treated soil as a homogeneous porous medium with constant permeability; although its river-flow predictions were accurate, I rejected it as scientifically misleading because the unrealistic homogeneity masked the actual causal pathways—channelized subsurface flow through macropores—that produced flooding during extreme weather.

Fourth, I accept a quantum-optics model that replaces actual multi-level atoms with idealized two-level systems, despite erasing hyperfine structure and Doppler broadening, because the simplification tracks a real isolable interaction—the Rabi cycle—without altering its causal structure.

Fifth, I am genuinely uncertain about an epidemiological model that introduces a fictitious “behavioral feedback” parameter, representing population-level risk aversion as a deterministic continuous function derived from prospect-theory value curves; the parameter improves prediction, yet I can identify no micro-level mechanism that corresponds to it.

Now consider this new case. A recent integrated assessment model for climate-economy analysis employs a damage function that aggregates all climate impacts—agricultural yield loss, heat-mortality, coastal infrastructure damage—into a single quadratic term in global mean temperature. The function assumes perfect substitutability across sectors and treats irreversible ice-sheet collapse as a smooth marginal cost rather than a discontinuous tipping point. Over the next century the model predicts global GDP trajectories with reasonable accuracy, but the aggregation erases sector-specific vulnerabilities and obscures the heterogeneous causal pathways through which climate forcing operates.

If I wish to be consistent with my five prior judgments, should I accept this model as legitimate, reject it, or classify it as predictive-only but not explanatory? Which of my earlier cases is the closest analog—the Ising model, the Lotka-Volterra system, the hydrological model, the two-level atom approximation, or the epidemiological feedback model? I suspect my precedents may genuinely underdetermine the answer: one rule might privilege predictive accuracy regardless of causal transparency, another might require that unrealistic assumptions isolate rather than obscure causally heterogeneous mechanisms, and a third might demand that any acceptable idealization be traceable to an identifiable micro-level counterpart. If my verdict depends on which rule I adopt, what principle am I committing myself to in each case?

Finally, to help me see which rule I am actually running, propose a further case—perhaps a neuroscience network model that replaces dendritic morphology with point-neuron dynamics, or an ecological agent-based simulation that assigns deterministic rules to individual organisms—that would distinguish whether I am applying a Friedman-style predictive criterion, a mechanism-isolation criterion, or a causal-transparency criterion.
