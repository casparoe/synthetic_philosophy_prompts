Please argue that a printed book—say, one containing the full transcript of Einstein's brain chatting away in a jar, like that thought experiment in The Mind's I—could never be conscious, even in principle, but not for the usual substrate reasons everyone trots out. Make it hang on a kind of "single-access constraint": consciousness needs its representaions to be counterfactually available to one intergrated control system in real time, whereas a book only actualises its content in discrete, reader-dependent episodes that never form a unified loop. You can grant the book holds all the information, and that different people might realise Einstein's mind from it, but insist that without a single continuous system that can both produce and consume its own representaions, there's no subject there. Head off the obvious reply that running the text on hardware in a loop fixes this—explain why that just moves consciousness to the hardware, or why it still lacks the specific diachronic unity you're after. Don't bother with citations or a reading list, and don't worry about being too polished—just lay out the steps clearly.
