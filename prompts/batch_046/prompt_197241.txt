For a 90-minute final examination in an upper-level module programme on distributive justice (final-year undergraduates or Master's-level candidates, approximately 1,200 words), draft a single essay question together with a model answer and a marking scheme. The question must turn on a constructed institutional case rather than on standard textbook summaries of luck egalitarianism, and it must not ask for bibliographies, reading lists, or biographical citations.

Set out the following case: the Risk-Conversion Scheme (RCS) permits citizens to enrol voluntarily in a pooled-compensation arrangement for specified misfortunes. Enrolment requires passing a cognitive-screening assessment that correlates with, but does not perfectly measure, actual exposure to those misfortunes. A heritable cognitive bias—call it anticipatory distortion—affects both risk-assessment and screening performance: it can cause an individual either to overestimate risk and enrol successfully, or to underestimate risk and fail the screening, despite the underlying brute-luck factor being identical in both cases.

Introduce two subjects: J carries the bias, overestimates her risk, passes the screening, enrols, suffers the misfortune, and receives compensation; K carries the same bias, underestimates her risk, fails the screening, is barred from enrolment, suffers the identical misfortune, and receives nothing.

The essay question should require candidates to do three things. First, apply the control principle and the option-luck/brute-luck distinction to determine whether J’s compensation is legitimate and whether K has a claim, specifically addressing the anticipatory-option problem—whether entering the RCS converts the underlying brute-luck factor into option luck when the very capacity to enter depends on that same factor. Second, compare how Cohen’s egalitarian ethos and Dworkin’s hypothetical-insurance scheme would treat K’s claim, identifying the precise point at which each framework risks collapsing either into a desert-based distribution (rewarding prudent risk-assessment) or into a welfare-based distribution (compensating for all disadvantage regardless of choice). Third, propose one modification to the RCS rules that preserves voluntary enrolment while bringing the scheme into alignment with luck-egalitarian justice, and defend that modification against the charge that it merely relocates brute-luck inequality from the compensation stage to the enrolment stage.

Provide a model answer that demonstrates the required philosophical moves without reciting standard summaries. Then provide a marking scheme built around five must-hit points (total 20 marks). For each point, state the marks available, the specific conceptual move required, and a one-line note on the typical misunderstanding or failure that should cost marks. The difficulty must come from navigating the circular structure of the RCS and the interaction between screening access and compensation entitlement, not from recalling labels or theorists’ biographies. Ensure the model answer and marking scheme are written in British English, and include an explicit note on how the anticipatory-option problem undermines a naive application of the choice/circumstance distinction.
