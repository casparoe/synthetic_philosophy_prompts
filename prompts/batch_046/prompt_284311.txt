I have recently started working at a company that uses an automated arbitration system for internal employee disputes, and its protocol has left me philosophically unsettled. The manual directs staff never to direct resentment or moral anger at the system itself—since it lacks intentions—yet when a harmful ruling traces back to a design flaw that engineers knowingly ignored, those engineers face disciplinary blame. If, however, the harm arises from an unforeseeable interaction between the training data and particular user inputs with no negligence involved, the firm offers compensation but assigns no blame whatsoever, treating the event as a morally neutral accident. I want to understand what implicit theory of moral responsibility this bifurcated practice presupposes. Does it require Strawson’s distinction between reactive attitudes and objective attitudes, treating the AI as permanently fit only for the latter? Or can a control-based account of blame explain why negligent designers are punished while the system that executed the harm is never resented? Is the practice compatible with an outcome-sensitive view of responsibility, or does the refusal to blame the system for unforeseeable outcomes show that moral agency—not mere causation—is being treated as a precondition for reactive attitudes? Reconstruct the implicit theory, distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what it merely accommodates, and say where it is philosophically underdetermined—especially regarding whether a Strawsonian framework can justify holding designers accountable for foreseen risks while withholding all reactive attitudes toward the non-agential system that produced the harm. Please state how confident you are in your reconstruction and single out the specific point about which you are least certain.
