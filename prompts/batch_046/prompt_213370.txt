My philosophy teacher has classified several examples according to Sartre's analysis of radical freedom and bad faith. She judged that the waiter who treats his social role as an essence is in bad faith; that the woman who, on a date, treats her own hand as a mere object to avoid deciding is in bad faith; that the soldier who claims he could not have refused orders is in bad faith; that the person who insists genetic determinism removes all responsibility is in bad faith; and that the musician who fully acknowledges her body, her history, and her contingent talents, yet still chooses to create despite the risk of failure, is authentic rather than in bad faith. Next week she will consider a new case: an individual born with a severe congenital condition that severely restricts physical movement, who insists that consciousness is pure transcendence entirely unburdened by facticity, rejects all medical or adaptive assistance, and argues that accepting any bodily limitation would itself be bad faith. I would like you to predict her verdict. Does she operate with Sartre's complete dialectic—in which denying either facticity or transcendence constitutes bad faith—so that this refusal of limitation is itself a denial of facticity and therefore bad faith? Or does she operate with a narrower principle focused only on flight from freedom and responsibility, under which this insistence on pure transcendence might be read as an authentic, if extreme, acceptance of radical freedom? Which interpretation best coheres with her treatment of the determinist versus the musician? Finally, propose a further case—perhaps an artificial system capable of rewriting its own core code but originally constructed by external programmers—that would sharply distinguish whether her implicit rule is the full two-pole dialectic or the narrower flight-from-freedom account.
