Take the 2016–2018 Irish Citizens' Assembly — the body of 99 randomly selected citizens convened to deliberate on abortion law reform, climate policy, and related issues, whose recommendations were advisory to the Oireachtas and preceded the 2018 referendum — and analyze it as a disciplined case study in lottocracy and sortition theory. Do not summarize the assembly’s proceedings; instead, treat the episode as a test of the normative architecture of random selection, mapping precisely who plays which institutional role, what counts as evidence for the theory’s claims, and where the mapping breaks.

Specifically, address the following:

1. Descriptive representation versus deliberative scale. The assembly used a small-N sample (99 citizens) selected to mirror demographic proportions. Examine whether this design resolves or merely disguises the tension between statistical representativeness (which improves with larger samples) and the face-to-face, information-rich conditions that lottocratic theories cite as the epistemic justification for random selection. Did the assembly secure descriptive representation in a meaningful sense, or did the small sample reduce statistical mirroring to a symbolic gesture?

2. The filtering claim and preference intensity. One normative argument for sortition is that random selection filters out the strategic distortions and intense minority preferences that dominate electoral politics. Analyze the abortion deliberation specifically: did the recommendation to repeal the Eighth Amendment reflect a filtered, “ordinary” public judgment, or did it reproduce the polarized framing (religious versus secular) that the filter was meant to eliminate? What does the subsequent referendum result indicate about whether the assembly captured a latent popular judgment or helped construct one?

3. The advisory mandate and the downstream veto problem. The Oireachtas retained full legislative authority and could have ignored the recommendations. Treat this arrangement as a test of whether sortition can function as a standalone democratic mechanism or requires a binding decision rule to generate legitimate authority. Does lottocratic theory have the resources to distinguish a legitimate advisory assembly from an elite-convened focus group? If democratic legitimacy was effectively borrowed from the referendum that followed, does that undermine the claim that sortition produces authority independently of electoral validation?

4. Silence on power, agenda control, and information curation. The assembly was convened by parliamentary decision, its mandate was government-set, and expert testimony was curated. Identify what the theory says — and, more importantly, does not say — about who gets to invoke random selection, who frames the questions, and who controls the informational environment. Does the Irish case reveal a structural vulnerability to “elite capture at the point of design,” and is that vulnerability a contingent institutional failure or a gap the theory has never addressed?

Conclude by stating whether this episode confirms, complicates, or exposes an unanticipated gap in the normative case for replacing elections with sortition, and be explicit about which theoretical commitments the case never anticipated and which it forces into the open.
