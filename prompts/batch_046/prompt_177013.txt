I'm trying to stabilize my position on rational belief revision and the so-called dogmatism paradox, and I'd like a consistency audit of the following five commitments before I present them in a workshop. Here is where I stand:

(1) The paradox is genuine: if an agent treats every piece of counterevidence—including meta-evidence about the reliability of her own reasoning—as equally weighty in updating, then she either oscillates wildly in response to every challenge (spinelessness) or, by implicitly privileging her prior self-trust, renders herself immune to correction (dogmatism).

(2) There is a structural asymmetry in rational revision. First-order beliefs should remain responsive to direct evidence, but second-order beliefs about one's general reasoning competence ought to be governed by a stronger conservatism principle—revision at this level requires not merely conflict but demonstrable malfunction.

(3) In peer disagreement, even within one's domain of specialist training, one ought to reduce confidence in the disputed first-order proposition, because treating one's own assessment as epistemically privileged at that level reinstates exactly the dogmatism warned about in (1).

(4) There is no defensible, non-arbitrary "credal stability threshold" that cleanly separates permissible steadfastness from impermissible dogmatism; any proposed cutoff looks like an ad hoc rescue rather than a principled boundary.

(5) Even so, an agent can rationally maintain a high-credence belief in the face of an apparently defeating argument if she has executed what I call an "internal coherence audit"—a targeted check that her reasoning processes remain intact—without being required to perform a full external reassessment of the belief's evidential basis.

Please go through these and tell me which pairs are in genuine tension, which only appear to conflict, and what the least costly revision would be in each case. Rank the tensions by severity rather than treating them as equally damaging. In particular, I am worried that (3) undermines the conservatism defended in (2), that (4) makes the audit procedure in (5) look arbitrarily drawn, and that (1) might merely redescribe (5) rather than solve the underlying problem. But I also want you to check for hidden tensions I may have missed—say, between (2) and (4), or between the asymmetry thesis and the peer-disagreement norm. For each tension, specify which commitment has to give, or whether a reinterpretation can save both at acceptable cost, and then identify the single revision that would clean up the most conflicts overall.
