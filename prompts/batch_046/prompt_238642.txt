At our philosophy meetup, Maya and Jordan got into a real back-and-forth about Hart vs Fuller and whether a law can be so evil it stops being law. Maya said Hart's seperation thesis is basically right: imagine a Biometrics Sovereignty Act, passed with every procedural check, but it mandates total biometric harvesting and eliminates any opt-out. That's still law, just monstrous law. Jordan countered that Fuller's internal morality isn't just about making rules work, it's about law's minimal decency, and a statute that treats citizens purely as data reservoirs violates the reciprocity condition so deeply it isn't law at all, just coercion with a statute number. Maya replied that Fuller's eight principles are really functional conditions for efficacy -- generality, promulgation, non-retroactivity, etc -- not hidden moral tests, and if the Act is consistently enforced and understood, it meets them. Jordan said that's a misreadng: Fuller's idea of fidelity to law requires rules not demand the impossible or crush human dignity, and biometric totalism does both. They turned to me. Please adjudicate point by point: is Fuller's internal morality genuinely moral or just instrumental? Does Hart's separation thesis survive once we admit law needs minimal reciprocity? And who's closer right overall, plus what are they both missing?
