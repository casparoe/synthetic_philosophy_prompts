i run a small philosophy-of-religion semenar — about 12 grad students, one 75-min session — and i want them to feel why Jain anekāntavāda isn't just "everyone's opinion counts" but a specific logical pressure-cooker that makes unconditional action almost impossible. design a classroom game or structured deliberation for this.

the setup: split the group into four "standpoint teams" mapped roughly onto different naya — substance, mode, space-time, and collective intention. give them one concrete, high-stakes fictional case: a town council must vote whether to demolish a centuries-old shrine to build a flood wall that saves ten thousand homes. each team gets a hidden "truth-card" listing what is genuinely true about the shrine and the wall from only that standpoint — e.g. from substance the shrine is eternal, from mode it's decaying, from space-time it's blocking flow, from intention its destruction causes shared trauma. they have 30 minutes to draft a single collective resolution, but there's a speaking rule: every factual claim must be prefixed with one of the syādvāda-style conditional modes (perhaps, in some respect, inexpressibly, unconditionally false, etc.). they score collective points that drop every time someone makes an unconditional assertion, but they also lose points if they fail to reach an actual yes/no decision by the timer.

i need the rules, materials (cards, score sheet, timer setup), and a payoff scheme that doesn't use real money — maybe prestige points or a shared "town reputation" bar. the key is that participants should run into the coordination failure themselves before anyone names it: they should feel the paralysis that comes from holding every predicate in suspension, then feel the temptation to drop the conditions just to vote. include a debrief that maps specific moments — someone breaking conditionality to push the vote, someone invoking "unspeakable" to dodge contradiction — back onto the logical structure of anekāntavāda and syādvāda. also be frank about what the game can't reproduce: the ascetic/spiritual context in which Jain logicians actually deploy this, and the fact that real syādvāda is metaphysical, not just a classroom speaking rule. tell me what will predictably go wrong — probably one dominant speaker collapses all standpoints into relativism, or the group confuses "partial truth" with "anything goes" — and how to handle it without lecturing.
