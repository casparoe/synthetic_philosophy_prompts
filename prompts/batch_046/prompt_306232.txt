I am presenting a thesis defense in two weeks to a joint philosophy-of-religion and philosophy-of-mind workshop. The thesis I am defending is a conventional-functional interpretation of Buddhist anattā: I argue that in Theravāda Abhidhamma and Madhyamaka commentarial traditions, the term puggala (person) operates as a conventionally real (sammuti-sacca) designator for a causally continuous stream of skandhas structured by karmic intentionality (cetanā), whereas attā (self) is denied only as a metaphysically substantial, permanent, autonomous entity (paramattha). On this reading, Buddhism can preserve diachronic moral responsibility across rebirth and practice-based personal continuity without reducing the person to a mere aggregate bundle, and modern analytic bundle-theoretic interpretations miss the point because they conflate conventional and ultimate registers of discourse.

The audience has three factions. Faction A is analytic metaphysicians who think anattā necessarily collapses into reductionist bundle theory and therefore cannot ground moral agency across lives. Faction B comprises phenomenologists of selfhood who maintain that contemplative practice presupposes a minimal, pre-reflective self-consciousness (a minimal phenomenal self) that anattā wrongly denies, rendering the doctrine phenomenologically self-undermining. Faction C includes one specialist in classical Indian philosophy who knows the Pāli Canon uses puggala conventionally but will press me on whether my reading anachronistically imports later Abhidharmic systematization back into the suttas.

Conclude with the objection I am hoping nobody asks — the one that targets the gap between my claim that karmic intentionality supplies normative continuity and the Abhidharmic denial that cetanā itself is ultimately real (i.e., lacks svabhāva).
