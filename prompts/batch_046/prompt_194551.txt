I have been trying to work out, in my own terms, when it is acceptable to put someone you love ahead of strangers and whether there is a single principle behind it, or whether I am only rationalizing my instincts. Here are my concrete verdicts.

If my partner is trapped in a burning building and I can rescue them or rescue two strangers, I should rescue my partner, and it would feel like a kind of failure to pause and count heads before acting. Verdict: right, and introducing that extra calculating step would be wrong.

If I am hiring for a job and I choose my unqualified partner over a clearly better candidate, that is wrong. Verdict: wrong.

If I have savings enough either to fund a treatment that saves my partner's life or to fund a program that saves ten strangers, I believe I may spend it on my partner, but I feel uneasy in a way I do not feel in the burning-building case. Verdict: permissible, though uneasy.

If I am a doctor in an emergency room and my partner and a stranger arrive with identical injuries, I should treat whoever arrived first; giving my partner priority is wrong. Verdict: wrong.

If I promised my partner I would stay with them during a crisis, and a stranger asks for help that I could provide without leaving my partner in danger, I may stay; but if helping the stranger requires leaving my partner alone in real danger, staying is right. The boundary feels thin, but my verdict is that loyalty permits staying in both sub-cases. Verdict: right, though the line is hard to draw precisely.

If my partner has committed a serious crime and lying to the police would protect them but would cause an innocent person to be investigated, I cannot settle my view. One part of me says that loyalty demands the lie; another part says that this is precisely the wrong kind of extra consideration—placing one person's claim above the purpose of the institution I am addressing. Verdict: genuinely uncertain; this is the case I keep returning to.

My tentative thought is that favoring a loved one is permissible when I am acting as a private individual facing a direct threat or need, but not when I occupy a role—employer, doctor, juror—whose purpose is to be impartial. Yet this does not explain why the donation case feels different from the fire case, and it does not settle the lying case, since lying to the police is not part of any institutional role I hold.

Is there a coherent principle—perhaps one involving integrity, or the idea that there can be one moral thought too many—that captures these judgments? If so, what does it say about the donation case and about the lie? And which of my verdicts must be abandoned: the uneasy permission to donate, the wrongness of treating my partner first in the emergency room, or my wavering on the lie?
