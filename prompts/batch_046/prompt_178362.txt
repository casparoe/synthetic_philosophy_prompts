I chair the curriculum review committee for our university's interdisciplinary history program. We must approve, reject, or conditionally approve a proposal to restructure the required survey course on twentieth-century international relations. The proposal replaces the traditional unit "Who Started the Cold War?"—which frames American and Soviet actions as competing intentional causes requiring blame attribution—with a unit titled "Systemic Emergence," which treats the conflict as arising from structural anarchy, security dilemmas, and institutional path dependence. Its central historiographical claim is that asking "who started it" is a category error: it applies agential moral psychology to macro-historical dynamics in which no single actor possessed sufficient causal autonomy to initiate the conflict. Proponents argue this corrects a pedagogical distortion that makes students confuse moral culpability with causal explanation; opponents argue it erases the moral agency of specific policymakers, prevents students from evaluating culpability for concrete decisions such as the formulation of containment policy or the Berlin blockade, and substitutes systems theory for historical judgment. Please draft the committee's decision memo for circulation before our vote: recommend one of the three options with concrete reasoning, address whether the "category error" objection holds up under scrutiny or collapses into a false dichotomy between agency and structure, and give an honest, substantive treatment of the strongest argument for the option you reject—particularly the concern that removing blame-language from the syllabus does not merely refine analysis but actively sanitizes history. The memo should be suitable for authors, peer reviewers, and faculty senators, and should not request bibliographies, reading lists, or source citations.
