Imagine you are advising a small philosophy research group that has one postdoctoral year and one doctoral student to devote exclusively to Berkeley’s master argument—the claim that it is impossible to conceive of an object existing entirely unperceived. They do not need a survey; they need a defended, resource-sensitive agenda. Identify the two or three genuinely open questions about this argument that should absorb their limited time, and rank them. Your ordering should be justified by a combination of tractability for purely conceptual work (no empirical apparatus), upstream importance for Berkeley’s broader immaterialism or for contemporary philosophy of mind, and current neglect relative to their potential payoff.

In particular, weigh carefully what I will call the double-layered conception problem: whether Berkeley’s reasoning implicitly requires a second-order mental act in which one conceives one’s own failure to conceive the unperceived object, and if so whether that reflexive structure collapses the argument’s intended self-application. Set this against at least two other unresolved tensions: first, whether the argument survives if mental representation is construed in non-imagistic, purely functional or propositional terms rather than in Berkeley’s own idea-based psychology; second, whether the argument’s scope is genuinely restricted to sensible objects or whether it can be coherently extended to abstracta, relations, or modal properties without generating reductio-style counterexamples.

For each prioritized question, explain precisely what makes it worth the investment rather than the nearby alternative you deprioritized. Specify what kind of philosophical progress—clarification of conceptual dependency, resolution of an internal tension, or a restricted scope condition—would count as genuinely settling it, and identify what development over the coming year (a new conceptual distinction, an overlooked logical feature, or a demonstrated instability in a reconstruction) would cause you to reorder your recommendations. Please avoid bibliographic lists, requests for citations, appeals to obscure secondary literature, or lists of readings: the reasoning should stand entirely on its conceptual and logical merits, accessible without external reference.
