In a working group on comparative historical justice, we have reached an impasse that has lasted for months. Every participant agrees on the descriptive record—death tolls, bureaucratic intent, duration, group-targeting—regarding three cases: the Herero and Namaqua genocide, the Holodomor, and the Indonesian mass killings of 1965–66. Everyone also agrees that each was a profound moral catastrophe. Yet half the group insists that establishing an ordinal severity ranking is not only permissible but necessary for any coherent theory of reparative priority; the other half holds that any such ordering treats incomparable moral singularities as entries on a single scale, and that reparative justice should be non-competitive and non-aggregative. The arguments have become circular: the ranking side accuses the other of mystifying comparison into a false uniqueness, while the anti-ranking side accuses the first of smuggling a utilitarian calculus into historical accountability. I want a diagnosis rather than a verdict. Is this disagreement verbal—turning on whether 'ranking' implies substitutability or mere ordering? Is it substantive—about whether moral reasons can be weighed across distinct historical contexts? Is there a hidden empirical claim about whether ranking produces harmful political effects, or is it a bedrock clash between those who think justice requires commensurability and those who think it requires preserving the irreducible particularity of each event? Given your diagnosis, what would actually move it: a conceptual distinction between comparative severity and allocative priority, a formal argument about the logic of cross-contextual moral comparison, or is the impasse a sign that the framing itself is ill-posed?
