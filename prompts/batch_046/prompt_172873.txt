I'm a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, and during a recent architecture review my team began using the Ship of Theseus to reason about our legacy platform: every original service has been rewritten, every database schema migrated, and every founding engineer replaced, yet we still release under the same product name and semantic version lineage. Several colleagues treat this as proof that technical identity is purely conventional—merely a continuity of user-facing function and institutional naming, with no deeper persistence conditions—while others object that software permits exact replication, distributed version control, and explicit specification documents in ways physical repair never allows, so the analogy may be licensing conclusions it cannot support. I want to audit this comparison with precision. Please provide an explicit correspondence table mapping the ship, its individual planks, the repair crew, the harbor, and the reconstructed duplicate built from the discarded original planks onto elements of a long-lived engineered system—code modules, maintainers, users, deployment infrastructure, and forked repositories—then identify exactly where the mapping fractures. I am particularly interested in whether the analogy quietly imports assumptions about material continuity, the irrelevance of original substrate, or the authority of the builder that fail to survive translation to systems where bit-for-bit replication and version-controlled histories constrain replacement in ways ship maintenance does not. Does the conclusion that identity is purely conventional or functionally defined survive once these disanalogies are fully on the table, or was the analogy doing all the argumentative work? If the comparison collapses under scrutiny, suggest what a more appropriate framework for thinking about persistence in maintained technical systems might be.
