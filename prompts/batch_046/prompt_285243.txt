Student answer:
Hofstadter's core claim is that translation requires understanding, and understanding requires consciousness and intentionality. This is basically the same argument Searle makes with the Chinese Room: if you don't have subjective mental states about the symbols, you're just manipulating syntax. Statistical MT is shallow because it's like the person in the room—no comprehension, just rule-following at scale. Neural MT makes this worse because now we have black-box embeddings that hide the pattern-matching even from engineers.

As for part c, I think the critic is wrong. Human translators do build an explicit interlingual mental model—a kind of universal semantic substrate—before they render the target text. They don't just rely on intuition; they actively construct a language-independent representation of the proposition being expressed. So the comparison to statistical intuition fails. Humans have this intermediate representational layer, and machines don't, regardless of how deep the network is.

One other thing Hofstadter emphasizes is that true translation requires shared cultural embodiment, which no algorithm can replicate because algorithms lack bodies. So even if neural MT gets the words right, it misses the embodied context that makes meaning possible.
