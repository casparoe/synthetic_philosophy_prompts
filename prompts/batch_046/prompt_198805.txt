I keep running into a specific shape of argument about knowledge and inference that I can't name, and I'm wondering whether epistemology has an established concept for it. Picture someone who genuinely knows a set of premises and uses a long, reliable-but-opaque derivation—say, an automated proof assistant—to reach a conclusion. She can check the premises herself, and if they were false she wouldn't believe them; but if the premises are true and the assistant makes a hidden mistake that produces a false conclusion, she wouldn't catch it, because her skills are tuned to verifying inputs, not auditing every intermediate step. So she knows the premises, she correctly applies the derivation, and she ends up believing the conclusion, yet there's a clear sense in which she doesn't "track" that conclusion in the way she tracks the premises: in nearby situations where the conclusion is false but the premises hold, she still believes it. This creates a closure puzzle—she has knowledge of the premises and of the logical connection, but not of the result—yet it feels different from ordinary closure failures, because her reasoning isn't careless or irrational; the gap is structural, created by the mediation of a tool she trusts but can't fully inspect. Is there a recognized term for this kind of tool-mediated closure breakdown, particularly in the literature that combines closure principles with tracking conditions on knowledge? If the answer is no, can you propose a precise term, define its boundaries—distinguishing it from both generic closure counterexamples and from standard tracking-based objections to closure—and explain what makes the mediated case distinctive?
