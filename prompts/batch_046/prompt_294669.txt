I'm a doctoral candidate in philosophy of science, and next Thursday I'm defending the central chapter of my dissertation at our department's epistemology working group. The thesis is that the canonical Gettier counterexamples do not refute a suitably reconstructed JTB analysis of knowledge; rather, they expose a structural feature of justification I call scaffold-dependent epistemic reliance. On my view, a Gettier belief fails to be knowledge not because its truth is accidental, but because its constitutive justification traces through a false intermediate proposition that functions as an indispensable logical scaffold—what I term a scaffold-active falsehood—while the belief's actual truth depends on an independent, subject-unknown error-correction mechanism that cancels the scaffold's failure. Once we distinguish constitutive justification (the reasoning structure the subject actually deploys) from reconstructive justification (the path we retrospectively assign to salvage rationality), Gettier cases show that justification is non-monotonic with respect to intermediate falsehoods precisely when those falsehoods are scaffold-active rather than scaffold-inert. I apply this to the standard Smith-Jones automobile case and to a modified scientific-instrument case in which a miscalibrated spectrometer yields correct readings only because an unanticipated atmospheric interference accidentally corrects the calibration error.

The audience is specific and dangerous: three formal epistemologists specializing in modal conditions for knowledge, one of whom has recently defended a sensitivity-based account in print; two philosophers of science who regard Gettier-style counterexamples as artificial armchair constructions with no relevance to actual scientific practice; and one metaphysician trained in formal logic who insists that any distinction worth making must survive explicit formalization. They have received only my two-page circulated summary, which introduces the scaffold-active/scaffold-inert distinction and applies it to both cases, but offers no extensive bibliographic defense.

Prepare me for the objections I will actually face, ordered by the damage each would do to my position if mishandled. Include the objection I am most hoping nobody raises—specifically, either the charge that my scaffold-dependence distinction merely renames Nozick-style tracking conditions without acknowledgment, or the charge that my scientific-instrument case is empirically implausible and therefore philosophically inert.
