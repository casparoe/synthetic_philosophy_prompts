For my upper-level undergraduate seminar in comparative epistemology and metaphysics, I am composing the final examination. I want a single essay question—designed for a 90-minute response, carrying 40% of the course grade—on Jain anekāntavāda (the doctrine of many-sidedness) and its logical apparatus, especially syādvāda (the doctrine of qualified assertion) and the saptabhaṅgī (the seven-fold scheme of predication).

The question must not be answerable by reciting standard textbook summaries. To keep it conceptually demanding yet fully answerable without online access, inject the following framing: students must evaluate whether anekāntavāda can serve as a committed, non-relativist resolution to deep normative disagreement—specifically, whether its practice of qualifying every assertion with syāt ("in some respect") allows the doctrine to retain critical force against genuinely harmful standpoints, or whether this qualification inevitably collapses into a quietism that forfeits any adjudication between them. The question should force students to take a definite position on this crux, not merely list the seven predicates or describe the doctrine in general terms.

Please provide:

(1) The exam question itself, written in formal academic prose appropriate for a philosophy professor.

(2) A model answer of approximately 750–850 words. The answer must be committed: it should defend a specific verdict—for example, that anekāntavāda either does or does not preserve critical normative purchase—and justify it by appealing to the internal structure of the doctrine, particularly the distinction between naya (a partial standpoint) and the recognition that no single naya is exhaustive, as well as the conditional logic of syāt-qualification. It should not end non-committally, and it should not rely on bibliographic citations or appeals to specific secondary literature.

(3) A marking scheme that distinguishes an A answer from a B answer by naming the specific philosophical moves each must contain—for instance, correctly explaining syāt-qualification; distinguishing anekāntavāda from simple perspectivism or modern epistemic relativism; confronting the quietism objection with an internal resource such as the conditional nature of predication or the hierarchical ordering of standpoints—and the specific mistakes that should cost marks, such as treating the saptabhaṅgī as licensing arbitrary contradiction, failing to take a committed stance, or misrepresenting Jain pluralism as indifference to truth.

The difficulty should come from crux-selection and conceptual precision, not from obscure historical detail. Do not include requests for bibliographies, reading lists, or citations, and do not depend on verification of specific textual editions or dates.
