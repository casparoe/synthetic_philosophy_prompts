In seminars on political theology, I keep hearing the claim that Dostoevsky's Grand Inquisitor is essentially the prototype of the modern welfare-state administrator, or even of algorithmic choice architects: both recognise that most people cannot endure the anxiety of autonomous decision, and both respond by substituting material security, managed pleasure, and pre-emptive guidance for the burden of freedom. The analogy is deployed to argue that benevolent governance—whether through social-democratic provision or predictive recommendation—is structurally contemptuous of human dignity, merely secularising the Inquisitor's offer of bread, spectacle, and authority in exchange for surrendered will. I want to audit how far this mapping actually holds before I accept the critical conclusion it is meant to carry.

Please construct the correspondence explicitly: for each element of the Inquisitor's argument—the diagnosis of human weakness, the three temptations as substitutes for freedom, the explicit claim that love of humanity justifies coercion—identify what, if anything, plays the analogous role in modern welfare administration, in libertarian paternalism, and in democratic consent mechanisms. Then set out the fractures: does the Inquisitor's theological framing, in which free will is a divine burden that humanity rightly rejects, have a genuine secular counterpart, or does its absence alter the moral status of the substitution? Is the Inquisitor's open contempt for the masses present in democratic institutions, or is it an artefact imported by the analogy? Assess whether the conclusion—that contemporary benevolent governance is Inquisitor-like in the morally relevant respects—survives once these disanalogies are separated into those that matter for the critique and those that do not. If the analogy collapses, suggest whether a closer parallel might be found in epistemic paternalism in clinical medicine or in predictive policing, and briefly indicate why that mapping might be tighter or still defective. I am working through this for an essay on freedom and happiness, so I would particularly value a distinction between the structural features the analogy genuinely illuminates and those it merely borrows for rhetorical effect.
