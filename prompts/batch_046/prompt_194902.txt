I have been thinking on my own about what we might owe the dead when filmmakers turn their lives into drama, and I have constructed an argument that now strikes me as suspicious because its conclusion is far too permissive. I would like to set out the steps clearly. One: harm, in any meaningful sense, requires a subject who can experience it; since the dead have no consciousness, they cannot be harmed by anything said or shown about them after their death. Two: if an audience knows a film is a dramatisation with invented scenes, then no living person is deceived, and if the subjects of the story are dead, there is no one left to suffer an injury. Three: therefore an act that harms no one is not morally wrong. Four: it follows that inventing scenes in a biopic — even scenes that portray a real dead person as having done something shameful or cruel that they never did — is morally permissible, provided the audience understands it is fiction. Five: nevertheless I have a strong intuition that some inventions cross a line; for instance, I think there is something genuinely wrong, not merely inaccurate, in inventing that a known victim of persecution willingly betrayed others to save himself, when in fact he did not. Six: so step three must be false, because moral wrongs can exist even without a victim who feels them. Seven: yet if we allow victimless wrongs, we seem able to condemn harmless private behaviour or fictional representations that merely offend sensibilities, which feels like an intolerable expansion of moral judgement. I suspect the mistake is hidden somewhere between step six and step seven, perhaps in assuming that rejecting the harm principle forces us into condemning everything that is merely distasteful, but I cannot see the exact mechanism. Which step ought I to reject, and what does rejecting it commit me to? I am particularly reluctant to surrender the intuition in step five or to embrace the sweeping conclusion in step seven. I have started calling this problem narrative trespass, the idea that a life leaves behind a kind of moral residue that does not depend on legal ownership or on anyone being deceived, but I am unsure whether that notion can bear the weight I am putting on it.
