Write an exam question for an upper-level philosophy seminar on value and future generations, together with a model answer of about 600 words and a marking scheme. The topic is Samuel Scheffler’s “afterlife conjecture” — the claim that human extinction is bad not only because of the deaths it involves, but because the anticipated absence of future people undermines the value of present activities and projects that depend on collective continuation. To prevent a recitable summary, the question must require students to do three specific things: first, distinguish Scheffler’s claim from a straightforward utilitarian aggregation of lost future welfare; second, apply the conjecture to a concrete case — a scientist dedicating her career to a climate-restoration project expected to require four centuries — and explain whether her work loses value if humanity will not survive to complete it; third, address the objection that Scheffler illicitly treats humanity as a single “super-subject” whose survival is intrinsically valuable, and assess whether the conjecture can survive this critique without collapsing into collective egoism. The marking scheme should separate an A answer from a B answer by naming the specific conceptual moves each must contain — especially the distinction between project-dependent and outcome-independent value, and the correct diagnosis of the super-subject objection — and should assign marks out of 20, with a one-line note on how candidates typically fail on each point. Write in American English. Separate what is settled interpretation of Scheffler’s view from what is genuinely contested among philosophers of death and value, and do not present a contested interpretation as the consensus.
