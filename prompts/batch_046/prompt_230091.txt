I just spent the better part of an evening arguing with a close friend about whether attributing beliefs to large language models via Dennett's intentional stance is philosophically legitimate or merely a category error dressed up in pragmatism. To organize my thoughts, I drafted the following short essay, and I want your critical comments on it.

The essay argues that applying the intentional stance to AI does not simply extend an existing heuristic; it exposes a hidden dependency in the stance itself. For biological agents, predictive success is tied to evolutionary design, which gives belief-attribution a naturalistic anchor. For LLMs, predictive success is engineered but not evolved, and there is no unified agent whose survival depends on those predictions. Consequently, attributing beliefs to an LLM is structurally closer to attributing beliefs to an institution—what organizational epistemology describes as ghost beliefs, propositions that function in collective reasoning though no single individual endorses them. This comparison yields a dilemma: either LLM attribution is legitimate, in which case human belief-attribution may also rest partly on explanatory debt rather than internal representational states, or the absence of evolutionary anchoring renders LLM attribution a pure fiction, which in turn invites skepticism about whether our own attributions are as well-anchored as we assume. The essay then considers the standard Dennettian reply—that the stance is purely instrumental, so evolutionary history never mattered—and responds that even instrumentally, the stance requires a bounded system to be predicted. LLMs blur that boundary because their outputs depend on distributed training data, prompt contexts, and human feedback loops in ways that resist localization to a single agent. Thus belief-attribution to AI may not be false so much as misplaced: we are treating a socio-technical distribution as if it were an individual mind.

Please comment specifically on three things. First, is my characterization of the evolutionary anchor in Dennett's view accurate, or does it impose a naturalistic requirement he never intended? Second, does the comparison to institutional ghost beliefs genuinely illuminate AI belief-attribution, or does it illegitimately import assumptions from organizational theory? Third, is the boundary-objection a substantive challenge to the intentional stance applied to AI, or only a practical limitation? I am also interested in whether the central inversion—that AI attribution reveals something about human attribution rather than the reverse—is philosophically interesting or forced.

Lead with your bottom-line assessment in the first sentence—say plainly whether the essay's core move succeeds or fails—then give your reasoning. No preamble, and do not restate the essay's argument back to me.
