I'm serving as a peer-grader for the junior-level seminar Conceptual Histories of Knowledge, and I'm working through responses to our take-home exam on periodization. The prompt asked students to evaluate whether the conventional European division into the Middle Ages, the Renaissance, and the Scientific Revolution marks real discontinuities in knowledge practices or operates as what our course materials call a "narrative prosthesis"—a retrospective scheme that projects coherence onto heterogeneous practices in order to organize modern self-understanding. Specifically, respondents had to separate (a) the descriptive problem of whether the boundaries correspond to shifts in "epistemic regimes"—by which the exam means the institutionalized rules governing credible knowledge production, not merely the content of beliefs—from (b) the normative problem of whether the triad implicitly ranks forms of life by treating empirical-institutional science as a hidden telos. Students were told to reason without bibliographies, citations, or appeals to specific editions.

Here is one answer, submitted without a name attached. "The conventional triad is largely an invention of nineteenth-century historiography rather than a feature of the past itself. Medieval universities sustained empirical observation and logical analysis that clearly anticipate modern scientific methods, so the boundary with the Renaissance is largely artificial. Meanwhile, the Renaissance itself was constructed by Burckhardt to celebrate Italian civic humanism, not to describe a pan-European transformation. The Scientific Revolution is equally dubious: figures like Galileo and Newton worked within explicitly theological frameworks, so labeling their work 'modern science' imposes a Whiggish teleology that reads present values into the past. Nevertheless, these categories remain useful heuristics. Even if boundaries are fuzzy, they capture real institutional differences—monastic and courtly settings versus academies and royal societies—so we can retain the triad as long as we describe gradual regime shifts rather than sharp ruptures. The main flaw in the invention thesis is that it conflates the constructed nature of period labels with the claim that no underlying historical change occurred at all; once we see that construction does not entail absence of change, the normative worry also dissolves, since describing a regime shift does not by itself privilege modern empirical methods."

Please treat this as a grading exercise. Assign a specific score—say, out of ten—and deliver a point-by-point assessment that goes through the answer paragraph by paragraph. Identify the visible strengths (the student does grasp the basic descriptive/normative distinction and avoids the crude error of claiming the past was identical to the present), but also flag the precise subtle mistakes: any conflation between the existence of some anticipation and the absence of a boundary, any mischaracterization of what the stronger invention thesis actually denies, and any step in the reasoning about theology and Whiggishness or about construction and normative ranking that does not strictly follow. Do not signal your own agreement or disagreement with the thesis being graded; just justify the mark with specific references to sentences in the answer above.
