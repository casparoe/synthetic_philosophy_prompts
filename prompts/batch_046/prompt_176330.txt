Design a classification instrument—specifically a four-node binary decision tree—for a graduate seminar in philosophy of mind. Its purpose is to take a student who already grants that phenomenal consciousness is fundamental and ubiquitous and sort them into one of the live positions on the combination problem, using only commitments they can endorse without knowing the view's name.

The four branch nodes must be concrete philosophical claims, not labels. They should cover: (1) whether macro-experiential subjects are ontologically derivative from micro-subjects or prior to them; (2) whether phenomenal combination requires a sui generis "phenomenal bonding" relation that outstrips physical or functional integration; (3) whether micro-subjects can exist as independent, un-combined entities; and (4) whether the apparent failure of bottom-up combination forces us to deny macro-subjects exist, to treat them as emergent in a strong sense, or to relocate consciousness to a universal substrate.

Map the resulting logical space to named positions. The leaves must include constitutive Russellian panpsychism (smallism), cosmopsychism, hard emergentist denial of macro-subjects, and the view that macro-subjects are real but combination is brute and unexplained. Identify any logically possible combinations that are unoccupied—e.g., a view that asserts macro-subjects are prior yet also built from micro-subjects via phenomenal bonding—and briefly diagnose whether the gap is due to incoherence, unattractiveness, or mere neglect.

Finally, flag any pair of leaves that your tree cannot genuinely separate despite their being distinct in the literature, and state what extra distinction (outside the four nodes) would be needed to split them. Do not include reading lists, citations, or requests for bibliographic references. Keep the instrument focused on diagnostic reasoning rather than historical taxonomy.
