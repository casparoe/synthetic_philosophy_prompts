Having spent forty years in experimental biophysics, I have recently turned to the philosophy of social science, and I find myself troubled by an argument I have constructed regarding Weber's thesis on the Protestant ethic. The argument seems to demonstrate that cultural values cannot be genuine causes, which I take to be an absurd result, yet each step appears defensible, especially to someone trained to think in terms of intervention and physical closure. I present it below and ask for your diagnosis in continuous prose, without headings, bullet points, boldface, tables, or other structural markers, as one writes a direct letter.

(1) For any factor to serve as a cause of a macro-social outcome, it must satisfy an interventionist condition: one must be able, in principle, to alter that factor while holding constant all non-cultural conditions, and thereby observe a change in the outcome. (2) The Protestant ethic, as Weber describes it, is a particular configuration of religious values, beliefs regarding predestination, and norms of worldly asceticism. (3) Any such configuration is fully realized in the psychological states and behaviors of individual agents, which themselves possess complete physical descriptions at the neural and behavioral level. (4) Consequently, any intervention upon the presence or absence of the ethic just is an intervention upon those underlying physical and psychological states. (5) By the causal exclusion principle—standard in discussions of mental causation—if a physical base is causally sufficient for an effect, then a property that supervenes upon it is either identical with that base or causally redundant. (6) Therefore the Protestant ethic is either reducible without remainder to aggregates of individual psychology and physiology, in which case it is not an independent cause, or it contributes no causal power beyond them, in which case it explains nothing its base does not already explain. (7) It follows that Weber's claim that the ethic contributed causally to the rise of modern capitalism is not merely empirically dubious but conceptually incoherent: values can never be causes in any non-trivial sense, and all apparent cultural or institutional explanations collapse into either psychology or physics.

The conclusion strikes me as plainly unacceptable, since it would eliminate not only Weber's particular thesis but every form of social explanation that appeals to norms, collective representations, or institutional structures. I am inclined to preserve the intuition behind step (1)—that explanation requires some form of counterfactual stability—while avoiding both the reduction of sociology to physics and the acceptance of mysterious downward causal powers. I therefore ask you to identify the specific step at which this reasoning fails, to explain why that step seemed initially plausible—particularly to a mind accustomed to experimental manipulation and the closure of the physical domain—and then to specify what one must pay in order to reject it. Must I embrace a form of downward causation, or is there a middle path—perhaps involving the counterfactual stability of multiply realizable institutional properties—that allows culture to explain without violating physical closure? Please reply in a single continuous flow of paragraphs, without formatting devices, so that I may read your answer as an uninterrupted line of thought.
