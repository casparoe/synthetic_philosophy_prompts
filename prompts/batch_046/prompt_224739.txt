I'm trying to hold together a package of interpretive commitments about Aristotle on money and exchange, and I want an audit of whether they cohere. Here is where I've landed:

(1) Aristotle condemns usury (tokos) because money (nomisma) is essentially a conventional measuring instrument and therefore naturally barren—expecting it to generate interest is like expecting a ruler to produce more rulers.

(2) The real distinction in his account, however, is structural rather than ontological: acquisition becomes corrupt only when it lacks an internal limit (apeiron), whereas exchange that corrects surplus and deficiency remains natural regardless of whether coin mediates it.

(3) Modern fiat currency, having no commodity backing, actually strengthens (1): being pure measure with no use-value of its own, lending it at interest is precisely charging rent on a scale.

(4) Yet if money is purely conventional and barren by design, then its sterility is an institutional feature, not a metaphysical constraint. So there is no natural-philosophical wrong in lending it at interest; any objection must be distributive or political.

(5) I want to preserve Aristotle's core insight—that acquisition without a terminal point is unnatural—but I think it survives only if we reject (1) and ground the criticism of usury in (2)'s limit-structure rather than in money's barrenness.

(6) I also maintain that limited retail trade is permissible within the polis, because its natural boundary is fixed by the needs of household self-sufficiency.

Do these hang together? Specifically: do (3) and (4) pull in opposite directions about whether conventionality strengthens or dissolves the metaphysical objection? Is the tension between keeping (1) and endorsing (5) more severe than it appears? And is the compatibility between allowing limited trade in (6) and condemning unlimited acquisition in (2) only apparent—does (2) actually commit me to condemning all profit-seeking exchange, not merely the unlimited kind? For each pair in genuine tension, rank them by severity, say what the cheapest revision is, and identify which single change cleans up the most. Also flag any pairs that seem to conflict but don't.
