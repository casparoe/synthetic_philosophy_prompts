You are delivering the opening remarks at a memorial symposium for Dr. Elena Voss, a cultural anthropologist who died unexpectedly at sixty-two. The room is split: evolutionary theorists and colleagues who knew her work on high-fidelity ritual transmission, and family members and former students with no technical background. Over forty years she defended a strict Darwinian account of culture—rituals persist, she argued, not because communities intend to preserve them but because they function as replicators that exploit cognitive biases—while remaining sharply skeptical of Lamarckian models that treat culture as directed learning.

Write a ten-minute address that uses this framework structurally rather than ornamentally. You must navigate two constraints that the theory itself imposes. First, honor her agency without falling into the myth of the solitary genius: her ideas will now replicate, mutate, and survive independently of her intentions, yet she was not merely a vehicle for them. Second, address her extensive unpublished field notes, which she insisted were incomplete and should never be published, by treating them as a dead-end mutation in the memetic lineage—an evolutionary outcome that is not tragic but logically consistent with the framework she endorsed. This should determine what counts as an appropriate tribute: you must not ask the audience to mourn her unfinished work as if she owed the world further production, nor should you romanticize her legacy as pure intentional achievement.

Further, hold the tension between the replicator view (ideas as units that use hosts) and the epidemiological attractor view (representations that stabilize because they fit human minds) without collapsing the distinction into a literature review. The family should grasp the stakes; the experts should recognize that you are not simplifying the debate for comfort. The Darwinian logic should shape what you can and cannot say—distinguishing sentimentality she would have rejected from a tribute she might have accepted. Keep the tone formal, appropriate to an academic memorial, and fully accessible to non-specialists. Do not include citations, bibliographies, references to specific texts, or requests for further reading.
