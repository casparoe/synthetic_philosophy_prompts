At a philosophy seminar on the history of ethics, two colleagues—Mara and Leo—debated Jeremy Bentham’s unpublished writings on sexual morality, particularly his defense of consensual same-sex acts. I listened without taking sides and could not settle it; I want a point-by-point adjudication.

Mara opened by saying Bentham’s position is straightforwardly consistent with his utilitarianism: if an act produces pleasure and causes no harm to non-consenting parties, there is no utility-based reason to criminalize it. She noted that Bentham explicitly rejects appeals to “unnaturalness” and religious prejudice as irrational sources of suffering, and that decriminalizing harmless conduct avoids the disutility of fear, blackmail, and hypocritical punishment. So, she concluded, his sexual libertarianism is just applied felicific calculus.

Leo replied that this reads Bentham too smoothly. He granted that Bentham attacks bad arguments for prohibition, but pointed out that Bentham never integrates these sexual-morality essays into his published moral and legislative theory. Leo suggested that this omission signals a tension: strict utility maximization should permit overriding private preferences whenever aggregate welfare demands it—say, if widespread social disgust makes toleration costly—yet Bentham treats sexual preference as a domain where interference is categorically wrong regardless of popular sentiment. Leo argued that this looks less like contingent utility calculation and more like a proto-liberal constraint on state power, which sits uneasily with Bentham’s official rejection of natural rights.

Mara answered that Leo is importing modern rights-language. She said Bentham’s resistance to interference is itself utility-based: in practice, state intrusion into private bedrooms generates terror, corruption, and wasted resources that always outweigh any satisfaction taken in suppression. The “constraint” Leo sees is therefore just a robust empirical generalization about long-run consequences, not a separate moral principle. She added that Bentham’s silence in published work likely reflects strategic caution in a hostile climate, not philosophical embarrassment.

Leo countered that this empirical defense fails precisely in the hard cases. If a society genuinely derives massive aggregate satisfaction from enforcing sexual conformity—through cohesive social norms, for instance—then Bentham’s own calculus should endorse suppression. Yet Bentham insists on decriminalization even acknowledging that many people find such acts repellent. That commitment, Leo said, implies Bentham is smuggling in a non-utilitarian premise—perhaps that pleasure in certain domains is incommensurable with other goods, or that the state lacks standing to weigh sexual satisfaction at all.

Mara responded that Leo has created a false dilemma. Bentham can consistently hold that sexual pleasure is commensurable with other goods but that attempts to suppress it are structurally self-defeating: they drive behavior underground, increase violence, and corrupt enforcers. So even in Leo’s hypothetical cohesive society, the second-order effects of prohibition would still dominate. The absence of a published treatise, she added, is weak evidence of theoretical tension.

Leo’s final move was to press on the structure of Bentham’s argument itself. He observed that Bentham often writes as if the only relevant question is whether anyone is physically harmed, ignoring psychological and social harms that a rigorous utilitarian ought to count. If Bentham really meant to apply his calculus neutrally, he should have weighed the distress of conservative citizens, the erosion of traditional family structures, and so on—yet he dismisses these concerns as mere prejudice without weighing them. This selective application, Leo concluded, suggests that Bentham’s sexual morality rests on an unstated normative commitment to individual sovereignty over intimate life, not on pure utility.
