After a long argument with a friend about whether consequentialism is paralyzed by ignorance, I keep returning to a specific pattern I do not have a name for. A person accepts that we are radically ignorant of distant outcomes—say, the century-scale effects of a climate policy—then invokes that ignorance to reject an ambitious global intervention, but immediately endorses a different ambitious intervention in the same domain, defending it solely by its clear local benefits without addressing whether its distant effects are equally opaque. The cluelessness objection functions not as a uniform epistemic barrier but as a directional veto: it is deployed to block conclusions the speaker dislikes, then suspended for conclusions they favor, despite comparable uncertainty. I know this is not ordinary hypocrisy, because the speaker genuinely holds the cluelessness view; it seems closer to an asymmetrical burden of proof that shifts with the scale or political valence of the proposed action. Is there an established term in the consequentialist literature for this selective, directionally applied cluelessness—using ignorance to veto one course while licensing another of similar opacity? If not, what precisely distinguishes it from consistent localism on one hand and from motivated reasoning on the other?
