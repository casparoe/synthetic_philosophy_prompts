Compare the causal-provenance account of why original artworks are valued over perfect forgeries with the phenomenological-encounter account, which locates value in the specific qualitative relation available only to the genuine object. Then consider the retroactive-attribution problem raised by conceptual art practices: an object's status as original or forgery can be institutionally revised years after its creation, apparently altering its aesthetic and market value without changing any physical or perceptual feature. Determine whether either account can accommodate this retroactive instability without collapsing into pure conventionalism, and state which theory, if either, provides the more robust explanation.
