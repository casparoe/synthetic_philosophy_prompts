I'm trying to work out whether my intuitions about procreative selection, within the framework of liberal eugenics, cohere around a single principle or remain ad hoc. Selecting against a severe monogenic disorder such as Huntington's disease seems clearly permissible, perhaps obligatory. Selecting for malaria-resistance alleles in a high-prevalence region feels prudent, yet selecting for height or general cognitive ability solely for competitive social advantage feels like an unjustified arms race. Selecting against deafness by hearing parents, where deafness carries a flourishing cultural identity, seems wrong, as does selecting for perfect pitch that offers no welfare gain and might canalise the child towards music. The case that divides me is selecting for a slightly elevated baseline mood if this comes at the cost of reduced emotional range and perhaps diminished capacity for certain moral responses.

My tentative principle is that genetic selection is justified only when it expands the child's modal range of autonomous life-plans without privileging any particular conception of the good, and unjustified when it either narrows that range or optimises the child for a specific valued outcome. Does this capture all five verdicts, or does it misclassify the malaria case as competitive optimisation? Must I drop one of my judgements about the mood-range case, or can the principle be repaired with a distinction between constitutive and instrumental goods? Finally, what does the principle predict about a hypothetical intervention that made a child constitutionally incapable of experiencing racial prejudice?
