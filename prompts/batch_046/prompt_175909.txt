For a graduate-level ethics workshop with about 12 students over 90 minutes, design a structured, runnable simulation that makes participants feel the force of the suffering-happiness asymmetry in negative-utilitarian population ethics, without opening with a lecture or naming the view until the debrief. The participants should act as members of a Future Generations Council tasked with allocating a fixed resource budget between two macro-policies: one that creates a large population with very high average welfare but guarantees a small, identifiable subset with severe suffering, and one that eliminates all suffering by forgoing creation of those lives (and with them all the happy lives). To avoid generic cost-benefit framing, inject a stochastic profile mechanism: in the first 20 minutes the group must draft its own charter from an intuitive moral principle they supply; then each member draws hidden cards representing potential individuals with unknown suffering and welfare distributions, and the council can observe only aggregate risk statistics, not individual outcomes. Arrange the rules and roles—Risk Auditor, Welfare Aggregator, Dissent Recorder, and Charter Guardian—so that participants discover for themselves that any nonzero risk of severe suffering functions as a veto on creation under their charter, and that they must either abandon the charter or accept an empty-world outcome. Include a precise timeline, material requirements, and a payoff/incentive structure using only social stakes (e.g., public justification rounds, recorded dissent). The debrief should map specific moments in the deliberation onto the formal asymmetry thesis, explain what will predictably fail on the first run (participants smuggling in expected-utility aggregation or treating potential people as actual rights-bearers), and be explicit about which feature of the view the activity cannot reproduce—namely, the metaphysical comparison between non-existent and existing beings—so the facilitator must supply that. Do not request bibliographies, reading lists, citations, or references to specific texts or authors; the deliverable should be self-contained and meaningful without web access.
