I spent thirty years working in statistical mechanics and cosmology, and since retiring I have been trying to understand the philosophy of decision theory—particularly the problem of evidential cooperation in large worlds. Having read the standard treatments, I find myself drawn to a position that strikes me as underexplored, though I am unsure whether it is defensible or merely a physicist's prejudice.

The position—let me call it the Causal-Epistemic Horizon Thesis—is this. The standard argument holds that if the universe is large enough to contain many near-copies of me, then my local choice becomes evidentially linked to theirs: by conditioning on my own decision, I should update on what those copies do, and therefore act in ways that aggregate across all instances. I propose that this correlation should be truncated by something analogous to a cosmological particle horizon. Specifically, evidential cooperation applies only between agents that share what I term an accessible information manifold: a region of spacetime within which causal signals or structural correlations can, even in principle, propagate or be established. Copies lying beyond an event horizon, or in causally disconnected branches of a multiverse, do not belong to my epistemic peer group—not because they do not exist, but because no mechanism of correlation, evidential or causal, connects my reasoning to theirs. The result is that local moral agency is preserved: I cooperate with copies I could, in principle, influence or be influenced by, but I do not treat distant, causally isolated duplicates as extensions of my own decision problem.

This view predicts that in a spatially infinite but causally homogeneous universe, cooperation demands scale with the volume of the accessible region rather than with the total number of copies globally. It also risks being accused of ad hoc boundary-drawing: why should epistemic peerhood depend on causal accessibility rather than on structural identity alone?

What I want is an honest assessment of how exposed this thesis actually is. What formal results in decision theory or correlation reasoning—say, about the invariance of expected utility under partitioning of the sample space—would undermine the claim that horizons can legitimately restrict the peer group? Which intuitions about symmetry or fairness across copies does the thesis violate, and are those intuitions ones its defenders should concede? Could a committed proponent absorb the damage by retreating to a weaker claim—perhaps that horizons determine the weight rather than the existence of cooperation—and would that retreat collapse the view into the standard large-world position? I would also like to know whether the physical analogies I am importing (event horizons, information manifolds) do any genuine philosophical work here, or whether they merely decorate an arbitrary cutoff. Finally, rank the potential defeaters: which would be fatal, which merely awkward, and does the thesis have any hidden insulation—say, in the ambiguity of correlation across causal boundaries—that makes it unfalsifiable?

Please comment on the accuracy of my framing, the interest of the position, and whether I have missed an obvious objection that renders the horizon move redundant.
