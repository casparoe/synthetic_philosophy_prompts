I'm coordinating a volunteer-run archive with three long-term colleagues, and we've all committed to a strict provenance protocol requiring complete donor documentation for every acquisition. Recently I discovered that our senior coordinator, Mara, has been selectively omitting donor names from a few recent entries—clearly violating our shared agreement. When I raised this with her privately, she dismissed my concern by pointing out that I have occasionally misfiled reference cards in the past, though never with any intent to deceive. Meanwhile, a newer volunteer, Leo, publicly criticized Mara's omissions in our group chat, despite having failed to complete his assigned cataloging for two consecutive months; he attributes those delays to an unpredictable work schedule rather than negligence. The group is now split: some members argue that Leo's own lapses strip him of any authority to criticize Mara; others contend that Mara's senior position makes her violations more consequential regardless of who points them out; and a few have suggested that my own history of misfiling means I should never have been the one to initiate the conversation at all. I keep wondering whether there is a principled way to distinguish whose prior failures actually undermine their right to object, whether intent and severity should matter, and whether being directly affected by someone else's breach changes the moral calculus—separate from practical questions about how to run the archive smoothly. Can you help me understand whether these competing objections track something genuinely important about moral standing and the conditions for appropriate blame, or whether they are largely rhetorical strategies to deflect criticism?
