A colleague recently summarized Einstein's hole argument for me, and I want to test its accuracy against specialist understanding. As she presented it: Einstein showed that in any matter-free region—the 'hole'—the gravitational field equations together with boundary conditions outside fail to determine a unique metric inside. Assuming substantivalism, where manifold points possess intrinsic identity, one can apply a passive coordinate transformation restricted to the interior that reassigns metric values to the very same points, generating physically distinct worlds that agree on all observable measurements outside the region. This violates determinism, so Einstein concluded that spacetime points lack independent reality, a result he later reinforced with his point-coincidence argument that only the intersection points of worldlines carry physical meaning. The entire difficulty was eventually resolved once physicists recognized that these interior remappings are gauge symmetries—mere redundancies of description—so that the apparently different metrics represent one and the same physical state. Consequently, the hole argument is now generally treated as a solved problem that illustrates gauge freedom in general relativity and retains little remaining philosophical bite.

Something about this feels like a competent popularization, but I suspect it flattens distinctions that matter. I am inclined to accept that gauge symmetry dissolves the original puzzle, yet I have heard that specialists still dispute whether modern substantivalism has genuinely answered the challenge or only relocated it, and that popular accounts often blur the hole argument with Einstein's later reasoning.
