I am serving on a joint student-faculty committee tasked with drafting a governance charter for our school's new shared courtyard, which will be converted from an unused lawn into a garden, outdoor study area, and weekend community space. The administration recently approved opening the area to neighborhood residents after school hours, and now we must decide who holds formal voting authority over rules regarding noise limits, planting choices, maintenance schedules, and access hours. Several competing proposals have emerged, and I find myself unsure which principle should guide the boundary of the decision-making group.

One faction argues that authority should rest solely with currently enrolled students, since the land is school property and students bear the direct burden of any disruptions during the school day. Another group contends that anyone residing within a ten-minute walking radius should have equal say, on the grounds that evening use affects local traffic, parking, and noise levels regardless of enrollment status. A third proposal suggests granting votes to anyone who registers as a regular user, but this raises the concern that individuals from distant neighborhoods could sign up specifically to influence rules—such as pushing for later closing times—without bearing any local costs. Complicating matters further are alumni who funded part of the renovation through a recent donation drive, as well as incoming freshmen who will use the space next year but are not yet enrolled, and a small group of non-resident teachers who maintain the garden but live outside the radius.

Rather than asking for simple procedural advice on running a meeting, I am trying to understand whether there is a coherent philosophical structure behind these disagreements about who belongs to the governing body. Is there a principled reason to prefer membership based on institutional affiliation, geographic proximity, actual usage, or being affected by outcomes? And if multiple criteria conflict—as they clearly do here—what considerations should determine which one takes precedence? I want to grasp the underlying logic of the dispute so that I can contribute meaningfully to the charter rather than simply advocating for the group I happen to belong to.
