As a retired physicist who has recently turned to political philosophy, I find myself preoccupied by a conceptual tension in the doctrine of armed neutrality that the standard treatments I have encountered do not seem to resolve. Switzerland, Sweden, and Finland have long represented distinct institutional answers to how a state can remain militarily non-aligned: Switzerland through a militia-based conscription system woven into direct-democratic civic identity; Sweden and Finland, until their recent NATO accessions, through hybrid models that paired selective conscription with professional cores. With Finland and Sweden now inside the alliance, Switzerland stands nearly alone in Europe in combining formal neutrality with universal male conscription. This raises a philosophical question rather than merely a strategic one.

Write an essay on the following controversy: Is armed neutrality conceptually coherent without conscription, or does the legitimacy of a neutrality claim depend upon a distributed, civic-duty-based defense posture? Weigh the view that neutrality is essentially a strategic posture maintainable by any credible force structure—including a fully professional volunteer army—against the view that neutrality requires what might be called "structural neutrality," in which defense is inseparable from widespread citizen obligation and therefore from a particular form of republican civic identity. In your reasoning, address whether the gradual professionalization of defense forces in formerly conscript-based neutral states represents an organizational modernization compatible with neutrality, or rather a conceptual erosion that severs the link between citizenship and defense. Reach and justify a specific conclusion: should Switzerland understand its retention of conscription as philosophically essential to the coherence of its armed neutrality, or can the two commitments be cleanly separated without undermining either?

I am not asking for historical chronology, policy recommendations for Bern, or any bibliography; I want a conceptual argument with an explicit defended conclusion, one that takes seriously the possibility that neutrality might be hollowed out from within even when its formal diplomatic status remains unchanged.
