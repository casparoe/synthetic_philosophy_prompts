You are a senior lecturer in political philosophy with deep experience in experiential pedagogy and seminar design. Before you construct the full plan, briefly brainstorm the core mechanics—information asymmetry, hidden rubrics, and non-monetary stakes—that would cause eight graduate students to feel, in real time, how arbitrary but unexercised power reshapes strategic behavior. Design a self-contained, 75-minute classroom activity for eight graduate students in a political philosophy seminar. The objective is for participants to experience the gap between negative liberty (the absence of actual interference) and republican liberty (the absence of domination), specifically through the behavioral phenomenon of anticipatory compliance. The idea must emerge from what participants do; no upfront lecture should name the distinction before the debrief.

Frame the session as follows. One student is assigned the role of the Arbiter. The Arbiter possesses an opaque, unreviewable rubric for vetoing any proposal made by the other seven students. The Arbiter is secretly instructed never to exercise the veto during the session. However, the rubric shifts each round according to hidden criteria that the other students can detect only through noisy, indirect signals—for example, subtle changes in the Arbiter’s non-verbal feedback or in the framing of round announcements. The remaining students must allocate limited tokens—representing speaking slots, coalition partnerships, or research priorities—across four timed rounds to maximize individual point totals. They know that any proposal could have been blocked, and they can observe that the rules of acceptable proposal change without explanation.

Ensure the design injects the niche conceptual mechanism of anticipatory compliance as a measurable shift: compare participant risk-taking and proposal diversity in round one, before the opacity is felt, with round three, after the group has learned to self-censor.
