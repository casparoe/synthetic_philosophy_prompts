I have adopted the view that Maimonides, in the Guide for the Perplexed, regards the sacrificial laws not as intrinsically valuable worship but as a strategic divine accommodation: they redirect a pre-existing, idolatrous human impulse toward concrete ritual into a monotheistic framework, with prayer and intellectual contemplation representing the genuinely ideal form of service that sacrifice only approximates because abrupt abolition would have been psychologically and socially impossible for the Israelites. On this reading, sacrifice is pedagogically necessary but metaphysically and ethically suboptimal; its value is entirely instrumental to weaning a people away from pagan practice.

What would have to be true for this accommodative reading to fail? I want an honest map of its exposure, not a list of objections that defenders already dismiss out of hand.

Specifically:

1. Which passages or structural features in the Guide or the Mishneh Torah, if read without the prior assumption of accommodation, would suggest that Maimonides assigns sacrifice a non-instrumental role — for instance, as maintaining cosmic order, mediating between embodied human beings and an immaterial deity through the sublunar sphere, or expressing a permanent feature of the human-divine relation rather than a transitional stage?

2. How decisive would it be if Maimonides' Aristotelian cosmology actually requires material, sensory ritual as a permanent bridge between corporeal creatures and a purely intellectual deity? Would this collapse the distinction between "concession to human weakness" and "necessary condition of embodied worship," and would defenders of the accommodative view concede this point or reinterpret it away?

3. What about comparative evidence from how Maimonides treats other commandments — prayer, dietary laws, purity regulations? If he treats some of these as having enduring intrinsic value rather than purely transitional function, does that weaken the claim that sacrifice belongs uniquely to the concession category?

4. Is there a risk that the view has been insulated by qualification — for example, by saying that any textual evidence of intrinsic value in sacrifice is itself part of the pedagogical strategy, or that messianic restoration of sacrifice in Guide III:32 is compatible with its current suboptimality? If so, is that insulation a legitimate hermeneutic move or does it render the position unfalsifiable, and is unfalsifiability a defect for an interpretive claim of this kind?

5. Finally, what internal tensions in Maimonides' broader philosophy — say, his account of animal cognition and suffering in his medical writings, his legal theory about the purposes of commandments (ta'amei ha-mitzvot), or his insistence that divine wisdom must be rationally intelligible — would count as genuine defeaters rather than merely awkward adjustments?

Please rank the potential defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, indicate which ones a committed accommodative reader would themselves accept as counting against them, and say whether the position, if well insulated, remains intellectually respectable or has been qualified into emptiness.
