Consider the conceptual landscape surrounding heritability—specifically, what heritability coefficients do and do not imply about causal mechanisms, trait malleability, between-group differences, and the rational design of social interventions. I am not asking for a literature survey or a bibliography. Instead, imagine you are advising a small interdisciplinary group—quantitative geneticists working with philosophers of science—who have three years and limited staff to resolve conceptual confusions that actually distort policy and practice. Which three open conceptual problems or lines of work should they prioritize, in what order?

Defend the ranking explicitly: for each problem, state whether it is upstream of the others (resolving it restructures the framing of the rest), how tractable it is with current statistical and philosophical tools, what specific misconception or institutional error it addresses, and what evidence or argument over the next few years would cause you to reorder it. Also identify one issue that is frequently treated as central—perhaps the relationship within-population heritability and between-group differences, or the interpretation of genome-wide association study effect sizes—but that you would deprioritize, explaining whether it is largely settled, downstream of your top three, or too empirically underdetermined to merit conceptual investment now.

To avoid a generic textbook answer, incorporate and evaluate the following niche framing: how heritability estimates for complex behavioral traits interact with institutional-level interventions—specifically, whether high SNP-based heritability for educational attainment within a given school system constrains, is orthogonal to, or actively misleads reasoning about the efficacy of restructuring that system. Your agenda should take a clear position on whether this institutional-interaction question belongs in the top three or should be set aside, and why.
