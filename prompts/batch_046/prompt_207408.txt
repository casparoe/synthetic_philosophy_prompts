As a retired physicist who has only recently begun reading philosophy seriously, I want to understand Al-Ghazālī's occasionalism—not as a historical curiosity, but by testing whether it can be rendered in the conceptual vocabulary I actually used for forty years: Hamiltonian dynamics, conservation laws and Noether symmetries, effective field theories with decoupled scales, statistical ensembles, and boundary conditions that constrain rather than cause evolution.

Please restate Al-Ghazālī's core occasionalist position—his denial that there is any necessary connection between what we call cause and effect, his insistence that God is the direct and immediate cause of every particular event (including the blackening of cotton when fire is near), and his explanation of observed regularity as the habitual course (sunnat Allāh) rather than as a law binding upon nature—entirely within that physical vocabulary. Do this element by element. For each component, tell me precisely what translates without loss, what must be distorted to fit (and in what direction the distortion pulls), and what has no counterpart at all in the language of dynamics, symmetries, and effective theories.

In particular, I want you to examine one specific analogy that tempts me: the idea that occasionalism resembles the modern view that low-energy effective laws are not ontologically fundamental but depend on deeper dynamics that could, in principle, be different. Is this analogy a legitimate translation, or does it smuggle in assumptions—such as the closure of the physical domain and the absence of downward or external causation—that occasionalism explicitly rejects?
