Write the opening remarks—about five minutes, formal and affectionate—for the retirement symposium honoring Dr. Elena Vasquez, a historian of early modern philosophy whose work traced Hobbes’s attempt to model civil science on synthetic geometric demonstration. The audience is split: mathematicians and historians of science know that Hobbes pursued the quadrature of the circle well into old age and that Wallis publicly dismantled his synthetic methods, while family and university administrators know only that he revered geometry; the remarks must honor Vasquez without lecturing either half. Let Hobbes’s own criterion for true knowledge—that we understand a figure completely only when we construct it ourselves—do real structural work in the tribute: it should license praise for Vasquez’s rigorously constructed interpretations and for the intellectual objects she built across her career, but it must simultaneously forbid any comparison that implies political philosophy can be made as demonstrative as geometry, since Hobbes’s very failure to achieve that transformation is the central thread of her scholarship. In other words, the geometric ideal should determine not merely your metaphors but which achievements you can celebrate and which analogies you must explicitly reject. Include one brief, non-technical allusion to the Wallis dispute that both halves of the room can grasp without mathematical detail, and keep the tone one of genuine gratitude rather than academic summary.
