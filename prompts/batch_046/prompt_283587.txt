I'm preparing for a seminar discussion on Quine's critique of the analytic-synthetic distinction, and a persistent disagreement has emerged that seems to call for an adversarial collaboration rather than further informal debate. One group — the Radical Holists — reads Quine's arguments as showing that the collapse of the distinction eliminates any principled boundary between conceptual revision and empirical update: since all beliefs face experience only as a corporate body, and since revision is governed solely by pragmatic virtues like conservatism and simplicity, there is nothing left to explain why altering the application conditions of a term like bachelor feels categorically different from revising the gravitational constant. The opposing group — the Entrenchment Gradualists — grants Quine's demolition of Carnapian reductionism and synonymy-based analyticity, but they propose that Quine's own web metaphor supports a continuous revision-cost topology: beliefs can be mapped onto a formal network in which each node carries a centrality score measuring the number and weight of downstream adjustments required for its revision, producing a gradient that explains the phenomenology of conceptual versus empirical change without reinstating any binary analytic property. They insist this is not a revival of the old distinction but a structural clarification of Quinean holism.

Please draft the joint statement these two camps should produce after good-faith collaboration. Begin with a numbered list of agreed claims about Quine's central arguments — specifically the failure of definitional reduction, the circularity of attempts to ground analyticity in synonymy, and the holistic thesis that no statement is immune to revision — phrased neutrally so both sides can sign. Then present a disagreements table that states each position in wording its own adherents would accept: the Radical Holist claim that pragmatic criteria are fully sufficient to explain all patterns of belief change, versus the Gradualist claim that network centrality captures a real structural feature of belief systems that pragmatic talk alone obscures. Identify the exact cruxes — whether the felt difference between conceptual and empirical revision requires explanation beyond pragmatics; whether formal belief-revision algorithms actually generate the predicted centrality gradient in realistic agents; and whether Quine's pragmatism implicitly licenses conceptual pluralism — and for each crux specify a concrete case or theoretical result both sides agree bears on it, such as a specific historical episode of scientific theory change or a formal property of coherence-maximizing revision procedures, recording in advance how each camp interprets or predicts that case. Conclude by diagnosing whether any portion of the dispute is merely verbal, particularly whether the term analytic can be reconstructed as a cluster concept anchored to high-centrality nodes, and if so, state precisely which part both sides can agree is terminological rather than substantive and why.
