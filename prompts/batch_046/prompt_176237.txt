I am trying to work out my own position on guilt and shame for a paper in my moral psychology course, and I have reached a cluster of verdicts that I cannot quite reconcile, so I would like to know whether there is a coherent principle behind them or whether one must be dropped. Here are my judgments, case by case. First, when someone breaks a specific promise to a close friend and can make direct amends, guilt strikes me as the appropriate and better emotion, whereas shame feels like an overreaction that misidentifies a repairable act as a stain on one's entire character. Second, when a person realizes that an unconscious status bias has led them to systematically dismiss a colleague's contributions over many months, shame seems correct and guilt feels evasive, as though reducing a diffuse pattern of being a certain kind of person to a discrete action that can be apologized away. Third, I think a culture that systematically replaces guilt with shame for every kind of wrong—including minor social breaches—would be worse, because guilt preserves a path to reconciliation that shame often forecloses through isolation. Fourth, yet I also think a culture that eliminates shame entirely and channels even deep character failures, such as cowardice in the face of injustice, into guilt, is seriously impoverished, because guilt's structure of specific repair does not fit a failure that is essentially about who one is rather than what one did. The tension is this: I am inclined to say that guilt fits what I am calling transactional wrongs and shame fits what I am calling constitutive wrongs, which sounds like an objective mapping of emotion to the structure of the failure, but I also want to say that a culture can legitimately choose to privilege one emotional register over the other for reasons of social coordination—implying a guilt-culture versus a shame-culture—suggesting the choice is partly conventional rather than dictated by the nature of the wrong. Is there a single principle, perhaps one that distinguishes the moral work an emotion must do in an individual case from the social technology of emotional regimes, that preserves all four verdicts? If not, which verdict should I abandon—the claim about objective fit, or the claim about cultural choosability? I would also like to know what your principle predicts for a culture that inverts the mapping, using guilt for cowardice and shame for broken promises: is that incoherent, or merely unconventional?
