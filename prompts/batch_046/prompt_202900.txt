I'm working through a problem in a philosophy-of-history seminar about whether labels like Middle Ages, Renaissance, and Scientific Revolution denote real discontinuities or are retrospective impositions on continuous change. In class, one student offered an analogy that historians often use implicitly: just as geologists divide Earth's history into epochs anchored by genuine boundary events — say, the K-Pg impact — historians divide human time into periods anchored by real ruptures like the fall of Constantinople or the publication of key works in natural philosophy, so that the names are conventional but the divisions track actual breaks. A second student objected by invoking the term Neolithic Revolution, noting that it imposes a single, abrupt transformation on a diffuse, regionally varied process spanning millennia, and argued that Scientific Revolution does the same to scattered 16th- and 17th-century developments. Our instructor then introduced a less familiar comparison, treating period labels as analogous to ring species in biology: a continuous gradient of populations is segmented into discrete taxonomic units for practical classification, even though gene flow makes any sharp boundary artificial. I want to audit this ring-species analogy specifically, because it seems to do different work from the geological one. Can you lay out the explicit correspondence — what plays the role of the geographic gradient, the named segments, the taxonomists, and the practical purpose of classification — and then identify where the mapping breaks down? In particular, does the analogy quietly assume that historical change is uniformly continuous like gene flow, thereby ignoring genuinely discontinuous events such as the collapse of the Western Roman Empire? Separating the disanalogies that undermine the conventionalist conclusion from those that don't, I want to know whether the claim that these three periods are later inventions survives once the analogy is fully unpacked, and if there is a better analogy that captures both the conventional and the real elements of periodization.
