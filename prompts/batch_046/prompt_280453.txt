In the course of drafting a novel about a labor commune, I have been observing a philosophical deadlock that I intend to let my characters inhabit rather than resolve. The dispute concerns whether modern demands for leisure—embodied in the campaign for the thirty-five-hour week—constitute a moral correction to the Protestant work ethic or its logical culmination. One faction cites karoshi, death by overwork, as evidence that the ethic has been corrupted into lethal economic extraction, stripping labor of its spiritual vocation; the opposing faction argues that karoshi is precisely the result of severing work from its original kairotic calling and reducing it to mere chronometric productivity, and that the thirty-five-hour week risks completing that reduction by treating rest solely as recuperative absence rather than as space for meaningful vocation. Both factions accept the same sociological data on Japanese labor mortality, both invoke Weber without disputing his historical thesis, and both concede that suffering is bad. Yet in three years of discussion—both in my notebooks and in a seminar I convened—the positions have not shifted.

What kind of disagreement is this? Is it a hidden empirical dispute about whether reduced hours restore psychological agency or erode vocational identity? Is it verbal, turning on whether "leisure" denotes negative freedom from labor or positive freedom for a calling? Or do the sides answer different questions—one about distributive justice in capitalism, the other about the ontology of work—while sharing the phrase "right to leisure"? Given your diagnosis, what would count as progress: a conceptual distinction, an empirical study, a change of framing, or an argument that the impasse is bedrock?
