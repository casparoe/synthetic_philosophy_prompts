I've been arguing in my philosophy of knowledge tutorial that skepticism about the external world doesn't really threaten ordinary knowledge claims, but another student keeps falling back on the line: "You haven't proven you aren't a brain in a vat, so you can't claim to know anything." I want to understand exactly what work that line is doing in his argument. Could you disambiguate the slogan into the separate precise principles it might express—distinguishing, for instance, whether "proven" means deductively eliminated, whether the standard for knowledge is evidential adequacy or logical certainty, whether knowledge requires ruling out all metaphysically possible alternatives or only those that are genuinely epistemically open, and whether the argument tacitly assumes a closure principle according to which knowing one thing requires knowing all its known entailments? For each reading, indicate whether it is trivially true, philosophically substantive, defensible on standard epistemological grounds, and—crucially—whether it actually licenses the sweeping dismissal of ordinary knowledge he intends.
