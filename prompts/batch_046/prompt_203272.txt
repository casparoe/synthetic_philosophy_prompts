In my philosophy of art seminar this term, we have been stuck for weeks on a debate about historical fiction and biopics that refuses to advance. One camp—call them the archivalists—argues that dramatizing real dead people imposes a stringent obligation of fidelity: filmmakers and novelists should not invent private psychological states, undocumented conversations, or counterfactual actions, because audiences systematically treat dramatized scenes as epistemically authoritative, producing both misrepresentation of the historical record and a distinctive harm best described as posthumous indignity. The opposing camp—the autonomists—replies that fiction is governed by dramatic coherence and emotional truth, not by archival accuracy; they claim that generic conventions already signal invention to competent audiences, that dead persons lack interests capable of being harmed, and that treating a biopic as a defective documentary is a category mistake that illegitimately extends epistemic norms into artistic domains.

What makes the stalemate puzzling is that both sides largely agree on the empirical premises: any dramatization requires substantial invention, and viewers often blur fictional portrayal with documented fact. Yet the archivalists treat this convergence as grounds for constraining artistic license, while the autonomists treat it as grounds for better audience education rather than for artistic restriction. I cannot tell whether we are facing a substantive normative disagreement, a verbal dispute about whether “representation” in art carries assertoric force, or two groups answering different questions—one about the ethics of using a life as narrative material, the other about the ontology of fictional works—under the single heading of what we owe the real dead. There may also be a hidden empirical question about audience psychology, or a bedrock intuition about whether human lives are ever appropriate raw material for plot architecture, that neither side can argue for directly.

I want a diagnosis rather than a verdict: Is the impasse driven by a disagreement about the speech-act status of fiction, by a clash over whether posthumous dignity can ground obligations to strangers, or by an unstated divergence in what counts as a moral reason versus what counts as an artistic reason? Please engage concretely with what each side actually commits to—especially their shared premises and their divergent inferences from those premises—and tell me whether the disagreement is ill-posed, verbal, or genuinely hard. Finally, given your diagnosis, what would actually count as progress? Would a sharper conceptual distinction—say, between “epistemic contamination” of an audience and “narrative extraction” from a biography—move the debate, or should we expect no resolution and instead reframe the question entirely?
