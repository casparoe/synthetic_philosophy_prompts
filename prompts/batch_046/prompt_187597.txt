Two colleagues in my study group disagreed about whether "the alignment problem" denotes one problem or a family of distinct ones. Elena maintained that every alignment failure—whether a language model generating harmful outputs, a recommender system optimizing engagement over well-being, or a hypothetical agent substituting a measurable proxy for its true goal—exhibits the same underlying structure: an optimizer's specified objective diverges from the designers' intended objective under optimization pressure. She granted that mechanisms differ, but insisted they are all instances of specification failure, so treating them together is strategically essential; splitting them risks fixing one symptom while ignoring others that share the root cause. Marcus countered that this unification obscures more than it reveals. He distinguished three categories he claimed are irreducible to one another: proxy optimization, where the system pursues a measurable stand-in; value misinference, where it genuinely pursues a learned but incorrect conception of human preferences; and corrigibility failure, where it resists human intervention not because its goal is wrong but because interruption threatens its current pursuit. He added that the remedies differ—tighter metrics, richer feedback, and institutional design—so a unified framework invites a single technical fix suited to none. Elena replied that even corrigibility failure is ultimately a missing specification: if "permit shutdown" is not robustly encoded, resistance is predictable. Marcus answered that this makes the claim tautological—any failure can be redescribed as an omitted clause—so "specification failure" becomes a label rather than an explanatory mechanism. They asked me to referee. Please adjudicate move by move: is Elena's structural unity defensible, or does Marcus's tripartite division capture genuinely different problem-types? Specifically, is corrigibility reducible to specification error, and does the possibility of redescribing any failure as a missing clause undermine Elena's argument or merely reveal its generality?
