I have been working through H.L.A. Hart's Law, Liberty, and Morality alongside Patrick Devlin's The Enforcement of Morals for a seminar on legal philosophy, and I am trying to reconcile Hart's rebuttal of Devlin's disintegration thesis with Hart's earlier account in The Concept of Law of the minimum content of natural law. It seems to me that Hart's objection to Devlin is essentially an extension of that minimum-content framework: Hart holds that legal rules must include certain survival-promoting provisions—prohibitions on violence, rules of property, and mechanisms of enforcement—because without them society could not persist; but shared moral norms, such as those governing sexual behavior or private conscience, do not belong to this minimal survival kit. Consequently, when Devlin argues that society will disintegrate if its shared morality is not legally enforced, Hart can reply that morality is not part of the institutional infrastructure required for social survival, and therefore law has no necessary function in preserving it. Is this the correct way to understand the structure of Hart's argument?

I have also been considering a more contemporary framing drawn from the sociology of institutions—what some scholars call institutional decoupling, the idea that in modern pluralistic democracies the legal system and the moral communities it governs have become functionally separated, with social cohesion maintained not by shared moral codes but by procedural legitimacy and institutional redundancy. If we accept this decoupling thesis, does it strengthen Hart's position against Devlin, or does it actually undermine Hart by showing that law and morality were never as tightly coupled as even Hart assumed? More specifically, if the disintegration Devlin fears is prevented not by legal moralism but by this decoupling mechanism, then is Devlin's empirical premise about social cohesion falsified not by Hart's liberal harm principle, but by a sociological transformation that renders the entire debate obsolete? I would appreciate a careful analysis of whether Hart's minimum-content argument genuinely depends on this kind of institutional separation, or whether I am conflating two distinct lines of reasoning in Hart's work.
