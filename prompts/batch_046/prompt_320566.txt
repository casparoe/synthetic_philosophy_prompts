One faction insists that Millbrook possessed a lingering craft ethos, an unwritten norm that competitors should share technical knowledge rather than hoard it, and that this cultural orientation is what allowed collective adaptation. The opposing faction argues that survival was purely structural: Millbrook's property titles were so fragmented that large-scale liquidation was legally impossible, forcing owners to stay and improvise, so the so-called ethos is just a post-hoc narrative that survivors told to dignify necessity. We are now deciding whether to direct our grant funding toward cultural programming—apprenticeship rituals, shared governance workshops, cooperative branding—or toward legal reforms that replicate Millbrook's fragmented ownership elsewhere. What troubles me is that these explanations seem to consume each other: if the ethos only arose because the legal structure trapped people in place, treating culture as an independent cause feels wrong; yet if we impose structural reforms without any corresponding shift in norms, people probably will not cooperate, which suggests values are doing real causal work. I am also struck by the possibility that the relationship is not one-way—that the initial constraint may have selected for particular values, but those values have since become autonomous, guiding behavior even where the original constraint is gone, and perhaps even determining which structural reforms succeed or fail. Given all this, how should we think about whether culture can genuinely cause economic outcomes, or whether it is always reducible to underlying material conditions?
