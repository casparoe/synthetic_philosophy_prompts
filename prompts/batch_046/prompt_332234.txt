I am drafting the outline for a seminar paper in an advanced philosophy of science course, and I have been trying to work out a tension in Quine's naturalism that my instructor raised only briefly in a discussion about ontological commitment. The core idea is this: Quine's indispensability argument for mathematical entities turns on whether quantification over those entities is genuinely unavoidable in our best total scientific theory, and he extends that standard rigorously to physics and its mathematical apparatus. At the same time, Quine treats moral values as culturally posited expressions—closer to the early "myth of physical objects" in its pragmatic utility, or to sentiment-based discourse that lacks causal-nomological integration—so that they do not earn comparable ontological standing. In my essay, I want to press the comparison in the opposite direction. I argue that if we take Quine's own indispensability standard seriously—that we should accept entities that are indispensable to our best explanatory theories—then moral values may satisfy it better than he admits, at least within the human sciences. Specifically, I claim that explanations in evolutionary psychology, behavioral economics, and parts of sociology routinely treat normative commitments, shared moral frameworks, and evaluative dispositions as causally efficacious properties of groups and individuals. If Quine allows mathematics into his ontology because it is woven into physics, should he not, on pain of internal inconsistency, allow at least a deflationary realism about moral values into his ontology because they are woven into our best social-scientific explanations?

The essay then considers three objections that a Quinean might offer. First, moral discourse can always be paraphrased away into purely descriptive behavioral talk, whereas mathematical discourse cannot be paraphrased away from physics without crippling predictive power. Second, Quine might invoke his broader distinction between entities that earn full ontological commitment through indispensable theoretical integration and those that remain pragmatic, cultural, or expressive posits lacking that integration, placing moral values firmly in the latter camp. Third, he might deny that social science belongs to the "best total theory" in the same privileged sense as physics, so that indispensability within psychology or economics carries no ontological weight. I attempt to answer the first by pointing to cases in which normative predictions—such as cooperation breakdowns triggered by perceived unfairness—resist clean paraphrase into non-normative terms without loss of predictive specificity; the second by arguing that the line between serious theoretical integration and pragmatic positing is under-motivated if both types of commitment are genuinely unavoidable in explanation; and the third by questioning whether Quine's privileging of physics over the human sciences is consistent with his broader holism about confirmation.

I have not written the full draft—only these notes and a rough outline—so I am not asking for line editing. Instead, I would be grateful for your conceptual and structural comments on several points. First, is my characterization of Quine's view on moral values accurate, particularly his tendency to treat them as culturally posited or sentiment-based rather than causally integrated into scientific explanation? Second, does the comparison with mathematical indispensability genuinely expose an internal tension in Quine's naturalism, or am I importing a standard that Quine applies selectively and intentionally, with good reason? Third, in the three objections I sketch, where am I most likely missing a well-known Quinean response, and which of my replies seems weakest? I am especially interested in whether this argument collapses into a generic moral realism debate, or whether it can be kept tightly focused on Quine's internal commitments regarding ontology and explanation. Please do not provide a bibliography or reading list—just feedback that could help me refine the outline before I begin writing.
