For a conceptual-analysis workshop I am writing up my philosophical profile on the phrase "Western values" and I want a consistency check before I present. Here is where I stand:

(1) The phrase lacks distributive coherence: there is no consistent set of norms shared across all societies labeled "Western," and the cluster contains contradictory elements (e.g., secular governance alongside deep religious tradition, radical individualism alongside collective solidarity).

(2) Despite (1), there is a real, historically traceable institutional trajectory—constitutional limits on power, rights-based discourse, and rule of law—that emerged in specific Western societies and was subsequently globalized through colonial and post-colonial transmission.

(3) In actual political argument, invocations of "Western values" almost always function as boundary-work: they define an in-group against immigrants, rival states, or domestic dissenters rather than describing a stable normative package.

(4) Even so, one can construct a defensible normative synthesis—call it liberal constitutionalism—that captures the best elements of the trajectory in (2) without claiming they are exclusive to, or fully representative of, the West.

(5) I think it is intellectually dishonest to deny that the trajectory in (2) exists—this is what I call the continuity fallacy in reverse, treating a concept's tainted genealogy as proof of its descriptive emptiness—and equally dishonest to treat that trajectory as an essential possession of Western peoples or cultures.

(6) At the same time, I believe the phrase "Western values" should be abandoned in serious normative discourse and replaced by precise institutional descriptions, because its provenance is too entangled with essentialist and exclusionary uses to be rehabilitated.

(7) But I also worry that abandoning the phrase entirely obscures the specific path-dependent historical explanation we need—why modern rights frameworks emerged in this trajectory and not elsewhere—which makes comparative political theory harder.

(8) Finally, I hold that provenance coherence (a concept having a unified historical origin) is never sufficient for normative coherence (a concept being defensible), yet I sometimes write as if recognizing the origin in (2) is necessary for defending the reconstructed norm in (4).
