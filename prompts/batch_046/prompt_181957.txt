Here is an argument I have been turning over about Scanlon's contractualism and the aggregation problem, and it strikes me as suspicious because it seems to collapse the view into an aggregative theory, which cannot be right. (1) Contractualism holds that an act is wrong only if it is permitted by no principle that those affected could not reasonably reject. (2) Reasonable rejection turns on whether the burden of accepting the principle, for the affected party, is outweighed by the burden of living under it. (3) Consider a principle allowing any particular person to drive a gasoline car for necessary commuting. For any specific driver, the climate harm caused is infinitesimal and distributed across billions; no identifiable other person bears a burden large enough to outweigh that driver's need for mobility. (4) So no one can reasonably reject that principle as applied to that particular driver. (5) By repetition, every individual commute is permitted. (6) This result is reinforced by what we might call the principle of modal proximity: in contractualist evaluation, only harms that are modally close to the agent—those directly and determinately caused—enter the burden calculus, while diffuse structural harms are treated as background conditions not attributable to the principle being evaluated. (7) Applying this, even a principle allowing universal driving is permitted, since for each affected individual the climate harm remains modally distant and distributed, and the burden of universal prohibition on each remains enormous. (8) But catastrophic climate change is clearly a state that anyone could reasonably reject being subjected to. (9) So contractualism either accepts that catastrophic result or must smuggle in collective evaluation at step (7), abandoning its individualist foundation. (10) Hence Scanlon's view cannot solve the aggregation problem without becoming extensionally equivalent to rule-utilitarianism.

If I must preserve premises, I would rather keep (1) and (8) than (9). Where does the argument slip—particularly between (5) and (7), or in the appeal to modal proximity? Which step should be rejected, and what does rejecting it cost for the individualist structure of contractualism? Please say what specific consideration or counter-example would change your verdict, and identify where your reasoning here is most vulnerable to failure.
