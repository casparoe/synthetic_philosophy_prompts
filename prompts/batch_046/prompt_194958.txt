I'm giving the opening remarks at my aunt Marge's retirement do for the Millbrook Weekly, the paper she ran for thirty-two years. Half the room are staff who actually know deadlines and ink, the other half are family who just remember her bringing cookies. I've been flipping through this old Danish writer Kierkegaard in a thrift-store book, and he talks about how the press turns people into the public — this big anonymous crowd where no one has to own their words. Marge had this hard rule: no anonymus letters, every contributor signed their real name, and she answered every angry call herself. I want the speech to hang on that — to say her paper fought being part of that faceless crowd — but without making her sound like a philosophy experiment or pretending Kierkegaard was secretly writing about small-town news. It needs to feel like a family toast that also makes the journalists nod. About four minutes, warm, specific, and please don't correct my spelling in front of everyone. Use that public-vs-person thing to shape what I can and can't claim about her.
