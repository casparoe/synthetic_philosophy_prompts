Debates about moral revolutions—why practices like dueling and chattel slavery disappeared—often rely on structural analogies that do more work than is usually admitted. One common comparison treats moral norms as load-bearing architectural elements: they persist not because each is independently justified, but because the surrounding social framework distributes weight among them, and they collapse only when that framework is redesigned. This picture seems to accommodate both Appiah’s claim that honor codes sustained dueling and the Williams thesis that rational moral argument rarely topples a norm on its own. A rival comparison treats norms as currencies: they circulate only so long as agents accept them in exchange, and abolition occurs when the exchange rate collapses—slaveholding loses economic and diplomatic value, dueling loses prestige-value—without any structural failure. Audit both analogies: lay out the explicit correspondence table for each (norm, supporting institution, mechanism of disappearance, role of argument versus interest, and what counts as the “surrounding framework” or “market”); identify the disanalogies that matter for distinguishing an honor-based explanation from a pure interest-based or rationalist one; determine whether either analogy can genuinely carry the conclusion that arguments played little causal role, or whether each smuggles in that conclusion through source-domain features it quietly imports; and assess whether a different analogy—perhaps norms as immune responses that attack foreign practices, or as dialects that lose speakers—better captures what Appiah and Williams need without importing misleading mechanics.
