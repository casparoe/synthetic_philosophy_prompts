I've noticed a recurring structural failure in attempts to justify deferring to one's own future reasoning. A scientist wants to treat a replication study she'll run next year as authoritative over her current belief, but she realizes that if she could now establish, with full confidence, a general principle guaranteeing her methods are sound, she wouldn't need to wait—she'd already possess the justification. Similarly, an institution creates an appeals panel to correct its present errors, yet to trust the panel's future verdict it must first endorse, in the present, its own judgment that the panel is competent; the authority that was meant to be deferred collapses back into immediate self-certification. In both cases, any attempt to ground confidence in a future self-correction mechanism seems to require a present endorsement of that mechanism's reliability, which absorbs the very authority that was supposed to be deferred.

Is there an established name for this specific collapse—where deferred self-trust turns back into present self-certification—particularly when it arises in an agent reasoning about its own rationality? How exactly does it differ from ordinary epistemic circularity or from bootstrapping one's own reliability? Does it apply only to individual agents reflecting on their reasoning, or does it extend to distributed or institutional cases where a collective tries to defer to its own future corrected judgment? And if there is no precise term that captures just this boundary—between genuinely deferring authority and covertly retaining it—can you propose one, with clear conditions for when the collapse occurs and when it does not? I am not looking for a bare label; I want to know what the phenomenon is, what it is not, and where its edges lie.
