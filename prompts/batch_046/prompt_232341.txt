I am preparing an exercise for an advanced seminar on classical Indian epistemology, specifically on how the Nyāya school adjudicates conflicts among the pramāṇas when they arise in concrete, non-scriptural settings rather than in Vedic exegesis. To avoid the usual classroom examples about yogic perception or scriptural authority, I want a realistic scenario—set in a contemporary forensic chemistry lab, lasting no more than one paragraph, with no supernatural, mystical, or exotic elements whatsoever—engineered so that pratyakṣa (direct perception of a specimen), anumāna (inference from observed chemical markers), and śabda (testimony from a senior expert) all appear relevant, but in which perception and inference converge on identifying the substance as compound A, while the expert’s testimony points to compound B. Crucially, the case must be built so that testimony fails to count as a pramāṇa not because the speaker is globally unreliable, dishonest, or deluded, but because of a precise, technically specifiable failure of one of the standard conditions for valid śabda—such as ambiguity in the utterance (vyākulatā), a domain-specific lack of authority (āptatva) despite general expertise, or interference by tarka (hypothetical reasoning that exposes an internal contradiction in the testimony’s implications). After presenting the scenario, lay out the verdict each pramāṇa delivers and why: for perception, show that the cognition is yathārtha and not defective (e.g., not an illusion or misapprehension); for inference, demonstrate that pakṣadharmatā and an unbroken vyāpti hold; and for testimony, identify exactly which condition fails and why that defeat is local rather than global. Then identify the single structural feature of the case that does the work in breaking the tie, explain what happens to the verdicts if that feature is removed, and describe how a defender of śabda’s supremacy in this instance would try to rehabilitate the testimony by reinterpreting the failed condition. Lead with your bottom-line judgment about which cognition should be accepted and the feature that secures it; then provide the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
