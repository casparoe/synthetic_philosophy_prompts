Write a short story about a physician in a near-future clinic equipped with an "uncertainty engine"—a diagnostic tool that does not forecast clinical outcomes but quantifies the user's moral ignorance about each treatment path, displaying "opacity scores" for the affected lives rather than probabilities. A patient arrives whose condition forces a choice among three interventions, each preserving a different incommensurable good (autonomy, relational integrity, bodily continuity) while necessarily destroying the others. The engine returns identical, maximal opacity for every option, including refusal to act, and the physician comes to suspect that the score is not measuring her personal ignorance but mapping a structural feature of the dilemma itself: no further information could resolve it because the values at stake are not rankable on a single scale. Write the story to explore whether moral uncertainty in such cases is merely epistemic—curable with better data—or whether some choices are opaque in principle. After the story, include a brief note identifying where the narrative takes liberties with standard philosophical treatments of moral uncertainty (for instance, treating opacity as an objective property of the choice rather than a subjective epistemic limitation) and state how confident you are in those interpretive moves, singling out the specific point about which you are least certain.
