Write a sestina—six stanzas of six lines with rotating end-words, followed by a three-line envoi—on historical contingency as captured by the proverb for want of a nail. Choose six concrete nouns that form the causal chain, for instance nail, shoe, horse, message, battle, crown. In each stanza, reorder these same six elements according to the sestina's formal rotation, but vary the causal narrative so that the sequence amplifies differently: by the fourth stanza the same nail should have led to both the preservation and the loss of the kingdom, depending on which intermediate link fails. The form's mechanical repetition should embody deterministic chaos—identical rules producing divergent trajectories—while the envoi compresses all six words into a final arrangement that refuses to declare which path was the counterfactual baseline. Keep the tone analytical rather than elegiac; someone familiar with sensitive dependence on initial conditions should recognise the structural analogy, and the poem should stand without footnotes or attribution. Use British spelling throughout.
