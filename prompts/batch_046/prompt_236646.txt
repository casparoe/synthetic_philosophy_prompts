Here's a weird argument I cooked up after trying to combine Molinism with machine-learning talk and now I'm stuck. (1) Molinism says God knows counterfactuals of creaturely freedom (CCFs) — like "if Peter were in situation S, he would deny Christ" — prior to any decision to create. (2) These CCFs are contingent, not necessary, so their truth isnt grounded in God's nature. (3) They're also not grounded in the actual world, since God hasnt created Peter yet. (4) So they need some other ground, or they're just brute modal facts. (5) Now the twist: suppose we train a neural net on every actual human choice we have data for, and it learns to predict counterfactual choices with perfect accuracy. Call its output "synthetic middle knowledge" (SMK). (6) The net's predictions are contingent and pre-actual (it didnt create the people), just like God's. (7) If Molinists say God's knowledge is okay despite lacking a ground in God's will or the world, then they should also say the net's predictions are okay despite lacking any ground at all — they're just true computation outputs. (8) But if computation outputs can be brute modal truths, then modal realism collapses into computationalism, which is absured. (9) So to avoid that, Molinists must deny that the net has real knowledge of CCFs, saying it only approximates correlations. (10) But then the same reply applies to God: if there's no ground, "knowing" a CCF is just tracking an ungrounded correlation, not knowing a modal fact. (11) Therefore either CCFs are grounded in something (destroying Molinism's distinctiveness) or they dont exist (destroying middle knowledge). Where's the error? I really want to keep (4) and (9) if I can, but I feel like I'm smuggling an equivocation between epistemic access and metaphysical grounding somewhere between (6) and (10), and I cant name it exactly. Which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost?
