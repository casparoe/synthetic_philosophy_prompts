I am a doctoral researcher in political philosophy working on transitional justice and collective moral responsibility. Rather than surveying existing literature, I want to press a specific conceptual tension: Karl Jaspers's fourfold taxonomy of guilt—criminal, political, moral, and metaphysical—was developed for a present-tense population confronting its immediate past, yet contemporary constitutional democracies now face reparative demands generations after the original wrongs, when every direct agent is deceased. To avoid both the reification of nations as moral persons and the reduction of collective responsibility to aggregated individual blame, I want to test whether Jaspers's categories can survive intergenerational translation under one added constraint: that metaphysical guilt is strictly non-transferable to institutions, yet individuals may inherit what I call structural complicity—participation in social practices that reproduce the conditions of past wrongs without direct causal contribution. Please describe the most important implications of this constrained translation for how we should design reparative constitutional mechanisms. Specifically, address which guilt-types retain normative force across generations, whether political guilt can be institutionalized without collapsing into mere symbolism, and what this means for the boundary between legitimate collective redress and illegitimate collective punishment. Keep the analysis conceptual and practical rather than historical or bibliographic; I need the architecture of the argument and its stakes for institutional design, not a review of sources.
