I'm a novelist, and I've been asked to deliver the keynote at the retirement ceremony for Judge Elena Vasquez, who spent three decades building restorative-justice protocols for a regional court. The room will be split: jurists, social workers, and administrators on one side; family members and former program participants—including people she once sentenced—on the other. I want the address to turn on a conceptual framing I have been calling "the uncollected debt": a rigorously secular, non-supernatural reading of karma as moral desert, in which a wrong generates a real claim to redress that does not vanish merely because no authority enforces it, yet also carries no guarantee of cosmic settlement or personal reward. This idea should do real structural work in the speech. It must prevent me from saying she "balanced the scales"—she manifestly did not; many debts remain open—and it must prevent me from implying that her work was therefore futile, since the reality of an unpaid claim is independent of its collection. Please write a twelve-minute address in a literary but precise register, honoring what she actually accomplished—registering moral debts with institutional care rather than canceling them—without offering false comfort to the family and without sliding into despair before those who lived through her processes. Do not include citations, bibliographies, or references to named scholars; the argument should live entirely in the language of the occasion.
