I'm working through a philosophy-of-science reading group focused on scientific discovery and historical contingency, and our discussions of simultaneous invention keep collapsing into a stale binary between "great-man" heroics and vague assertions that "progress was inevitable." The Structural Inevitabilists hold that, given the formal and empirical resources available in the relevant scientific communities by the mid-seventeenth and mid-nineteenth centuries, the core results—calculus and natural selection—were structurally overdetermined. They grant that individuals executed the work, but claim that if Newton, Leibniz, Darwin, or Wallace had never lived, functionally equivalent theories would have emerged within roughly a generation, differing only in notation or rhetorical emphasis. The Agent-Contingentists accept that social and intellectual conditions made these discoveries possible, but deny structural overdetermination. For them, the specific conceptual framing—Newton's fluxional geometry versus Leibniz's differential notation, Darwin's Malthusian-mechanistic framing versus Wallace's biogeographic framing—is not cosmetic packaging but part of what was discovered; and they maintain that without the particular cognitive styles, personal networks, and contingent life-events of these four figures, neither calculus nor evolutionary theory by natural selection would have arrived in the form and timing that shaped subsequent science.
