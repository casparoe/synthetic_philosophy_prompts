I am leading a single seventy-five-minute seminar for third-year philosophy students who have read some basic epistemology but have not studied modal logic. My aim is not to survey the literature but to have them grasp Fitch's paradox of knowability as a structural conflict among three commitments rather than as a skeptical threat. Do not explain the paradox itself; instead, plan the explanatory sequence. First, identify the dependency order: which distinction or concept has to be secured before I can introduce the knowability principle, and why the common preliminary — distinguishing metaphysical possibility from psychological feasibility — is either essential or a red herring. Second, propose the one carrying example or scenario that should bear the weight of the derivation; specify what internal structure it must have (for instance, a case in which knowledge of a conjunction is present while knowledge of one conjunct is absent, with an entailment running between them) and why the generic "there exists an unknown truth" formulation is inadequate as the central illustration. Third, map the predictable misconceptions in the order they will arise — for instance, that the conclusion asserts actual omniscience, that "knowable" is being read as "will eventually be discovered," or that the paradox depends on a specific theory of mind — and for each indicate the precise point in the sequence where it should be blocked and how. Fourth, design the closing check: one focused question or brief exercise that distinguishes students who see the logical tension from those who have merely registered an uncomfortable result about human limits. Finally, state explicitly what should be omitted at this level — whether Church's formalization, the verificationist genealogy, or analogies to Gödel's modal ontological argument — and justify each omission in terms of the session's limited objective.
