I have been reflecting on Arthur Eddington’s 1919 eclipse expeditions to Sobral and Príncipe, and I want to analyse the episode through a particular lens on underdetermination—one that goes beyond the familiar Quine-Duhem thesis. Rather than treating the problem as a purely logical gap between evidence and theory, I am interested in what I shall call calibration-dependent underdetermination: the situation in which the instruments and procedures used to gather confirming evidence are themselves interpreted through the very theories being tested, so that adopting a rival theory would not merely require revising the explanation of the data but revising the data’s very status as measurements. In the 1919 case, the salient facts are these: Einstein’s general relativity predicted a stellar deflection of approximately 1.75 arcseconds near the solar limb; a Newtonian treatment treating light as corpuscles yielded about half that value; Eddington’s teams obtained photographic plates showing deflections near 1.61 and 1.98 arcseconds, closer to Einstein; and there was subsequent controversy about whether plates affected by cloud cover at Sobral were improperly excluded.

I want a careful mapping: who were the epistemic agents with standing to interpret the plates, what auxiliary assumptions about telescope optics, plate calibration, and gravitational effects on the instruments themselves were in play, and whether a committed Newtonian could have retained the rival theory not by denying the photographic results but by reinterpreting the calibration conditions that turned those results into measurements of deflection. Then I want an honest account of where this form of underdetermination is silent or breaks: was there in fact a genuinely available rival calibration framework in 1919, or does the episode show that predictive divergence at this scale collapses the logical space for rivals? Finally, say what this case reveals about whether calibration-dependent underdetermination is a live epistemic threat or merely a reconstructed possibility after the fact, and identify the point in your reasoning that is most vulnerable—specifically, what kind of historical or conceptual evidence about the 1919 instrumentation would force you to revise your verdict.
