I'm serving on the charter revision commission for the Lake Meridian Regional Authority, and for the first time I'm personally responsible for deciding which safeguards should be written into our founding document as binding precommitments—clauses that tie the hands of future boards rather than merely advising them. The philosophical literature distinguishes Ulysses contracts, in which an agent binds her own future will to prevent predictable failure, from constitutional precommitments, in which a present assembly binds successors because it distrusts their judgment or values. Our commission has ninety days, no constitutional lawyers on staff, and any clause we embed can be amended only by a two-thirds referendum, so the stakes are high and our expertise is thin. I need a reusable decision procedure my fellow commissioners can run on any proposed "Ulysses clause": an ordered checklist with explicit stop-rules for rejection, explicit hand-off points where the question is one of democratic values rather than institutional design, and clear markers for steps that remain judgment calls no matter how formalized. The procedure should distinguish precommitments that correct a foreseeable, systematic failure mode in future deliberation from those that merely entrench present preferences under the guise of epistemic humility, and it should flag the specific misfire risk that present boards overestimate the stability of the conditions they are locking in.

Please apply the procedure to three concrete proposals: (1) a thirty-year carbon-price floor that future boards cannot lower without a unanimous vote of all member municipalities; (2) a permanent prohibition on selling watershed conservation land to private entities, amendable only by a supermajority plus an independent ecological review; and (3) an emergency-borrowing clause requiring unanimous board consent for any debt exceeding ten percent of annual revenue, intended to block panic-driven spending during crises. I want one of these—specifically the carbon-price floor, which I personally support for climate reasons—to come out as a rejection if the logic demands it, with the reasoning made explicit. Also state plainly what kinds of disputes the procedure cannot settle, include a brief "misfire warning" for cases where predictive confidence is disguised as institutional wisdom, and—since I must defend the commission's recommendations—identify the central distinction in your procedure that is most vulnerable to collapse, and tell me exactly what argument or empirical finding would lead you to overturn that distinction and revise the whole framework.
