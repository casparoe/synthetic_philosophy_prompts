I'm in a small reading group on economic planning and we've been spinning our wheels on the socialist calculation debate for months now. We all know the standard moves — Mises saying dispersed knowledge can't be summed up by a central board, Lange answering with trial-and-error pricing in a market-socialist model, Hayek pushing back that discovery is procedural and not just a computation problem — and yet nobody in the room has budged since we started. It feels like we're replaying the same 1930s-40s script with different vocabulary and talking past eachother, which makes me wonder: is the stall happening because both sides are actually talking about different things (static equilibrium vs dynamic adaptation), or because there's a hidden empirical claim about cognitive limits and information costs that neither side has pinned down? Or maybe it's not substantive at all, just a verbal tangle around what "calculation" or "knowing a price" is supposed to mean?

Lately I've been thinking that part of the deadlock comes from an implicit picture of the planner as a single brain or computer, which feels weird now that distributed systems can learn locally without a central node. I don't want to force tech analogies onto the debate if they don't do real work, but I also wonder whether a formal result about convergence in distributed optimization, or a sharper distinction between allocative computation and epistemic discovery, could actually split the knot. So my ask is: diagnose the disagreement properly. Is it bedrock intuition about the value of markets, a hidden factual question about information processing, or two different research programs hiding under one name? And whatever you think it is, tell me honestly whether anything — a theorem, an experiment, a conceptual distinction — could actually move it, or if this is one of those debates that's stalled for good reason and should stay that way. Don't give me a literature survey; I want to know what kind of beast we're dealing with.
