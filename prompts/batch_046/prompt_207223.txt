Read Michel Gondry’s film *Eternal Sunshine of the Spotless Mind* through the lens of the ethics of memory, specifically the tension between a duty to remember shared experience and a legitimate case for forgetting. Focus on Joel and Clementine’s decision to erase each other, the traces that survive that erasure, and their final choice to begin again with full knowledge of the prior failure. How does the film treat intimate memory: as an archival record that can be cleanly deleted, or as a performative, affective trace that reshapes the present even when its explicit content is gone? Where does the narrative illuminate Margalit’s idea that shared memory is constitutive of ethical community and that dissolving it is a kind of moral failure toward the other, and where does it align with Rieff’s suspicion that compulsory remembrance—or, conversely, technologically enforced forgetting—produces a distorted, second-order suffering? Examine specific, well-known scenes—the train encounter, the collapsing beach house, the tape-listening sequence, and the closing repetition of the first meeting—and ask whether the film’s circular structure implies that forgetting is impossible without leaving residues that generate a new form of injustice, or whether it treats clean erasure as a self-deceptive fantasy that ultimately undermines the autonomy of the beloved. Finally, consider whether the work adds something to the philosophical debate that abstract argument alone misses: does its portrayal of romantic repetition suggest that the value of remembering lies not in the preservation of factual record but in the acknowledgment of the other’s irreducible otherness, and does that make the case for forgetting stronger, or collapse it into a different kind of betrayal?
