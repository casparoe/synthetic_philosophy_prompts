I am writing a feature on the wrong kind of reasons problem in contemporary epistemology, and I have constructed the following argument for discussion. It strikes me as suspicious because it appears to derive an untenable conclusion from premises that are each individually attractive.

(1) The canonical resolution of the wrong-kind-of-reasons problem holds that belief is constitutively governed by a norm of truth: a consideration counts as a right-kind reason for belief only if it bears on the truth of the proposition believed.

(2) From this it follows that pragmatic, moral, or psychological considerations—which do not alter a proposition's truth-value—are always wrong-kind reasons for belief.

(3) Let me introduce a distinction I have been calling 'epistemic partiality in maintenance': the practice of sustaining a belief not because of its evidential support, but because revising it would carry unacceptable practical, emotional, or social costs. This is not belief-formation guided by non-evidential factors; it is belief-preservation guided by them.

(4) If the norm of truth governs belief as such—not merely its acquisition but its continued retention—then any non-evidential cost of revision becomes a wrong-kind reason for maintaining the belief.

(5) Consequently, if holding belief B becomes psychologically damaging, one has a wrong-kind reason to continue holding B (namely, avoiding harm), and therefore, by the canonical resolution, one ought to revise B regardless of its evidential standing.

(6) But this implies that whenever the practical costs of holding a true belief rise, rationality demands abandoning it, which seems to collapse the distinction between practical and epistemic rationality entirely.

(7) Hence, either the canonical resolution is false, or we must deny that the truth-norm extends to belief-maintenance.

Where does the argument fail? I am inclined to defend the canonical resolution and the idea that belief aims at truth, but I want to understand precisely which step I must reject and what that rejection costs me conceptually. Please provide your diagnosis in British English, with particular attention to whether the move from (3) to (4) smuggles in an ambiguity about what it means for a reason to 'govern' a mental state.
