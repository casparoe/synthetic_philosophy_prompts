Exam question: Explain the Frege-Geach problem for moral expressivism. Use both a conditional example (If stealing is wrong, then concealing stolen goods is wrong) and a disjunctive example (Either lying is wrong or breaking promises is wrong) to show exactly why the standard expressivist account of unembedded moral sentences struggles to preserve logical validity in embedded contexts. Then evaluate the proposal that moral attitudes should be reconceived as structured planning dispositions—mental states that specify which actions to avoid or pursue conditional on other actions being avoided or pursued. Does this genuinely solve the problem, or does it merely relocate the difficulty? In particular, address whether such a proposal avoids cognitivism or instead smuggles in belief-like representational structure.

Student answer: Moral expressivism, in the Blackburn-Geach tradition, holds that to call an action wrong is not to describe it as having a moral property but to express a non-cognitive attitude—typically disapproval—toward it. In unembedded contexts this is straightforward: uttering Theft is wrong expresses my disapproval of theft.

The difficulty arises in embedded contexts. Following Frege's observation about indirect discourse, Geach pointed out that in sentences like If theft is wrong, then concealing stolen goods is wrong, the embedded clause theft is wrong must carry the same content it has when unembedded; otherwise the inference from the premises to the conclusion would be invalid. Yet on the expressivist account, the unembedded clause expresses disapproval, and there is no disapproval being expressed in the antecedent of a conditional—someone can sincerely assert the conditional without disapproving of theft. So the expressivist seems unable to assign a uniform meaning to the clause across contexts.

One might try to solve this by treating moral attitudes as structured planning dispositions. Instead of a simple feeling of disapproval, an agent has a mental state that specifies: avoid concealing goods, given that one avoids theft. Asserting the conditional then expresses this conditional disposition. If the agent also holds the unconditional disposition to avoid theft, she can derive the unconditional disposition to avoid concealing goods. Because these remain dispositions—causally efficacious states guiding action rather than representational beliefs—this preserves logical relations without collapsing into cognitivism.

The same structure handles disjunctions. Either lying is wrong or breaking promises is wrong expresses a disjunctive planning disposition: avoid at least one of the pair. This can enter into further inferences with other dispositions in a fully compositional way. Thus the planning-disposition model solves the Frege-Geach problem by giving embedded moral clauses a uniform, structurally complex content that is still non-cognitive.
