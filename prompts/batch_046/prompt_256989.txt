Write a dialogue between a philosopher defending Kierkegaard's concept of the teleological suspension of the ethical and a critic who denies it can be secularized. Ground their debate in the following concrete case: a physician breaks clinical trial protocols to administer an unproven therapy to a terminal patient, insisting her justification is not consequentialist—she expects no measurable benefit—but stems from a private, incommunicable conviction that the universal ethical rule must be suspended for a higher, singular purpose. The defender should argue that this mirrors Abraham's movement—first infinite resignation to the ethical, then recovery by virtue of the absurd—and that the act's social unrecognizability is a feature, not a flaw. The critic should respond that without a verifiable transcendent command, the physician's "suspension" is indistinguishable from arbitrary preference, and that invoking Kierkegaard's own criterion of the knight of faith's invisibility actually undermines the analog, since Abraham's faith was directed toward God rather than an unverifiable inner voice. Ensure real back-and-forth: each speaker must directly address the other's prior point rather than restating their own position, and neither should be treated as a strawman.
