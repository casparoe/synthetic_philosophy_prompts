ok so i've been reading about mertons multiples thesis — the idea that discovery gets overdetermined once a field is 'ripe', so the same result pops out of different heads. but heres what's actually confusing me: if newton and leibniz both cookd up calculus in the 1670s, and darwin and wallace both hit selection mechanics by 1858, doesnt that mean the individual scientist is basically a replaceable node? like, wallace sends his letter, darwin panics and publishes — but if wallace had died of malaria in 1855, wouldnt some other naturalist (bates? hooker?) have synthesized the same mechanism by 1862? and then people say the priority fight was just ego, but if the result was structurally inevitable, arguing over 'who discovered it' is like fighting over which raindrop filled the bucket.

but wait — leibniz's notation actually won, and newton's fluxions kind of stalled in england. so if the 'same' discovery can come in incompatible forms with totally different historical trajectories, doesnt that break the multiples argument? because now it's not one inevitable result with redundant paths, it's one abstract result that fragments into non-interchangable versions. so is inevitability about the theorem itself, or about the actual technology that gets adopted? and if it's only the abstract theorem, doesnt the 'who matters' question sneak back in — not about credit, but about which version gets to live and shape the field?
