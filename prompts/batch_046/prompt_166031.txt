Read The Great Gatsby through Plato's account of symposia in the Laws and the Symposium. For Plato, regulated drinking is an epistemic stress-test: the symposiarch controls dosage so wine reveals true character without destroying judgment, turning the party into a laboratory for civic virtue. Gatsby's West Egg gatherings are the anti-symposion—no master, no philosophical aim, pure anonymity—yet Nick Carraway observes them with a sober, filtering consciousness that seems to do exactly what Plato assigns the symposiarch: he extracts moral data (Tom's brutality, Daisy's carelessness, Gatsby's delusion) that the drinkers miss. Does Nick therefore function as a hidden symposiarch, or does his very detachment prove Plato's point that observation without shared risk yields only spectacle? I want the reading to stay honest in both directions: show where Fitzgerald vindicates the Platonic idea that intoxication reveals what sobriety hides, and where the novel resists it—especially since Gatsby himself is almost teetotal and his sobriety is inseparable from his fatal self-deception. Add one more twist. Plato in the Laws suggests symposia build democratic trust only when citizens drink together across social ranks; Gatsby's parties mix old and new money precisely this way, yet they generate alienation rather than solidarity. Is Fitzgerald showing that Plato missed a necessary condition—perhaps shared political purpose—or is the failure proof that Platonic civic drinking is impossible under modern capitalism?
