Write a poem of exactly twenty-one lines, arranged in seven tercets, that enacts the Jain doctrine of anekāntavāda—many-sidedness—and its logical instrument syādvāda, the doctrine of conditional predication. Choose one concrete subject (for instance, the persistence of a flame, the identity of a river across seasons, or the moral status of an act of letting-be). Each tercet should correspond to one of the seven modal combinations: syāt asti, syāt nāsti, syāt asti nāsti, syāt avaktavya, and the three compound combinations involving avaktavya. Do not label the modes; instead, let the grammar and imagery of each tercet perform the qualification—the first asserting from one angle, the second denying from another, the third holding both in tension, the fourth falling silent or gesturing toward inexpressibility, and the final three compounding these relations. Include a single refrain line that closes every tercet, shifting in grammatical role and semantic weight as the modal context changes, so that by the seventh tercet the refrain has accumulated all seven sides without resolving into a single thesis. The poem should not preach pluralism; it should demonstrate structurally why any one-line answer to the subject is inadequate. Make it accurate enough that someone familiar with syādvāda recognizes the sequence of conditions, and compelling enough as verse that the form carries the philosophy rather than merely illustrating it.
