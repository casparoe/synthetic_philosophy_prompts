I have been following the acquisition choices of my town's small regional archive, which maintains a collection devoted to history from below. So far the committee has kept a tenant farmer's handwritten grocery list from 1934 but discarded the county agricultural census for that year; preserved an oral interview with a retired dockworker about a 1956 strike but rejected the shipping firm's official strike report; saved fragments of a hand-copied folk-healing manual kept by a local midwife but excluded the printed medical textbook her university-trained brother used; and retained graffiti scratched into the wall of a demolished workhouse but not the workhouse director's annual report.

Now they must decide between two items describing the same 1912 factory fire: a cheap street ballad printed for sale and signed only with the words a neighbor, and the factory owner's private letter to his insurer describing the damage.

If they want to stay consistent with their own track record, which item should they acquire? Identify the closest analog among their four earlier decisions, lay out the competing principles that could jointly explain all four—such as privileging voices without institutional power versus privileging records never intended for official posterity—say what each principle predicts about the ballad and about the letter, and give your best guess about which rule they are actually running, with your reasons. Be explicit about what principle they would be adopting if they chose the ballad, and what different principle they would be adopting if they chose the letter. Finally, propose one further case that would genuinely distinguish those two principles.
