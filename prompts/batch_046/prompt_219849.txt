After a lengthy argument with a friend about whether major historical outcomes were structurally inevitable or genuinely contingent, I would like you to write a dialogue in British English between two equally capable thinkers. One holds a position we might call structural inertia: once socio-technical systems cross an institutional lock-in threshold, counterfactual deviations become self-punishing to such a degree that large-scale outcomes were effectively unavoidable, irrespective of individual agency. The other holds a position of counterfactual fragility: even within apparently rigid structures, trajectories remain sensitive to micro-perturbations, and appeals to inevitability are largely hindsight fallacies that mistake observed path-dependence for causal necessity.

The dialogue should feature genuine back-and-forth rather than parallel monologues. In particular, the fragility advocate should press the point that lock-in thresholds are often retrospective constructions—imposed on history by analysts who already know the outcome—rather than real causal barriers that existed ex ante. The inertia advocate should reply by distinguishing path-dependent irreversibility, where reversing course remains technically possible but institutionally self-destructive, from modal closure, where the counterfactual is not merely costly but conceptually incoherent given the system's internal rules. Treat the gap between these two notions—whether it marks a substantive distinction or merely a rhetorical shift in scale—as the unresolved core of the exchange. Do not provide bibliographies, citations, or reading lists, and avoid treating either speaker as a strawman.
