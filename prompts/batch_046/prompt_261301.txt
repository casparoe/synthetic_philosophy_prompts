I work as a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy, and I have been puzzling over an institutional design pattern in distributed systems that seems to carry hidden assumptions about where meaning lives. In our organization, every inter-service contract is defined schema-first through Protocol Buffers with gRPC, and the .proto file is treated as the single authoritative source of truth. The build pipeline strips all human-readable documentation comments and replaces field names with numeric IDs; our automated logs and monitoring systems record only those IDs together with serialized byte values. When an interoperability failure occurs, the standard debugging protocol compares schemas and field IDs: if the frames align, the message is judged correct regardless of what the original developer intended. By contrast, during security audits investigators ignore the schemas entirely and interview the authors to reconstruct intended meaning, treating the developer’s original purpose as the real content. Meanwhile, the actual interpretation or internal state formed by the receiving service is never granted authority; we do not ask what the consumer understood, only whether the frame matched.

This bifurcation reminds me of the distinction drawn in Douglas Hofstadter’s Gödel, Escher, Bach between frame messages (those that carry instructions for their own decoding), outer messages (the content intended by the sender), and inner messages (what the recipient actually decodes). I want to understand what philosophical commitments about the location of meaning our practice implicitly presupposes. Does the debugging protocol strictly require that meaning resides in the frame message—the schema and numeric IDs—such that outer intent and inner interpretation are philosophically irrelevant to correctness? Or can the same practice be reconstructed under a rival view in which meaning is always in the outer message, with schema comparison serving only as a pragmatic heuristic for tracking intent? Is our security audit practice consistent with the debugging practice, or does it quietly depend on a different theory? If we genuinely adopted the view that meaning is located in the inner message—in what the receiving system actually interprets—what concrete changes would our logging, debugging, and audit procedures have to undergo? Please reconstruct the implicit theory of meaning that our institution operates with, distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what it merely accommodates, and say whether the practice is philosophically underdetermined—compatible with several theories—or whether one view is genuinely indispensable.
