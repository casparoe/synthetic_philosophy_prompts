Design a decision tree for an advanced undergraduate philosophy-of-mind seminar that sorts a student’s commitments about the Chinese Room argument into named philosophical positions. The root node should ask whether purely syntactic computation can, in principle, produce semantic understanding. From there, construct branches that ask about the source of any deficit—whether it lies in biological substrate, in the absence of conscious experience, or in the lack of causal-environmental coupling—without requiring the student to know the labels in advance. Include a dedicated branch for the distributed-room variant, in which each grammatical rule is executed by a different agent so that no single individual understands Chinese, and require that the tree either assign this variant to an existing leaf or mark it 'unnamed' with a one-sentence diagnosis of why it falls outside standard positions. Every leaf must carry the name of a view (biological naturalism, functionalism, the intentional stance, eliminativism, or similar) and a brief note on its most awkward commitment in this context. The tree should also flag any pair of leaves it cannot genuinely separate—such as certain forms of functionalism and interpretivist accounts—rather than pretending to distinguish them. Phrase each branching point as a concrete yes/no or either/or question, and ensure the instrument does not depend on bibliographic knowledge or citation.
