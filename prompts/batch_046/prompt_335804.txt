I am a doctoral researcher in philosophy of science preparing a single ninety-minute graduate seminar on Nelson Goodman's "new riddle of induction" for a mixed audience of philosophy and cognitive science students who have already encountered Hume's problem of uniformity but have not yet worked through Goodman's original formulation. I wish to move well beyond the standard emerald-and-grue illustration that they will have encountered in undergraduate surveys, so I am asking you to design a teaching plan rather than deliver the explanation itself; please write the response in British English.

Please outline the explanatory sequence in precise dependency order: which conceptual distinction must be secured before "grue" can even be introduced without collapsing into a mere linguistic trick, how the asymmetry between observed and unobserved instances should be staged so that it feels like a genuine puzzle about predictive entitlement rather than a contrived paradox, and where in the arc I should introduce Goodman's notions of projectibility and entrenchment without allowing the session to dissolve into a taxonomy of predicates. For the carrying example, I want something that avoids gemology entirely—perhaps a case drawn from adaptive clinical trial design or predictive policing algorithms—provided it preserves the temporal asymmetry and the predicate-dependency that makes the riddle bite; specify exactly what properties the example must possess and what it must lack, and defend why it outperforms the emerald case for this particular audience.

Next, map the misconceptions I should anticipate in the order they are likely to arise: the belief that "grue" is merely an artificial colour predicate with no real-world analogue; the assumption that simplicity or Occam's razor resolves the asymmetry without further argument; the confusion of Goodman's puzzle with general scepticism about induction; and the tendency to think that statistical regularity alone determines projectibility. For each, indicate the precise point in the sequence where it should be intercepted and the single sentence or move that neutralises it without derailing the flow.

Finally, propose one diagnostic question I can pose at the close of the seminar that would reveal whether the students have grasped that the problem is normative—about which predicates we are entitled to project—rather than merely descriptive or linguistic; and state explicitly what material should be left out at this level (for instance, the formal machinery of Goodman's later symbol systems, or specific debates about natural kinds in contemporary metaphysics) with a brief justification for the omission. The plan should avoid any request for bibliographies or further reading, and should not depend on knowledge of highly specialised post-1970 literature in formal epistemology.
