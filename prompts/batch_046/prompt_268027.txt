I'm on my university's Student Flourishing Committee and we've been handed this pilot proposal that's doing my head in. Philosophy and art history want to run a "Spielsemester" for final-year students — basically swapping one graded module for an ungraded, open-ended collaborative programme with no learning outcomes, no assesment, and no employability metric attached. They're grounding it in Moritz Schlick's view that meaning only comes from activity pursued for its own sake, not as a means to grades or jobs, and they claim our current model treats all study as "work" in Schlick's sense, which is partly why mental-health referrals have spiked.

The opposition is fierce. Careers and widening-participation say Schlick's work/play split is elitist — students who need to support families can't afford a semester with zero measurable output — and that if we let this through we'll breach our access agreement and confuse UKVI about what counts as full-time study. There's also the philosophical counter: even if Schlick is right, universities have a duty of care that includes economic preparation, not just letting people play at the institution's expense.

Can you draft the short memo I'll circulate before Thursday's meeting? I need a concrete recommendation — approve, reject, or approve-with-conditions — with an actual time frame and criteria we could vote on. It should explain Schlick's distinction properly (what he meant by play versus work, not just buzzwords) and take the careers/access objection seriously rather than dismissing it. No bibliographies please, I just need something the committee can act on.
