I'm a phd student in decision theory / phil of action and I'm running a 90-minute workshop for about 14 grad students on commitment races. I want a structured in-class game — roles, rules, point-based payoffs, no real money — that makes participants feel the pressure to commit first rather than just hear about it. The twist I'm playing with is what I'm calling an "interpretive lock-in race": the commitments aren't just actions but public framings of what the payoff matrix even is. Once a player commits to a framing, everyone else has to respond inside it, but they can also commit to a counter-framing that rewrites the payoffs for the whole group. I want the rules arranged so people rush to frame the situation before they understand it, then discover they've locked themselves and others into a bad equilibrium.

Please include a timeline, the role structure, a debrief that maps moments in the game onto the standard commitment-race dynamics, and an honest note on which feature — probably the second-order irreversibility of framing commitments — the activity can't reproduce and I'll need to supply verbally. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time (confusion about whether they're playing for points or playing to win the framing debate?) and how to handle it. The participants shouldn't hear the term "commitment race" until after the debrief, and I want them to construct the sequence themselves and only notice where they've arrived at the end. Oh, and there should be one feature that rewards waiting, but the reward has to be just small enough that rushing still dominates — I think that's important for the feeling I'm after, though I'm not sure how to calibrate it so please build that tension in.
