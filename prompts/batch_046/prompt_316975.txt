I serve as the external philosophy advisor to a regional teaching hospital's Clinical Allocation Review Board, which meets weekly to adjudicate proposals that restructure clinical resources—staffing, funding, and treatment protocols—between identifiable patient subgroups and larger populations. The board has forty-five minutes per case, includes clinicians and administrators with no philosophy training, and must sign a brief public justification for every decision. Its recurring difficulty is structural aggregation: proposals that do not directly sacrifice one patient for another, but that alter baseline care standards so that a small, identifiable subgroup bears disproportionate risk or burden for diffuse benefits across a large group. I want to translate T.M. Scanlon's contractualist idea—that wrongness consists in violating principles no affected party could reasonably reject—into a reusable decision procedure the board can run without reading philosophy. The procedure must distinguish numerical aggregation (counting beneficiaries against those burdened) from structural aggregation (systemic redistribution that obscures direct comparison), must include explicit stopping rules, must flag where judgment is unavoidable, and must be applied to three cases, including one that yields an answer the board will likely find unwelcome.

Please organize your response exactly as follows, with approximate word counts:

1. Core Translation (c. 250 words): State the contractualist principle in one non-technical sentence suitable for the board. Then list, in strict order, the four questions members must ask about any proposal. For each question, specify what counts as a sufficient answer, what evidence is required, where a negative answer stops the process with a rejection, and where it proceeds.

2. The Aggregation Protocol (c. 300 words): Explain how the board applies the principle when benefits and burdens fall on different numbers of people. Provide separate decision rules for severe-but-concentrated burdens versus mild-but-widespread burdens. Explicitly address both numerical and structural aggregation. Identify the precise point at which the procedure must hand the decision to a human value judgment rather than apply a formula.

3. Non-Eliminable Judgment Points (c. 200 words): Identify three points in the procedure where judgment cannot be eliminated—such as defining a severe burden, weighing second-personal complaints from an identifiable subgroup, or determining whether a burden is genuinely unavoidable. For each, state what the board member must do, what documentation is required, and who must sign off.

4. Three Worked Cases (c. 450 words): Apply the full procedure to:
   a) A proposal to consolidate pediatric cardiac surgery into two regional centers, improving aggregate survival rates but forcing families with a specific congenital heart defect to travel over 200 miles and lose continuity of care;
   b) A mandatory post-operative antibiotic protocol that reduces infection rates hospital-wide but causes life-threatening allergic reactions in patients carrying a specific, testable genetic marker;
   c) A funding shift that denies a $2 million gene therapy to one identifiable patient with a rare mutation, freeing resources for preventive screening of 800 at-risk patients.
   For each, report the procedure's answer, note any ambiguity, and identify which case produces an answer the board will likely reject on intuitive grounds and why.

5. Limits and Misfires (c. 200 words): State clearly what types of proposals fall outside the procedure's scope, the risk of false positives and false negatives, and whether the procedure can resolve deep conflicts between extending life and preserving quality of life. Do not provide a bibliography, reading list, or citations.
