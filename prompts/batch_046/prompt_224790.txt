I am a software engineer without formal training in philosophy, and after a long dinner discussion with friends about artificial intelligence and moral obligation, I have kept returning to a specific interpretation of Bostrom-style simulation reasoning that I think should dampen, rather than amplify, extreme longtermist fanaticism about the far future. The view is not the casual shrug that "we might be simulated, so nothing matters," but a structured claim: if ancestor simulations are both numerous and typically short-lived—running only long enough to observe critical decision points before termination—then the base-rate of real observers who actually inhabit a deep future is vanishingly small relative to ephemeral simulated ones. That means the expected moral weight of outcomes in, say, the year 10,000,000 CE collapses, not because the future is unreal, but because the overwhelming majority of future-conscious entities are truncated artifacts with limited lifespans. I also lean toward an ontological discount: simulated consciousness, however phenomenally rich, lacks the full moral status of base-reality consciousness, which further weakens longtermist obligations. Taken together, this seems to undermine the urgency of optimizing for astronomically distant welfare, yet I want to know whether the package can survive serious scrutiny.

What I need is a precise map of what would genuinely count against this position—sorted from decisive to merely awkward—including observations, arguments, intuitions, or conceptual distinctions that defenders of extreme longtermism could themselves concede as damaging rather than only what critics assert. I am especially interested in whether the view can be defeated without abandoning the simulation premise entirely: for example, could one argue that simulation status is morally irrelevant, or that base-reality civilizations are precisely those that run long, high-fidelity simulations of successful futures, turning simulation prevalence into a marker of longtermist success rather than its negation? I also want an honest audit of whether I have qualified my claim into unfalsifiability—say, by retreating to "this applies only if simulations are short and low-fidelity"—and whether that insulation is a fatal flaw for a view meant to guide practical reasoning. Please distinguish what my view genuinely risks from what it merely deflects, rank the tensions by severity, indicate the least costly revision—whether I should drop the ontological discount, the branching-truncation premise, or the framework altogether—and conclude with whether a well-insulated position of this kind remains intellectually respectable or has been rendered useless.
