I am trying to stabilize my position on Weber’s Protestant ethic argument together with the methodological problem of explaining historically with cultural values, and I want a consistency audit before defending the package in a seminar. Here are the commitments I hold simultaneously:

1. The Protestant ethic thesis is best read as an argument about elective affinity: Calvinist ascetic values did not mechanically generate capitalism, but they formed a cultural matrix that made rationalized economic conduct meaningful, sustainable, and selectively advantageous for actors within emerging institutional settings.

2. Cultural values can be genuine historical causes, but only through what Weber called adequate causation—meaning a value-orientation counts as causally relevant only when one can demonstrate a non-accidental, intelligible link between the subjective meaning actors attach to it and the specific course of action taken, not merely a statistical correlation.

3. To avoid treating norms as physical forces, cultural causation must always be mediated by individual meaning-interpretation: values cause behavior only insofar as actors subjectively orient their conduct to them; there is no causal pathway that bypasses consciousness.

4. Despite this mediation, culture is not reducible to individual psychology. The explanatory work is done by intersubjective value-systems that constrain and enable action in ways that no single actor intends or fully controls, and these systems can stabilize across generations.

5. Weber’s value-freedom requires the sociologist to bracket her own normative commitments when tracing these causal chains—so explaining capitalism’s origins via Protestant values is compatible with neither endorsing those values nor treating them as universally valid norms.

The tensions I sense but cannot resolve: commitment (2) demands adequate causation at the level of individual orientation, while (4) treats intersubjective systems as doing independent causal work, which risks making the system-level claim epiphenomenal. Meanwhile, (1) describes values as selecting among institutional possibilities in a way that sounds structurally determinative, yet (3) insists that all causation runs through subjective orientation, so elective affinity begins to look like either a redundant redescription or a hidden structural mechanism. Finally, (5) seems to sit uneasily with (4): if value-systems are genuinely causally powerful, it is unclear how the analyst can fully bracket normative assessment of them without losing explanatory grip—yet if she does not bracket them, she violates value-freedom.

Please rank the actual tensions by severity rather than treating them all as equally damaging, indicate which pairs are only apparently in conflict and dissolve on closer inspection, and specify what the least costly revision would be to restore consistency—without assuming I am willing to abandon the claim that culture can be a cause.
