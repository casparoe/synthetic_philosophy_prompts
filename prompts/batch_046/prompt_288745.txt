In discussions comparing meat consumption to wild-animal suffering, a common compressed claim runs: "Switching from beef to chicken is ethically required because it lowers both direct slaughter and the crop-cultivation deaths that feed livestock." Disentangle the precise theses this sentence could express. Separate, first, empirical claims about feed-conversion efficiency and pasture-versus-feed land use; second, counterfactual claims about whether reducing beef demand spares wild animals by shrinking agricultural frontiers or harms them by intensifying feed-crop production; third, normative claims about whether wild-animal suffering from habitat conversion belongs in the same calculus as farmed-animal deaths; and fourth, the implicit "ought" that makes the switch mandatory rather than merely preferable. For each reading, indicate whether it is tautological, empirically falsifiable, or a substantive moral premise, and note which combinations actually support the conclusion and which undermine it—for example, if pasture-based beef avoids the crop-related wild suffering that chicken feed entails. Do not provide bibliographies or citations; resolve the ambiguity through conceptual analysis alone.
