At a recent workshop on anthropic reasoning, two philosophers—Dr. Lin and Dr. Vasquez—disagreed sharply over whether reference-class restriction can block the presumptuous philosopher objection without begging the question. Lin argued that if one applies the self-sampling assumption strictly within a reference class defined as agents who have formulated the presumptuous philosopher argument, then the existence of a single such agent provides no differential support for cosmological models that differ in total observer count, because both models contain astronomically more agents outside that restricted class. Vasquez objected that this restriction is ad hoc: selecting a reference class by a property—having encountered the argument—that is itself distributed differently across cosmological hypotheses effectively smuggles a self-indication bias into the sampling frame, violating the neutrality that self-sampling requires. Lin countered that Vasquez confuses the causal origin of membership with the logical conditions for sampling; awareness of the argument is a post-existential filter, not a generating cause of existence, so conditioning on it does not alter the prior probability of being any particular observer within the class. Vasquez then pressed what she called the presumptuous referee problem: if Lin's strategy is legitimate, then any defender of self-sampling can always neutralize self-indication objections by carving out a reference class small enough to erase the statistical discrimination, rendering self-sampling unfalsifiable and therefore vacuous as a rational constraint. They asked me to referee and I could not settle it. Say who is more right overall, and identify what both parties overlook regarding how indexical uncertainty interacts with epistemic peer disagreement about the very boundaries of the reference class.
