I have been arguing with friends in a philosophy of religion seminar about whether secularization necessarily undermines the deepest forms of social trust. Rather than revisiting familiar empirical debates about charitable giving or reported happiness, I want to isolate a stricter conceptual problem. Define what it would mean for a society to be genuinely 'more moral' in a non-reductive sense: not as higher aggregate welfare or stricter deontic compliance, but as the institutionalization of what we might call 'ontological reciprocity'—the shared recognition that other persons are ends-in-themselves rather than strategic nodes in exchange networks—and explain why sustaining this recognition demands more than dispersed individual virtue.

Then evaluate whether religious frameworks possess unique structural resources—such as shared eschatological accountability, sacramental views of personhood, or communal ritual practices—for generating and preserving this reciprocity across generations, treating these not merely as psychological incentives but as institutional architectures. Consider, in turn, whether rigorous secular moral philosophies, particularly Kantian or neo-Aristotelian traditions, can replicate the same recognition through purely immanent means without eventually collapsing into instrumental rationality or contractual cooperation. Reach and defend a specific conclusion about whether secular societies can maintain this thicker form of trust over the long term, and address the strongest counter-argument that any secular substitute will prove unstable because it lacks a metaphysical guarantee that others' dignity is non-negotiable.
