Write a deadpan parody of a peer-reviewed scientific article, approximately 650 words, structured with an abstract, introduction, methods, results, discussion, and a references heading that contains no entries. The venue is the fictional Journal of Normative Calibration, and the voice should be that of a rigorously neutral empirical researcher reporting a failed breakthrough.

The target of the satire is the methodological practice in empirical moral psychology of attempting to filter normative biases out of moral intuitions in order to reveal underlying preferences, treating the filter itself as if it were not normatively loaded.

To place the prompt in the weeds, build the parody around an invented conceptual apparatus: the Moral Palatometer, an imaginary instrument that measures first-order axiological preference by subtracting reflexive normative contamination. Define reflexive normative contamination as the alteration in moral valence that occurs when a subject becomes aware that her intuition is being classified as either bias or preference, and first-order axiological preference as the putative baseline that remains after statistical correction. The satire should derive from treating these distinctions with extreme technical seriousness—complete with calibration curves, a null-bias baseline, and described tables—while demonstrating that every purification step smuggles in a specific normative framework.

Specifically, include a described table comparing three conditions—Raw Intuition, Statistically Corrected, and Double-Blind Virtue—across invented metrics such as Contamination Residual and Framework Transparency. The results section should report that the Double-Blind Virtue condition achieves near-zero contamination only by encoding a utilitarian aggregation norm so completely that subjects can no longer distinguish harm reduction from preference satisfaction.

Maintain sustained deadpan irony throughout: never wink, never break character, and never acknowledge the absurdity of quantifying moral taste. The piece should be readable to someone familiar with debates in moral psychology and the distinction between normative and descriptive claims, but it should not depend on knowledge of obscure texts, named living researchers, or specific empirical studies. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, citations, or requests for further literature.
