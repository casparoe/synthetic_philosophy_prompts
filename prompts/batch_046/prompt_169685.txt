Since my mother’s dementia has progressed, I have noticed my own idioms and childhood memories surfacing in her speech without her recognition, while I find myself unable to distinguish whether certain recollections belong to me or to her. Reading Hofstadter on strange loops has made this feel less like mere loss and more like a literal redistribution of self across two brains—symbols activating patterns that outlive their origin—yet I want no sentimental resolution. Write a villanelle of nineteen lines in which the two refrains, one asserting that an I can persist in another brain and one conceding that such persistence may no longer contain anyone, exchange speakers across the stanzas so that by the final couplet the voice is indeterminate; allow the pronouns within the refrains to shift, but keep the rhyme scheme intact. The language must treat the loop mechanism precisely—patterns of activation rather than ghosts or souls—without turning into jargon, and the poem should close on an open tension rather than consolation. It will be read at a small gathering marking the end of her cognitive life rather than her biological death, so the tone should be rigorous, intimate, and unresolved.
