Write a dialogue between a Theravada Abhidhamma scholar and a Madhyamaka-influenced phenomenologist, both equally well-versed in Buddhist philosophy and Western personal-identity theory, about whether moral agency can survive the complete dissolution of autobiographical self. The scholar should defend a reductionist view: the person is a conventional designation over discrete, momentary skandhas, and continuity—especially moral continuity—is carried by the causal efficacy of cetasika and cetana across successive citta-vīthi (mind-moment processes), with no need for a persisting substrate. The phenomenologist should defend an anti-reductionist view: reducing the person to aggregates merely relocates the illusion of substance, since the aggregates themselves are dependently originated and empty of svabhāva; moral continuity operates through a conventionally designated narrative thread that has no ultimate bearer but also cannot be decomposed into discrete parts that inhere in one another.

Center the exchange on a specific, somewhat niche scenario: a practitioner who, after decades of sustained maranasati (mindfulness-of-death) and vipassanā, has deliberately de-reinforced all episodic autobiographical memory to the point where no narrative self is accessible, yet spontaneously and skillfully fulfills a complex ethical vow made thirty years earlier—without any felt sense of being the original agent and without any retrievable memory of having taken the vow. The scholar should argue that this is precisely what reductionism predicts: the vow’s intentional imprint remains encoded in the continuum of mental factors and activates appropriate volition regardless of narrative identity. The phenomenologist should counter that this explanation smuggles in a hidden substrate—if there is no self in the moments and no self in the aggregates, there is nothing in which the imprint can inhere, and the fulfillment is better explained as the dependently arisen convergence of conditions (bodily habit, social recognition, linguistic framing) that conventionally counts as honoring a vow, without requiring any cross-temporal bearer at all.

Require real back-and-forth: each should respond directly to the other’s points—especially whether the reductionist appeal to citta-vīthi avoids or merely disguises a persistence problem, and whether the phenomenologist’s appeal to conventional designation can genuinely distinguish moral continuity from accidental behavioral repetition. Keep the discussion concrete to the scenario, avoid asking for bibliographies, citations, or reading lists, and do not invoke obscure texts or figures outside well-known Abhidhamma and Madhyamaka philosophy. The dialogue should be polished and formal, with neither interlocutor treated as a strawman.
