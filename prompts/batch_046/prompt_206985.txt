I'm writing a paper for a graduate seminar in normative ethics on the internal stability of deontological constraints, and I keep returning to a tension my instructor sketched informally, which she referred to as "threshold contamination," though I have not been able to locate that phrase in the standard literature. The classic paradox of deontology, as we have discussed it in class, arises because deontological theories appear to forbid aggregating rights-violations across persons or scenarios, yet when violations are unavoidable they seem to direct us to minimize them — a move that looks structurally identical to consequentialist optimization, but arrived at through a logic of constraints rather than of maximization. By contrast, "threshold contamination" describes a different, perhaps more insidious dynamic: once a deontological framework permits a single threshold-based exception — for instance, allowing deception only when it prevents catastrophic harm — the reasoning that licenses that exception can be reapplied iteratively. Each new application is individually defensible, but the cumulative effect is that the original prohibition is gradually hollowed out and replaced by an implicit cost-benefit calculus, without the theory ever explicitly abandoning its deontological self-conception. I want to understand whether these are genuinely distinct pathologies or merely two angles on the same problem. Specifically, is the paradox of deontology primarily about the impossibility of consistent aggregation rules within a constraint-based system, whereas threshold contamination concerns the diachronic erosion of categorical boundaries through successive, locally justified exemptions? Or does one reduce to the other? Furthermore, if "threshold contamination" is not an established technical term in moral philosophy, what is the nearest recognized concept — perhaps something in the literature on moral thresholds, slippery slopes, or the structure of exceptions — and how should I distinguish the phenomenon I am describing from both the classic paradox and from ordinary slippery-slope arguments?
