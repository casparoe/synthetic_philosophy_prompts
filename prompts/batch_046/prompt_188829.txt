ok so here's my rough position on just war and moral equality — want a consistency check before I present it in seminar. I hold:

(a) The moral equality of combatants holds: soldiers on both sides have symmetric permissions to kill in battle, regardless of jus ad bellum, so long as they stick to discrimination and proportionalty.

(b) Killing is only morally permitted when it prevents an unjust threat; an unjust aggressor lacks that justification, and being part of a collective doesn't cancel that.

(c) Most soldiers in unjust wars are excused rather than justified — they defer epistmicly to state authority and act within an institutional role that distributes moral labour.

(d) I don't think excuses ever generate permissions; being blameless for X doesn't mean X was right.

(e) But I also think institutional roles can generate genuine permissions that don't depend on the justice of the particular war — the role itself licenses the soldier, not just an excuse.

(f) Conscripts should be treated differently from volunteer professionals: the former have full excuse, the latter have assumed the role knowingly and so can't claim the same protection.

(g) International humanitarian law should treat both sides symetrically, but that's purely pragmatic and doesn't reflect moral equality.

It feels like (a), (b) and (e) are pulling in different directions, and (f) might mess with (a) too. Also (d) seems to block using (c) to defend (a). Is (g) genuinely in tension with (b) or is that a false alarm? Can you go through the pairs, rank the tensions by severity, and for each say what's the cheapest fix and what it costs? I also want to know which apparent clashes dissolve on inspection — especially whether (g) really conflicts with (b). I haven't decided which commitment I'd drop, so just audit the set.
