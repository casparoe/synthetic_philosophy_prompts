As a retired experimental physicist who has only recently begun serious study in philosophy of science, I am to deliver the opening remarks tomorrow at an interdisciplinary workshop, The Uses of Falsehood in Scientific Practice. The audience is split evenly between practicing scientists—mostly physicists and evolutionary biologists who work inside idealized models daily—and philosophers of science and graduate students. I want to speak for roughly eight minutes, and I want the remarks built structurally around Milton Friedman’s F-twist, the claim that unrealistic assumptions are defensible so long as predictions succeed. Rather than simply rehearsing that defense, I want to introduce a distinction drawn partly from my own laboratory experience with lattice approximations: between predictive idealization, which is false but forecast-accurate, and what I will call ontologically productive misrepresentation, in which a false assumption actively reshapes the conceptual ontology of the domain, generating entities and relations with no direct observational counterpart that nevertheless become indispensable to reasoning. This distinction must govern the speech’s architecture: it should determine which examples I invoke, constrain whether I can conclude that science progresses by converging on truth or by strategically diverging from it, and allow me to address both the scientists—who will think of the Ising model and Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium—and the philosophers—who will think of representation and realism—without reducing either side to a foil. The remarks should avoid citations, bibliographies, and appeals to shared texts, and they should land as a genuine philosophical argument rather than as a nostalgic anecdote from a former researcher.
