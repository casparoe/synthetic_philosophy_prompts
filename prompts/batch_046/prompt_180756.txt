I'm currently studying comparative democratic politics, and I've been struck by a recurring pattern in recent debates about democratic backsliding — in Hungary, Poland, Turkey, and in some analyses of the United States — that does not quite match the standard vocabulary I have encountered. Rather than openly dismantling electoral institutions or suspending constitutional provisions, these governments appear to preserve the formal architecture of democracy: elections continue, parliaments sit, courts function, and opposition parties are not formally banned. Yet the actual space for collective political decision-making seems to erode through a different mechanism. Contested questions are progressively shifted out of the political sphere and into administrative and judicial channels; opposition groups find themselves consumed not by explicit prohibition, but by a proliferation of technically neutral regulatory requirements, funding audits, compliance reviews, and procedural delays that disproportionately burden anyone outside the incumbent coalition. Over time, democratic forms remain intact, but meaningful contestation is replaced by a kind of procedural exhaustion — every significant challenge is redirected into a labyrinth of bureaucratic and legal hurdles that only the state apparatus can navigate efficiently. Critics describe this as democratic erosion, but defenders respond that the rules are applied uniformly and that opposition weakness reflects strategic failure rather than structural bias. I am aware of concepts such as executive aggrandizement and stealth authoritarianism, yet these seem to emphasise the centralisation of power in the executive rather than this specific mechanism — the replacement of political conflict with procedural attrition through bureaucratisation and juridification. Is there an established term for precisely this configuration, in which democracy is hollowed out not through institutional abolition but through the slow redirection of opposition into exhausting administrative and legal procedures? How should it be distinguished from ordinary regulatory capture, from the normal growth of administrative complexity in mature democracies, and from related ideas like depoliticisation or lawfare? If no precise concept exists, what defining features would be essential, and where exactly should the boundaries be drawn so that it does not simply collapse into a general description of weak opposition?
