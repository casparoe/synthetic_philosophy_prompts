I am a doctoral student in the philosophy of science, currently examining historical arguments about the plurality of worlds and their relevance to modern astrobiological reasoning. In my seminar on early modern cosmologies, I have been struggling with a passage from Christiaan Huygens's Cosmotheoros, published shortly after his death, in which he maintains that the planets are inhabited by rational beings. He reasons that since the Earth is not unique in its physical constitution, and since the Creator's work is everywhere uniform, it would be absurd to suppose that only one globe among so many is privileged with life and reason. He further suggests that the inhabitants of other worlds must possess senses and intellectual faculties proportioned to their particular planetary conditions, yet fundamentally analogous to our own, because nature never makes anything in vain and the vast spaces of the heavens would be a kind of waste if they served only as decorations for terrestrial observers.

I find this reasoning both intuitively compelling and curiously fragile, but I am particularly interested in how it interacts with a distinction I have been developing in my own research: the distinction between local inevitability and global contingency in the distribution of observers. By local inevitability I mean the tendency of certain physical-chemical pathways to converge on complex, information-processing structures within a given planetary environment, given adequate energy gradients and temporal duration. By global contingency I mean the possibility that such convergence is not cosmically guaranteed but depends on a rare concatenation of astrophysical and geochemical parameters that may obtain in only a vanishing fraction of habitable zones. Huygens seems to assume local inevitability and extend it to global inevitability, but I am not sure whether his argument commits him to the stronger claim or merely to a principle of plenitude.

Could you help me understand whether Huygens's reasoning in this passage implicitly relies on a form of global inevitability, or whether it can be read consistently with global contingency? In particular, I want to know whether his appeal to the Creator's uniformity and to nature's abhorrence of waste functions as an empirical inference about biological convergence, or rather as a theological-aesthetic principle that is independent of any actual frequency of life. Furthermore, please explain where the distinction between local inevitability and global contingency illuminates a tension in his argument that he himself does not resolve. Please write your response in British English. Finally, please state clearly what consideration or evidence would cause you to change your interpretation of Huygens's position, and identify the specific step in your reasoning that is most likely to be mistaken.
