World-historians investigating the Great Divergence frequently pose the Needham question in a sharply contrastive form: not “why did Britain industrialize?” but “why Britain and not China?”, treating eighteenth-century Britain and Qing China as matched baseline cases that diverged around 1750. What philosophical commitments about historical explanation, counterfactual dependence, and comparative causation does this framing presuppose? Must one hold that macro-historical outcomes are isolable for contrastive analysis, or that the absence of industrialization in China is causally symmetric with its presence in Britain? Does the practice commit its practitioners to a particular theory of causal selection—say, that explanatory relevance requires presence in one case and absence in another—or can structural and path-dependent accounts accommodate the same framing? And is the contrastive formulation consistent with the discipline’s broader reluctance, in other contexts, to treat societies as interchangeable experimental units? Reconstruct the implicit theory of explanation that makes this framing seem natural, distinguish what it strictly requires from what is merely heuristically attractive, and indicate where the practice remains philosophically underdetermined.
