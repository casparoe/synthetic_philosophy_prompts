I am preparing the closing section of a long feature on philosophical accounts of extinction for a science and ideas magazine, and I need a poem that treats the concept with formal precision rather than elegiac generality. Write a villanelle—nineteen lines, five tercets and a final quatrain, with two refrains that alternate and finally converge—on Samuel Scheffler's afterlife conjecture: the thesis that a substantial part of the value we assign to our present activities and long-range projects depends not on personal post-mortem survival but on the expectation that human life, culture, and the conditions for meaning will continue after us. To keep the poem from becoming a familiar meditation on legacy, frame the dependency as an intergenerational credit structure: present work borrows its significance against a future that may never be called upon to honor the debt, and the conjecture is the implicit assumption that the debt will be collected by successors. Let one refrain articulate this borrowing—something to the effect that we are spending meaning we have not yet secured—and let the other articulate the default, that the future holds no guarantee to settle our accounts. The form should do the philosophical work: through the tercets the refrains must recur in altered contexts, showing how every present project is shadowed by its contingent continuation, and in the closing quatrain the two lines must appear together without resolving which condition is prior, so that the reader sees the conjecture and its withdrawal as structurally interlocked rather than sequential. Use concrete, non-catastrophic imagery—archival work left uncatalogued, instruments calibrated for observers who will never arrive, languages taught to students not yet born—rather than apocalyptic scenes. A philosopher familiar with Scheffler's argument should recognize the specific claim that collective posterity underwrites present value; a general reader should feel the formal logic of borrowed continuity. Do not include bibliographic references, citations, or publication dates within the poem, and do not resolve the tension with a sermon about intervention or acceptance.
