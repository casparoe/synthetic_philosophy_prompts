You are preparing for a seminar defense of your interpretation of David Hume's essay on suicide. Your thesis is that Hume's argument should be read not as a proto-utilitarian defense of self-destruction, but as an exercise in applying his theory of calm passions and the distinction between artificial and natural virtues to a reflexive case in which the agent is simultaneously subject and object of moral judgment; specifically, you claim that Hume's suppression of the essay reflects an internal philosophical tension between his naturalism and the normative authority of general rules when applied to oneself, rather than mere prudential fear of religious backlash. The audience is a seminar of eight to ten scholars of early modern philosophy who specialize in Hume's moral psychology and the history of ethics, none working in decision theory or contemporary applied ethics. They are deeply familiar with the Treatise of Human Nature and the Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding, skeptical of anachronistic existentialist or libertarian framing, and inclined to defend the standard view that Hume withheld publication for theological and social reasons. They value close textual attention to Hume's discussions of sympathy, custom, and the mechanics of belief, and they will press you on whether your reading imports conceptual machinery from the Treatise that Hume deliberately suspended in the essay.

Draft your Q&A prep sheet. List the six or seven objections you will actually face, ordered from the most damaging to the least damaging, each phrased in the vocabulary and conceptual framework this audience uses—appeals to textual economy, the scope of sympathy, the status of artificial virtues, and the boundary between explanatory and normative claims in Hume's science of human nature. For each objection, provide: (a) a reply you can deliver in roughly two minutes, (b) an explicit note on where that reply is weakest or concedes ground, and (c) the likely interlocutor (for example, a historian of Scottish philosophy versus a moral psychologist). Identify the one objection you are hoping nobody raises, explain why it is lethal to your framing, and sketch a brief defensive move you could attempt if it does come up. Include a short sketch of your core argument so the objections can target it precisely. Do not ask for bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; the audience expects argument from the texts themselves, and the prep should assume no access to external sources during the session. Keep the tone formal and suited to a philosophy seminar defense.
