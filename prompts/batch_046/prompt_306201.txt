Analyze the My Lai massacre of March 1968 as a case study in the debate between situational and cultural-character explanations of perpetrator behavior, using the frameworks associated with Browning and Goldhagen. Set out the salient facts: soldiers of Charlie Company killed hundreds of unarmed Vietnamese civilians in a few hours; only helicopter pilot Hugh Thompson and his crew actively intervened; most participants had no prior violent records and were not ideologically committed to killing civilians. First, apply the situational framework—peer pressure, incremental authorization, dehumanization of targets, and deference to command authority—to explain widespread participation and the rarity of refusal. Second, apply the cultural-character framework, which emphasizes deep-seated, voluntary ideological hatred, and identify precisely where it strains: the absence of a pre-existing American eliminationist culture directed at Vietnamese villagers, and the rapid reversibility of behavior once the immediate situation changed. Conclude by assessing what the episode shows about the portability of each explanation beyond its original context, which account better explains the variance between participants and refusers, and whether the case functions as an anomaly or a confirmation for either view.
