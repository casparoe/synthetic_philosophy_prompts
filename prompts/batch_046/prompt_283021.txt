For our seminar on the philosophy of mind, please write a short story in continuous prose—no headings, bullet points, boldface, or tables—exploring Douglas Hofstadter’s idea that the self is a strange loop: a symbol, specifically the first-person pronoun I, that acquires its meaning not by pointing to a fixed substance but by referring back to the very pattern of cognition that produces it. To avoid the well-trodden ground of artificial intelligence narratives, imagine an isolated linguistic community that has formalized this insight into its grammar. In their language, I is not a simple indexical but a recursive symbol: every legitimate utterance of it must include a complete self-description, and if that description fails to close consistently, the speaker is provisionally denied grammatical personhood until the loop is repaired. Your protagonist should be a grammarian who, while archiving old texts, discovers an obscure clause in the community’s recursion charter—an exception allowing a speaker to maintain two mutually referring descriptions at once, neither of which resolves into a single voice. Through this discovery, the protagonist should confront the possibility that selfhood might not be one loop but an entangled pair that never collapses into unity. The story should stand entirely on its own conceptual feet; do not include bibliographies, citations, references to specific published works, or appeals to outside sources. Write it as polished literary fiction suitable for philosophical discussion, and present it as uninterrupted prose, as one might write in a letter.
