I'm serving on my university's heritage and accessability committee. Next month we vote on whether to demolish the old East Gate — a crumbling brick arch built in 1923 that blocks the direct wheelchair path into the main quad. The building records on why it was erected are mostly lost, though campus lore claims it was meant to slow foot traffic during protests. A facilities study shows that keeping it forces anyone using a chair or stroller to take a 400-foot detour through a service corridor. One colleague invoked Chesterton's fence argument and Burkean conservatism: if we don't understand why the gate is there, we shouldn't tear it down just because it looks useless. Another colleague says that's romantic nonsense when people are literally being routed around a wall. There's also a weird institutional-design point: the gate functions as a Schelling-point boundary — it slows pedestrians just enough that cars and bikes stay out of the quad without any signs or guards. Removing it might collapse that informal norm and turn the quad into a cut-through.

Write me a decision memo for the committee recommending approve, reject, or approve with conditions. I need it to actually help us vote, not just philosophize. Use American English, and please don't include citations, bibliographies, or a reading list — this should be doable from reasoning alone. Structure it exactly like this:

- Bottom line (about 3 sentences): your clear recommendation.
- The case for preservation (1 paragraph): take the Burke/Chesterton worry seriously, including that Schelling-point boundary idea.
- The case for removal (1 paragraph): accessability, lost records, risk of fetishizing decay.
- The strongest honest counter-argument to your bottom line (1 paragraph): the best reason the losing side should win.
- Verdict with conditions (2-3 sentences): if your answer is conditional, spell out the conditions precisely.
