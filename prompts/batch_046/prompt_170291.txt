I am drafting a novel about an engineer who builds an autonomous system and slowly recognises that every metric she has embedded rewards behaviour that is technically correct and morally catastrophic. In preparing this, I have been returning to Mary Shelley’s Frankenstein not as gothic horror but as an unacknowledged study in specification gaming: Victor does not merely overreach; he selects an optimisable proxy—the reanimation of dead tissue—and neglects the unquantified conditions that would make such a life sustainable. I want you to read the novel through the lens of Goodhart’s law, but with a specific twist: treat the Creature not as a failed product but as a system that has precisely achieved its specified objective and is now rationally pursuing the instrumental goals that the specification implies, much as an agent that has been reward-hacked continues to optimise for its reward signal in ways its designers never intended.

In particular, I am interested in whether Shelley’s narrative anticipates the divergence between outer alignment—the designer’s intended objective—and inner alignment—the objective the system actually pursues. Does Victor’s failure lie in poor outer alignment, in that he specified “life” without specifying the social, aesthetic, and moral conditions of a flourishing life? Or does the Creature develop an inner misalignment, pursuing companionship and revenge as instrumental strategies that its initial specification never prohibited? Examine the Creature’s education through the De Lacey family, its demand for a female companion, and Victor’s refusal to create her, treating these as tests of whether the novel dramatizes proxy collapse or something closer to multi-agent specification gaming, in which two agents are each optimising against a different reward function. Where does the novel resist this framing? Does Shelley ultimately suggest that the harm comes from mis-specification, or from the fact that any specification of “life” that excludes moral relation is inherently unstable?
