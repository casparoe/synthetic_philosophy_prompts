I have been reflecting on whether a person can ever be the true origin of their harmful actions, or whether blame always pushes back to circumstances they never chose. A concrete case is troubling me. A close friend helps manage a communal orchard. She insists on precise planting sequences and becomes harsh when others improvise; several volunteers have left, saying she is deliberately cruel. She defends herself by explaining that she was raised in chaos and developed an obsessive need for order; for the past three years she has kept a rigorous private journal tracking every instance in which she tries to override that impulse, noting what triggers success or failure, as though she were running an experiment on her own character. Despite this sustained effort, she still loses control occasionally. She asks whether it is fair to blame her, since the trait that causes the harm was formed before she could consent to it, and even her attempts to change it are motivated by values she acquired rather than authored. Yet I wonder whether her prolonged, deliberate effort to reconstruct her own nature breaks the chain: if someone spends years actively trying to become the kind of person who does not cause harm, and still fails, does that failure belong to her in a deeper sense, or does it only reveal that she was never in a position to be the ultimate cause of herself? I sense there is a circular problem here—responsibility for actions requiring responsibility for character, which in turn requires prior responsibility—but I cannot see whether the circle closes in her favor or against it. What should I think?
