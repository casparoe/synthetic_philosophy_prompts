As part of my doctoral research on embodied cognition and artificial symbol systems, I am evaluating how well existing and hypothetical solutions to the symbol grounding problem handle a deliberately engineered, non-obvious case. Please rank the following four accounts—(i) Harnad’s total-grounding proposal, which requires that every symbol be anchored in robotic sensorimotor capacities; (ii) the enactive/embodied approach associated with Varela and colleagues, which treats grounding as structural coupling rather than representational correspondence; (iii) an externalist-social model inspired by Putnam’s division of linguistic labor, in which an artificial agent’s symbol meanings are distributed across and fixed by a community of processors; and (iv) a hypothetical ‘thermodynamic-resonance’ framework, in which a neural network acquires a symbol for negative temperature solely through feedback loops involving physical work extraction in its own substrate, with no statistical correlation to sensory input, no language pretraining, and no pre-encoded symbolic rules—according to how well each resolves the following challenge: the system must reliably distinguish negative-temperature states from extremely low positive-temperature states using only gradient sensors, and it must maintain this discrimination without any linguistic or symbolic scaffolding. Present your answer as a tier list (e.g., S / A / B / C or 1 through 4), with one sentence per account explaining precisely which feature of the account succeeds or fails on the negative-temperature discrimination task. Then, in a brief concluding paragraph, identify which structural feature of the scenario—the absence of direct sensory correlation, the presence of substrate-level work feedback, or the removal of linguistic scaffolding—is doing the primary philosophical work, and state whether removing that feature collapses the problem back into a standard statistical-learning task that all four accounts could address with roughly equal success.
