In a philosophy of computation seminar, a dispute has arisen over whether worst-case intractability in welfare aggregation and long-horizon outcome prediction undermines consequentialist ethics at the conceptual level, or whether it merely imposes practical limits on implementation. One participant contends that if identifying the globally optimal action requires solving problems that are provably intractable — for instance, multi-agent preference aggregation with combinatorial complexity — then a normative principle of 'ought implies can', properly extended to epistemic-computational feasibility, rules out any obligation to maximise expected value in the global sense; she argues that this forces a reconceptualisation of moral obligation around polynomial-time approximability, yielding what she calls a 'feasible-set ontology' in which rightness is defined relative to computable heuristics rather than unreachable optima. A second participant responds that this mistakes the object of moral evaluation: consequentialism assesses states of affairs, not the mental or institutional algorithms by which agents navigate them, so intractability is an engineering constraint for artificial or collective decision systems, not a conceptual flaw in the theory itself; on his view, the normative standard remains the globally optimal distribution, and bounded rationality should be addressed through institutional division of labour rather than moral revision. I want to introduce a further distinction that neither has developed clearly — between first-order intractability, which concerns the difficulty of computing outcomes, and second-order intractability, which concerns the difficulty of computing which approximation strategy is itself best — and ask whether this distinction succeeds in mediating between them.

Please write an essay that weighs these positions and reaches a justified conclusion on whether computational intractability genuinely threatens the coherence of consequentialism or only its practical applicability. Your argument should engage with the claim that normative feasibility includes computational feasibility, the counter-claim that moral theories evaluate outcomes independently of selection algorithms, and the proposed first-order versus second-order distinction, assessing whether the latter collapses the difference between optimisation and satisficing. You should separate what is settled — such as the formal intractability of certain aggregation problems and the standard consequentialist framing that evaluates states of affairs — from what is genuinely contested among philosophers working at the intersection of ethics and complexity theory, particularly whether intractability carries normative weight or merely institutional significance. Do not present any contested interpretation, especially regarding the extension of 'ought implies can' to computational bounds, as though it were the consensus position.
