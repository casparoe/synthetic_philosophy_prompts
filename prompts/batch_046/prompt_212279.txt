As a PhD student in philosophy of religion studying Kierkegaard's account of the press and the public, I have constructed an argument that seems to extend his principle beyond its intended scope and need help locating the precise failure. The argument runs as follows. First, for Kierkegaard the press generates the public, an abstract phantom in which no individual is genuinely present, and this absence of presence is what dissolves moral responsibility. Second, if the absence of individual presence in a representational medium eliminates responsibility, then any collective decision mediated by representation—including democratic voting, corporate board action, and academic peer review—must also eliminate responsibility for those involved. Third, democratic citizens voting through representatives are present only as an abstraction, structurally analogous to the newspaper-reading public. Fourth, therefore, on Kierkegaard's own principle, democratic citizens bear no moral responsibility for the governments they elect. Fifth, this consequence is plainly false, since citizens are routinely and correctly held accountable for collective political outcomes. Sixth, so the principle linking representational absence to the dissolution of responsibility must be false, which entails that Kierkegaard's critique of the press collapses entirely. Please respond with exactly five numbered items, each one or two sentences long and with no introduction or conclusion: identify the step you reject, explain why that step initially seemed valid in light of Kierkegaard's texts, state the cost to his broader social philosophy of rejecting it, propose the least damaging alternative point of failure, and indicate what survives of his press critique if that step is removed.
