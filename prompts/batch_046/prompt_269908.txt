I run a small practice working with researchers and engineers who describe themselves as chronically failing to execute multi-month projects despite sincerely endorsing them, and I need a reusable diagnostic procedure I can administer in a single ninety-minute session without psychometric instruments or neuropsych testing. The framework must distinguish three failure modes that look identical from the outside but require radically different interventions: classical akrasia, in which the agent acts against an all-things-considered judgment they actually hold at the moment of choice; hyperbolic discounting, in which the agent’s preference ordering itself reverses as an immediate reward draws near, producing intertemporal bargaining between present and future selves; and a third pattern I am calling attentional fragmentation, where the agent’s long-range evaluative ranking remains stable but their working memory for distant reasons decays faster than their working memory for immediate task cues, so that at the critical moment they never actually form the relevant judgment rather than forming it and overriding it. Design an ordered procedure—interview questions in sequence, proposed behavioral tests I can run in the room, and explicit decision rules that yield a classification or a stop—specifying what counts as sufficient evidence for each category, where the procedure must halt and hand the decision to values rather than facts, and where it is guaranteed to misfire. Apply it step-by-step to three cases: a physicist who abandons a grant proposal every evening to play chess and reports afterward that the chess was “clearly not worth it”; an engineer who switches from writing documentation to debugging a minor feature and reports that at the switch the documentation “suddenly seemed less important” despite unchanged long-range valuation; and a writer who misses deadlines, insists the work is vital, but describes the missed days as “blank,” with no recollection of deciding to do anything else. In one of these applications, the procedure should yield a classification that conflicts with the client’s own self-narrative; state exactly what that conflict is and what the procedure still cannot resolve.
