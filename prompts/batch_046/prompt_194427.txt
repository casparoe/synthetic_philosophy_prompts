I am preparing to close a graduate seminar on Renaissance political thought, and I want a poem that captures a specific interpretive tension we kept returning to in our discussions of Machiavelli’s The Prince, particularly the river metaphor in Chapter XXV. Rather than treating fortune and virtù as simple opposites—fortune as blind chance, virtù as human mastery—I want the poem to stage the ambiguity between two competing readings of the dike imagery: one anticipatory, in which virtù means constructing embankments in calm weather to forestall future floods, and one improvisational, in which virtù is the capacity to reshape one’s course only after the waters have already breached the banks. Write a sestina—six stanzas of six lines each, followed by a three-line envoi—using six end-words (for instance, river, dike, flood, hand, shape, time) that rotate through the prescribed sestina order. Let the repetition structurally enact the problem: as each word returns in a new position, its meaning should shift between predictive control and reactive adaptation, so that the envoi leaves the speaker suspended between having built the dike and having been rebuilt by the flood. The poem should be accurate enough that a reader familiar with Machiavelli’s text recognizes the interpretive dispute rather than merely the vocabulary, and it should avoid resolving the tension into either triumph or resignation.
