I'm currently in a graduate seminar on formal epistemology, and after an intense discussion with friends about whether rational belief is nothing more than expected-accuracy maximization, I kept returning to an objection they raised: in high-stakes domains like medical diagnosis or climate forecasting, being wrong in one direction—say, a false negative—seems epistemically worse than being wrong in the opposite direction, even when the magnitude of error is held constant. This has led me to invite, for a student podcast I'm hosting, an unnamed philosopher who recently defends a modified accuracy-first framework. Instead of a symmetric linear scoring rule, she employs an anisotropic epistemic utility function that assigns different weights to directional errors—errors falling on one side of the truth versus the other—while also imposing a cross-world robustness condition: a belief counts as fully rational only if it remains accurate across a defined neighborhood of epistemically accessible worlds, not merely at the actual world. She insists this preserves the accuracy-first commitment without collapsing into practical decision theory, because the weights are justified by purely epistemic values—specifically, by a conception of cognitive resilience that treats unstable true beliefs as deficient in a distinctively epistemic way, not merely as practically risky.

Write me a sequence of eight to ten interview questions that go well beyond having her restate the framework. I want at least one question that presses on how she distinguishes her directional-loss weights from risk-averse practical reasoning smuggled into epistemology; anticipate her likely reply—that the weights derive from the intrinsic epistemic disvalue of fragile accuracy rather than from practical costs—and include the sharp follow-up that exposes whether she has an independent criterion for fragility. Another question should target the interaction between her robustness condition and the margin-for-error principle: if robustness requires stability across worlds, does she avoid sharp cutoffs in belief-status, or does she merely relocate them to the boundary of the neighborhood? Include the anticipated defense and then the follow-up that reveals the tension. I also want a question about how her view handles Bayesian updating when new evidence shifts not just the probability but the directional risk profile of a belief—does she need a non-standard update rule? Anticipate her answer and push on whether that rule is derivable from accuracy-maximization alone. End with a question about what kind of empirical or formal result could actually count against her view—something that wouldn't be dismissed with the reply that the objection merely marks an unfinished agenda rather than a genuine flaw. The questions should assume she is technically fluent but speaking to an educated non-specialist audience; avoid generic biography questions and do not ask for bibliographies, citation lists, or reading recommendations.
