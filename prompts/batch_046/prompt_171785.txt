I am developing a novel whose protagonist, in the wake of an intimate loss, attempts to inhabit a philosophical posture drawn from Zhuangzi rather than merely to invoke it as ornament. In surveying the conceptual landscape, I find that discussions of Zhuangzi’s view of death usually orbit three well-known nodes: the seasonal-transformation narrative anchored in the story of his drumming at his wife’s funeral; the radical equivalence of life and death advanced in the discussion of equalizing things; and the dissolution of fixed perspective suggested by the butterfly dream. These frames, however, seem to me to suppress a more troubling tension that I want the narrative to actually inhabit rather than resolve.

Specifically, there is a conflict between conceiving death as a kind of skillful, unresisted transition—analogous to Cook Ding moving through the ox with perfect attunement, encountering no nodes of resistance—and recognizing that death entails the complete extinction of the very viewpoint from which that attunement could be experienced. If the self that transforms is not continuous with the self that dies, then the consolation of transformation risks collapsing into a form of cosmic indifference that renders personal grief conceptually illegitimate. Conversely, if one insists on continuity, one may smuggle in precisely the metaphysical substantialism that Zhuangzi’s philosophy of transformation is meant to dissolve. I am less interested in scholarly taxonomies than in knowing which of these open interpretive problems genuinely restructure the others.

Given this, I ask you to prioritize the following lines of inquiry for a novelist who has no access to specialized archives and must rely solely on the conceptual resources present in the core texts: first, whether the tension between skillful transition and the loss of experiencing perspective is a false problem imported from Western metaphysics of personal identity, or whether it represents a genuine aporia within Zhuangzi’s own framework; second, whether the practical posture of fasting the mind can accommodate the emotional authenticity that fiction demands, or whether grief must be treated as a failure of philosophical discipline; and third, whether the rhetoric of transformation should be read primarily as an aesthetic strategy for dissolving attachment rather than as a metaphysical claim about the nature of death. Rank these according to which, if pursued rigorously, would most fundamentally reshape a narrative attempt to live inside this philosophy, and defend the ordering by identifying the criteria—such as dependency among questions, tractability without external scholarship, and resistance to reduction—that justify placing one upstream of the rest.
