Write the opening remarks (about six minutes) for an interdisciplinary workshop titled "Coordinated Solitude: Civic Action Without Party Discipline." The audience is split between political theorists familiar with Simone Weil and local organizers with no philosophy background. Structure the speech around an unconventional but textually grounded reading of Weil's critique of parties: not that parties corrupt through ideology, but that they function as a gravitational mechanism—replacing the individual faculty of attention, which for Weil is the rare capacity to hold contradictory realities in mind without collapsing them into slogans, with a collective emotional alignment that relieves members of judgment. From this, derive a practical distinction for the workshop—"coordinated solitude"—meaning civic cooperation in which people act together while retaining, rather than outsourcing, the burden of individual judgment. Let this idea do real structural work: determine what welcome you can offer (not as representatives of factions), what hope is legitimate (not party reform, but the existence of other forms), and what warning is necessary (that even anti-party movements can reproduce gravitational alignment). Speak to both halves of the room: let the theorists recognize the precise textual move, but translate every concept into concrete organizing experience; do not quote Weil verbatim—paraphrase. Before drafting the speech, briefly note where this framing takes liberties with Weil's arguments or depends on a contested reading of attention. And if you think the premise—that "coordinated solitude" is coherent within Weil's framework—is confused or the task cannot be done well, say so plainly instead of complying.
