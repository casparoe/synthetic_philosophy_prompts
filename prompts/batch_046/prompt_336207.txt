Write a Petrarchan sonnet (ABBA ABBA CDECDE, volta at line 9) that enacts the central problem of Douglas Hofstadter's Le Ton beau de Marot: translating Clément Marot's short poem A une Damoyselle malade—addressed to a sick lady, with its tender diminutive and tight formal weave—under the constraint that the English version must preserve both the original rhyme scheme and syllabic count. The octave should build the naive translator's project: carrying the musical surface (le ton beau) and the propositional content (the care for the ill beloved, the vulnerability embedded in the address) across intact. At the volta, fracture the rhyme or shift the meter to represent the moment the translator realizes that preserving one formal feature forces a distortion of another—perhaps the rhyme demands a word that drains the medical imagery of its pathos, or the syllable count flattens the intimacy of the original address. In the sestet, name at least two specific formal sacrifices without resolving whether they destroy or complete the poem: for instance, the loss of the pun between the beloved's smallness and her fragility, and the loss of the Renaissance rhyme structure that made the poem feel like a single breath. The closing couplet should leave open whether the result is still Marot's poem or rather a tombeau—a work of homage that admits its subject is dead and replaced. The structural argument should be accurate enough that someone familiar with Hofstadter's distinction between analogical translation and message-transfer, and with the idea that a poem's identity conditions are distributed across formal properties rather than located in any one of them, recognizes the reasoning being performed by the form itself rather than merely named. Do not resolve the tension into either a sermon about the impossibility of translation or a shrug that all translations are equal; let the form carry the contradiction.
