Consider the following argument, advanced by a study partner who thinks it shows that deontological ethics collapses into practical paralysis. (1) Deontology holds that there is a stringent agent-centered constraint against violating rights, and that this constraint prohibits not only intentional violations but also actions in which the agent knowingly initiates causal chains that carry a non-negligible risk of eventually producing a rights-violation. (2) Any ordinary action—walking to work, purchasing food, speaking in public—initiates causal chains with non-zero probabilities of eventually resulting in harm that violates someone's rights, whether through supply-chain exploitation, unforeseen accidents, or the indirect funding of unjust institutions. (3) If an agent performs such an action without taking every available measure to drive that remote risk precisely to zero, then the agent is culpably risking a rights-violation. (4) But for any finite agent, driving all such remote risks to zero is epistemically and physically impossible; there will always be unknown unknowns and unavoidable side effects. (5) Therefore, every action a finite agent takes involves culpable risk of rights-violation. (6) Hence, under deontology, every action is morally prohibited. (7) A moral theory that prohibits every action is self-defeating and must be abandoned.

The conclusion is that deontology is incoherent. I find the premises individually plausible, especially the idea that an agent-centered morality should care about what one sets in motion, not merely what one intends. But I cannot accept (6) or (7). Tell me which premise to reject and explain why it looked defensible. In particular, discuss whether (1) misstates deontology by conflating the doctrine of double effect—where merely foreseen harm is permissible if not intended and proportionate—with a blanket prohibition on risk; whether (3) illicitly suppresses a threshold of probability or proximity, shifting from "foreseeable" to "culpable" without justification; whether (4) overstates our epistemic limits in a way that would also undermine any consequentialist attempt to minimize harm; and whether saving (1) and (3) forces the deontologist into a version of the paradox of deontology—where preventing aggregate rights-violations sometimes requires allowing individual ones—such that the view becomes indistinguishable from a rule-consequentialist safeguard. I would most like to keep the agent-centered structure in (1) and the inevitability claim in (4), but I am willing to sacrifice (6) if the cost of preserving the other premises is lower.
