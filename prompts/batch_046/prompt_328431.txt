You are a senior heritage-policy analyst with training in archaeological epistemology, and you should think carefully before constructing the deliverable. First, briefly list the failure modes that make artifact-to-belief inference unreliable—functionalist overreach, semiotic overdetermination, and the slide from individual practice to collective institution—so that your procedure guards against them explicitly.

My office needs a reusable decision procedure for reviewing archaeological reports that claim an artifact assemblage licenses a specific institutional belief (for example, “this settlement maintained a formal inheritance court” or “this community practiced a state-mandated purification doctrine”). The reviewers are competent generalists, not philosophers; each review must finish in under thirty minutes; and the output must be one of three things: (a) a licensed inference with explicit confidence bounds, (b) a hard stop saying the pattern does not support the belief claim, or (c) a hand-off to a values board because the remaining gap is normative rather than evidential (e.g., whether to grant protected status despite interpretive uncertainty).

Translate the underlying principle—material patterns can justify institutional-belief claims only when they satisfy pattern stability, functional exclusion, and institutional counterfactual dependence—into an ordered sequence of concrete yes/no gates. At each gate, specify what counts as a sufficient answer, where the procedure stops with a “no,” and where judgment is ineliminable and must be flagged rather than hidden. Include a short “red-flag” checklist for reports that embed conclusions in descriptive language. Mark the points where the procedure will likely misfire even when run correctly.

Then apply the procedure gate-by-gate to two cases:

1. A storage complex yielding standardized weights, stamped seal impressions, and administrative tablets listing grain distributions; the report infers “a centralized taxation institution with written accountability.”
2. A cemetery with uniform east-facing burials and identical copper amulets; a developer’s consultant infers “a legally enforced eschatological doctrine requiring eastward interment” and seeks excavation clearance.

For each case, show the result at every gate, identify the point where the procedure produces an answer the reviewer will not like, and state what remains unsettled. Finally, state the procedure’s hard limits: which classes of belief claims it can never license regardless of evidence, and what inputs it requires that a small team without specialists may lack. Be decision-theoretically honest—do not dress value judgments up as factual gates.
