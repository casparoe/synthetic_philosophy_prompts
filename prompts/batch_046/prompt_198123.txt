I write a weekly science-and-ideas column and routinely have to quote, paraphrase, or headline texts—peer-reviewed papers, institutional reports, translated manifestos, interview transcripts—under a forty-five-minute deadline, with my editor required to sign off on the reasoning. I need a reusable decision procedure, drawn from the philosophy of language on authorial intention versus textual or institutional meaning, that tells me, in ordered steps, whether a given passage should be interpreted as anchored to what its originator meant or to what its public framing conveys when the two diverge. The procedure must be runnable by a journalist with no formal philosophy training, must flag the points where judgment cannot be eliminated, and must include clear stop conditions that tell me when to hand the decision to an editor rather than resolve it alone. Please construct it as a flowchart in words: the questions to ask in sequence, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure yields a definite instruction, and where it stops with an explicit “judgement call—do not proceed without editorial consultation”.

The procedure should be designed for high-frequency use—several decisions per week—with stakes that include factual accuracy, fair representation of individuals, and legal exposure if intent is misattributed in a belief report or headline. It must handle hard cases such as: collective authorship where one named signatory privately dissented; translations that insert modal qualifiers absent in the original; quotations embedded in belief reports (“Dr. X said that…”); and historical scientific texts whose terms have shifted meaning. Apply the procedure to three worked examples: (a) a translated philosophical passage in which the English version adds a normative “ought” not present in the original manuscript; (b) a government science report signed by a single principal investigator but written in the institutional “we”, where the journalist knows the signatory privately rejected the conclusion; and (c) a nineteenth-century biologist’s letter using a now-pejorative term that, in its original disciplinary context, carried a purely technical meaning. In one of these cases the procedure must deliver an answer I will dislike—specifically, that I must report the institutional or textual meaning and suppress the known private dissent or historical nuance in the headline, with an explanation of why the philosophy forces that result. Finally, state explicitly what the procedure cannot do: which categories of interpretive dispute lie outside its scope, and under what conditions a journalist should abandon it entirely and seek specialist scholarly input. Write in formal British English, avoid bibliographic recommendations, and do not depend on any citation or quotation I could not verify without web access.
