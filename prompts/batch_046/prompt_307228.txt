I spent yesterday arguing with a friend about whether Dennett's two-bitser argument actually damages Searle's distinction between original and derived intentionality. To defend Searle's side, she used the analogy that derived intentionality is like a photocopied contract: it can direct action and carry obligations, but only because an original signed document once existed. She then pressed it further, claiming that large language models are essentially libraries of photocopies with no original signer ever present — pure derived structure with no upstream source of original intentionality — which means Dennett's claim that syntax can become semantics through interpretation fails for them, since there is never an original meaning to be distributed across bits in the first place. Can you audit how far the photocopy analogy holds? I want a correspondence table — what corresponds to the original contract, the photocopier, the notary, the enforcement mechanism — followed by the points where the mapping breaks, particularly on whether repeated photocopying necessarily degrades meaning or whether new binding terms can emerge from the copies alone. Finally, tell me whether her conclusion — that LLMs sit permanently outside the reach of original intentionality and that Dennett's argument is therefore irrelevant to them — survives once the disanalogies are exposed, or whether the analogy was carrying the entire argument.
