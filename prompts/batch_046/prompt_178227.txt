I've been following a dispute in my city that feels like it touches on deeper principles, though I can't quite name them. There's an older quarter—the Old Quarter—that was incorporated into the municipality about sixty years ago through a state statute that didn't require a local referendum. The residents share a linguistic and cultural background distinct from the city's majority, and they have long maintained informal institutions—market cooperatives, religious councils, a local arbitration practice—that predate annexation. Recently, the municipal government approved a redevelopment plan that would demolish the quarter's historic commercial center to build a regional transit hub. In response, a neighborhood council issued a declaration arguing that the original incorporation lacked democratic authorization, that the community never genuinely consented to being governed by the city, and that they intend to establish an autonomous administrative district with authority over zoning, taxation, and cultural preservation within their boundaries. They frame this not as secession from the state, but as a restoration of prior self-governing status.

What makes their argument philosophically interesting is a specific claim they advance about the nature of consent itself: they argue that democratic authorization is not transitive across generations when the "basic structure" of the association has been fundamentally altered. Specifically, they contend that the shift from a self-governing rural market community to an urban district embedded within a majority-culture metropolis represents a change in the kind of collective they are, not merely a change in policy. Therefore, they say, even if their grandparents had implicitly consented to incorporation, that consent cannot bind descendants to an association whose character has been transformed without their approval. Opponents—both city officials and some quarter residents—reply that this view makes democratic association impossible, since every generation could demand renegotiation whenever values diverge; they argue that sixty years of shared infrastructure, tax contribution, and interdependence have created obligations of solidarity that supersede the historical lack of consent, and that the proper remedy is deeper representation within existing institutions rather than withdrawal.

I'm trying to understand whether this intergenerational non-transitivity claim has real moral weight, or whether it collapses into ordinary dissatisfaction with policy. Can you take a definite position: is the quarter's claim to autonomous district status morally justified, and specifically does their historical lack of consent—compounded by this argument about generational binding—provide a live reason for restructuring the association, or has it been superseded by subsequent participation and mutual dependence? Please address whether there is a principled distinction between ordinary disagreement and the kind of structural transformation they describe, and say what you think the fairest institutional resolution would be, with your reasons.
