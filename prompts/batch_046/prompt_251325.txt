Two researchers in my department have been arguing about a concrete case of higher-order defeat, and I am reporting it as a bystander seeking point-by-point adjudication rather than personal advice.

Dr. Maya Chen has completed a complex statistical analysis that, on first-order review, strongly supports conclusion C. She also possesses independent, externally anchored evidence—objective sleep logs and peer-reviewed literature on cognitive fatigue—that she has been severely sleep-deprived during the final week of analysis. She takes this as higher-order evidence that the reasoning process producing her belief in C is unreliable, and therefore lowers her confidence in C accordingly.

Leo, her colleague, objects that she should not reduce her first-order credence in C on this basis. He argues that if she discounts her analytical judgment because she is fatigued, she must also discount the second-order judgment that her fatigue is severe and that it specifically undermines this kind of statistical reasoning—since those assessments were formed by the same tired cognition. Applying the discount consistently either produces a destabilizing regress or collapses her beliefs altogether. Hence, Leo says, the fatigue concern should motivate external verification but cannot rationally function as an internal epistemic defeater.

Maya responds that Leo conflates the source of the higher-order evidence with the reasoning being evaluated. The sleep data and general scientific findings about fatigue do not depend on her current analytical reasoning, so she can treat her fatigue as a localized defeater for the model-checking process without undermining the evidential basis for the defeater itself. She adds that if Leo were correct, no expert could ever rationally correct for known bias.

Leo counters that even if the fatigue data are externally anchored, the particular inference that this specific level of fatigue undermines her judgment about this specific model is itself a substantive first-order judgment formed under the same conditions. He further warns that if professionals routinely apply such discounts, high-stakes fields would suffer from perpetual indecision, since some higher-order concern is always available.

Who is right about the logical structure of the defeat? Is Leo’s regress objection sound against Maya’s position? And what, if anything, are both missing about how epistemic akrasia should be handled in professional practice?
