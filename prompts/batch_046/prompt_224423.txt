I'm trying to work out what I actually think about rigid designators and necessary a posteriori identities, going by my gut on cases. So: water is H2O necessarily, and in a world with XYZ in the lakes people just don't have water. That feels right. Gödel is rigid, so in a world where Schmidt stole the proof Gödel still exists but didn't do it. Fine. But here's one I'm wobbly on: suppose there's a world where H2O never existed at all, but there's a liquid with all the superficial water-properties that locals call 'water'. Does 'water' fail to refer there, or does it pick out the XYZ? I lean toward failure, but I'm not sure. Then there's heat: I think 'heat' should be rigid too, so 'heat is mean molecular motion' is necessary a posteriori. But imagine we discover heat is actually something else in every actual case, while in a counterfactual world it's molecular motion. Is the term still anchored to our actual stuff? I'm torn. For names introduced via desription: Neptune was named via perturbation descriptions, but if in another world Leverrier never calculated and the planet was found differently, I still think 'Neptune' refers to our planet, not whatever fits the desription there. So descriptions don't fix reference. But then take fictional names — if in some world a real detective exactly matches Doyle's desription of Holmes, I don't think Holmes refers to him. Is that because there's no actual referent to rigidify onto, or is the mechanism totally different? I can't tell. Is there one principle that captures all these verdicts about natural kinds, proper names, and fictional terms, and tells me when an identity is necessary? Or do I need to split the account? And whatever principle you land on, what does it predict for a case I haven't listed — say scientists coin 'florpium' for element 117, but it turns out they were always misdetecting element 118, and in another world the misidentification never happens but 117 exists with different properties. Where does the name point?
