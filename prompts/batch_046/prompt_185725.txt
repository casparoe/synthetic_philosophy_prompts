Retired physicist here, only got serious about philosophy after I left the lab, so please bear with me if I'm using the wrong words. I keep noticing a strange move in debates about subjective experience - the "what it's like" stuff, qualia, whatever you want to call it. Someone insists there's this special inner property, like the hurt of a headache or the redness of red, and they defend it by saying you have direct, unmediated access to it, you can't be wrong about your own state, it's immediately present. Then if you ask why that means it's not just a brain state, they switch and say well it's private, other minds can't observe it, so science can never reach it. But then when you point out that my spleen is private too and nobody thinks that's non-physical, they slide right back to the immediacy claim as if it already answered the reductionism question. It's not plain equivocation because they aren't just changing word meanings; they're rotating between two defenses that each sound okay alone but point in opposite directions - one about perfect transparency to the subject, one about essential hiddenness from everyone else. I read somewhere there's an argument that the very concept of qualia is incoherent because it tries to be both immediately known and essentially inaccessible, which sounds like exactly this tension. So is there an established name for this specific defensive cycling in the philosophy of mind literature? Or if not, does the fact that people keep rotating between immediacy and privacy without noticing the contradiction actually support that incoherence view, and where exactly is the boundary between this pattern and just being sloppy with arguments? I don't want a bibliography, just a clear carving of what the phenomenon is and isn't, written in continuous prose please - no headings or bullet points, just like an email.
