I'm trying to clarify my own position on the ethics of voting by testing whether my case judgments form a coherent pattern. Here are my verdicts, which seem mostly sensible but contain at least one hard tension.

First, in a safe district where the outcome is predetermined by a large margin, I think a well-informed citizen has no stringent duty to vote and may abstain without fault. Second, in that same district, if a voter is largely ignorant of the candidates and would be choosing essentially at random, I think it is wrong for them to vote—better to withhold an uninformed contribution than to inject noise into the collective decision.

Third, when a decent but deeply flawed candidate faces a genuinely dangerous opponent, I feel there is a positive duty to vote for the flawed candidate, even if one strongly rejects parts of their platform; this is the lesser-evil obligation. Fourth, however, if the voting system requires a public endorsement—say, an open ballot with mandatory attribution—I think voting for that same flawed candidate becomes wrong, because one's vote expresses approval one does not hold.

Fifth, I think casting a symbolic vote for a candidate with no chance of winning—writing in a protest figure—is permissible and can have expressive value, provided the race is not close. Sixth, but if the election is within a single percentage point, I think that same symbolic vote is morally equivalent to supporting the dangerous opponent, and therefore wrong.

Seventh, I distinguish sharply between abstaining out of principled rejection of all available options and abstaining out of pure apathy; only the latter strikes me as a failure of civic duty. Eighth, nevertheless, I think a principled abstainer in a genuinely close race still bears some moral responsibility if the worst candidate prevails, which pulls against the idea that principled rejection fully excuses non-participation.

Is there a single principle—perhaps combining expected causal impact, expressive integrity, and epistemic responsibility—that captures all eight judgments? If not, which verdicts must be overturned to reach coherence, and why? In particular, address the tension between the lesser-evil duty in the third case and the expressive prohibition in the fourth, as well as the conflict between the excuse in the seventh and the responsibility in the eighth. Finally, apply the principle you defend to a case I have not mentioned: a voter in a proportional-representation system who can split her ballot, giving part of her vote to a small values-based party with no governing chance and part to a larger defensive party. What should she do, and does your answer depend on whether the race for the largest bloc is close?
