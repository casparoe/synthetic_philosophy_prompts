I am working through a problem in the metaphysics of science for an advanced seminar, and I find myself stuck on how ontology should be fixed when the information needed to distinguish competing categories is permanently beyond our epistemic reach. I want to compare two approaches to this problem. On one side is a Quinean strategy: ontology is determined by regimenting our best scientific theories and identifying what the bound variables of our formalized quantifiers must range over, with the idea that permanently inaccessible distinctions—those that make no difference to any observable or theoretically derivable phenomenon—simply fall out of the ontology because they do not affect theory choice governed by structural simplicity and predictive power. On the other side is a view I have been developing informally, which I will call counterfactual-access ontology: an entity belongs in our ontology only if it remains invariant across all counterfactual scenarios in which our epistemic access is maximally expanded yet still bounded by physical possibility, meaning that ontology tracks modal stability under improved-but-finite knowledge rather than tracking only the quantificational commitments of our current best theories.

To make the comparison concrete, consider a hypothetical case drawn from particle physics: suppose there are two putative fundamental entities, A-ions and B-ions, that are identical in every interaction, decay pattern, and measurable property accessible under our actual physical laws, and that differ only in a property that would manifest solely under a cosmological constant far outside any physically realizable range for observers like us. How would the Quinean strategy and the counterfactual-access ontology respectively adjudicate whether both, one, or neither should be included in our ontology? Which criterion offers the more defensible response to genuinely inaccessible information, and where does each view risk either ontological profligacy or undue elimination?
