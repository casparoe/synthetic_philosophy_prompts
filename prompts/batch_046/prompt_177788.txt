In debates about whether biography can teach universal morality, defenders of Plutarch-style parallel lives often say the pairing works like a natural experiment in moral psychology: you hold the domain of achievement roughly constant—both figures are generals, statesmen, or philosophers—while varying cultural and personal background, so you can observe which virtues are portable and which are culture-bound. The conclusion they want is that biography lets us isolate character from context and therefore teaches universal moral principles rather than local norms.

Audit this analogy: build the correspondence table—what plays the role of the control condition, the treatment variable, the observation protocol, the possibility of replication? Then point out where the mapping breaks. Does Plutarch actually hold achievement constant, or does he select for exceptional success and omit failure? What about selection bias, the absence of negative cases, and the fact that the biographer shapes the data rather than recording it neutrally? Identify which features of scientific experimentation—value-neutrality, replicability, falsifiability—are quietly imported by the analogy but absent from hagiography and biography, and say which of these absences actually matter for the conclusion that biography reveals universal virtue. Does the claim survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or was the analogy carrying the argument all along? If the natural-experiment framing collapses, propose a more accurate analogy for what Plutarch's pairing actually accomplishes.
