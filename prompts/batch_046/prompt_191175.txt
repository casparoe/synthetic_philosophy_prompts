My philosophy of religion professor's rulings on belief-ethics cases this term: she permitted belief in God when the option is live and transforms moral life despite inconclusive arguments; permitted a physician's belief in an unproven treatment when suspension means the patient's death; forbade belief in a conspiracy theory supported only by explanatory satisfaction; forbade a grieving parent's belief in an afterlife purely for emotional comfort with no evidential basis; permitted a researcher's tentative acceptance of an anomalous evolutionary hypothesis before replication; and permitted a juror's guilty verdict on technically sufficient but barely adequate evidence. Next week's case is a climate activist who lacks the statistical expertise to evaluate climate models personally, yet adopts strong belief in imminent catastrophic warming specifically because she judges this belief necessary to motivate collective preventive action, explicitly noting that while experts provide evidence, she herself cannot assess it. Predict her verdict, say whether she is implicitly running a Jamesian live-forced-momentous framework, a Cliffordian evidentialism with an unstated practical exemption, or a moralized epistemology that licenses belief when it serves justice, and specify the further case that would distinguish these rules.
