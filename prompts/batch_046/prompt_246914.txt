I am a graduate student in a seminar on aesthetics and moral philosophy—specifically a course on the ethics of representation—preparing to defend a thesis for our midterm presentation. My thesis is that historical fiction and biopics incur a representational obligation to the real dead that is not reducible to avoiding harm to survivors, respecting descendants, or preserving factual accuracy; I call this obligation dramaturgical debt. It arises when a dramatization substitutes a closed thematic arc—catharsis, moral redemption, or ironic comeuppance—for the necessarily unfinished narrative structure of a life that ended without such resolution, especially when the dead figure is used primarily as a vehicle for the audience's emotional needs rather than acknowledged as a subject whose story remains irreducibly open. I am not claiming the dead have posthumous welfare interests or that all fictionalization is wrong; I am arguing that a specific mode of narrative closure can violate a structural fidelity owed to the figure represented, independent of whether anyone living is offended.

The audience will be the seminar itself: twelve participants including two Kantian moral philosophers who are skeptical of obligations without corresponding rights-holders and who worry about smuggling metaphysics into ethics; one historian of film who defends artistic license and insists that all historical reconstruction involves selection and emplotment; four aesthetics students who are inclined toward an Epicurean line—namely, that death extinguishes the subject and therefore eliminates the possibility of being wronged, so any "debt" must be metaphorical or reducible to duties to the living; and one analytically trained philosopher who will press on whether "owing" requires a recipient with standing. They know the standard literature on harm to the dead and on artistic freedom, but they have not encountered my specific framing of dramaturgical debt or narrative residue.
