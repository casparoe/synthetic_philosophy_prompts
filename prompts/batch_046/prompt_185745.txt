For my independent study in philosophy of religion, I want to test whether Buddhist anatta (the doctrine of no-self) can explain sudden, catastrophic changes in personality, or whether it was designed only for gradual contemplative transformation. Analyze the well-known 19th-century case of Phineas Gage—the railroad foreman who survived a penetrating frontal-lobe injury from an iron rod, remaining biologically alive but with documented alterations in temperament, social judgment, and emotional regulation—through the specific framework of the five skandhas (rupa, vedana, sanna, sankhara, vinnana) and the explicit rejection of a permanent substrate (atman). First, map each aggregate onto Gage’s pre- and post-injury condition: identify which skandhas show continuity and which show rupture, and argue whether the disruption of sankhara (mental formations and volition) and the altered stream of vinnana (consciousness) constitute, in Buddhist terms, the death of one person and the emergence of another, or merely a severely transformed bundle with no owner. Second, explain what anatta predicts about identity across trauma—particularly its denial of an enduring self that could be “damaged” rather than replaced—and assess whether Gage’s survival supports that prediction, or whether the case is an anomaly because Buddhist psychology traditionally addresses incremental mental change rather than abrupt neurological restructuring. Third, identify one feature of the episode that the skandha model illuminates more clearly than standard Western substance-based or narrative-identity accounts (for example, the absence of a core “owner” of the changed behavior), and one feature it misdescribes or leaves silent (such as social attribution of moral responsibility to a biologically continuous agent). Finally, state whether this case strengthens anatta as a general account of human identity, or whether it reveals that the framework’s explanatory power is confined to the contemplative contexts for which it was originally developed. Be disciplined about the mapping: do not force the theory to speak where it is silent, and do not invent historical details beyond what is commonly known about the accident and its documented behavioral effects.
