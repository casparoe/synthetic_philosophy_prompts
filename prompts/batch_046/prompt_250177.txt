I have been returning to the problem Douglas Hofstadter examines in Le Ton beau de Marot (1997): translating Clément Marot's brief Renaissance poem A une Damoyselle malade—known to Hofstadter as Ma mignonne—into English under the strict requirement that rhyme, meter, and stanzaic architecture survive intact. The original is addressed to a sick young woman, and its effect depends on a particular marriage of tender diminutives and formal elegance. In practice, any English version that honors the rhyme scheme and syllabic count must replace almost every lexical item with something that fits the acoustic frame, often shifting imagery, register, or emotional temperature.

This raises a conceptual question I would like to have unpacked intuitively but precisely. If we model the poem's formal structure as a kind of canonical frame—a fixed boundary-condition set in a high-dimensional space of possible utterances—then a translation that preserves that frame but substitutes nearly all vocabulary looks analogous to a canonical transformation in physics: the coordinates change entirely, yet the symplectic structure (here, the acoustic and rhythmic relations) is conserved. On this view, the poem's identity might lie not in its specific words but in the trajectory it traces through that space. But if the poem is instead a specific coupling of semantic content and sound—if the diminutives and medical metaphors are not ornament but the actual substance—then preserving the frame while discarding the vocabulary produces not a translation but a structurally isomorphic new poem, like a different point that happens to lie on the same constraint surface.

Can you explain, without recourse to technical proof, where the identity of such a poem actually resides? Is formal preservation a sufficient condition for translation, or does it merely act as a compression algorithm that forces semantic drift so extreme that the result is better classified as re-creation? I am particularly interested in whether there is a coherent intermediate position—perhaps one that treats the rhyme scheme and meter not as carriers of content themselves, but as boundary conditions that restrict the translator to a specific region of meaning-space, within which multiple semantically distinct but structurally equivalent poems can exist. How would that intermediate view distinguish a faithful translation from an elegant forgery?
