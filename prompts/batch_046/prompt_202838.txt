I am preparing a feature on conceptual analysis that collapses under its own weight, and I wish to pressure-test an argument I have sketched concerning Wittgenstein's notion of family resemblance. The reasoning runs as follows. (1) Wittgenstein demonstrates that "game" possesses no common essence; its instances are linked solely by family resemblance. (2) If family resemblance governs all general terms, then "language" itself lacks any essential feature shared by every instance. (3) Consequently, "language game" also lacks an essential feature; there is no single property that all language games share. (4) Wittgenstein's central claim—that meaning is use within a language game—purports to state something universal about every language game. (5) But if (3) holds, then no such universal claim can be genuinely essential; at best it describes a family resemblance among particular uses. (6) Therefore Wittgenstein's thesis is either false (there is an essential feature after all) or self-undermining (it asserts an essentialist claim that its own framework prohibits). (7) By the same token, every philosophical thesis that employs general concepts is rendered self-undermining, which proves far too much. I should like to know which step to abandon. I am inclined to preserve (1) and (4), but I want to understand precisely what surrendering (3), (5), or (6) costs—whether it forces a return to essentialism, into a deflationary quietism, or into accepting that philosophical assertions are merely grammatical reminders lacking universal scope. Could you diagnose where the argument slips, explain why the rejected step initially seemed plausible, and price the alternatives? Please respond in British English.
