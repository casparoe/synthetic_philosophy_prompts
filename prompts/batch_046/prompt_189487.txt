In preparing the reading guide for our graduate seminar on contractualist moral philosophy, I came across a colleague's summary of T.M. Scanlon's framework, particularly as it bears on what is often called the aggregation problem, and I find myself uncertain which parts are faithful and which reflect a subtle slide into aggregative reasoning. The summary states that Scanlon holds an act wrong if it violates a principle that no affected party could reasonably reject, where the assessment of reasonable rejection is conducted by comparing the burden that one individual bears under the principle against the cumulative benefits that the principle produces for all other affected parties taken together. From this characterization, the summary infers that when a large number of people each stand to gain a small benefit, their collective reasons can outweigh one person's substantial burden, so that the harmed individual cannot reasonably reject the principle that permits the harm. The summary then concludes that Scanlon's well-known resistance to aggregation is therefore not structurally grounded in the architecture of contractualism but is rather a rhetorical preference, and that the view ultimately collapses into a form of prioritarian weighting in which the severity of individual harm is balanced against the number and size of distributed benefits.

This gloss strikes me as suspicious, especially in its claim that contractualist justification aggregates individual benefits into a collective counterweight against a single complaint, yet I want to be precise about the errors before I correct my students. Could you audit this summary in detail? Specifically, I would like to know: first, whether Scanlon's criterion of reasonable rejection is indeed assessed by weighing one person's burden against the summed benefits to others, or whether the structure of justification is fundamentally pairwise and non-aggregative; second, whether the summary's inference about large numbers of small benefits correctly captures Scanlon's treatment of cases involving distributed harms and benefits, or whether it misrepresents the role of separate, independent complaints; third, whether the charge that Scanlon's anti-aggregation stance is merely rhetorical survives once the contractualist framework is properly understood; and fourth, what exactly distinguishes contractualist reasoning about aggregation from prioritarian or utilitarian aggregation, particularly regarding whether benefits to many can ever combine to justify a serious burden on one. I am not asking for a bibliography or a list of sources, but rather for a conceptual clarification that will help me distinguish faithful exposition from mischaracterization in the seminar room.
