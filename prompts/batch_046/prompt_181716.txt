In my metaphysics and philosophy of physics course, a visiting lecturer summarized Thomson's lamp argument and its implications for supertasks. She described the familiar setup in which a lamp is toggled on and off at intervals that halve successively—one minute, thirty seconds, fifteen seconds, and so on—so that infinitely many toggles are compressed into a two-minute span. At the two-minute limit, she claimed, the lamp occupies a contradictory state: it must be on, because the toggle immediately preceding that instant switched it on, and it must simultaneously be off, because the toggle immediately preceding that switched it off. She took this not merely as a puzzle about one artifact, but as proof that no physical system with continuous state functions can undergo an actual infinite sequence of discrete changes in finite time, since every such system must have a well-defined state at every real-numbered instant. She then argued that this result dissolves Zeno's dichotomy paradox, because Zeno assumes that a runner can complete infinitely many successive half-distances, and Thomson's lamp shows that completing an actual infinity of successive steps is metaphysically impossible. I find the lamp puzzling, but I am unsure whether her reasoning is sound. Which parts of her summary correctly capture Thomson's concern about determinacy at a limit point? Where does she overreach by turning a local indeterminacy into a general impossibility proof for all physical supertasks? And does her appeal to Zeno hold up—does the dichotomy paradox depend on the claim that an infinite sequence of actions can be completed, or does it rest on a different premise about the structure of successive addition and divisibility?
