State your credence that strong moral particularism is correct: specifically, that moral reasons are holistic in that their contribution to a situation's moral status depends on the presence or absence of other reasons in a non-additive way, that consequently there are no true moral principles with counterfactual force — not even defeasible ones — and that moral judgment is therefore best understood as the exercise of a perceptual capacity for salience rather than the application of rules. Decompose your answer: (1) the probability that moral reasons are genuinely holistic rather than merely complex; (2) given holism, the probability that all general moral principles are false or empty; (3) the probability that moral knowledge is perceptual rather than inferential; and any other factor you include, each with a number between 0 and 1 and a brief justification. Combine them into an overall credence. Then identify which component you are least confident about, and describe what specific evidence — for instance, a pattern of cross-cultural moral convergence or a finding about moral learning in children — would shift that component the most. Finally, say where a moral generalist's decomposition would most likely diverge from yours.
