I teach an upper-level philosophy of action seminar and I'm writing the final exam — 45 minutes, about 800 words, worth 40 marks. I want one essay question on weakness of will that students can't answer by just reciting Aristotle's taxonomy of incontinence and then describing hyperbolic discount curves. Instead I want them to wrestle with what I'm calling the 'continuity problem': on the hyperbolic model, preference reversal isn't a discrete event where you suddenly 'give in'; it's just the discount curve crossing as the reward gets closer. But Aristotle treats akrasia as a specific failure where practical knowledge is present but gets dragged around by appetite at a particular moment. So there's a real tension: the discounting model gives you one continuous preference structure that shifts with proximity, while Aristotle gives you two parts (rational and appetitive) that conflict at a specific point.

The question should ask students to defend one of three positions: keep the Aristotelian two-part framework and map appetite onto steep local discounting; drop the two-part framework entirely and treat akrasia as an illusion caused by comparing distant and near rewards with different weights; or synthesize them somehow. Make them use the distinction between global life-plans and local temptations, and make them address whether there's still a phenomenological 'giving in' if the shift is continuous.

Please write the exam question, a model answer of about 600 words that actually takes a position — maybe that a synthesis works only if we reconceive the 'knower' not as a seperate faculty but as a meta-preference that operates over long timescales, though this still leaves a gap for the feeling of being pulled — and a marking scheme. The scheme should separate an A (explicitly frames the continuity problem, distinguishes global vs local, addresses the phenomenological gap, takes a clear position) from a B (accurately describes both theories but doesn't see the tension) and from a C (just summarizes Aristotle and hyperbolic discounting without engaging the problem).

Don't ask for citations or a bibliography — students won't have access to search and I don't want them guessing at paper titles. Keep it casual; a typo or two is totally fine. And please don't lean on obscure named theories — the idea should come from the course concepts.
