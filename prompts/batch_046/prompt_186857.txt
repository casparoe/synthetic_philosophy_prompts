I come to philosophy of mind from a long career in experimental physics, and I find the standard software-hardware analogy for mental causation too loose to be useful. A more disciplined comparison, I think, is with thermodynamic macro-causation: temperature and pressure seem to drive macroscopic dynamics—a piston, a heat engine—even though every interaction is ultimately between molecules governed by conservative forces. Some philosophers invoke this to defend non-reductive physicalism, treating mental properties as real, causally efficacious macro-features that do not compete with their neural substrates any more than temperature competes with molecular motion. I want a rigorous audit of this analogy in the context of the causal exclusion argument. Please provide an explicit correspondence table: mental state to temperature, neural realization to molecular kinetic energy, behavioral effect to piston displacement, and physical closure to conservation of energy-momentum. Then identify precisely where the mapping fractures: does mental causation require downward constraint that thermodynamic properties do not? How does intentional content map onto statistical aggregates? Does the analogy implicitly assume that mental properties are purely descriptive conveniences, and if so, does that undermine rather than save mental causation? Finally, assess whether the analogy can support a coherent middle position—genuine mental efficacy without violating the causal closure of physics—or whether, once the disanalogies are fully exposed, one must choose between eliminativism and an emergentism that abandons closure. I am not asking for a literature survey; I want the conceptual engineering done in the mapping itself.
