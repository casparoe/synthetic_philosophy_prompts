At a recent seminar on historical epistemology, two scholars—Maya and Leo—argued over whether source criticism permits knowledge of events before widespread literacy. Maya held that Thucydides set the correct standard: only events within living memory, verified by cross-examined eyewitnesses, yield historical knowledge, so Herodotus’ narratives of the Persian Wars remain conjectural, resting on oral transmission that degrades exponentially with each testimonial link. Leo replied that this imports a modern, individualist epistemology that mischaracterizes oral societies. He argued that Herodotus was not following a linear chain of hearsay but drawing on collectively maintained traditions—competing versions preserved across different poleis—which function more like a distributed error-correction system than a game of telephone. Maya countered that collective preservation supplies no independent check: a community can stabilize false traditions, and Thucydides’ explicit skepticism about the Trojan War demonstrates he recognized precisely this danger. Leo answered that Thucydides targeted poetic embellishment rather than all oral tradition, and that archaeological confirmation of Persian administrative structures in Herodotus shows these traditions were not pure fiction. Maya conceded the peripheral confirmations but insisted they do not validate central narrative elements—battle sequences, speeches, strategic decisions—which remain materially unverifiable. Leo then pressed the analogy: we accept much twentieth-century history through documentary chains we have not personally verified; why treat orally transmitted material so differently? Maya responded that material documents permit cross-reference and preservation in ways ephemeral oral performances never do, so for the deep past we should remain agnostic about specific narratives, not merely cautious.

A bystander who heard the exchange asks you to adjudicate it point by point: who is right about the model of oral transmission, about the target of Thucydides’ skepticism, about what archaeological corroboration can and cannot establish, and about the analogy with modern documentary history? Say who wins on each step, who is more right overall, and identify what—if anything—both parties are missing about how source criticism actually operates from Herodotus through contemporary practice.
