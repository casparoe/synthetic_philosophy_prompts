Here's an argument my study partner came up with about Buridan's ass and I'm not sure where it slips. (1) If two options have exactly equal value, there's no reason to prefer one. (2) If there's no reason to prefer one, then there's also no reason to choose either over not-choosing at all. (3) So in a perfect tie, rationality is completely silent. (4) But real agents break ties all the time, usually with an arbitary rule or coin flip. (5) If using an arbitary rule still counts as rational, then rational choice doesn't really need reasons — any mechanism will do. (6) Apply this to moral choices: saving five lives vs saving five plus a negligble extra is technically tied at some precision level. (7) So moral reasoning always collapses into arbitrary tie-breaking, unless we accept that rational agents literally can't choose in tied cases. (8) Therefore either we drop rational choice in ethics, or we accept preference laundering — delegating the tie to an internal sub-routine — which just pushes the arbitrariness back one level and makes rationality circular. Where does this go wrong? I'd like to keep (1) and (5) if I can, but I want to know what I'm paying.
