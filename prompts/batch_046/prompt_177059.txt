I cover science and ideas for a regional publication, and I am trying to work through a conflict I encountered while reporting on a cooperative research collective—one that mirrors tensions I see in other shared endeavors. The group has no formal contracts or legal agreements; over years, members have contributed field notes, translations, and oral histories to a shared repository under an implicit understanding that any significant use of the material requires collective deliberation. The reason is not conventional ownership but a shared belief that individual contributions are meant to be woven into a collective narrative that no single person authored. Recently, one longtime member compiled several years of shared notes into a personal monograph, arguing that since she performed the actual writing and synthesis, norms of individual expression and personal authorship should allow her to publish independently; the rest of the collective feels this severs the moral fabric that made collaboration meaningful, treating interdependent labor as if it were atomistic production. I find both intuitions compelling—her claim to self-direction sounds weighty, yet the group’s sense of betrayal also feels morally substantial rather than merely sentimental. Is there a principled way to adjudicate this without assuming that either individual autonomy or communal loyalty must automatically prevail? In particular, I want to understand whether the harm here is best conceived as a failure of distributive fairness, as a disregard for the way the community constituted the very capacities that made her authorship possible, or as a different kind of wrong altogether. Please indicate what argument or consideration would lead you to change this assessment—for instance, evidence that the collective’s norms were coercively imposed rather than genuinely shared, or proof that her synthesis introduced genuinely independent value that severs the moral link to the source material—and identify where your reasoning is most likely to fail.
