In a faculty seminar on institutional reform, a senior colleague blocked a proposal to replace our university's adversarial peer-review protocol with an open collaborative model by invoking only Chesterton's fence: don't tear down a fence until you know why it was put up. He seemed to mean that the old protocol embodies accumulated practical wisdom we may lack the vocabulary to articulate, and that dismantling it without that understanding risks destroying hidden value. I want to bring a precise disambiguation to the next meeting rather than just trading intuitions.

Please lay out, as a clearly separated menu, the distinct precise theses that Chesterton's fence could be expressing in this context. I want at least: (1) an epistemic-humility claim about the limits of reformers' knowledge of institutional functions; (2) an evolutionary or functionalist claim that long-surviving practices have been implicitly selected for benefits not visible to participants; (3) a normative claim about where the burden of proof lies when altering shared institutions; (4) a metaphysical or traditionalist claim that the past carries authority independent of our ability to justify it; and (5) any other philosophically substantive reading that is not merely rhetorical. For each thesis, say whether it is a platitude, whether it is defensible within a Burkean conservative framework, whether it is false or question-begging, and — crucially — whether that specific reading actually supports blocking reform until the reformers can articulate the original purpose.

Then tell me which of these readings is most likely the one being invoked when people use the slogan to halt an institutional change argument, and whether a consistent Burkean position can be maintained without relying on the false or question-begging readings. Finally, please say explicitly what would change your mind: which argument about institutional design, the epistemology of social practices, or the history of peer review would lead you to weight these readings differently, and where in your reasoning you think you are most likely to have gone wrong.
