I've been thinking a lot about the way history is chopped up into the Middle Ages, the Renaissance and the Scientific Revolution, and I've started to feel these are less like seasons and more like chapter headings that later writers invented to tidy up the past. I want to share this with a small group of interested adults at our local library — people who watch history programmes but have never studied the subject formally. Most will arrive assuming these periods are as real as the Bronze Age, and some will be unsettled by the idea that historians 'made them up'.

Please do not explain the concept to me directly; instead, plan how I should lead them through it in one session. I need the plan structured as follows, with each section roughly the length indicated.

The dependency chain (about 250 words): What is the very first distinction or idea that must be in place before anyone can even ask whether a period is 'real'? Lay out the order in which the pieces have to come, explaining why each depends on the previous one. You should build in a comparative moment — for instance, showing how the same labels do not fit Japanese history, where scholars talk of the Kamakura and Muromachi periods rather than a Japanese Renaissance — but only to expose the geographical anchoring of the categories, not to turn the session into a world-history survey.

The carrying case (about 200 words): Choose one concrete, self-contained comparison that can carry the whole argument. It should involve the year 1453, the fall of Constantinople, viewed from the perspective of a Venetian merchant, a Ming bureaucrat, and an Italian humanist. Say exactly what this case must demonstrate and what it must avoid; it must not require knowing specialist historiography, and it must not rely on dramatic fictional details.

Misconceptions and deflections (about 300 words): Map the wrong turns in the order they are likely to arise: first, that 'invented' means 'false'; second, that if boundaries are fuzzy then nothing changed; third, that rejecting European labels means ignoring real cultural differences. For each, specify the point in your sequence where you would intervene, and the exact distinction or phrase you would use to head it off.

The understanding check (about 100 words): Design one brief question or exercise at the end that reveals whether they have grasped the difference between 'a useful narrative frame' and 'a natural kind', rather than just repeating that historians are biased.

What I leave out (about 100 words): State openly which part of the debate you would refuse to enter — for example, specific scholarly disputes about whether a Scientific Revolution can be said to have occurred in Song China or the Islamic Golden Age — and say exactly how you would frame that refusal so it does not sound patronising.

Write in British English. Do not include a reading list, bibliography, or citations. Keep the language accessible but precise.
