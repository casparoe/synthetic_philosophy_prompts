Following my father's recent diagnosis with Alzheimer's disease, I attended a family-care seminar at our hospital in which the consulting ethicist offered the following summary of the philosophical debate over advance directives for patients with advanced dementia: "The standard account, derived from Dworkin's theory of precedent autonomy, holds that an advance directive functions like a constitutional amendment made by the competent self: it binds the later, demented self because personal identity is preserved through psychological continuity, giving the earlier self a property-like authority over future bodily states. The directive should therefore be treated as absolute unless one can demonstrate that dementia has destroyed the metaphysical person, which is what critics like Dresser and Buchanan actually claim. Their objection is not about welfare but about identity: they think the patient after dementia is literally a different subject, so honoring the old document is like enforcing a contract on a stranger. Consequently, the clinical default should be strict adherence to the written directive, with any deviation requiring proof of discontinuous identity." I am trying to understand whether this captures the actual philosophical landscape, or whether it collapses distinctions I should understand before participating in decisions about my father's care. Please audit this summary carefully: which sentences reflect defensible positions, which conflate claims that the literature keeps apart—particularly regarding personal identity, the nature of the critics' objections, and the source of the directive's authority—and which overstate its legal or moral weight? Finally, does the speaker's conclusion that deviation requires proof of metaphysical discontinuity survive once those corrections are made?
