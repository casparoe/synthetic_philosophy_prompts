I recently encountered Donald Davidson's theory of radical interpretation in an independent study on philosophy of language, and I have become persuaded that interpreting another person's speech and mental states requires assuming they are largely rational and that their beliefs are mostly true—the principle of charity. Before I fully commit to this view, I want to understand exactly what could defeat it.

To test the framework's limits, I have constructed a hypothetical scenario I call the "Gradient Consensus" problem: imagine a distributed environmental-monitoring system composed of many local nodes that do not hold discrete propositional beliefs but instead continuously adjust a shared probabilistic field. The system tracks real-world changes with high accuracy, yet from any individual node's perspective its adjustments appear systematically irrational, violating consistency conditions that Davidson treats as prerequisites for interpretation. If Davidson's account applies only to agents with discrete, rational beliefs, then this system falls outside its scope—but if the framework is meant to cover all genuinely meaningful interaction, then either it must be stretched to accommodate apparently irrational distributed cognition, or it must exclude cases that seem interpretively significant.

What observations, arguments, or conceptual results would genuinely count against Davidson's radical interpretation? I am not asking only for the standard objections about circularity or incommensurability, but for the evidence or cases—real or hypothetical, including ones structurally like Gradient Consensus—that should move a defender of the view. Which of these defeaters would Davidson's own supporters concede as damaging, and which would they dismiss as misapplications of the framework? Please distinguish between fatal objections and merely awkward ones, rank them by how decisive they would be, and assess whether the principle of charity has been qualified so heavily that it risks unfalsifiability. If it has been insulated in that way, is that a serious defect for a philosophical account of interpretation, or a legitimate feature of a transcendental condition?
