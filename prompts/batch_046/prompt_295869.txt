I have recently begun working in a clinical-policy setting where rising adult-diagnosis rates for attention and dissociative-spectrum conditions are being interpreted either as epidemiological discovery or as instances of diagnostic feedback reshaping the phenomena it describes. This debate is no longer abstract for me. I am asking you to draft the joint statement that two researchers — call them the Looping Skeptic and the Taxonomic Realist — could both endorse after a sustained adversarial collaboration on psychiatric classification and Hacking's account of looping effects.

The Looping Skeptic holds that psychiatric categories are interactive kinds: once a classification is available, people alter their self-understanding and symptom presentation in response, and this loop is especially pronounced for conditions like dissociative identity disorder or adult ADHD, where phenomenology is highly plastic and culturally mediated. They maintain that such loops undermine the claim that the category tracks an independent dysfunction.

The Taxonomic Realist accepts that classification can influence experience but denies that this dissolves validity. They observe that awareness of diabetes or hypertension also changes patient behavior without rendering those categories fictitious, and they insist that looping can be distinguished from discovery whenever the underlying causal structure remains stable across feedback.

Please produce the following, without bibliographies, reading lists, or citations to specific works:

1. A numbered section of agreed claims — about what looping formally involves; about which historical classifications both sides agree exhibited strong feedback; about the difference between psychiatric and somatic categories in terms of symptom accessibility; and about the empirical patterns, such as rising prevalence following media attention, that both acknowledge require explanation.

2. A disagreements table stating the precise points of divergence in wording each side would accept for its own view. Identify at least three cruxes. I suspect one concerns whether the temporal order of classification and symptom-clustering proves causation or mere correlation; a second concerns whether a loop's origin in clinical language versus biological mechanism matters for ontological status; and a third concerns the hypothetical case of a proposed category, chronic digital dissociation, defined partly through behaviors that patients had not previously distinguished from ordinary distraction. For this third crux, state whether preemptive clinical framing before any epidemiological baseline exists counts as destructive looping.

3. For each crux, specify a concrete study design or thought experiment that both sides agree would bear on it, with each side's prediction recorded in advance. You may construct a plausible hypothetical; do not invoke specific real-world datasets by proper name unless they are common knowledge.

Keep the language precise and formal. Do not place phrases in quotation marks that derive from specific published texts; paraphrase freely instead. The document should read as something both parties could sign, with the residual disagreement made exact rather than rhetorical.
