You are grading a take-home exam for an upper-division course in contemporary political philosophy. The prompt given to students was: "In John Rawls's theory of justice, explain the relationship between the veil of ignorance and the difference principle. Specifically, does the veil of ignorance by itself entail the difference principle? What role does the maximin rule play in the justification? And how does the difference principle relate to Rawls's other principles of justice—equal basic liberties and fair equality of opportunity?"

It is genuinely mixed: it correctly identifies the informational purpose of the veil, the worst-case reasoning of maximin, and the content of the difference principle, but it also contains subtle errors—specifically, a conflation between the veil’s informational function and the decision-theoretic function of maximin, and an imprecision about the scope of the difference principle relative to the other two principles and their lexical ordering. For each major assertion in the student’s response, indicate whether it is accurate, partially accurate but imprecise, or mistaken, and state the correction with conceptual precision. Student answer:

"Behind Rawls's veil of ignorance, parties lack knowledge of their social position, natural talents, and conception of the good. This informational constraint eliminates bias, but it also creates radical uncertainty about one's future standing. Rational parties resolve this uncertainty by applying the maximin rule: they evaluate each possible social arrangement by its worst outcome for them and select the arrangement with the best worst case. Once this decision rule is adopted, the difference principle follows directly—the principle that permits social and economic inequalities only if they work to the greatest benefit of the least advantaged. In other words, the veil of ignorance entails the difference principle given risk-averse rationality.

The other two principles—equal basic liberties and fair equality of opportunity—are also chosen behind the veil, but they serve mainly as side constraints on the distributive scheme. Because the difference principle governs the allocation of all primary goods other than basic liberties, it carries out the central distributive work in the theory. Critics sometimes mistake maximin for a form of utilitarianism, but Rawls is explicit that maximin is a rule for choice under uncertainty in the original position, not an everyday decision procedure. Taken together, the veil ensures impartiality and the difference principle expresses the fair terms of cooperation that result."
