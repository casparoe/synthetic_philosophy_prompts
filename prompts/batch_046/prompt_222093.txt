I'm working through Dennett's heterophenomenological method for an upper-level philosophy of mind course, and I've constructed an argument that seems to push the method into self-defeat. The steps are these:

(1) Heterophenomenology directs us to treat every first-person report about conscious experience—not as direct disclosure of an inner phenomenal reality, but as a text that constitutes a heterophenomenological world, the world as that subject takes it to be.

(2) The method denies anyone privileged introspective access that could verify whether these reports match actual phenomenal states; science can study only the reports themselves and their patterns.

(3) Apply the method reflexively. The heterophenomenologist herself makes judgments—this subject is confabulating, that pattern supports a multiple-drafts model, there is no Cartesian theater—which are first-person cognitive events occurring in her own mental life.

(4) By (1) and (2), her own judgments are not windows onto the true structure of consciousness, but merely further elements of her own heterophenomenological world.

(5) So the conclusions of heterophenomenology are not objective scientific discoveries about consciousness, but only descriptions of the scientist's own heterophenomenological world.

(6) Yet the method is presented precisely as an objective, third-person framework that avoids solipsism and yields genuine knowledge about other minds.

(7) Therefore heterophenomenology either denies its own objectivity, or it must exempt the scientist's judgments from (1), granting her the special access it denies everyone else.

(8) If it grants that exemption, it collapses back into the first-person privilege it was designed to eliminate.

If I must choose, I want to preserve (1) and (6)—the anti-privilege core and the scientific ambition—since abandoning either seems to destroy the project entirely. But then I need to know exactly where the slide happens between (3) and (5). Is there an equivocation between a subject's report of her experience and a scientist's theoretical inference about reports? Or is the argument valid, and the real cost is accepting that heterophenomenology can never claim objectivity about consciousness?

Please take a committed position: identify the specific step that fails or the premise that must be sacrificed, explain why that step looked unavoidable, and price the alternatives—especially what saving (1) and (6) actually costs. Don't just lay out options; say which one you endorse and why.
