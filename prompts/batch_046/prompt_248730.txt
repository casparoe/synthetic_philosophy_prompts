Here's an argument about societal collapse that I now find suspicious because it seems to prove that every major transformation is a collapse. (1) Define collapse as the cessation of a society's central record-keeping, monumental-building, and state-level institutional practices, coupled with sharp demographic decline. (2) By that definition, the Classic Maya lowland polities (c. 800–900 CE), the Late Bronze Age palace economies, and Easter Island's moai-building culture all collapsed. (3) But if losing those specific institutional forms counts as societal failure, then the transition from Roman imperial administration to localized post-Roman polities, or from feudal monarchies to modern bureaucratic states, must also be collapses followed by births of new societies. (4) So either "collapse" is coextensive with "major institutional change," rendering it redundant, or we are implicitly identifying "the society" with the particular power structures—palaces, temples, archives—that left durable traces, and treating their disappearance as the death of the people themselves, even when languages, rituals, and trade networks persist. (5) Yet I want to preserve the intuition that the Maya, Bronze Age, and Easter Island cases represent genuinely severe disruptions, not mere rebranding. Where is the error? Is it in treating institutional form as an identity-condition for society, in a hidden modal slide from "this state ended" to "this society failed," or in smuggling a normative preference for centralized, monument-building complexity? Please diagnose the weakest step, explain why it looked plausible, and price the cost of giving it up. In doing so, separate what is broadly settled in the archaeological and historical literature from what is genuinely contested—especially regarding whether Easter Island represents ecological catastrophe or adaptive reorganization—and do not present any contested interpretation as the consensus.
