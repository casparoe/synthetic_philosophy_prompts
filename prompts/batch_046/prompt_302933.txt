In our philosophy of epistemology reading group we have spent the better part of a term working through Wittgenstein's On Certainty, and the discussion has settled into a loop that feels increasingly structural rather than textual. One faction maintains that hinge propositions—the certainty that the earth has existed for many years, that I have hands, or that 2 + 2 = 4—are not beliefs or knowledge-claims at all, but rather the immovable scaffolding, like the bed of a river or the axis of rotation, that makes doubt and justification possible; the opposing faction, drawing on the same passages about world-pictures and inherited frameworks, treats these hinges as exceptionally well-entrenched empirical convictions that sit at the foundation of our belief-system and can, in principle, be revised or overturned when a scientific revolution or a radical change of life-form displaces them. Both sides agree that Wittgenstein rejects Cartesian scepticism, both cite the riverbed metaphor, and yet after months no argument on either side has shifted anyone's view. I am inclined to think the deadlock is not merely verbal—though 'belief', 'knowledge', and 'certainty' are clearly being pulled in different directions—but rather that one side is asking a logical or grammatical question about the conditions under which a language-game functions, while the other is asking a psychological or historical question about how human beings actually hold fast to world-pictures and what might cause them to let go.

Are we therefore facing a substantive disagreement about the nature of epistemic entitlement, a disguised dispute about standards of evidence in intellectual history, or two distinct inquiries—one normative-structural, one descriptive—operating under the single heading of hinge epistemology? Are there hidden empirical commitments about cognitive architecture or about the dynamics of conceptual change doing silent work? And if the diagnosis is that the sides are answering different questions, or that the very framing of 'hinge' as a uniform category obscures important distinctions among grammatical rules, hardened empirical generalisations, and biographical certainties, what kind of conceptual distinction—not further textual exegesis—would actually allow progress? Please reply in British English, with a diagnosis supported by what each side actually says rather than a survey of interpretations.
