I hold a reconstructed Epicurean position—call it baseline-neutral truncation—about whether death is bad for the one who dies. The standard Epicurean claim is that death is not harmful because there is no subject present to suffer. Baseline-neutral truncation goes further: it holds that a life’s welfare is fully determined by its actual occurrent and retrospectively accessible experiential content, so potential future goods never enter the subject’s welfare baseline in the first place. On this view, truncation does not deprive anyone of anything they were already entitled to; there is no counterfactual comparison against a baseline that includes unrealized future experiences, and therefore death cannot be deprivationally harmful even in principle. This makes the view stronger and more exposed than ordinary Epicureanism, because it must deny not merely experienced harm but any structural or narrative harm that depends on comparing a completed life against a longer counterfactual one.
