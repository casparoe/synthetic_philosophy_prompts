For a philosophy-of-science workshop on theories that appear to outstrip direct empirical access, write a decision tree that sorts participants by their actual commitments regarding the confirmation of string-theoretic landscape claims and multiverse hypotheses. The tree should begin from a single starting question—whether a physical theory can remain scientifically credible if it makes no predictions that are in principle distinguishable from its rivals by any feasible observation—and then branch through four further nodes. Each node must be phrased as a concrete claim or commitment that a participant can accept or reject without knowing the philosophical label it corresponds to; the nodes should address: (i) whether mathematical consistency, uniqueness of formal derivation, or internal coherence can serve as standalone evidential conditions in the absence of novel predictive success; (ii) whether anthropic reasoning within a vast parameter space counts as a legitimate explanatory strategy or as a methodological surrender; (iii) whether convergence across independent theoretical formalisms on the same low-energy structure can substitute for direct observational access; and (iv) whether empirical inaccessibility undermines scientific status per se, or only practical utility and technological applicability.

The leaves should locate at least the following positions: strict empirical rejectionism (the view that in-principle untestability disqualifies a theory); landscape instrumentalism (treating the multiverse framework as a calculational tool without ontological commitment); structural realist pluralism (accepting that some theoretical structures are confirmed by cross-formal robustness despite observational inaccessibility); anthropic explanatory acceptance (treating selection effects as genuine explanations); and at least one unnamed or hybrid combination that the logical space permits but that the tree predicts should be unstable or currently unoccupied. For each named leaf, include a one-sentence diagnosis of its most awkward commitment—e.g., that it must regard successful predictions elsewhere in the theory as fortuitous correlations, or that it collapses the distinction between physical explanation and statistical selection.

Finally, explicitly flag any pair of leaves that the tree cannot genuinely separate—meaning they share all branch answers but differ only in rhetorical framing or emphasis rather than in substantive commitment. The instrument should sort people by what they believe about confirmation under empirical deprivation, not by which label they prefer, and should avoid asking for bibliographies, citation lists, or references to specific papers. Keep the vocabulary conceptual rather than technical, accessible to someone with a general grasp of physics and philosophy but without access to external sources.
