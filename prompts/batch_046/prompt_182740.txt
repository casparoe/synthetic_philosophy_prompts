As a retired experimental physicist who has recently turned to philosophy of science, I wish to plan a single ninety-minute seminar for science graduate students on Imre Lakatos's methodology of scientific research programmes. Do not explain Lakatos to me; instead, lay out the teaching plan. First, state the dependency order in which the hard core, protective belt, and the distinction between progressive and degenerating problem shifts must be introduced, so that students do not mistake the belt for mere ad hoc immunization. Second, propose one carrying example from the history of physics or chemistry—explicitly not the familiar Newton-to-Einstein trajectory—that exhibits both a progressive and a degenerating shift within one programme. Third, identify the three misconceptions scientifically trained students are most likely to arrive with—likely including that Lakatos is merely Popper with extra steps, that the protective belt is unfalsifiable by construction, and that degenerating equals empirically false—and specify the exact point in the sequence where each should be defused. Fourth, formulate one closing question that tests whether they have understood the normative, not merely descriptive, dimension of the methodology. Fifth, state what you would deliberately omit from this session and why. The plan should be rigorous yet accessible; do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations, and avoid invoking specialized post-1970 debates that depend on literature the students will not have encountered.
