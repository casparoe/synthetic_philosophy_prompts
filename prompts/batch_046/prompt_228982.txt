A friend has walked me into this argument and I cannot see the exit. (1) The backward-induction resolution of the centipede game requires only common knowledge of rationality, not any particular payoff structure: at each node, a rational player compares the utility of stopping immediately with the utility of continuing one round and then stopping. (2) If the immediate payoff exceeds the discounted continuation payoff, rationality demands stopping; induction then pushes this reasoning back to the first node. (3) This logic applies to any sequential decision with a definite endpoint and with common knowledge of rationality—so apply it to a finite cooperative scheme, such as a three-month collective project in which each participant may contribute or exit at the start of any week. (4) At the final week, a rational agent exits if costs exceed benefits. (5) Knowing this, at week eleven the agent anticipates no further cooperation, so she should exit at week eleven; and so on, back to week one. (6) Hence backward induction proves that rational agents should never enter finite cooperative ventures with nonzero exit costs—yet we regularly do enter them, and we do not regard ourselves as systematically irrational. (7) My friend concludes that the premises of backward induction must be abandoned wholesale, but he admits that rejecting (1) undermines the standard game-theoretic account of common-knowledge rationality. Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it commit me to?
