I've been convening a small interdisciplinary reading group on the political economy of hunger, and we have reached an impasse that feels less like a live disagreement and more like a ritual repetition of fixed positions. We began with Amartya Sen's entitlement analysis of the Bengal famine of 1943—where available rice actually exceeded levels of prior years yet entitlement collapses killed millions—then turned to the Irish famine of the 1840s and the Great Leap Forward. By now everyone in the room accepts the same empirical contours: in Bengal it was not food availability decline but wartime procurement, inflation, and labor-market disruptions destroying purchasing power; in Ireland a biological blight triggered the crisis but exports, relief structures, and policy priorities shaped the mortality curve; in China meteorological anomalies contributed, yet the catastrophic scale was driven by procurement targets, statistical misreporting, and institutional incentives. The split is between those who classify these events as "political crimes" and those who insist they are "natural disasters with severe political dimensions." For over a year the crime camp has pointed to avoidable mortality and institutional choice, while the disaster camp has pointed to biological or meteorological triggers that no state fully controls, and neither side has moved an inch. Is this disagreement verbal—hinging on whether "crime" requires malicious intent, or on what causal weight a contributing factor must carry to become the defining cause? Is there a hidden empirical question about whether institutional design can be treated as a form of agency? Or are the two camps answering different normative questions—one about moral responsibility, the other about the appropriate causal explanation—while using the same three cases as ammunition? Given your diagnosis, what would actually count as progress: a formal distinction between structural causation and criminal negligence, a model of institutional causation that assigns precise weights, or an admission that the dispute tracks bedrock intuitions about whether mass suffering without a malicious agent can still be named a crime?
