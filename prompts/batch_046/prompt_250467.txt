I've mostly bought the idea that using LLMs for reasoning doesnt actually erode human understanding — it's structurally no different from relying on a telescope or a skilled assistant, and all the 'erosion' talk just confuses cognitive offloading with real epistemic harm. The specific risk I'm worried about is something I'd call epistemic mirroring: the model's training distribution becomes invisible scaffolding, so you think you're reasoning on your own when you're actually reproducing a hidden statistical consensus. What would actually count against this overall view? I dont want generic 'people get dumber' studies — I want defeaters aimed at the telescope analogy and at the mirroring claim specifically: which results in philosophy of science about instruments versus agents, which empirical patterns of overreliance, and which internal tensions in saying 'it's just a tool' when the tool generates genuinely novel propositions? Rank them fatal to awkward, and be honest about whether the position has been qualified into unfalsifiability — and whether that insulation is itself a problem for a claim of this kind.
