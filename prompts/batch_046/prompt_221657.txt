I am a doctoral student in philosophy serving as a rotating research-integrity referee for my university's interdisciplinary publication board. Every month we must adjudicate two or three disputes about whether a contested empirical claim—drawn from anthropology, psychology, or history—counts as pramā (knowledge) or remains in saṃśaya (doubt) or viparyaya (error). The board members are not trained philosophers, so I need a reusable decision procedure, grounded in the Nyāya theory of the four pramāṇas (pratyakṣa, anumāna, upamāna, śabda), that I can run in about thirty minutes and present as a signed recommendation to the board chair. The procedure must include: (1) an ordered sequence of tests to identify which pramāṇa primarily supports the claim; (2) for each pramāṇa, the specific conditions of prāmāṇya (validity) that must be met—distinguishing nirvikalpaka (indeterminate) from savikalpaka (determinate) perception; requiring a valid pakṣa, hetu, and invariable vyāpti for anumāna; demanding that upamāna rest on an essential rather than accidental similarity; and verifying the speaker's domain-specific competence and āpta-status for śabda; (3) a conflict-resolution flowchart in words for cases where pramāṇas disagree, with explicit rules (for instance, direct perception overriding testimony when both satisfy their conditions); (4) three worked applications—one involving an unformed perceptual report that remains nirvikalpaka, one involving an analogy that collapses because the shared feature is not causally relevant to the inferred property, and one where authoritative testimony must be rejected because the speaker, though generally reliable, lacks competence in this specific domain; (5) clear markers of irreducible judgment, especially in assessing whether a resemblance is essential and whether an authority is genuinely āpta here; and (6) an honest statement of what the procedure cannot do, particularly regarding modern probabilistic or statistical arguments that do not map cleanly onto classical anumāna syllogisms. Do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; build the procedure directly from the conceptual architecture of Nyāya epistemology itself.
