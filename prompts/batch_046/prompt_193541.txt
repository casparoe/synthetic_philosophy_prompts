I am drafting a novel in which a sculptor deliberately destroys her own marble figure to reclaim the raw stone, and I have been trying to understand whether the destroyed work and the reclaimed block could be considered, in any rigorous metaphysical sense, the same object. At a recent MFA workshop, a visiting lecturer offered a summary of what she called the standard "material constitution" view: the statue and the marble are numerically identical, but they possess different modal properties—if the statue were shattered it would cease to exist, whereas the marble would survive—so the apparent distinction between them is not ontological but descriptive, a matter of how we predicate properties across different contexts rather than of two things occupying one place. She then concluded that talk of "coincidence" is essentially a linguistic artifact, that there is only ever one object present, and that metaphysicians who insist on two distinct entities are merely confused about the flexibility of language. I have the impression that this slides too quickly from modal divergence to linguistic relativism, and that it suppresses the well-known alternative according to which the statue is constituted by the marble without being identical to it; yet I am unsure whether the lecturer's framing counts as a defensible popular simplification or as a genuine misrepresentation of the debate. Is this summary fair? Please separate what accurately captures the classical identity problem, what conflates constitution with mere redescription, and whether any serious metaphysician would defend the conclusion that spatial coincidence is "just linguistic." I want to use the concept precisely in my fiction, so I need to know where the philosophical ground actually lies rather than where a confident gloss places it.
