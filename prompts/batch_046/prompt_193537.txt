As a retired physicist now turning to philosophy, I am grading an undergraduate response to the following question: In Spinoza's Political Treatise, Spinoza argues that democracy is the most natural form of sovereignty; explain how he connects the collective power of the multitude to democratic stability, and discuss whether his exclusions of those not sui iuris rest on natural incapacity or on social dependence. The student wrote: Spinoza holds that the state's right equals its collective power, so democracy—where the multitude retains sovereignty—is the form closest to the state of nature, in which every individual has equal natural right. Because democratic citizens are guided by reason toward common utility, their judgments coincide and produce a unified general will that transcends private interest, making democracy uniquely stable and free of faction. Spinoza excludes women, servants, and children from the council, but this is purely a matter of social dependence: since they stand under the power of husbands or masters, they are not sui iuris. Should those social conditions disappear, nothing in nature would prevent their full inclusion. Thus democracy achieves absolute sovereignty without internal division, provided all participants reason correctly. Please respond with exactly five numbered items, each one or two sentences long, with no introduction or conclusion. The first item should state the grade you assign; the remaining four should each isolate one specific strength or one subtle error—such as a misattributed concept, a misread exclusion criterion, or an unwarranted inference—indicating in each case whether it touches the central argument or is peripheral.
