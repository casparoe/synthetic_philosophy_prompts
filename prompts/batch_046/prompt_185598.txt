I've been trying to map the implicit epistemology behind a curricular practice that seems widespread in honors programs and leadership-development institutes, and I'm struck by how philosophically loaded its design choices are. The practice assigns paired "exemplary lives"—third-person biographies of figures drawn from unrelated domains, such as a field naturalist and a civil-rights organizer, selected not because they influenced each other but because the syllabus claims they exhibit an identical "moral structure" like epistemic humility or resilience under institutional corruption. The syllabus explicitly excludes memoir, autobiography, and fictional narrative on the grounds that first-person or invented accounts "distort the formation process," and it treats rigorous historiography that complicates the virtue-narrative as pedagogically harmful. Students are then assessed through "character-response" essays asking not what the figure did, but what the student would have done in an analogized situation; meanwhile, abstract virtue-ethics texts are largely absent, treated as too thin to calibrate judgment.

What philosophical commitments about moral learning and virtue ontology does this institutional design presuppose? Does it require a Plutarchan view that comparative biography is the primary vehicle for moral knowledge, or is it compatible with a hagiographic tradition that uses narrative mainly for affective identification? Does the exclusion of autobiography presuppose that self-report is systematically unreliable for moral calibration—perhaps because of motivated reasoning—or does it rest on a stricter claim that virtue is only visible from an external, third-person stance? Is the cross-domain pairing committed to a form of moral realism in which virtues are trans-contextual natural kinds, or could a constructivist account license the same pairing by treating the syllabus itself as defining the virtue-category?

I'd also like to distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what merely fits well with it. Could the same curriculum survive if one replaced its implicit model with a "biographical surrogacy" framework, in which exemplars are not imitated but used as low-cost simulation environments—allowing the learner to project possible selves into narrated futures without bearing real-world failure costs? If that replacement were made, which design features—the exclusion of fiction, the preference for paired lives, the rejection of critical historiography—would have to change, and which could remain intact? And where is the practice philosophically underdetermined, such that several rival theories of moral learning would license it equally?
