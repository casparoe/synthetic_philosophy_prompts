A recent essay in a philosophy-of-science reader summarizes Otto Neurath's ISOTYPE project in the following way. Working with the graphic designer Gerd Arntz, Neurath developed a standardized system of black-and-white pictograms meant to function as a universal empirical language that could replace ordinary prose in scientific reporting. Because quantitative differences are represented by repeating identical icons rather than scaling their size, the symbols avoid visual distortion and are presented as direct, convention-free copies of observable social and natural facts—thereby circumventing both the ambiguity of natural language and the theory-ladenness that logical empiricists like Neurath himself attributed to protocol sentences. The essay claims that Neurath intended ISOTYPE not merely as an educational or museum display tool, but as a complete formal language capable of expressing every scientific proposition, including theoretical statements about unobservable entities, with the same rigor as symbolic logic but purged of metaphysical vocabulary. It also notes that Neurath banned color and directional arrows from the system, treating them as vehicles of emotional loading and metaphysical causation incompatible with physicalism. From this, the author concludes that ISOTYPE necessarily failed, since pictorial syntax cannot encode modal relations or support theoretical inference, proving that visual representation can never substitute for logical form in science. I suspect the summary overstates Neurath's ambitions and mischaracterizes his view of pictorial convention, but I am unsure exactly where it slips from simplification into error—especially regarding the relation between ISOTYPE and protocol sentences, the treatment of theoretical terms, and whether his exclusion of arrows and color really rested on the reasons given.
