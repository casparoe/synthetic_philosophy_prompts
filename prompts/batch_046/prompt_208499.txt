Question: A municipal energy board must choose between an 80-year geothermal infrastructure program and a shorter-term fossil-fuel expansion. Because the long project will alter migration, economic development, and family formation, the specific individuals residing in the region in 2100 under the geothermal plan will not be numerically identical to those who would reside there under the fossil-fuel plan. The board asks whether selecting the fossil-fuel option can be said to harm anyone, given that without that choice the affected future residents would never exist. Analyze whether this case exemplifies Derek Parfit’s nonidentity problem, distinguishing carefully between open-population effects and closed genealogical networks, and evaluate whether that problem undermines the board’s duty to protect future generations.

Student answer:
This is a classic open-population instance of Parfit’s nonidentity problem. The central difficulty is that identity-affecting choices change who exists: because the fossil-fuel trajectory produces different conditions of conception and social formation, there is no particular future person who exists in both outcomes and can therefore be said to suffer harm. One might suppose the problem vanishes in a closed genealogical network, where the same families persist regardless of policy, but in fact the challenge concerns numerical identity rather than relational continuity, so it applies with equal force even when family lines are preserved.

Parfit resolves the tension by adopting a strictly person-affecting restriction: an action is wrong only if it makes some identifiable individual worse off. Under this restriction, the board has no direct moral reason to prefer the geothermal plan, since no specific person is harmed by the fossil-fuel choice. The remaining consideration is aggregate welfare, yet this invites the repugnant conclusion—that a vast population of lives barely worth living might be preferable to a smaller, happier one. Given the implausibility of that conclusion, welfare maximization should be rejected, and the board should treat its obligations to the future as essentially prudential rather than moral. Although the geothermal plan yields lower aggregate emissions, this impersonal advantage cannot ground a duty to any particular future people. Consequently, the nonidentity problem does undermine intergenerational obligations here.
