I've been arguing with my flatmate about whether history is genuinely chaotic or just looks messy from the outside, and I've been trying to pin down my gut reactions to specific "what if" scenarios. I'm taking a course on philosophy of history and find the contingency literature a bit vague, so I'm testing my own tentative principle: a small change is historically decisive only when it disrupts a specific bottleneck that has no near substitutes — call these structural bottlenecks — and not when it's just altering the timing of an event that was going to happen through other channels anyway. But I'm not sure it holds up.

Here are my verdicts. If Franz Ferdinand's driver takes the correct turn in Sarajevo and misses the assassin, WWI is delayed but breaks out within months — alliances were already primed, this is just the spark, not the powder keg, so it's not a structural bottleneck. But if the Byzantine defenders had properly locked the Kerkoporta gate in 1453 and repelled Mehmed's initial assault, the Eastern Roman Empire survives for at least another century — that gate was genuinely load-bearing for the geopolitcal structure of the Eastern Mediterranean, so that's a clear bottleneck. Meanwhile, if James Watt and Matthew Boulton never form their partnership because Boulton's factory burns the week before they meet, the steam engine improvement is delayed by maybe five years at most — convergent technology, lots of near substitutes, not decisive. Then there's Cleopatra's nose: if she'd had a slightly different face, Mark Antony doesn't fall for her, and Western civilisation splits differently. That feels obviously absurd — personal aesthetics don't determine imperial politics — so clearly not a bottleneck, just nonsense causation. But the case I keep going back and forth on is Lehman Brothers: if their bankruptcy filing on 15 September 2008 had been adminstrative delayed by 48 hours due to a clerical error, was the crisis genuinely milder, or was the collapse already baked in? Sometimes it feels like the Kerkoporta gate — a specific administrative failure revealing systemic fragility — and sometimes like Ferdinand's driver, just the visible crack of an inevitable collapse.

Is there a coherent principle that saves my first four verdicts and settles the Lehman case? Or does getting them all to line up force me to drop one? I'd like the principle stated precisely, with the clause that handles each case, and then tell me what it predicts for a scenario I haven't listed — say, if the specific weather pattern over the Channel had been different by six hours on D-Day in 1944. Also, be honest: which of my five verdicts does your best principle fail to accommodate?
