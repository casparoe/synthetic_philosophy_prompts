I'm preparing the final examination for an upper-level philosophy of mind seminar, and I need a single essay question that students should be able to answer thoroughly in about 75 minutes. The topic is illusionism about phenomenal consciousness, but I want to avoid anything that can be satisfied by summarizing standard positions associated with figures like Frankish or Dennett. Instead, I want students to confront a specific conceptual tension: the interaction between illusionism and the thesis of phenomenal transparency, specifically as that thesis applies not merely to first-order perceptual experience but to the meta-cognitive monitoring processes that illusionists often invoke to explain why phenomenal illusions persist and feel so compelling. The question should ask students to evaluate whether an illusionist can consistently maintain that phenomenal consciousness is an introspective misrepresentation without either attributing phenomenal properties to the monitoring mechanism itself—which seems to reintroduce the very consciousness being explained away—or conceding that the monitoring process is phenomenally blank, in which case it becomes obscure how it could generate a richly structured phenomenal illusion rather than a mere cognitive mistake. Please write the exam question, then provide a model answer of approximately 650 to 750 words, followed by a marking scheme.

The marking scheme should be built around four essential moves that a top answer must make, with marks distributed as follows: first, a precise characterization of illusionism that clearly distinguishes it from both eliminativism and standard representationalism, and that identifies what it means to call phenomenal properties "illusory" rather than non-existent (5 marks); second, an accurate exposition of phenomenal transparency and a clear explanation of how the thesis might be extended from first-order experience to the meta-cognitive monitoring of that experience (5 marks); third, a rigorous articulation of the dilemma facing the illusionist—explicitly laying out both horns, explaining why each looks damaging, and avoiding any false resolution that merely re-describes the problem (6 marks); and fourth, a reasoned judgment about whether the illusionist can escape the dilemma, including at least one concrete response an illusionist might offer and one substantive objection to that response, with explicit attention to what each option costs (4 marks). For each move, include a brief note on the typical mistake that should cost marks—such as conflating illusionism with eliminativism, treating transparency only as a thesis about perceptual content, presenting the dilemma without actually pricing the alternatives, or offering a judgment that fails to engage with the meta-cognitive framing. The total should be 20 marks, and the scheme should clearly separate an A-level answer, which handles all four moves with conceptual precision, from a B-level answer, which gets the structure right but misses the specific bite of applying transparency to monitoring.
