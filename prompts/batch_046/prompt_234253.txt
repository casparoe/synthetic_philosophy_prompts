I've been trying to map out my own philosophical profile on mental representation for a discussion group I'm joining, and I want to check whether these commitments actually cohere or pull in incompatible directions. I hold that mental representation is fundamentally combinatorial and syntactic: thoughts possess an internal language-like structure that explains productivity and systematicity, so that grasping "the cat chased the dog" immediately yields the capacity to grasp "the dog chased the cat" without separate learning. I take the productivity argument to be the strongest case for a language of thought, though I also recognize that large-scale statistical models can generate apparently systematic behavior without explicit symbolic architecture, and I do not take that to show human cognition is statistical—only that productivity alone may not uniquely mandate LOT. At the same time, I want to maintain that non-linguistic animals possess intentional states such as beliefs and desires, but that these are organized through associative networks rather than compositional syntax, which means they lack full propositional structure. I further maintain that phenomenal consciousness is not representational at all but involves a direct, non-conceptual acquaintance with experience, so it need not be structured in any language of thought. Finally, I think the explanatory gap is best addressed by treating phenomenal concepts as special in that they refer to mental representations through a direct mode of presentation, which explains why purely physical descriptions always seem to leave something out.

Please go through these commitments and tell me which pairs are in genuine tension, which only appear to conflict but can be reconciled on closer inspection, and for each real tension how severe it is and what the cheapest revision would be. I am particularly unsure whether my claim that phenomenal consciousness is non-representational can sit comfortably with my view that phenomenal concepts refer to representations via direct access; whether my acceptance of animal intentionality without syntax undermines my insistence that syntax is essential to representation; and whether my acknowledgment that statistical models exhibit productivity weakens the argumentative foundation I still want to rely on. If there is one single revision that would clean up the most conflicts without abandoning the core idea that thought has a linguistic structure, I would like to know what it costs. For each pair, please order them by severity and be explicit about what has to give.
