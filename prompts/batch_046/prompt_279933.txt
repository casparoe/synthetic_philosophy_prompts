I serve on the institutional review board for our university’s neural-interface pilot program. A study linking two volunteers through a bidirectional cortical interface—designed to share episodic and affective states—has produced an unanticipated outcome: one participant died of unrelated causes, yet the link remains active. The survivor reports persistent “co-presence” of the deceased’s cognitive patterns, and neuroimaging shows those patterns are stably integrated into the survivor’s prefrontal dynamics. The deceased’s family argues that terminating the link would destroy the only remaining substrate of the deceased’s self, invoking the Hofstadter-style view that a self is not skull-bound but a symbolic pattern capable of persisting across brains. They ask the board to recognize that the survivor now hosts a distributed continuation of the deceased’s personhood, with associated rights—including veto over termination and access to shared data. The research team urges immediate termination, citing psychological risk to the survivor and data-security obligations. Our charter addresses individual human subjects but offers no framework for distributed or posthumous identity.

Please draft the short written opinion I will circulate before Thursday’s meeting. Recommend a concrete course of action with an explicit time frame; treat the family’s claim with philosophical seriousness rather than dismissing it as mere metaphor; and include an honest acknowledgment of the strongest consideration on the losing side. End with a single bottom-line line in this exact form: Verdict: [your recommendation].
