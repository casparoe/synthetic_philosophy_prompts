In my graduate seminar on modal metaphysics, the dispute between Lewisian counterpart theory and the Kripkean commitment to transworld identity has reached what feels like a permanent equilibrium: students reproduce the canonical moves—counterpart theorists invoke qualitative similarity relations across worlds and reject literal identity as ontologically profligate, while defenders of transworld identity insist that only genuine identity can ground de re modal attributions and that counterpart talk is a misleading redescription—without anyone conceding ground. I have come to think the stall may be structural rather than substantive, but I want a sharper diagnosis.

My working hypothesis is that the two sides operate with incompatible success conditions for modal claims. Counterpart theorists appear to treat “a could have been F” as a comparative judgment about qualitative similarity in nearby worlds, where the explanatory burden is specifying the resemblance relation; transworld-identity theorists treat the same sentence as a direct attribution to one and the same individual, where the burden is showing that F is compatible with that individual’s essential properties. They share stock examples—Humphrey could have won the election, this table could have been made of ice—but apply different norms for what counts as having explained the modal fact.

However, I want to test whether a hidden pressure point is masked by these familiar examples. I have been considering cases involving relational properties embedded in contingent institutional frameworks: for instance, whether a specific treaty negotiator could have signed a different agreement if the counterparty state had been represented by different officials, and whether counterpart relations apply symmetrically to both the individual and the institutional role she occupies. Counterpart theory seems forced to generate counterpart relations for both relata, including the state or office, whereas transworld identity preserves the actual individual but appears to require that the institutional framework travel with her across worlds, which is implausible if the framework is itself contingent. Is this asymmetry a genuine ontological problem for one view, or merely an artifact of mixing modal metaphysics with social ontology?
