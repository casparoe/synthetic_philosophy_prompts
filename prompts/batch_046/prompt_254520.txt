I spent three hours arguing with a friend last night about whether entities that remain permanently unobservable should still be counted as real, and whether our failure to identify them is ever genuine evidence against their existence rather than just a limitation of our instruments. We kept circling back to Flatland: if A. Square can never perceive the third dimension, is the Sphere merely a theoretical construct for him, or does it possess an ontology independent of any flatlander's capacity to identify it? Read Flatland through the lens of inaccessible information and ontology identification. Specifically, treat Square's sensory limitation not as a mere practical deficit but as a case of principled epistemic inaccessibility, and ask what Abbott's narrative implies about whether existence depends on the possibility of observational identification. Where does the novel show the flatlanders dismissing the Sphere precisely because their identification procedures cannot accommodate an object that violates their dimensional framework, and where does it resist a verificationist reading by treating the Sphere's predictive success and physical interventions as legitimate grounds for belief in an unobservable reality? I want a critical reading that takes both Abbott's social satire and the philosophical problem seriously: does Square's final imprisonment for reporting the truth dramatize the idea that communities deny ontological status to whatever exceeds their epistemic frameworks? And what does the novel contribute to the debate—between inference to the best explanation and verificationist denial—that a purely conceptual argument about inaccessible information misses?
