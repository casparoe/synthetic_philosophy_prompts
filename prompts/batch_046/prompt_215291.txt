I recently began serving as a teaching assistant for an advanced seminar in political ethics, having spent the previous fifteen years in municipal administration before returning to graduate study; grading these responses has become unexpectedly personal, as several hypothetical cases closely mirror dilemmas I witnessed in my former office. I am calibrating my assessments with colleagues and would appreciate a precise, point-by-point evaluation.

The examination asked students to apply Michael Walzer’s concept of dirty hands to the following scenario: a democratically elected mayor receives credible intelligence that an unaffiliated extremist faction intends to exploit upcoming, lawful demonstrations organized by a nonviolent civil rights coalition in order to incite mass violence. To preempt this, the mayor authorizes covert electronic surveillance of the coalition’s private communications, knowing that the measure violates established civil liberties but believing it necessary to save lives. Students were instructed to determine whether this is a genuine case of dirty hands, whether Walzer’s account requires the mayor to experience personal moral anguish, and how Walzer’s position differs from a straightforward consequentialist justification. They were also asked to consider whether delegating the actual surveillance to a subordinate agency alters the moral status of the act for the mayor herself.

Below is one student’s complete response, reproduced exactly as written.

The mayor clearly faces a case of dirty hands, because she intentionally violates the privacy rights of innocent citizens in order to secure a greater public good, which is precisely Walzer’s formulation: political leaders must sometimes commit acts that are morally wrong in themselves in order to fulfill their responsibility to protect the community. However, Walzer maintains that democratic legitimacy transforms the moral quality of such an act. Once the mayor acts within her constitutional authority and is answerable to an elected body, the wrongness is institutionally contained, and she should not experience personal guilt or anguish. The guilt would only be appropriate if she were acting as a private individual; in her public role, democratic authorization cleanses the act of its residual stain. This distinguishes Walzer sharply from consequentialism. A pure consequentialist justifies the surveillance by weighing lives saved against liberties lost, but Walzer rejects this calculus because he focuses on the leader’s institutional role rather than on outcomes. For Walzer, the act remains technically wrong, yet the democratic mandate cancels the wrongness, leaving the mayor with clean hands in practice.

As for delegation, if the mayor assigns the actual surveillance to a subordinate intelligence agency, the moral burden shifts downward to those who execute the order. The mayor retains only political accountability, not moral culpability, because she has separated the decision from its dirty execution. This division of labor is consistent with Walzer’s framework, since it allows the leader to preserve her moral integrity by ensuring that she does not personally carry out the wrongful act. In conclusion, the mayor is justified in ordering the surveillance, should feel no personal anguish, and successfully avoids dirty hands by distributing the moral labor through proper bureaucratic channels.
