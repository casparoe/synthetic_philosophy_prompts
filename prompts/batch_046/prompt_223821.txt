I have been reading, in a lay capacity, about Kurt Gödel’s rotating-universe model and his philosophical argument that time is not an objectively real feature of the world. I understand that in his solution to Einstein’s field equations the cosmos rotates in such a way that no universal clock synchronisation is possible, and that this global structure allows closed paths through spacetime along which an observer might return to a region of their own past. I also understand that Gödel took this not merely as a mathematical curiosity but as evidence that temporal flow is not fundamental.

In thinking about this, I have noticed a distinction that popular accounts do not seem to name clearly. There is the ordinary special-relativistic effect in which two observers in relative motion disagree about elapsed time, but Gödel’s universe seems to involve something stronger: a topological property of spacetime itself that makes the ordering of events depend on the path one takes through the cosmos, not merely on velocity or acceleration. I do not know whether physicists have a specific term for this path-dependent chronological ordering, but it strikes me as different in kind from standard time dilation, and I find myself needing a label to keep the two separate in my own thinking.

Given this, I have been wondering what Gödel might have said about two developments since his death in 1978. First, the late-1990s discovery that cosmic expansion is accelerating, which appears to establish a privileged cosmic time—the time elapsed since the Big Bang—that applies uniformly across large scales. Would Gödel have seen this as undermining his claim that objective time does not exist, because it supplies a universal clock? Or would he have treated cosmic time as merely one convenient coordinate among others, with no deeper reality?

Second, there are now quantum experiments in which the sequence of two operations appears to exist in superposition, so that neither “operation A before operation B” nor the reverse is definitively true of the system. Might Gödel have welcomed this as supporting his view that temporal order is not fundamental? Or would he have rejected it, perhaps on the grounds that the deterministic mathematical structure he favoured requires a definite ordering even at microscopic scales?

Please address both points, explaining not only what Gödel’s likely position would be but also how his rotating-universe argument bears on them. In particular, say what would change your mind: which argument about cosmic expansion or about quantum temporal ordering would lead you to a different conclusion regarding Gödel’s probable views, and where is your reasoning most likely to fail?
