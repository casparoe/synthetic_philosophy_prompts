I'm a doctoral candidate in philosophy of science, and I've been asked to deliver the closing remarks at my advisor's retirement colloquium on Thursday evening. The room will be split: about half are epistemologists and philosophers of science who know her technical work on defeasible reasoning and belief revision, and about half are family members, former students from outside the discipline, and university staff who have never engaged with formal epistemology. I need an approximately eight-minute speech that takes the dogmatism paradox—the tension that any rationally held belief must carry some resistance to counterevidence, yet that very resistance is structurally identical to the dogmatism we rationally condemn—as its backbone, not as decorative reference. Because the paradox genuinely structures what can be said, I must avoid both the triumphalist narrative that she never wavered from her core framework and the opposite humility narrative that she was always prepared to abandon it at the first anomaly. Instead, I want the speech to present her actual contribution: a model of what I will call calibrated stubbornness, the idea that belief can be maintained with context-sensitive, graded resistance rather than with binary openness or closure. The speech should turn on one concrete illustration—how a scientist ought to treat persistent anomalous measurements within a decades-long experimental program—without devolving into a lecture on non-monotonic logic or formal belief revision. The philosophers present should recognize that I have not mischaracterized the paradox or misrepresented her position; the family should leave understanding why her work on belief revision was not merely abstract logic but a practical guide to intellectual integrity. Please do not include any bibliography, citation, reading list, or reference to specific papers, books, or publication dates. The tone should be formal, affectionate, and precise, appropriate for an academic retirement occasion with a genuinely mixed audience.
