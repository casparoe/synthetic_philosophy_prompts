I'm working through the problem of old evidence in Bayesian epistemology, and I keep running into a tension that standard treatments don't seem to address head-on. The usual setup involves a scientist who learns of an already-known observation E and asks whether E can confirm a newly proposed hypothesis H, given that P(E) is already near 1 conditional on background knowledge. Most discussions focus on whether logical learning of H from prior beliefs can make P(E|H) > P(E), or on the distinction between propositional and doxastic confirmation. But I want to push on a different angle: what if the "old" evidence is not merely old in time, but old in the sense that it was previously interpreted through a now-discredited theoretical framework that systematically masked its relevance?

Consider this scenario. In 2023, a team proposes a new structural model M of glacier dynamics that predicts an unusual non-monotonic retreat pattern in certain alpine valleys. The team derives this as a novel consequence, and at the time of publication the prediction is unconfirmed. Then, in 2024, an archivist uncovers aerial survey photographs from 1978 that clearly show the predicted retreat pattern occurring in two specific valleys. At the time the photos were taken, glaciologists interpreted the patterns as measurement error or local noise, guided by the then-dominant linear-decline paradigm L. The photos sat in an archive, technically "known" in the weak sense that they existed, but epistemically inaccessible because the theoretical lens needed to see them as data was absent. Now the question: does the 1978 photographic record confirm M? On the one hand, the observations predate the theory, which is the hallmark of old evidence. On the other hand, they were never actually observed-as-evidence until 2024; the 2023 team didn't know about them, and the 1978 community lacked the conceptual apparatus to register them as supporting anything. Does Bayes' rule care about the temporal order of events, or only about the agent's epistemic state? More specifically, if we formalize the agent's background knowledge K at the moment of proposing M, should the archive's existence be included in K even though its content was misinterpreted? And if we say that confirmation requires a change in credence, whose credence matters — the individual scientist, the scientific community, or some idealized observer?

Write an essay that weighs these considerations and reaches a defended conclusion. You should consider at least two competing resolutions: one that treats the misinterpreted archive as genuinely old evidence that cannot confirm M (perhaps because P(E|K) is already fixed near 1 once we include the existence of the archive), and one that treats the theoretical re-interpretation as a kind of epistemic discovery that allows the data to function as new evidence (perhaps analogizing to seeing an ambiguous figure in a new gestalt). Be explicit about whether you think the problem is dissolved, relocated, or deepened by this kind of case, and justify your verdict with reference to the formal structure of Bayesian conditioning rather than just intuition.
