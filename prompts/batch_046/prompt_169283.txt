Two people in my study group got stuck arguing over Leibniz's claim that this is the best of all possible worlds. He proposed a trimmed-world objection: if perfection is genuinely a function of variety ordered against disorder, then there ought to be a compossible world that preserves nearly all of ours but eliminates one localized category of suffering without altering physical law or breaking internal consistency. If God surveys all compossible sets and picks the one with maximal perfection, that trimmed world should outrank ours, so either such a world is not actually compossible—which requires suffering and richness to be metaphysically coupled in an implausible way—or Leibniz's thesis is false. She answered that this treats suffering and richness as independent variables, but for Leibniz the moral law that permits free will is the same law that generates the richness of moral virtue; subtract the misuse and you subtract the conditions for the richness itself, so the trimmed world collapses into a simpler but less perfect state rather than a superior one. He came back that this makes the claim unfalsifiable: any feature of our world gets retroactively declared load-bearing for perfection, turning an empirical-sounding thesis into a tautology. She said it is not unfalsifiability but systemic holism—like removing a wall and changing the architecture—not a tautology but a substantive claim about how value is structured rather than added up. Can you go through their exchange round by round and adjudicate who is right at each step? Does the trimmed-world objection misunderstand how compossibility works for Leibniz, or does it expose a real gap in his calculus of perfection? And what, if anything, are both missing—perhaps an ambiguity between metaphysical and moral necessity, or between additive and systemic conceptions of the good?
