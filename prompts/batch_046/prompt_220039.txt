I serve on the Institutional Review Board for Behavioral and Social Science Research at my university. Before us is a proposal that extends the well-known asymmetry in intentionality attribution—where observers are more inclined to call a harmful side effect intentional than a beneficial one—into an under-examined setting: collective decision-makers whose awareness of the side effect is acquired through what the applicant terms a bootstrapped evidential loop, in which the prediction of the outcome relies on data that already include the outcome. The applicant will present vignettes describing either a corporate board or an algorithmic governance system adopting a policy; in the critical conditions, the agent's knowledge that the side effect will occur is either derived independently or derived from a model trained on instances that include the very effect being predicted. The study's cover story tells participants they are evaluating organizational comprehension texts, with full debriefing afterward. Half the board regards the design as a legitimate expansion of experimental philosophy into collective agency and epistemically circular knowledge; the other half argues that the manipulation conflates judgments about intentionality with judgments about epistemic reliability, and that the deception is unjustified because the vignettes are so conceptually complex that participants cannot provide meaningful informed consent even in a broad sense. Draft the board's formal decision memo—approve, reject, or approve with conditions—addressed to the principal investigator. The memo should state a clear recommendation with a concrete rationale, engage seriously with the strongest argument for the opposing view, and specify any conditions or time-bound requirements if approval is conditional. Please write in American English.
