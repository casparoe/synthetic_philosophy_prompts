I'm working through some notes from a philosophy-of-science discussion group that touched on archaeological inference, and one participant summarized what she took to be a recent interpretive approach. She explained that because mental states do not fossilize, archaeologists must reconstruct ancient religious beliefs through a direct analogical mapping: they match the formal properties of an artifact—say, a carved stone figure bearing specific geometric incisions—to documented ethnographic cases in which identical incisions denote shamanic authority, then read the ancient figure as direct evidence of shamanic belief at that site. Once enough artifacts have received these mapped meanings, she claimed, the researcher aggregates them into institutional conclusions, such as the presence of a hierarchical priesthood, without relying on any intermediate theoretical framework that connects material culture to social organization. She characterized the method as essentially inductive pattern recognition, said its only serious vulnerability is selecting an inappropriate ethnographic parallel, and suggested that critics who worry about circular reasoning are overstating the difficulty because the analogies are externally validated by their ethnographic sources.

I find the summary plausible, but it feels too streamlined—especially the jump from individual object interpretations to institutional structures, and the dismissal of mediating theory. Is this a fair characterization of how interpretive archaeologists actually reason? Please separate what captures real practice, what sounds like a common popular simplification, and what misrepresents the theoretical commitments—particularly regarding middle-range linking arguments and the possibility that different social processes might generate materially identical signatures—without offering a bibliography or reading list.
