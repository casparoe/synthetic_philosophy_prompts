I'm preparing a session for a small philosophy discussion group composed mainly of practicing lawyers and architects, all of whom have spent years with professional cost-benefit frameworks. They tend to treat any conflict of values as an optimization problem with hidden weights, and I want to teach them the structure of genuinely incommensurable values and hard choices without falling back on overused illustrations like career versus family or justice versus mercy. The carrying case I want to use involves a municipal decision about whether to preserve a decaying but culturally specific bridge built by an immigrant labor collective in the 1920s, or to demolish it for a modern transit corridor that would reduce commute times and emissions for thousands; the bridge carries a form of historical recognition and collective identity, while the corridor carries distributive efficiency and environmental benefit, and neither option dominates the other on any shared metric.

Plan the explanatory arc: which conceptual distinction—perhaps between incommensurability and mere difficulty, or between incomparability and unweighed comparison—must be firmly in place before I can present the bridge case as a hard choice rather than a poorly calculated trade-off. Explain how to build the example so that it carries the idea specifically, highlighting what the bridge example must include (both goods remain fully present and unrealized in the rejected option) and what it must avoid (reducing either side to sentimental attachment or to a quantifiable external benefit). Then map the misconceptions I should expect in the order they will arise: first that this is merely a political disagreement about priorities, then that we simply need a more sophisticated weighted scoring system, and finally that rationally choosing one option means treating the other value as less important or as already compensated for. For each, indicate the precise point in the sequence where it should be headed off. Include a single check question I can ask at the end that would reveal whether they understand that a hard choice leaves a rational residue of regret rather than being resolved by "discovering the correct weights," and state clearly what I should refuse to explain at this level and say so outright.
