As a journalist covering the intersection of metaphysics and engineered systems, I am commissioning a formal design brief for an experiential classroom module. The setting is an upper-level philosophy seminar of approximately fourteen students, convened for a single ninety-minute session. The conceptual target is material constitution—specifically the statue-and-clay problem—but I want the exercise to escape the familiar rehearsal of gradual replacement and instead immerse participants in a niche variant: a collectively maintained modular artifact whose design specifications are rewritten in real time by the students themselves as they substitute physical components, so that form and matter co-evolve rather than one tracking the other. The idea should emerge from the rules and incentives, not from any front-loaded lecture; participants should encounter the crisis before they hear technical labels such as mereological essentialism or four-dimensional stage theory.

Design a runnable activity—using only improvised materials available in a standard seminar room, assigned roles, or structured deliberation rules—that requires the group to reconstruct and authorize changes to a shared object under competing identity conditions. Arrange payoffs or decision structures—whether collective votes, veto powers, or authorization thresholds—so that students discover for themselves that any consistent criterion they adopt, whether tracking original matter, current form, functional trajectory, or an evolving blueprint, either severs continuity unexpectedly or smuggles in essential properties they initially denied. Specify the timeline minute by minute, the roles or rules, the physical setup, and the precise moment when the coordination failure should surface.

Then provide a debrief that maps each critical moment in the activity onto a corresponding position in the constitution debate, explaining what each breakdown reveals about the relation between object and process, and instructing the facilitator on how to supply the feature that the activity cannot reproduce—likely the diachronic vagueness of replacement across extended real time—without undermining the experiential frame. Predict what will reliably go wrong on a first run, such as students conflating functional preservation with numerical identity or allowing the co-evolution of specifications to collapse the object-process distinction, and advise exactly how to correct course without breaking the simulation. The deliverable should read as a polished pedagogical design brief suitable for an ideas feature, avoiding bibliographies, reading lists, citations, and references to obscure texts, and remaining fully intelligible to a reader or model without web access.
