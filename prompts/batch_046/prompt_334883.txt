I am preparing a seminar presentation for the annual meeting of the Society for Applied Philosophy, and I need a rigorous Q&A prep sheet. The thesis I am defending is that the three canonical failures of Athenian direct democracy—the reversal of the Mytilene decree, the disastrous Sicilian Expedition, and the trial and execution of Socrates—are not best explained as contingent moral failures or as the result of demagogic manipulation, but rather as manifestations of a single structural pathology that I call “deliberative compression”: the institutional tendency of direct democratic assemblies to collapse multi-dimensional judgments (involving temporal distance, expert epistemic authority, and legal-formal distinctions) into binary, affectively charged votes that erase precisely the distinctions required for sound long-range decision-making. I argue that this compression is not corrected by democratic self-reflection but is actually exacerbated by the very procedures—open debate, immediate voting, and the absence of mediating institutions—that are typically praised as democratic virtues.

The audience will be composed primarily of analytic political theorists working in democratic theory and republican freedom (several of whom are sympathetic to deliberative democracy), two classicists who specialize in Thucydides and Plato but are skeptical of anachronistic institutional analysis, and a political historian of ancient Athens who tends to view democratic failures as products of specific elite conflicts rather than systemic design flaws. I want the objections ordered by how damaging they are likely to be and how probable they are from this particular room, each phrased in the vocabulary those interlocutors would actually use—so the deliberative democrat should ask about the second assembly at Mytilene, the classicist should press me on whether I am treating Thucydidean speeches as sociological data, and the historian should challenge whether Alcibiades and the restored democracy’s legal machinery can be folded into a single “systemic” account. For each objection, provide a concise reply I can deliver in roughly two minutes, together with an explicit note on where that reply is weakest or concedes ground. Include the one objection I am hoping nobody raises—specifically, the comparison with Aristotle’s critique of democracy in the Politics and the demand that I explain why “deliberative compression” is not merely a redescription of his well-known arguments about the ignorance of the masses—together with a strategy for deflecting or absorbing it without derailing the presentation.

Please do not ask me for a bibliography, a reading list, or specific citations; I need arguments and counter-arguments that can stand on their own conceptual feet. If the framing of “deliberative compression” rests on a confused premise—if, for instance, it illegitimately assumes that modern institutional-design categories can be mapped onto Athenian practice without remainder, or if the three cases are too dissimilar to sustain a unified structural explanation—say so plainly rather than constructing a prep sheet around a flawed thesis. I invite direct pushback on whether this task is well-formed, and I want the response to flag any point where my own position is particularly soft rather than disguising it.
