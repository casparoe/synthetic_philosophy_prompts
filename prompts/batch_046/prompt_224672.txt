I'm working through a tension in global research governance with a colleague who studies institutional epistemology, and I'd like to understand the philosophical architecture beneath a specific administrative pattern. Many major funding bodies now mandate that all human-subjects research, regardless of field site, be evaluated through a single centralized ethics framework—typically one developed within Western legal and philosophical traditions. When researchers operate in communities with different practices of consent, risk assessment, or collective decision-making, those local practices are rarely treated as parallel ethical frameworks with independent standing. Instead, they are registered in a supplemental "cultural context" appendix and then mapped onto the universal template: a communal deliberation process becomes a group consent mechanism, a spiritual prohibition becomes a religious exemption clause, and so on. The underlying normative form never transforms; only the local content is translated into it.

What philosophical commitments does this practice presuppose? It seems to assume not merely that some ethical principles are universal, but something stronger about the relationship between the framework doing the describing and the practices being described—something one might call ascription universality: the view that one epistemic position can assign normative properties—valid, invalid, modified, exempt—across every context without undergoing reciprocal transformation of its own categories. Reconstruct the implicit theory here. Does the practice strictly require that local norms are deficient or incomplete relative to the universal grid, or can it be sustained by purely practical arguments about tractability, legal liability, and audit consistency? Is it philosophically coherent for the same institution to champion "local knowledge" in its methodological guidelines while treating local ethical norms as derivative adjustments to a pre-given framework? And if we rejected ascription universality—if we held that a normative framework can only legitimately ascribe properties within the epistemic conditions that shaped it—what concrete changes would be required in how these review boards are structured, how they document decisions, and how they relate to site-specific ethics committees?
