As a novelist developing a collection on the ontology of collective memory, I ask you to write a short story that dramatizes the conceptual boundary at which a legend becomes history. To avoid well-worn tropes, please build the narrative around an invented procedural mechanism: the “corroboration cascade,” the point at which a narrative is reclassified from legend to history not because new primary evidence emerges, but because institutional repetition—archival citations, bureaucratic footnotes, and state-sanctioned retellings—reaches a density where ambiguity itself becomes administratively untenable. Set the story in a society that maintains a formal Legend Registry and a Historical Canon, separated by a registrar’s office with precise rules of transfer. The protagonist should be the registrar tasked with evaluating a founding narrative—culturally weighty, analogous in symbolic function to traditions such as the Trojan War, the Arthurian cycle, or the Exodus—after discovering that every record supporting its historical status was generated by the same institutional cascade rather than by independent observation. Through concrete procedural scenes, dialogue with clerks or priests, and the physical handling of documents, explore what changes when a society crosses this threshold: not the acquisition of truth, but the loss of permissible doubt. Maintain a restrained, literary tone; avoid anachronistic technology; do not include explicit philosophical commentary or authorial explanation—let the concept emerge entirely from the registry’s rules and the protagonist’s decision. Aim for approximately 600 words.
