I have been drafting an essay on comparative exemplarity and the distribution of causal agency in biographical writing, specifically contrasting Plutarch’s Parallel Lives with early Christian hagiography. Rather than framing the difference as secular versus sacred, I argue that the two traditions construct different pedagogical contracts with the reader. In Plutarch, the synkrisis and paired structure ask the reader to act as a comparative judge: moral learning occurs through weighing ambiguous, often contradictory exemplars whose virtues and vices are both visible, with causal weight placed on the subject’s own choices—what I call a distributed-agency model focused on character. The anecdotal detail, the small things revealing how a figure treats a servant or responds to defeat, serves not as ornament but as data for judgment. By contrast, in texts such as the Life of Antony and the Life of Macrina, virtue is demonstrated primarily through resistance to demonic assault or miraculous endurance, framing the saint’s character within a causal field dominated by divine action; the reader is positioned not as judge but as witness recognizing transcendent value breaking into history. I further propose that Plutarch’s inclusion of oracles and omens does not collapse this distinction, because even supernatural signs are interpreted through the subject’s responsive choice, whereas in hagiography the miracle often supersedes psychological interiority. The upshot is that Plutarch trains practical wisdom in navigating moral ambiguity, while hagiography trains recognition of absolute value.

I want comments on the following points, not bibliographic recommendations or citations. First, does the causal-distribution frame genuinely illuminate these texts, or does it impose anachronistic analytic jargon? Second, am I wrong about Plutarch’s pedagogical intent—does the Parallel Lives tradition actually presuppose direct imitation of exemplary figures rather than comparative judgment, and if so, does that undermine the contrast with hagiography? Third, are there counter-examples within hagiography, perhaps in later medieval vitae or certain saints’ lives where ambiguity and internal choice are narrated in ways approximating Plutarch’s model, which would blur the distinction? Fourth, does the contrast hold given that Plutarch’s own moral framework is teleological and therefore not as ambiguous as I suggest? I am especially interested in whether the core claim—that biography can be taxonomized by the causal role assigned to the exemplar’s agency rather than by religious or secular orientation—is defensible, or whether it collapses into a merely verbal distinction. Please address structural weaknesses, weak analogies, and whether the essay’s central comparison risks insulating itself from counter-evidence.
