I was discussing with a friend recently whether the sheer number of insects in the world creates a hidden moral crisis. The reasoning, as I understand it, runs like this: even if the probability that any given insect is sentient is very low—perhaps one in many thousands—the fact that insects exist in numbers that dwarf all vertebrates combined means the expected total of suffering subjects could be enormous. My friend dismissed this as a statistical sleight of hand, arguing that when we are profoundly ignorant about whether consciousness is present at all, multiplying population size by an arbitrary small probability does not generate a real moral obligation but merely projects certainty onto a metaphysical blank. I have been trying to work out whether there is a principled middle ground, but I keep going in circles. Write an essay on whether the scale argument genuinely establishes that insect welfare should be treated as a major moral priority. Weigh the case that expected-value reasoning applies even under deep ontological uncertainty—where the very existence of a subject of experience is in question, not merely the outcome for a known subject—against the objection that such reasoning illegitimately treats a metaphysical unknown as if it were a lottery with fixed odds. Also consider whether a threshold approach, which directs moral concern only toward beings whose probability of sentience exceeds some cutoff, can be defended without arbitrariness and without silently collapsing back into the scale argument it was meant to avoid. Reach a definite conclusion and justify it: does the argument change what we owe to insects, or does it misunderstand the conditions under which moral consideration is properly triggered?
