I'm designing a syllabus module on Bayesian confirmation theory for an advanced philosophy-of-science seminar, and I've become dissatisfied with the standard textbook framing of the old-evidence problem. The core difficulty, as I understand it, is that if Bayesian updating requires conditioning on evidence genuinely new to the agent, then well-known facts—such as Mercury's anomalous perihelion prior to general relativity, or the periodic table prior to quantum mechanics—seem unable to confirm the theories that elegantly accommodate them, since they were already embedded in the shared prior. Rather than revisiting familiar historical case studies, I want to evaluate a deliberately granular taxonomy of proposed resolutions on strictly internal philosophical criteria, treating each proposal as a formal or conceptual amendment rather than a historical interpretation.

Consider the following six proposed resolutions or reconceptualizations of the old-evidence problem. (A) The Predictive-Novelty Constraint: a theory confirms old evidence only if it assigns that evidence a higher prior probability than rival theories did; if the evidence was already common knowledge, the likelihood ratio collapses to unity and no confirmatory increment occurs. (B) The Logical-Relation View: confirmation is a timeless logical or probabilistic support relation between statement and hypothesis; temporal ordering is epistemically inert, so old evidence confirms whenever the support relation holds, regardless of when the agent originally acquired the evidence. (C) The Internal-Bootstrapping Account: a theory can derive old evidence from subsets of its own axioms together with auxiliary premises; because that derivational pathway was unavailable before the theory existed, the agent can conditionalize on the derived statement, generating confirmation without requiring predictive novelty. (D) The Epistemic-Time Indexing Amendment: agents maintain a diachronic sequence of credence functions indexed to discrete epistemic times; old evidence is already incorporated into the prior at the moment of its original acquisition, and later theories are evaluated against that historical trajectory rather than against a static present prior, thereby dissolving the paradox. (E) The Mnemonic Asymmetry Thesis—an idea I've been developing in discussion with colleagues—which proposes that the confirmatory weight of evidence should be scaled by a retrieval-cost coefficient: evidence residing in long-term semantic storage at the moment of evaluation receives a discounted likelihood ratio unless actively reconstructed into working memory, introducing a cognitive, rather than logical, asymmetry between old and new evidence. (F) The Diachronic Depreciation-Accumulation Rule—another speculative formal amendment—which applies an exponential decay factor to the confirmatory weight of evidence proportional to the number of intermediate theoretical frameworks through which it has persisted unchanged; consequently, evidence that survives many theoretical shifts accumulates multiplied, rather than diminished, confirmation weight for any new theory that accommodates it.

Please evaluate each proposal on two dimensions. First, Fidelity to Core Bayesianism, scored 1–10, where 10 means the proposal requires no alteration to the standard rule of conditionalization on an agent's current credence function and 1 means it abandons or radically reconstructs that rule. Second, Overall Philosophical Defensibility, scored 1–10, where 10 means the proposal withstands the standard objections—such as ad hocness, confirmation by derivation alone, or collapse into subjective psychology—with minimal cost. Then assign each proposal an overall tier—S, A, B, C, or D—based on the combined profile. Present your evaluation as a JSON object with the following schema: a top-level key "evaluations" containing a list of objects, each with keys "label" (string, e.g., "A"), "proposal_name" (string), "fidelity_score" (integer 1-10), "defensibility_score" (integer 1-10), "tier" (string, one of S/A/B/C/D), and "justification" (string, 1-3 sentences explaining the scoring). After the JSON, provide a brief concluding paragraph of no more than 150 words summarizing which tier you think best represents a genuinely viable path forward for Bayesians and why, but keep the reasoning internal to the six proposals—do not cite external authors, request further reading, or invoke bibliographic authority.
