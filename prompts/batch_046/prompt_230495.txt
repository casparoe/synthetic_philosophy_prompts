I am preparing the question list for an upcoming episode of my philosophy-of-medicine podcast, in which I will interview Dr. Lena Okonkwo, a philosopher who has recently defended a domain-specific pluralist account of health and disease. Rather than choosing sides in the standard naturalism-versus-normativism debate—between biostatistical naturalists who treat disease as value-free dysfunction relative to a reference class, and normativists in the harmful-dysfunction tradition who insist that evaluative judgments are ineliminable—Okonkwo argues that the disagreement presupposes a false unity. She proposes a tripartite framework: clinical medicine operates with a broadly naturalist concept of dysfunction tied to statistical deviation from species-typical functioning; evolutionary medicine employs a mismatch-based concept that measures present physiology against an ancestral adaptive baseline; and psychiatric classification requires an explicitly normative concept tied to suffering, agency, and social functioning. I want the interview to go well beyond a restatement of this taxonomy and to press exactly where the existing literature applies pressure.

Please draft eight to ten interview questions that turn on the actual content of her position, with each major theme paired to a follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give. I want one question on the statistical-normality problem for clinical naturalism—specifically, how her framework handles conditions such as obesity or essential hypertension in populations where they are statistically modal—followed by a follow-up anticipating her reply that clinical judgment appeals to an ideal-type or design-based reference class rather than to actual population frequencies, and pressing whether that move does not already smuggle in normative selections of which functions are essential. I want one question on the evolutionary-mismatch domain asking which ancestral environment or ecological baseline sets the functional standard, given the heterogeneity of human evolutionary history across diverse ecologies, followed by a follow-up that anticipates her defense of a core metabolic design and asks whether selecting that core is not itself a value-laden decision masked as biological fact. I want one question on psychiatric nosology—perhaps whether conditions such as ADHD or major depressive disorder can be assigned to the normative domain without also presupposing some biological dysfunction claim—and a follow-up addressing the risk that her framework merely relocates the value-ladenness problem across domains rather than dissolving it. I also want a question that challenges the metaphysical integrity of the domains themselves, using a cross-domain case such as type 2 diabetes, which is simultaneously a clinical pathology, an evolutionary mismatch, and a socially shaped condition, and asking whether this shows that her taxonomy is pragmatic rather than ontologically deep.

At the end of the sequence, please include a direct question asking what specific evidence, cross-disciplinary finding, or philosophical argument would force her to abandon pluralism in favor of either a unified naturalism or a unified normativism, and ask her to identify the weakest joint in her own architecture—whether it lies in the boundary conditions between domains, the selection of evolutionary baselines, or the isolation of psychiatric norms from biological dysfunction.
