I'm working out a coherent position on distributed or shared selves for a philosophy of mind seminar, drawing on Hofstadter's idea that the self is a strange loop — a high-level pattern sustained by low-level neural dynamics rather than a Cartesian substance. Here are the commitments I want to hold together:

(a) The self is metaphysically a pattern or strange loop; it has no essential tie to a particular brain or body, and could in principle be realised in different substrates or even shared across them if the functional dynamics are right.

(b) Despite (a), for practical, moral, and legal purposes I think personal identity requires substantial spatiotemporal continuity of the underlying biological substrate; I am not inclined to treat a perfect digital copy of my loop, instantiated elsewhere, as literally me continuing.

(c) I accept that our brains routinely host high-fidelity representational fragments of other people — my partner's preferences, speech patterns, emotional dispositions — and that these fragments can become deeply integrated into my own cognitive architecture without constituting a second self.

(d) I also think that if my partner were to die, and my own brain came to sustain a dynamically active, autonomous version of her loop — not merely a memory or model, but a genuinely running pattern with its own narrative centre — then there is a real, non-metaphorical sense in which she persists inside me.

(e) Finally, I hold that a single self cannot branch: if an identical loop were running simultaneously in two separate brains, neither could claim exclusive ownership of the original identity; at most one is the genuine continuation, and the other is a derivative replica.

My worry is that these pull in incompatible directions. For instance, (a) and (b) seem to sit uneasily: if the loop is substrate-independent, why should biological continuity matter for practical identity? Meanwhile, (c) and (d) look like they blur a line I need: what distinguishes a deeply integrated representation from an incorporated co-self, and does (d) not violate the non-branching condition in (e) by placing her loop inside my substrate? At the same time, there may be only an apparent conflict between (a) and (e) — perhaps substrate independence does not entail that branching is genuinely possible, because a strange loop requires a singular locus of narrative gravity.

Could you audit this set? I want to know: which pairs are genuinely in tension, which tensions dissolve on closer inspection, and what is the least costly revision to restore consistency? Please rank the tensions by severity, indicating for each what commitment would have to be modified or abandoned and at what philosophical cost. I am not asking for a general lecture on Hofstadter; I want a consistency check on this specific cluster of views.
