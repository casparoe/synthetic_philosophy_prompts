Against the backdrop of Weber's Protestant work ethic, the institutional 35-hour ceiling, and karoshi as extreme overwork mortality, imagine a policy agenda that treats cognitive off-duty status—the absence of work-related mental availability for gig workers and caregivers—as a legally protected leisure right comparable to physical overtime limits. Which three open conceptual or normative questions should this agenda tackle first? Rank them and defend the ordering by identifying which are upstream (whose resolution determines the others), which are tractable without extensive empirical labor economics, and which carry the greatest practical stakes for preventing karoshi-type outcomes. In particular, address whether leisure rights should be measured in clock hours, in psychological recovery capacity, or in autonomy over temporal boundaries, and whether the appropriate remedy lies in labor regulation, public-health framing, or cultural moral reform.
