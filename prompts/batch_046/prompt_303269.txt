I've been following recent debates about whether consciousness or sentience extends into fundamental physical processes—fields, particles, or lattice structures—rather than being confined to brains. A recurring argumentative pattern keeps catching my attention, and I'm not sure whether philosophers have named it precisely.

The pattern works like this. A proponent of some form of pan-sentience concedes that if experiential properties are genuinely fundamental, then altering a physical system at the base level—say, perturbing an electromagnetic field or rearranging atoms in a crystal—must involve altering corresponding experiential states, however minimal. But when the ethical implication is pressed—does this mean we have reason to avoid needlessly altering fields, or that large-scale physical processes carry massive accumulated suffering?—the proponent introduces an organizational condition. They say suffering requires not just experience but a specific kind of structural integration, contrast, or feedback that only arises at macroscopic scales, so the base-level changes carry no moral weight whatsoever. The weight is not merely negligible; it is exactly zero. Yet if asked whether the base-level entities are genuinely conscious in any meaningful sense, they insist yes, and they reject the idea that consciousness suddenly switches on at a specific complexity threshold, preferring instead a continuous gradation. So they seem to hold both that experience is continuously present downward and that moral relevance discontinuously drops to nothing at the bottom, with the cutoff justified by organizational features that themselves come in degrees.

I've seen this move made with quantum fields, with vibrational modes in solids, and with thermodynamic processes in non-living matter. In each case, the speaker wants to preserve the metaphysical claim that consciousness is ubiquitous, while blocking the ethical conclusion that ubiquitous physical change implies ubiquitous harm. The maneuver feels different from an ordinary auxiliary hypothesis, because it is not protecting a predictive claim—it is insulating a metaphysical commitment from its normative consequences. It also does not seem identical to the combination problem, since the issue is not how micro-experiences combine into a macro-subject, but rather why micro-experiences that are acknowledged to exist are denied any standing in moral calculus despite being acknowledged as real.

Does this specific oscillation—between continuous metaphysical presence and discontinuous moral nullity, mediated by an organizational criterion that is itself gradational—have an established name? If so, how does it differ from related issues like the combination problem, the boundary problem for moral status, or simple threshold-based exclusion? If there is no precise term, how would you characterize its structure, its closest analogues in other domains of applied ethics, and the point at which it becomes inconsistent rather than merely pragmatic? I am not looking for a literature survey or citation list—just the conceptual carving: what exactly this pattern is, what it is not, and where its edges lie. Please write your answer in American English.
