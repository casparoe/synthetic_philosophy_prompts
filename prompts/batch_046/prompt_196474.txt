I'm presenting a paper titled "The Fiscal Decoupling of 439: How the Loss of the African Annona Made the Western Imperial Office Redundant Before Odoacer" at a session of the International Congress of Late Antique Studies. My thesis is that the dissolution conventionally dated to 476 CE was neither a catastrophic fall caused by barbarian military superiority, climate stress, or moral decline, nor a seamless cultural transformation, but an internal fiscal-legal decoupling: after the Vandal occupation of Carthage and the African provinces in 439, the Western court lost the grain-tax revenue stream that had monetized provincial surpluses into gold salaries for the field army. Without that monetization, regional commanders—beginning with Aetius's patronage networks and culminating in Odoacer's Italian administration—shifted to direct land-rent extraction, reinterpreting hospitalitas obligations as proprietary tenures not as a barbarian ethnic innovation but as an emergency fiscal substitution. By 476, imperial authority had become administratively redundant because military loyalty was already anchored to local land-rent rather than to central disbursement; Odoacer's coup was therefore a formal recognition of a decoupling that had been structurally complete for a generation.
