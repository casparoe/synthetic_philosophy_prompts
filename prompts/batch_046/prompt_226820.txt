I've been trying to understand why people do things they clearly regret, and I've come across two explanations that seem related but might not be the same thing at all. The first is the old philosophical idea of weakness of will, or akrasia: you genuinely judge that one course is best and still choose the worse one because desire pulls you away. The second is hyperbolic discounting from behavioral economics, where people don't discount the future at a steady rate; instead they sharply prefer smaller rewards now over larger ones later, and that preference can reverse if both options are pushed back in time.

I'm wondering whether these are describing one failure in two vocabularies, or two genuinely different failures. To press on that, think about precommitment: putting an alarm clock across the room so you must get out of bed to silence it. In the economic story, this looks like the present self binding a future self to correct a predictable time-inconsistency. In the philosophical story, it might look more like the state of self-control that philosophers call enkrateia—using external structures so that reason can prevail over appetite—without assuming that the future self is literally a different agent bargaining with the present one.

Now consider a specific case: someone sets the alarm across the room, gets up to turn it off, and then immediately climbs back into bed, fully aware that this defeats the purpose and feeling guilty about it. Is this best explained as hyperbolic discounting—the immediate comfort of bed being overvalued relative to the later benefit of waking up—or as akrasia—acting against one's all-things-considered judgment? Or does explaining it require both frameworks at once?

More generally, I'd like you to compare the two frameworks on three points. First, does hyperbolic discounting require that the agent's judgment actually changes between the planning moment and the acting moment, or can the judgment stay constant while only the motivational weight shifts? Second, does akrasia require a unified agent with one rational faculty and one appetitive faculty in conflict, or can it be reconceived as a negotiation between temporal stages of a person without collapsing into economics? Third, if people sometimes bind their own hands in advance, does that show that weakness of will is really just a problem of intertemporal bargaining, or does it show that reason can recruit external tools without reducing the conflict to preference curves?

I'm especially interested in whether there's a real distinction here or whether one framework just redescribes the other. If you think they diverge, can you construct an ordinary-life scenario—not a trolley case, just a mundane situation—where the economic account predicts one behavior and the philosophical account predicts a different one? And if they do converge, which feature of the case is doing the work: the temporal structure of the choice, the presence or absence of an explicit judgment at the moment of action, or the way the agent relates to her own future stages?
