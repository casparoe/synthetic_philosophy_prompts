I am composing this request in the voice of a novelist who spent years in moral philosophy before turning to fiction, and I need a retirement toast for Dr. Elena Vasquez, my former doctoral advisor, to be delivered at an intimate university dinner where half the guests are moral philosophers intimately familiar with her work, and the other half are family members who have never encountered technical ethics. Vasquez has long defended the austere position that supererogation is not merely uncommon but conceptually incoherent: she holds that there are only obligatory actions, permissible actions that carry no significant moral weight, and impermissible ones, with nothing left over for the category of good-but-not-required. She lived this view with uncommon consistency, refusing awards framed as recognition for exceeding duty and once returning a humanitarian citation with the explanation that if the labor had been genuinely valuable, it was already obligatory, and anything less was unworthy of praise. Because the philosophical idea must shape the speech structurally rather than decorate it, the toast must be deliverable only by someone who accepts her framework: every conventional form of gratitude must be reconstructed or abandoned, so that praising her for generosity beyond the call becomes impossible without self-contradictory sentiment.

The audience is divided precisely along the line of expertise, so the speech must work for philosophers who will notice whether I correctly identify the deontic distinctions she collapses, while also moving her sister and nephews who will not follow formal argument. I want an address of about five minutes, set in a formal but warm dining hall, that avoids both biographical lists and sentimental abstraction. Instead, it should build its emotional architecture around a single conceptual pivot: if there is no supererogatory realm, then honor must be reconceived not as applause for exceptional virtue but as acknowledgment of a life in which obligation and personal identity became indistinguishable. The toast should let the family feel the human weight of a life lived without the comfort of self-congratulation, and it should let the philosophers recognize that the language of praise has been rebuilt from within her own premises rather than applied from outside. Please write this so that it functions simultaneously as an act of personal respect and a philosophical demonstration — a speech that respects Vasquez's denial of supererogation by finding a vocabulary of gratitude that does not depend on the very category she spent her career dissolving.
