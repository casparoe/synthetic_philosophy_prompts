I am presently taking a course in Philosophy of Law and Punishment, and I shall be presenting a seminar paper next Thursday to a specialised audience of legal philosophers and constitutional theorists. The thesis I wish to defend is somewhat unconventional: rather than relying on the familiar consequentialist objection that capital punishment risks executing the innocent, I argue that the death penalty ought to be abolished because it creates what I call an asymmetry of moral repair that structurally undermines both retributive and deterrent justifications. On my account, the state possesses institutional mechanisms to rectify wrongful imprisonment—compensation, release, public acknowledgement—but it has no corresponding mechanism to undo an execution of an innocent person; this irreversibility generates a form of structural humility that ought to constrain punitive authority irrespective of how low empirical error rates might be driven through procedural safeguards. The audience is largely composed of scholars who are sympathetic to retributivism—several defend some version of proportionality or desert-based limits—yet they tend to treat the risk of wrongful execution as a contingent, administratively soluble problem rather than a categorical moral objection. They are also generally sceptical of appeals to epistemic humility that limit state power, viewing such moves as either insufficiently precise or as improperly importing decision-theoretic vocabulary into normative jurisprudence.

Please prepare me for the objections I am likely to face during the question period. I would like the objections ordered by how probable and damaging they are, phrased in the vocabulary and conceptual commitments that this particular audience would actually employ—distinctions between procedural and substantive justice, the separation of moral luck from institutional responsibility, and the boundary between epistemic risk and moral risk. For each objection, please indicate where it carries genuine force and ought to be conceded in part, where it can be deflected, and where my reply is weakest. Crucially, please identify the one objection that, if raised and not answered convincingly, will cause the proposal to fail with this group, and suggest the precise reply that must land within the brief time available for response. I should also like you to flag the objection I am secretly hoping nobody asks—perhaps whether my argument implicitly prohibits all irreversible state actions, or whether structural humility collapses into a general scepticism about punishment altogether. Please present this as a prep sheet rather than a full defence of my position, and please write your answer in American English.
