I'm taking a philosophy of mind course and trying to work out my own position on whether behavioral criteria alone can establish that a system genuinely understands something, particularly in cases where internal experience is either absent or inaccessible. Here are my verdicts on several concrete cases, followed by a tentative principle I'm testing.

Case 1: A large language model passes an extended, hidden-judge dialogue with apparent philosophical insight and contextual awareness. Verdict: It does not genuinely understand; the performance feels like sophisticated pattern retrieval rather than responsive comprehension.

Case 2: A locked-in patient communicates only via slow eye-tracking, constructing sentences with full grammatical and contextual appropriateness but almost no affective or gestural behavior. Verdict: Understanding is fully present despite the severely restricted behavioral output.

Case 3: A hypothetical "behavioral zombie" — physically and verbally identical to a conscious human, passing every conversational and practical test, but lacking any inner experience by stipulation. Verdict: There is no understanding or mind, regardless of behavioral perfection.

Case 4: An octopus in a laboratory learns to open a novel latch mechanism and visibly adjusts its tactic when the latch is altered, yet it possesses no language at all. Verdict: There is some form of practical understanding or intelligent adaptation, though not linguistic understanding.

Case 5: A child with profound autism who uses echolalic repetition — repeating heard phrases — but deploys them in precisely the right novel contexts to request aid or signal distress, adapting the repetition to the situation. Verdict: Understanding is present, embedded within a restricted but genuinely adaptive behavioral repertoire.

Case 6: A designed AI system that explains its own reasoning in detail, modifies outputs based on meta-level instructions, and displays adaptive restructuring in genuinely novel tasks; its designers confirm it operates via deterministic retrieval with no internal experiential modeling. Verdict: I am torn. The adaptive restructuring is present, but knowing the mechanism is fully transparent and lacks any internal modeling pulls me back toward denying understanding.

My tentative principle: Behavioral criteria establish understanding only when they exhibit "responsive flexibility" — adaptive restructuring of communicative or practical behavior in response to genuinely novel disruptions, rather than retrieval from a fixed repertoire — and only when this flexibility is not fully accounted for by a transparent deterministic mechanism that lacks any internal representational modeling.

This principle seems to capture cases 1, 2, 4, and 5, but it struggles with case 3 (the zombie might display responsive flexibility without understanding) and with case 6 (responsive flexibility is present, yet I still hesitate).

Could you help me refine, repair, or replace this principle? Specifically:

- Does it coherently accommodate cases 1 through 5, and if not, which clause needs revision?
- What does the resulting principle predict for a case I haven't listed: a sleepwalker who verbally responds to direct questions with grammatically correct but contextually inappropriate sentences, apparently retrieving scripted phrases?
- Is there a simpler principle that saves most of my verdicts only by overturning one — likely either case 3 or case 6? If so, which verdict is more defensible to drop, and what does the simpler principle say?

Please state the principle precisely, indicate which case motivates each clause, offer its verdict on the sleepwalker case, and be explicit about which of my original verdicts it cannot accommodate.
