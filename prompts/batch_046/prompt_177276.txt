In my aesthetics seminar, students frequently invoke the maxim “separate the art from the artist” as if it were a self-evident principle rather than a compressed family of claims. Recently, one student used it to dismiss two distinct challenges: first, that the discovery of a director’s financial fraud should lower our aesthetic evaluation of the film itself; second, that a museum has an institutional obligation to deaccession a sculpture once its creator is revealed to have committed sustained plagiarism. The student treated the slogan as resolving both disputes identically.

Please provide a precise disambiguation. Identify the separate theses the maxim could be expressing—ontological, aesthetic-evaluative, epistemic, institutional, and psychological—classifying each as either a near-triviality, a substantive and defensible position, or an actual falsehood. Then, for each of the two challenges I mentioned, specify which interpretation of the slogan is actually being relied upon, and whether that interpretation genuinely entails the student’s conclusion. Present your analysis as a menu of readings with verdicts, not as advocacy for a single interpretation. Do not supply bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; proceed entirely by conceptual analysis.
