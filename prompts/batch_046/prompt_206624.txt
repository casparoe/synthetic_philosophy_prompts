I am working out my actual view of valid consent, testing it against concrete judgments rather than adopting a pre-existing framework. Below are six verdicts, including one pair I find genuinely hard to reconcile.

Case 1 — Risk understatement in medicine: A surgeon states that the risk of paralysis is 2% when the documented risk is 18%. The patient consents. Later, upon learning the true figure, the patient insists they still would have consented regardless. Verdict: the consent is invalid.

Case 2 — Economic pressure for tissue donation: A well-funded research institution offers an impoverished farmer forty thousand dollars — approximately ten years of local income — for bone-marrow donation. The farmer fully understands the medical risks, needs the funds for his children's education rather than for immediate survival, and consents without any explicit threat. Verdict: the consent is invalid.

Case 3 — Fluctuating cognitive retention: A patient with mild cognitive impairment (MMSE 26/30) consents to chemotherapy. At the moment of signing she understands the risks, but she cannot retain them for more than two hours. She independently reconfirms consent at three separate pre-treatment visits. Verdict: the consent is valid.

Case 4 — Implicit institutional coercion: A prisoner is explicitly told that study participation is voluntary and will not affect parole. In practice, guards grant unacknowledged privileges to participants, and the prisoner is aware of this implicit reward structure when signing. Verdict: the consent is invalid.

Case 5 — Relational deception before intimate disclosure: Before consenting to share intimate images, one partner falsely claims to be in a monogamous relationship. The consenter later says they would have shared regardless, but that the lie created the emotional safety that made consent feel appropriate. Verdict: the consent is valid.

Case 6 — Contractual misrepresentation by omission: A job applicant is told verbally that the contract contains no non-compete clause; page twelve of the document includes one. The applicant signs without reading the full text. Verdict: the consent is valid.

Is there a single principle — integrating conditions on deception, coercion, and competence — that accommodates all six? If a unified principle requires overturning one verdict, say which and why. State the principle precisely enough that it predicts the outcome for an unlisted case: consent given to an AI-mediated diagnosis by a patient who understands the algorithm's recommendation but not the statistical properties of its training data. Finally, identify the specific pair of verdicts above that is genuinely incompatible rather than merely tension-ridden, and explain whether resolving that tension demands a revision to the deception condition, the coercion condition, or the competence condition.
