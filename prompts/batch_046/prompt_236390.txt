I'm working through my own stance on scientific realism and underdetermination for a philosophy of science seminar, and I'd like a consistency audit before I present it. Here are the commitments I currently hold together:

(a) A modest structural realist view: successful mature theories approximate real relational structures in nature, though we should remain agnostic about the intrinsic nature of the entities that occupy those structures.

(b) The Duhem-Quine thesis in its global form: for any theoretical system and any apparently recalcitrant experience, the system can be preserved by making suitable adjustments elsewhere in the auxiliary web.

(c) Underdetermination is not a temporary artifact of incomplete data but a permanent, pervasive feature: for every well-confirmed theory in a mature science, there will always exist genuinely distinct rivals that match it on every possible observation.

(d) Theoretical virtues—simplicity, explanatory unification, and coherence with established background knowledge—provide rational, non-merely pragmatic grounds for choosing among empirically equivalent rivals.

(e) Where two theories are empirically equivalent in principle, with isomorphic empirical substructures across all possible observations, they should be treated as representational variants of one underlying account rather than as competing descriptions of reality.

(f) Scientific progress is best understood as monotonic approximation to the truth, and we are sometimes epistemically justified in claiming that theory T2 is closer to the truth than T1 even when both are empirically adequate and no further observation could presently discriminate them.

Which of these actually pull against one another, which pairs only appear to conflict but can be reconciled, and what is the least costly revision to restore consistency? Please rank the tensions by severity rather than treating them all as equally damaging, and for each serious tension indicate what exactly would have to be given up or qualified. In particular, distinguish between what is broadly settled in the philosophy of science literature and what remains genuinely contested among specialists—such as the scope of the Duhem-Quine thesis, the epistemic status of theoretical virtues, and the viability of structural realism—and do not present any disputed interpretation as if it were the consensus view.
