Consider Aristotle's distinction in the Politics and the Nicomachean Ethics between oikonomia, the management of household goods oriented toward living well, and chrematistike, the art of acquiring wealth without natural limit; his claim that money (nomisma) was invented by convention solely to facilitate the exchange of necessary goods; and his argument that tokos—interest or usury—is contrary to nature because currency is unproductive and should not reproduce itself like a living organism. Now imagine Aristotle is asked to evaluate a modern institutional instrument: a dynamic social-impact bond in which private capital funds preventive public-health interventions, repayment is contingent upon independently verified outcome metrics such as reduced hospitalization rates rather than upon elapsed time alone, the yield scales inversely with the speed of achieving the target, and the contract is continuously repriced on a secondary market fed by real-time epidemiological data. Would Aristotle classify the primary purpose of this arrangement as serving genuine communal need, placing it within oikonomia, or as chrematistike? Would his specific objection to tokos extend to its yield structure, given that the return derives from measured social utility rather than from temporal lending? And how might his view of money's proper function—as a stable measure of need—bear on the algorithmic fluctuation of value tied directly to human health outcomes?
