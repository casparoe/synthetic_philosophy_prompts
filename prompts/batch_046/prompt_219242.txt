Please draft a short policy memo for circulation before Thursday's meeting of the University's Interdisciplinary Curriculum Oversight Board. You serve as a voting member.

A coalition from the physics and philosophy departments has proposed a graduate certificate in Non-Standard Computation and Physical Oracles. The curriculum would train students to design analog devices—relying on thermal noise, continuous dynamical systems, and quantum stochastic processes—that proponents claim can solve specific problem instances lying outside the class of Turing-computable functions. Their central argument is that the Church-Turing thesis ought to be read as an empirical claim about the limits of digital architectures, not as a definitional convention or a necessary mathematical truth. Consequently, they maintain, constructing and studying physical processes that appear to transcend those limits is legitimate scientific work, and refusing to credential it would be an unjustified philosophical prejudice.

Opposing board members argue the opposite: that the thesis functions partly as a definitional boundary—anything that genuinely computes can, in principle, be simulated by a Turing machine—so that the proposed program rests on a conceptual confusion between physical causation and computation. They warn that university approval would grant institutional legitimacy to inquiry that collapses this distinction and misdirects resources.

The board must vote to approve, reject, or approve with conditions. Our charter requires a concrete recommendation plus reasoning that addresses the strongest argument on the other side. Please write the memo recommending one of these outcomes, including an explicit timeline or set of conditions if you do not recommend outright rejection—for instance, a pilot cohort with an external review after eighteen months, or specific curricular requirements that separate empirical engineering from conceptual claims. The memo should engage seriously with the epistemic status of the Church-Turing thesis—whether it operates as a theorem, an empirical law, or a conventional definition—without treating the matter as settled by assertion. It should also respond directly to the concern that the university must avoid credentialing work built on a category error, giving that worry full weight even if the recommendation ultimately overrides it. State clearly what motion the board should adopt on Thursday and what follow-up actions, if any, are required before the end of the academic year.
