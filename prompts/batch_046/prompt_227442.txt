Write a pantoum—eight quatrains following the classic Malay repetition scheme in which the second and fourth lines of each stanza become the first and third of the next—for the closing of a graduate seminar that has spent the term comparing Buddhist anatta with Western theories of narrative identity. The speaker should be an internal voice attempting, moment by moment, to pin down a persistent self across successive states of awareness, yet the poem must never allow that voice to sound authoritative or settled.

Structure the repetition so that the only lines permitted to recur are the five skandhas—form, feeling, perception, mental formations, and consciousness—each introduced in its own quatrain and then carried forward, interlocking with the others across the stanzas. Every line that contains a first-person claim, a possessive pronoun, or a narrative continuity assertion ("I recall," "my past," "the same consciousness") must be one of the non-repeating lines: it appears once, is displaced in the next stanza, and never returns. Thus the form itself performs the doctrine: the aggregates persist and interweave, while the self is always a fresh grammatical insertion with no carry-over.

In the final two quatrains, bring all five skandha-lines together in a single stanza that contains no first-person reference whatsoever, followed by a closing couplet in which the repetition scheme resolves on the aggregates alone. The result should be readable as a lyric poem by someone unfamiliar with either pantoums or Buddhist metaphysics, yet precise enough that a specialist recognises exactly why the absence of repetition in the self-lines enacts anatta rather than merely describing it.
