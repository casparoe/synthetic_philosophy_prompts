As a high school student working through a debate on historical memory, I hold the view that comparing atrocities is not only permissible but necessary—provided we compare their “moral architectures” (the structured relations between intentional design, institutional complicity, and victim agency) rather than reducing them to body counts or aggregate suffering scores. Without such comparison, I think we risk a moral flattening that makes every tragedy equally unthinkable and therefore equally unlearnable, and we lose the capacity to distinguish catastrophic negligence from engineered extermination. But I want an honest map of what could defeat this position. Would it be undermined by a demonstration that any cross-case comparison necessarily instrumentalizes victims? By formal arguments that qualitative moral dimensions are incommensurable in principle? By historical or psychological evidence that societies which rank past evils consistently misallocate justice and memorial resources? I also want to know whether the defense of comparison has been quietly qualified into unfalsifiability—if defenders retreat to “we compare architectures but never rank them,” does that save the view or empty it of practical force? And is the opposing claim—that ranking atrocities is inherently obscene—a defensible moral stance rooted in respect rather than in emotional confusion? Please distinguish decisive defeaters from merely awkward ones, say what defenders of comparison should themselves concede, and assess whether this position actually risks anything.
