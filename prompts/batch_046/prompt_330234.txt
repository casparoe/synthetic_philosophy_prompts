I'm writing up my views for a metaethics and normative-theory reading group and want a consistency check before I present. Here is where I stand on moral dilemmas:

(1) Genuine moral dilemmas are possible: there are situations in which an agent is subject to two or more all-things-considered obligations that are jointly unsatisfiable, so that every available action violates a real obligation.
(2) The wrongness here is not merely epistemic: it is false that every apparent dilemma conceals a hidden correct option we have failed to identify; some conflicts are structurally unavoidable given the circumstances and the principles at stake.
(3) Moral realism holds: moral properties are mind-independent, and for every situation there is a correct normative description specifying what is required, permitted, and forbidden.
(4) The ought-implies-can principle governs culpability but not the existence of obligations: in a genuine dilemma the agent is blameless for the specific wrong she commits, since she lacked any morally acceptable course of action.
(5) Even so, she should feel a distinctive self-directed moral emotion—residual responsibility—that is not guilt and does not presuppose that she could have acted differently in any morally relevant sense.
(6) Reflective equilibrium should treat well-confirmed, structurally stable dilemmas as evidence that our principle-set is incomplete or needs finer distinctions, not as proof that moral reality itself contains contradictions; dilemmas motivate theoretical refinement rather than metaphysical conflict.

Audit this: which pairs are genuinely in tension, how severe is each, and what is the cheapest fix? Identify pairs that only look tense but dissolve on inspection, especially the relation between (4) and (5). Rank the tensions by severity, say what each fix costs, and tell me which single revision—dropping or weakening one commitment—would clean up the most overall. Be specific about whether (3) can survive intact if (2) is kept, and whether (6) forces me to abandon (1). Keep the audit to one paragraph, roughly 250–300 words.
