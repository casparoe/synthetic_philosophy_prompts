I am trying to advise a friend, Maya, who was publicly corrected and mildly mocked by a senior colleague during a team presentation. The colleague pointed out an error in her analysis with a tone that made her feel humiliated in front of clients. Maya now sees three possible responses, and she wants to know which one reflects the right way to value oneself. The first option is to demand a formal apology and some institutional acknowledgment that her professional reputation was unfairly damaged; she feels that letting the insult stand would make her complicit in her own diminishment. The second option is to treat the incident as irrelevant to her worth: she should correct the error, ignore the social slight, and proceed as if her value depends only on her competence, not on others' opinions. The third option is to treat the harm as a defining moral fact: she should document the episode, seek accommodations or redress, and frame her experience as a legitimate basis for special consideration, on the grounds that recognizing victimization is necessary to protect people like her. Maya also notes that the second option seems like a luxury available mainly to those who already enjoy high status, while the third risks locking her into a permanent identity defined by the injury. I want to help her, but I cannot tell whether defending one's social standing, asserting an unconditional internal worth, or embracing the injury as a source of moral authority is the most defensible stance. How should I think about the comparison between these three attitudes, and which one should I encourage her to adopt if I care about both her flourishing and the kind of culture our workplace should become?
