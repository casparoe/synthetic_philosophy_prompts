As a retired experimental physicist who has spent the past few years working through the philosophy of mind rather than the laboratory, I have become preoccupied with Daniel Dennett's dismantling of what he calls the Cartesian theater—the intuitive but erroneous picture of consciousness as a private screening room in which a unitary self watches a single, finished narrative unfold. In its place, Dennett proposes the multiple drafts model: there is no central observer and no canonical stream of consciousness, only competing, parallel narratives that are continuously revised, with "the" conscious experience being whatever draft happens to dominate at a given moment without ever receiving a final, authoritative edit. I find this framework compelling, yet I also wonder whether it fully captures the phenomenology of selfhood under conditions of radical mnemonic disruption, particularly when external artifacts begin to substitute for internal consolidation and when predictive processing—where cognition operates through hierarchical predictions updated by error signals—fails to correct a locked-in narrative.

With that in mind, I would like to read Christopher Nolan's film Memento through precisely this lens. The protagonist, Leonard Shelby, suffers from anterograde amnesia and attempts to maintain continuity by inscribing notes, Polaroids, and tattoos—essentially externalizing the drafting process that Dennett locates within the brain. The film's reverse chronological structure further denies the audience any stable, linear narrative, forcing us to assemble meaning retrospectively, much as Leonard must reconstruct a self from scattered, contradictory fragments. I am particularly interested in whether the film ultimately vindicates Dennett or quietly smuggles the Cartesian theater back in: does Leonard's relentless pursuit of revenge presuppose a single, enduring subject who authorizes each draft, or does his identity genuinely dissolve into the distributed network of inscriptions and interpretations he leaves behind? Furthermore, Leonard's tattoos declaring that "John G. raped and murdered my wife" function like top-down predictions that are never updated by bottom-up experience, locking one draft into permanent dominance. Does the film recognize this as a pathological failure of the drafting mechanism itself, or does it treat Leonard's constructed narrative as tragically authentic?

What I hope for is a reading that takes both the film and Dennett's philosophy seriously without reducing either to an illustration of the other. Which structural choices—say, the labeling of the Polaroid of Teddy, the ambiguity surrounding Sammy Jankis, or the final revelation about Leonard's own complicity—best illuminate the tension between distributed narrative construction and the longing for a unified observer? Where does Memento resist the multiple drafts model, perhaps by insisting that emotional coherence and moral accountability require something like a continuous self that transcends any particular draft? And ultimately, what does the film add to the philosophical debate about consciousness and personal identity that abstract argument alone tends to miss?
