I will defend the following thesis at next Thursday's philosophy-of-mind colloquium: that personal identity is not bounded by a single biological organism but tracks the topological continuity of an integrated cognitive manifold. Drawing on Douglas Hofstadter's idea that a self is essentially a persistent pattern capable of surviving in the minds of others, I argue that if two human brains are linked by sustained, bidirectional, high-bandwidth neural coupling—achieving full integration of working memory, affective valence, and deliberative control—then they do not constitute two selves in communion but one distributed "I" spanning two bodies. Pre-linking, there are two selves; post-linking, there is one self distributed across two crania, analogous not to conjoined twins but to a single brain whose hemispheres have been spatially separated into distinct skulls. The audience is precisely this mix: three analytic metaphysicians (all committed to some form of animalism or organicism about personal identity, including one who follows Peter van Inwagen's view that living organisms are the only composite objects), two cognitive neuroscientists with direct experience of split-brain and inter-brain BCI research, and one phenomenologist convinced that any functionalist account of the self necessarily misses the first-person structure of consciousness.
