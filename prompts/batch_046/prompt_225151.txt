I'm preparing a consistency check for my philosophy-of-science seminar, focusing on Quine's legacy for moral philosophy. Here is my package: (1) Quinean ontological naturalism—no moral properties belong in our best scientific ontology. (2) Rejection of moral error theory, on the grounds that moral judgments are indispensable nodes in the holistic web of belief, functioning analogously to theoretical posits without carrying ontological commitment. (3) The evolutionary debunking of morality is defanged by holism: since evolutionary biology itself is revisable within the web, its genealogy of moral belief cannot uniquely undermine moral convictions. (4) Nevertheless, moral norms possess an internal normative authority—recognizing wrongness supplies a non-derivative reason for action that is not analyzable as mere coherence-maintenance. (5) I reject the conventionalist reading of Quine on ethics and instead defend what I call austere moral cognitivism: moral truths are the unique equilibrium-fixed points of reflective equilibrium constrained by ontological parsimony, not by correspondence to non-natural entities. Do these hang together? In particular, does (4) inject a metaphysical extra that (1) and (2) forbid, or can it be read as a pragmatic feature of the web's architecture? Does (5) collapse into a realism that violates (1), and is there a stable middle ground? Also, is the tension between (2) and (3) genuine, or does it dissolve once we distinguish the revisability of evolutionary premises from the retention of moral nodes? Please rank the tensions by severity and, for each, state the least costly revision.
