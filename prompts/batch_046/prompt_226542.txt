You are grading a final examination for an upper-division seminar on the philosophy of labor and economic life. The examination asks students to evaluate a synthetic thesis that combines elements from Weber's analysis of the Protestant ethic with contemporary labor-pathology and labor-rights cases.

The exam question reads as follows:

"Consider the thesis that the Protestant work ethic, as Weber describes it, contains the normative resources both to justify bounded work time (exemplified by the institutional 35-hour week) and to produce unbounded labor that collapses leisure entirely (exemplified by karoshi, death through overwork). To assess this thesis, distinguish three internal structures of the ethic: (i) vocation or calling (Beruf) as a divinely assigned station in which labor is performed; (ii) labor as a potential visible sign of election, generating psychological pressure to demonstrate salvation through productivity; and (iii) ascetic self-denial specifically directed at consumption and pleasure rather than at labor itself. For each structure, explain whether it supports, undermines, or remains neutral toward the legal or moral institutionalization of bounded work hours. Then argue whether karoshi represents the logical extreme of the ethic or rather a structural failure of it, using your three distinctions as premises. Do not rely on external citations; build your argument from the conceptual logic of these three structures."

Grade it on a standard scale (for example, A through F, or a numerical score with justification), and provide specific point-by-point comments that justify the grade. You are grading another instructor's examination paper; do not signal your own agreement or disagreement with the thesis being evaluated, but assess the student's reasoning, use of distinctions, and logical steps with precision. Student answer:

"The Protestant ethic has three parts. First, the calling, or Beruf, means that every occupation is a divine command to stay in one's assigned role and work diligently within it. Second, labor proves election: because Calvinists could not know if they were saved, they treated hard work and economic success as signs of God's favor. Weber actually treats these two as the same mechanism—both are about seeing work as a religious duty that demonstrates salvation—so together they push people to work constantly. Third, asceticism is not about avoiding work but about avoiding consumption: you save and reinvest instead of enjoying leisure goods.

Using these, I can explain both cases. The 35-hour week is supported by the calling: if work is a station, you need rest to preserve your ability to fulfill it, so bounded hours become an instrumental requirement of the vocation. This is essentially the secular version of the Sabbath logic that was always inside Beruf. Meanwhile, karoshi happens when the election-anxiety part takes over: people work until death because they need to keep proving they are saved.

However, there is a deeper point. Weber says asceticism targets consumption, not labor time, so the ethic itself never sets a limit on hours worked. From this it follows that karoshi is not a failure or contradiction of the ethic, but its perfect fulfillment: if labor proves salvation and all consumption is forbidden, then every available hour should be devoted to production. The 35-hour week, by contrast, introduces leisure for its own sake—rest that is not instrumental to further labor—which directly violates the ascetic prohibition on non-productive enjoyment. Therefore the 35-hour framework is actually a betrayal of the Protestant ethic, while karoshi is its logical terminus."
