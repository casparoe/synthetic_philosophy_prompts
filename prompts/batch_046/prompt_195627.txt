In the novel I am drafting, one of my characters, Dr. Elias Vorn, is a historian of culture whose judgments on public memory projects have become a running motif. His rulings so far: he endorsed a city monument that celebrated a revolutionary organizer while embedding a critical gallery of his failures, arguing that honest inspiration requires both monumental and critical elements; he rejected a digital archive that proposed to preserve every handwritten grocery list of a defunct commune with no curatorial narrative, calling it antiquarian suffocation; he rejected a university syllabus designed solely to dismantle every heroic narrative of the national past without offering any shared framework, warning that pure critical history produces paralysis; he endorsed a family practice in which ancestral letters are preserved but each generation is permitted to burn those that no longer speak to present life, praising this as living antiquarianism tempered by critical selection; and he rejected a proposed memorial that listed only the betrayals of a revered figure, with no commemorative context, seeing it as critical history that forgets how to forget.

The museum board now proposes a temporary, mobile installation reconstructing a single decisive moment from a recent labor strike—using synthetic voices and haptic immersion, with no physical artifacts retained, no interpretive commentary, and no acknowledgment of the strike's internal divisions—intended solely for deployment at active picket lines to renew collective resolve. Predict Vorn's verdict, identify whether he is running a principle of life-service (history is valuable only when it enhances present vitality and agency, regardless of modal purity) or of modal integration (history is valuable only when it combines at least two of Nietzsche's monumental, antiquarian, and critical modes), state which of his prior rulings is the closest analog, and name the kind of further case—say, a hybrid practice that integrates all three modes yet clearly overwhelms present action—that would settle between the two rules. Please also say what argument or consideration would most likely change your interpretation of Vorn's implicit rule, and where your reasoning about his verdict is most vulnerable to failure.
