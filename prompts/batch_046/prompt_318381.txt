I was at a local rationalist meetup last week where two regulars—Jordan and Casey—got into a sustained disagreement about whether global workspace theory forces us to identify access consciousness with phenomenal consciousness, and they used a hypothetical architecture they called "partitioned broadcasting" to probe the boundary. I stayed neutral in the room, and I want a point-by-point adjudication: who is right about which specific move, what deeper crux are they actually circling, and is either of them missing an important distinction?

Jordan opened by framing global workspace theory as a theory of access consciousness: information becomes conscious when it is globally broadcast to multiple cognitive subsystems. If that is the mechanism, he said, phenomenal consciousness is either the same functional state or an unnecessary ontological duplicate. He pointed to blindsight as the canonical case—no broadcasting, hence neither access nor phenomenal consciousness present. The elegance of the theory, on his view, is that it explains all the behavioral and reportability data without positing a mysterious extra layer.

Casey introduced the partitioned-broadcasting hypothetical: imagine a cognitive architecture with two independent global loops running in parallel, each broadcasting information within its own loop but with no cross-loop integration. Within loop A, information about color is available to memory, planning, and verbal report; within loop B, information about shape is available to the same local subsystems. By Jordan's broadcasting criterion, both loops have access consciousness. Casey argued that there is no reason to postulate a single unified phenomenal field across the loops—there might be two separate phenomenal consciousnesses, or perhaps neither loop achieves true phenomenal consciousness because phenomenal unity requires a single workspace rather than parallel ones.

Jordan replied that Casey was smuggling in an unobservable metaphysical condition. If loop A can report "I experience red" and loop B can report "I experience square," then each loop has both access and phenomenal consciousness. To insist that "true" phenomenal consciousness requires cross-loop integration is to define phenomenal consciousness as whatever is missing in the partitioned case, which makes the distinction circular and unscientific. If the extra condition makes no functional difference, it is not a real distinction.

Casey pushed back that the functional difference lies in the structure of reportability itself. In a unified workspace, reports about different modalities are mutually accessible and can be synthesized into a single narrative—"I see a red square." In the partitioned architecture, loop A can report red and loop B can report square, but neither loop can report the conjunction. That structural limitation in integrated reportability is precisely what phenomenal unity explains, so the distinction is not circular but empirically anchored in the architecture of access.

Jordan then raised split-brain patients as a counterexample. In well-known clinical cases, the left hemisphere can report seeing a stimulus that the right hemisphere cannot access. Some researchers interpret this as one consciousness with partial access; others as two. Jordan said Casey's view predicts that whenever access is partitioned, phenomenal consciousness should also fragment. But the left hemisphere typically reports a unified, continuous experience despite lacking access to right-hemisphere information. Therefore, access can be fragmented while phenomenal consciousness remains unified within the left hemisphere—which undermines Casey's claim that phenomenal unity tracks workspace integration.

Casey answered that split-brain cases actually support her position. The left hemisphere's reports are unified precisely because its own internal workspace remains intact. The right hemisphere likely has its own unified phenomenal field, which we cannot observe because we only have access to the left hemisphere's reports. So partitioning does not destroy phenomenal consciousness; it creates separate ones. The point is not that phenomenal consciousness requires global access across the entire brain, but that within any given conscious system, access and phenomenal consciousness are co-extensive and unified. You cannot have access within a loop without phenomenal consciousness within that same loop, and you cannot have phenomenal consciousness without the corresponding access structures.

Jordan's final move was that Casey had made phenomenal consciousness unobservable in principle. Since we only ever observe access behaviors—reports, planning, integration—postulating a phenomenal aspect that always accompanies access within a loop but never comes apart from it adds nothing to the explanation. By the principle that we should not multiply entities beyond what the functional architecture requires, we should treat phenomenal consciousness as identical to the unified broadcasting state within a loop, not as a separate property.

Casey closed by saying that Jordan was conflating methodological parsimony with ontological identity. Just because two properties always co-occur within a system does not mean they are the same property. The question is whether there is a conceptual distinction with explanatory work to do. She claimed it does: the concept of phenomenal consciousness explains why unified reportability feels like something from the inside, whereas the concept of access only explains information flow.

Then identify the deeper crux they are actually disputing—whether it is about the coherence of fragmented consciousness, about the epistemic status of first-person reports, or about something else entirely—and say who, if anyone, is closer to getting that crux right. Finally, note what important consideration both of them seem to be ignoring in their discussion of global workspace theory and phenomenal unity. Do not provide citations, bibliographies, or reading lists; work only with the arguments as presented.
