I'm prepping a lecture on Mill's later political economy and keep circling back to how weirdly modern his concerns feel, except he never lived to see the actual machinery of modern capitalism. In his late writings he was genuinely sympathetic to socialist experiments—especially worker-owned cooperatives and profit-sharing—but he kept warning that any system run by a central administrative body would crush initiative and turn citizens into dependants. He died in 1873, so he missed the whole 20th-century debate about planning, and certainly never encountered the idea of algorithimic management.

Here's the hypothetical I'm stuck on: suppose we have a present-day proposal where gig-economy workers collectively own the platform they labor on—say, delivery drivers or content moderators own shares in the cooperative and vote on major policies—but the actual matching, pricing, and scheduling are handled entirely by an AI system trained on efficiency metrics, with no human bureaucrat in the loop. The workers own the capital and share the surplus, but the "brain" of the firm is automated and largely opaque even to them. Would Mill likely view this as the fulfillment of his cooperative ideal, because labor controls capital and there's no state planner? Or would he think that substituting an algorithmic black box for a market or a democratic assembly just reproduces the bureaucratic nightmare he feared—except now the bureaucracy is invisible, unaccountable, and technically beyond the workers' comprehension? I want to know which of his commitments—liberty against domination, sympathy for association, suspicion of centralized intelligence—he'd weight heaviest here, and whether there's a tension in his own framework that this case exposes.
