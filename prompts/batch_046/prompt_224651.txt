I came across a summary of the epistemicism-versus-supervaluationism debate in an online philosophy forum, and I am trying to assess how trustworthy it is for a project I am working on about vagueness in aesthetic judgment. The poster writes, in effect, that epistemicism—associated with Timothy Williamson—treats vagueness as pure cognitive ignorance of sharp semantic boundaries: for any vague predicate there is a precise, determinate cutoff in its extension, and borderline cases fall determinately on one side or the other, but our epistemic limitations prevent us from knowing which side. Supervaluationism—associated with Kit Fine and Rosanna Keefe—is said to treat vagueness not as ignorance but as a failure of linguistic determinacy: there are many equally admissible ways of sharpening a vague predicate, and a statement is true only if true in every sharpening, false only if false in every sharpening, and otherwise lacking a truth-value entirely. The summary claims that the two views therefore make opposite predictions about borderline cases—epistemicists assign them hidden, determinate truth-values, while supervaluationists assign them none—while agreeing that the sorites paradox is solved by denying the tolerance principle, since both views allow a sharp cutoff somewhere, whether hidden in reality or relative to a precisification. The poster then applies this framework to aesthetic predicates like "beautiful," suggesting that epistemicism implies there is an unknown exact degree of symmetry or proportion that makes a face beautiful, whereas supervaluationism implies that different aesthetic communities may operate with different admissible precisifications, leaving no fact of the matter between them.

Can you audit this summary systematically? Please go through it point by point and indicate which parts are accurate representations of the standard positions, which parts subtly conflate claims that careful defenders keep apart, which parts overstate a modest thesis, and whether the conclusion about aesthetic predicates actually survives once the views are corrected. In particular, please separate what is settled in the philosophical literature on vagueness from what is genuinely contested among specialists, and do not present a contested interpretation—such as the claim that both views solve the sorites in essentially the same way, or that epistemicism posits physically measurable cutoffs—as though it were the consensus view.
