The debate over whether the Anthropocene should be formalized as a geological epoch—and, if so, when it began—has stalled for decades among stratigraphers, Earth-system scientists, and critical historians. One side treats the problem as empirical correlation: selecting a globally synchronous marker, whether mid-twentieth-century radionuclide fallout, plastic deposition, or nitrogen-cycle perturbation, that can serve as a stratigraphic boundary. The opposing side rejects this framing, arguing that geological periodization is an act of political ontology and that fixing a start date—whether 1610, 1784, or 1950—imposes a universal narrative of human agency that erases colonial and capitalist specificities and mistakes ethical provocation for taxonomic science. A third position holds that the concept should remain informal, useful precisely because it resists formalization.

My hypothesis is that this is not one disagreement but at least three being compressed under the single heading "when did it start and who decides": an empirical dispute about boundary signals, a methodological dispute about whether human-caused change belongs in the Geological Time Scale at all, and a normative dispute about what naming an epoch authorizes. Is that diagnosis correct? Determine whether the stall is substantive, verbal, or a case of different questions answered under one name; identify whether hidden factual disagreements, differing standards of evidence, or bedrock intuitions about scientific authority versus historical narrative are doing the work; and say what, if anything, would count as progress given your diagnosis—whether a new formal distinction separating taxonomy from ethics, a conceptual separation that lets each side answer its own question, or an argument that no resolution should be expected and why.
