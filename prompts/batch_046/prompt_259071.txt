I am designing a ninety-minute session for philosophy and economics graduate students with no prior exposure to Arrow's formal proof. I want them to understand the impossibility theorem not as a comment on democratic voting but as a structural tension among four rationality conditions applied to a collective agent facing incommensurable values. The carrying case is a university committee that must rank three funding priorities—interdisciplinary breadth, disciplinary depth, and public impact—where every member has transitive preferences yet the group cycles. Plan the teaching sequence: what conceptual groundwork must precede the conditions, the order in which to introduce the four conditions so the contradiction feels inevitable rather than contrived, the misconceptions they will arrive with (that Arrow shows democracy is impossible, that strategic voting creates the cycle, that abandoning any condition is automatically irrational) and the precise moment in the arc to address each, a single end-of-session check that distinguishes understanding collective rationality from understanding electoral mechanics, and the part of the formal derivation I should openly refuse to present at this level.
