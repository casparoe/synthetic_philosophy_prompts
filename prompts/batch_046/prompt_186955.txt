I run a philosophy-of-economics seminar with 14 graduate students in a single 75-minute session. I want an in-class game that makes participants feel the epistemic pressure behind no-trade theorems—not as an information-asymmetry puzzle, but as a collapse that happens from inside a deal once you treat your counterparty's willingness to trade as Bayesian evidence. To keep it from being a standard asset-exchange exercise, I am injecting a twist: students do not trade goods but reflective contracts whose value depends on a hidden state and on a second-order parameter, each player's private meta-belief about the reliability of other traders' reasoning. The mechanism should create a situation in which a pair constructs what looks like a mutually beneficial exchange using private signals and meta-beliefs, posts the terms within a visible framework, and then must decide whether rational acceptance is still possible given that the other party—equally rational, with the same common prior—was willing to propose or accept.

Design the activity: specify group division, roles if useful, materials, rules of negotiation, a point-based payoff scheme with no real money, and a minute-by-minute timeline. Arrange the incentives and visibility conditions so that students run into the reflective update themselves before any debrief names it. Include a debrief that maps moments in the game onto the formal components—common prior, private signal, the posterior update triggered by observed willingness to deal, and the second-order update about collective reasoning reliability. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time (for example, students treating the update as strategic posturing rather than Bayesian conditioning, or ignoring the meta-belief layer) and how to correct it in the moment. End with an honest note about which feature of the formal no-trade result—such as the continuous belief-space assumption, risk neutrality, or the absence of noise traders—this classroom setup necessarily cannot reproduce and that you will supply verbally.
