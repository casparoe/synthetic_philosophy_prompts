For an upper-level undergraduate seminar in epistemology, design a final take-home essay question (1200 words, 48 hours, open-book but no web access required) on the structural tension between the KK principle—that knowing p entails being in a position to know that one knows p—and luminosity conditions on knowledge, constructed so that a student cannot answer by summarizing standard textbook presentations. The question should introduce, within the prompt itself, a distinction between uniform luminosity (the claim holds for every case in which S knows p) and band-limited luminosity (the claim holds only when S’s epistemic position lies outside a margin-of-error zone near the boundary between knowledge and ignorance), and should ask students to apply this distinction to a concrete perceptual scenario—such as knowing that the temperature is exactly 20°C versus knowing only that it falls within the range 19–21°C—without invoking empirical psychology or cognitive-science data. Include a model answer that demonstrates exactly how the margin-for-error principle undermines uniform luminosity while leaving band-limited luminosity intact, that clarifies why the KK principle is not reducible to a simple introspective-access condition, and that explains why the failure of second-order knowledge inside blur zones does not entail the loss of first-order knowledge. Provide a marking scheme that assigns specific weight to: (i) correctly identifying the logical form of the tension; (ii) deploying the band/uniform distinction precisely in the concrete case; (iii) explaining the asymmetry between first-order and second-order knowledge near epistemic boundaries; and (iv) avoiding the specific errors of conflating luminosity with transparency of mental states, treating the argument as an empirical claim about human cognitive limits, or substituting bibliographic citation for conceptual analysis. Deduct marks for answers that rely on named authors beyond the general framework, that request further reading, or that fail to engage the constructed distinction and instead revert to generic descriptions of the anti-luminosity argument.
