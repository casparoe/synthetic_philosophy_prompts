I'm a doctoral student working at the intersection of philosophy of law and moral psychology. At a recent workshop, two colleagues—let me call them Mara and Jonas—became locked in a sustained disagreement about just deserts and punishment, drawing repeatedly on Dennett's compatibilist account of moral responsibility and Caruso's public-health model of criminal justice. I am writing up my notes and want to be sure I have reconstructed the dialectic correctly. Would you adjudicate the exchange for me?

The disagreement centered on a hypothetical case: an offender whose criminal conduct was caused by a transient, now-fully-treated neurological tumor. The offender retains full memory of the act, expresses genuine remorse, and is neurologically typical going forward. The legal system must decide how to respond.

Mara opened with a Dennett-inspired argument. Even granting determinism and the tumor's causal role, she said, punishment can be deserved in a non-metaphysical, institutionally anchored sense. The offender's actions expressed a reasons-responsive pattern that was genuinely his own, and the community's reactive attitudes are not merely predictive instruments but appropriate responses to that expressed agency. She contended that Caruso's public-health analogy fails precisely because quarantine is non-expressive: we isolate contagious individuals regardless of their attitudes, whereas punishment addresses the offender as a moral interlocutor. This addressive dimension, she insisted, is irreducible.

Jonas replied from a Caruso-inspired position. Once we accept that the tumor was the decisive causal factor, he argued, talk of expressed agency is merely a narrative overlay on a medical condition. He granted that reactive attitudes feel appropriate, but claimed that this feeling is exactly what requires debunking: just as we do not blame someone for sneezing, we should not blame someone for tumor-driven behavior. Treating punishment as addressive, he said, is a category mistake that masks preventive goals behind the language of morality.

Jonas then introduced a hypothetical technology—call it moral repair—that can restore an offender's pre-tumor psychological profile without any punitive suffering. If such repair is available, he asked, does it not show that punishment serves no necessary function? Repair achieves both social protection and the restoration of moral relations, leaving no work for suffering.

Mara countered that Jonas was presupposing a false dichotomy between punishment and repair. Even with perfect repair technology, she argued, the offender's past act still demands a normative response: the community must mark the wrong done, not merely fix the agent, or else we treat moral violations as mechanical breakdowns. She claimed that Caruso's framework cannot accommodate this marking function because it treats all responses to wrongdoing as purely instrumental.

Jonas responded that marking the wrong is either a useful social signal—in which case repair plus public acknowledgment suffices—or it is an atavistic desire for suffering disguised as normativity. He accused Mara of collapsing into a form of expressive retributivism that requires suffering for its own sake, which is precisely the metaphysically bloated view that a genuinely compatibilist account was meant to avoid.

Could you please adjudicate this exchange move by move? Specifically:

1. Is Mara correct that Caruso's public-health model overlooks an addressive or expressive dimension essential to punishment?
2. Is Jonas correct that the tumor case undermines the coherence of expressed agency?
3. Does the hypothetical moral-repair technology actually defeat Mara's position, or does it instead expose a limitation in Jonas's instrumental framework?
4. Overall, who is more right about whether just deserts survives in a deterministic world that permits full medical explanation of wrongdoing?

Please be precise about where each argument succeeds or fails, and indicate anything important that both sides miss—particularly regarding how a determinist yet reasons-responsive view might integrate technological repair without reducing punishment to either pure prevention or pure suffering.
