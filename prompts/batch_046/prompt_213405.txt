You are a philosopher of mind with expertise in Daniel Dennett’s work on personal identity, especially the “Where Am I?” brain-transplant scenario. Before answering, briefly brainstorm the competing principles that could underlie the verdicts below.

Consider a philosopher who rules consistently as follows: if a whole brain is transplanted into a new body, the person survives in that body; if every neuron is gradually replaced by synthetic functional equivalents over years with consciousness uninterrupted, the person survives in the original body; if the brain is perfectly scanned, the original destroyed, and an exact digital replica is run on a computer, that replica is not the same person; and if the brain is split with each hemisphere placed in a different body, neither recipient is the original—the original ceases, since branching is not permitted.

Now the new case: the brain is gradually replaced neuron-by-neuron with synthetic equivalents, but instead of remaining in one skull, the synthetic neurons are distributed wirelessly across three remote robotic bodies in different cities, all integrated into a single continuous first-person experience. No biological tissue remains. At the same time, a complete digital snapshot taken just before distribution was accidentally instantiated on a server, producing a virtual claimant with identical initial patterns but no causal link to the distributed system.

Predict what this philosopher should say about the distributed synthetic system and about the accidental virtual copy if she wants to stay consistent. Identify two or three rules—such as causal-functional continuity, narrative unity, spatial unity of the center, or a uniqueness constraint—that jointly fit the four earlier verdicts, state what each rule predicts about both claimants in this case, give your best-guess verdict with reasoning, and name one further hypothetical case that would best distinguish the leading rules.
