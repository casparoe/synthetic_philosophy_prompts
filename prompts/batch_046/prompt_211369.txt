For a feature I'm preparing on the so-called original-affluence thesis and its critics, I have reconstructed an argument that keeps pulling me toward a conclusion I find hard to accept: either the agricultural revolution was enforced by coercive structures for which we have no archaeological evidence, or the claim that foragers lived better is wrong, which undermines the entire mistake framing. Here is the chain.

(1) For purposes of welfare comparison, a social system should be judged by the leisure, nutritional diversity, health, and freedom from permanent domination that it affords the median participant.

(2) Ethnographic and paleopathological evidence—ranging from Levantine Pre-Pottery Neolithic assemblages to well-documented foraging groups—suggests that hunter-gatherer economies typically yielded higher leisure-to-labor ratios, broader diets, lower infectious-disease burdens, and institutionalized reverse-dominance mechanisms that actively suppressed durable hierarchies.

(3) The agricultural package—sedentism, cereal monoculture, storable surplus, and rising population density—systematically reduced leisure, narrowed diet, increased pathogen exposure, and dissolved those egalitarian checks, thereby lowering median welfare and making surplus monopolization possible.

(4) If system A offers higher median welfare and suppresses domination, while system B offers lower welfare and enables domination, then rational agents embedded in A should resist transitioning to B unless coerced.

(5) The archaeological record indicates that the shift to agriculture was gradual, occurring over millennia through mixed economies, and that the earliest Neolithic societies lacked the centralized administrative or military apparatus necessary to coerce adoption across dispersed populations.

(6) Therefore, if premises (1) through (4) are correct, the widespread adoption of agriculture is inexplicable without mass coercion, which (5) rules out.

(7) To avoid that contradiction, one must reject either the welfare comparison in (1)-(3) or the rational-resistance premise in (4).

(8) But rejecting (1)-(3) collapses the normative argument—associated with Jared Diamond, Marshall Sahlins, and others—that agriculture constituted humanity's worst mistake; rejecting (4) requires explaining why rational agents voluntarily adopted a system that made them worse off and more dominated, which seems to demand either an implausible collective-ignorance thesis or a hidden benefit, such as reproductive success or status competition, that the welfare metric in (1) deliberately excludes.

The argument feels valid, yet both escape routes seem costly. Is there a hidden equivocation—perhaps between individual rationality and collective dynamics, or between welfare and evolutionary fitness—that allows me to preserve both the affluence thesis and the absence of mass coercion? Or is one of the premises genuinely the weak link, and if so, what exactly do I have to pay to give it up?
