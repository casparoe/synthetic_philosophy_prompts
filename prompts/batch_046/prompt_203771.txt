I have been reading case studies in policy failure and organizational behavior, and I keep encountering a pattern that feels adjacent to the dollar auction yet does not quite map onto it. A large public project—say, a transit corridor, a defense procurement program, or an energy infrastructure scheme—is launched with a discrete mission, a defined set of beneficiaries, and a bounded budget. When the original targets prove unreachable or the costs exceed the initial justification, the project is rarely terminated outright; instead, it is reframed, expanded, or redirected, which in turn generates a fresh population of dependents—contractors tied to the revised specifications, neighborhoods developed around incomplete infrastructure, labor unions anchored to maintenance roles, or subsidiary agencies whose budgets now depend on the project's continuation. These dependents were not part of the original cost-benefit calculus, and they often have no stake in the project's initial purpose, yet once they exist they become vocal advocates for further funding. Decision-makers then justify continued investment by citing benefits that flow precisely to these newly created stakeholders rather than to the original mission.

That produces a recursive structure: escalation does not merely accumulate static sunk costs; it actively manufactures its own constituency, and that constituency supplies the political rationale for the next round of escalation. In the classic dollar auction, a bidder keeps going because quitting means forfeiting everything already spent; in the pattern I am describing, quitting also means dismantling a social and economic ecosystem that the project itself brought into being, so the penalty for withdrawal grows endogenously with each stage and the justification for staying shifts to protect entities that did not exist at the outset. I am trying to determine whether this specific configuration—where commitment escalates not through simple sunk-cost aversion but through the sequential creation of stakeholder lock-in that rewrites the project's own rationale—has an established label in economics, political science, or organizational theory, and whether it is usefully distinguished from nearby concepts.

Is there a precise term for this loop, and if so, how exactly does it differ from ordinary escalation of commitment, path dependence, or the dollar auction itself? If the literature does not isolate this mechanism, how should one draw its boundaries—separating it, for example, from cases where a project genuinely discovers an unforeseen but legitimate beneficial use, or from straightforward regulatory capture by pre-existing interest groups? I am less interested in receiving a bare name than in understanding the conceptual carving: what exactly belongs inside the phenomenon, what lies outside it, and whether maintaining the distinction helps explain why some projects become politically irreversible long after their original purpose has failed.
