I'm prepping a grad seminar in philosphy of biology and I've been going in circles on the species problem with a colleague over coffee — she works on microbial ecology, I do metaphysics, and we keep talking past eachother. The usual map is familiar enough: BSC people talk reproductive isolation, phylogeneticists want smallest monophyletic clades, pluralists say "species" is just a family-resemblance term that does different jobs in ornithology versus bacteriology. What's frustrating is that the empirical ground has totally shifted — we've got massive hybrid zones, whole-genome sequencing, horizontal gene transfer turning microbial trees into webs, and these weird symbiotic consortia like lichens where the fungal genome stays constant but the photosynthetic partner switches, producing morphologically distinct reproductive units that share no single genome. You'd think that kind of data would break the deadlock, but instead everyone just absorbs it into their existing framework.

My working theory is that this isn't an empirical stall at all but a disagreement about what biological classification is for — whether it's tracking real causal mechanisms or just providing predictive clusters for conservation and ecology. But I wonder if I'm missing something deeper. Is this actually a verbal dispute where "species" means different things in evolutionary dynamics versus taxonomy? Or is there a hidden normative question about whether conservation law should protect genetic lineages or ecological roles? And given whatever diagnosis you land on, could anything actually move it — a formal result in cluster analysis, a discovery about microbial chimerism that breaks every concept, or is this just a case where progress means changing the question rather than answering it?
