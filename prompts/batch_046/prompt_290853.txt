I'm the dispatch director for a small, independent search-and-rescue collective. We operate one heavy-lift helicopter that, because of an approaching storm system, can complete exactly one extraction before conditions ground all aircraft for forty-eight hours. Yesterday we faced two concurrent emergency calls. Call A concerned a single climber stranded on an exposed ridge with rapidly worsening, life-threatening injuries; Call B concerned four hikers trapped in a flooded ravine with the same critical injuries and no alternative access route. Our helicopter can reach and evacuate everyone at either site, but not both. Our operational manual directs us to maximize the number of lives preserved, which points unambiguously to Call B.

What gives me pause is not mere sentiment but a feature of our collective's governance charter: every dispatch decision must be supported by a public justification addressed individually to each affected party. I can imagine explaining to the four hikers that their collective need was greater, but I find I cannot write to the climber that her claim was somehow weaker—only that it was outnumbered. Her claim to rescue seems, in itself, fully equal to each of theirs. This suggests that our manual treats moral urgency as a quantity that can be summed, whereas the justification requirement forces me to treat each claim as non-substitutable.

Is there a recognized philosophical position that captures this tension—specifically, one that rejects the idea that saving more people is automatically better in an exclusive forced-choice scenario because each person holds an independent, non-aggregable claim? If such a view exists, how should it guide actual decision-making without collapsing into arbitrary selection? And is there a meaningful distinction between maximizing aggregate welfare and the structure of moral justification that I should be appealing to?
