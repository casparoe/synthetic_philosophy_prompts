I'm planning a ninety-minute session for a philosophy reading group of advanced undergraduates and first-year graduate students who have just worked through Rawls's Theory of Justice but have not yet encountered Scanlon. The idea I need them to grasp is Scanlon's contractualist account of wrongness—an act is wrong if and only if it is disallowed by principles that no one could reasonably reject—together with the aggregation problem that arises when a principle permitting severe harm to one individual is rejected by that individual, while a principle forbidding the harm is rejected by the many who each suffer only a slight burden. I do not want them to leave with the slogan "numbers don't count"; I want them to see why contractual justification is structurally pairwise and why importing aggregative reasoning changes the subject.

Plan the session's explanatory arc. First, specify the dependency order: which conceptual component must be firmly in place before the aggregation problem can even be posed correctly, and why beginning with Rawlsian constructivism or the veil of ignorance is actually the wrong entry point and will generate a predictable misconception. Second, identify the single carrying example that should structure the discussion. It must involve a scenario in which saving many people from moderate harm appears to justify imposing catastrophic harm on one person, but it also needs a specific feature—something about the structure of the reasons involved, not just the magnitude of harm—that blocks students from resolving the tension through simple utilitarian arithmetic. Say exactly what that example must include and what it must deliberately exclude (for instance, probabilistic risk distributed across populations, or cases where the harm is already unavoidable). Third, map the misconceptions in the order they will likely arise: the conflation of "reasonable rejection" with pure self-interest; the importation of Rawls's decision procedure as if it governed Scanlon's justification; and the assumption that Scanlon is merely a deontologist with an aversion to counting. For each, state precisely where in the sequence it should be defused and with what distinction or counter-example. Fourth, design one targeted final check—either a question or a brief task—that reveals whether the students have understood the structural point about pairwise justification rather than just repeating that aggregation is forbidden. Finally, state explicitly which part of Scanlon's broader framework—his account of well-being, his theory of reasons, or his critique of preference-satisfaction—should be openly left out and acknowledged as beyond the scope of this session.
