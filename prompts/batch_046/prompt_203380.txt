I spent most of last night in a long, unresolved argument with a close friend about whether nuclear brinkmanship is best understood as a rational strategic gamble or as a form of self-induced epistemic blindness—specifically, whether an agent can deliberately construct a situation in which they genuinely do not know if they are still capable of reversing course, without thereby becoming genuinely irrational. Tomorrow morning I am delivering the opening remarks at a small, invitation-only retreat on strategic reasoning and its limits; the audience is split evenly between nuclear historians and formal decision theorists on one side, and philosophers of action and epistemologists who have never studied deterrence theory on the other. The remarks need to land for both halves without either feeling condescended to or left behind, and they must work as a genuine philosophical reflection rather than as policy commentary or a history lecture.

Please write me an eight- to ten-minute speech structured around a conceptual framing I want to call “executive opacity”: the idea that credible brinkmanship depends not merely on concealing one’s rationality from an adversary, but on actively engineering a decision environment in which one’s own first-person authority over the choice to de-escalate is genuinely suspended, so that the usual common-knowledge structure—both sides know the other is rational—breaks down in a specific, asymmetric way. This idea should do real structural work in the speech: it should shape what I can and cannot say, meaning I should not be able to comfortably urge transparency, clearer signaling, or mutual reassurance, since the logic being presented undermines the very possibility of such moves; at the same time, I must avoid celebrating pure irrationality, because the framework requires maintaining strategic control over the appearance of lost control. Open by using the personal argument with my friend as an entry point, then develop the conceptual tension through a concrete crisis-management scenario—perhaps an escalation ladder designed so that reversal becomes technically or organizationally impossible rather than merely politically costly—so that the non-specialists can follow the mechanics while the specialists recognize the philosophical stakes. Close with an observation about what this implies for our concept of agency under extreme risk, and keep the tone formal but conversational, appropriate for a room of intelligent generalists and specialists alike.

Do not include any bibliography, reading list, or citations, and do not ask the audience to consult specific texts. If you reference the well-known idea of leaving outcomes partially to chance in strategic interaction, do so in paraphrase without quotation marks. Avoid relying on obscure technical literature or highly specific historical episodes that a well-trained model might not reliably recall; instead, build the argument out from the framing I have given you, letting the conceptual distinction carry the weight. The speech should feel like it could only have been written for this particular occasion and this particular disagreement, not like a generic overview of deterrence theory.
