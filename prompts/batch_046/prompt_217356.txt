At a philosophy reading group, two members—Maya and Leo—got into a long argument about John Stuart Mill’s late economic writings. The disagreement began when Maya claimed that Mill’s later essays and revisions to his political economy show he had effectively become a socialist, since he predicted that voluntary worker associations would eventually replace private capital and the wage system. Leo pushed back, saying Mill never abandoned liberal individualism; he merely thought cooperative enterprise should be permitted to compete with capitalism, and he repeatedly warned that any form of socialism without prior moral and educational improvement would be disastrous.

Maya answered that Leo was confusing tactical caution with theoretical conclusion. She pointed out that Mill described the capital-labor relation as the great problem of the future and argued that association could resolve it by turning laborers into partners rather than servants. For Maya, this amounts to a structural critique of capitalism, not just a liberal plea for more organizational options.

Leo replied that Mill’s conditions—widespread education, self-restraint, and competitive pressure among associations—are so demanding that they preserve the core liberal framework. If socialism must remain voluntary, competitive, and compatible with the harm principle, then Mill is not advocating socialism but extending liberty into economic organization. Leo added that Mill praised well-run capitalist firms with profit-sharing, which suggests he saw cooperation as one form among many rather than a systemic replacement.

Maya then introduced a distinction she said commentators often miss: governance socialism, in which workers democratically control production regardless of formal ownership, versus ownership socialism, in which the state or collective holds title. She argued Mill endorsed the former but rejected the latter, making him a socialist about workplace authority yet a liberal about property rights. Leo countered that if governance socialism means only voluntary cooperatives competing in a market, then the label socialism is being stretched to cover ordinary liberal reformism, and Mill’s view collapses into a preference for diverse organizational forms rather than a revolutionary economic theory.

They turned to me and asked for a point-by-point adjudication: who is right about Mill’s position on worker association, about whether his conditions undermine or support a socialist reading, and about whether the governance versus ownership distinction clarifies his view or merely redefines terms? They also wanted to know what both are missing about how Mill’s concerns with the stationary state relate to his economic predictions.

Please go through their exchange step by step, rate the strength of each major move, and say who is more right overall and about which specific issues. Also address whether the distinction between governance socialism and ownership socialism is genuinely useful here, or whether it obscures more than it reveals.
