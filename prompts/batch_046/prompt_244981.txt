I'm working through a set of intuitions about when it's legitimate to apply a "presumption of independence" in probabilistic and causal reasoning—something my reading group has been calling the independence heuristic—and I'm finding that my gut reactions form a pattern I can't quite formalize. We're not talking about statistical independence in large datasets, but about the prior or structural assumption that two pieces of evidence, predictions, or variables don't influence each other except through specified channels, and I want to know whether there's a single principle behind my judgments or whether I'm just rationalizing case by case.

Here are my verdicts on concrete cases. First, two medical diagnostic tests that rely on entirely different biochemical pathways: I think it's correct to treat their error rates as independent conditional on the patient's true condition, and I would combine them multiplicatively. Second, two expert forecasts about the same election made by analysts who share the same underlying polling data but apply different statistical models: I feel strongly that I should not treat these as independent confirmations, even if the models are mathematically distinct. Third, in a Judy Benjamin-style scenario where an agent learns the conditional "if you are in the Red squad, then you are in Division Two" without learning the unconditional fact of which division she occupies, I think she should not update her belief about being in Red versus Blue; the conditional is screened off by her prior structure. Fourth, two separate scientific instruments measuring the same physical constant using different theoretical principles, both calibrated against the same reference standard: here I go back and forth. Part of me wants to treat the measurements as independent sources of error, but another part worries that shared calibration introduces a hidden dependency that violates the presumption. Fifth, a theory that makes two surprising predictions P and Q, where P and Q are causally unrelated according to the theory's own ontology. If both come true, my intuition is that the theory receives roughly twice the confirmatory boost it would get from either alone—but if I later discover that both measurements relied on a single unexamined auxiliary assumption not part of the theory itself, I immediately want to collapse the two confirmations into one, even though the causal independence of P and Q hasn't changed.

Is there a coherent principle that captures all of these judgments, or close to it?
