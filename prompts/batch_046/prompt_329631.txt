In my department's aesthetics seminar we have been deadlocked for two semesters on whether immoral art can be genuinely good art. The autonomists hold that formal innovation, emotional depth, and expressive coherence are evaluated independently of the artist's biography or the work's moral message. The moralists hold that when a work systematically distorts moral reality—what they call representational failure—that distortion is itself an aesthetic defect that undermines excellence. Both sides accept the same canonical examples, agree on their aesthetic properties, and agree on their moral properties; yet neither has moved the other. My working theory is that the stall is not about art evaluation per se but about whether aesthetic appreciation necessarily involves moral simulation—temporarily adopting the evaluative perspective embedded in the work—so that fully appreciating a morally corrupt work requires corrupting one's own evaluative stance. Is that diagnosis correct? I want an analysis of what kind of disagreement this actually is—whether it is verbal, about hidden empirical claims in moral psychology, about competing standards for what counts as aesthetic evaluation, or about bedrock intuitions neither side can argue for—together with an honest answer about whether anything, such as a conceptual distinction, an empirical finding about audience cognition, or a reframing of the question, could actually break the impasse, or whether progress should not be expected and why.
