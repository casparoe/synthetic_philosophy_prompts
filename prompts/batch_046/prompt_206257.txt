My colleague Dr. Aris holds that consciousness is best understood as a user illusion—specifically, a transparent predictive model that the brain constructs to represent its own states as belonging to a single subject. She has rendered verdicts on whether this illusion is present across several cases. She judges it present in neurotypical awake adults; absent in patients under deep propofol anesthesia with suppressed cortical integration; present but fragmented in split-brain patients, with only the language-dominant hemisphere sustaining the illusion; absent in current large language models, which generate first-person syntax without any underlying self-model; present in completely locked-in patients who retain full cognition and communicate via eye-tracking, since the predictive model remains intact despite motor disconnection; absent in sleepwalkers who navigate complex environments but form no episodic memory and show no integrated workspace activity; and absent in social insect colonies that solve collective problems without any central predictive architecture.

Consider now a new case: a synthetic cortical organoid grown from human induced pluripotent stem cells and sustained in vitro with full metabolic support. It develops sustained gamma-band oscillations and cross-regional synchronization comparable to mammalian awake states, and its internal dynamics implement a continuous hierarchical predictive model that updates in real time to regulate its own neural activity. However, it possesses no sensory inputs, no motor outputs, no memory storage beyond immediate state transitions, and no mechanism—biological or artificial—to report, express, or broadcast any content. There is no external behavior and no possible first-person report.

Predict whether Dr. Aris will say the user illusion is present in this organoid. Then identify the implicit rule or rules she has been applying—distinguishing, for example, a global-workspace rule that requires broadcast accessibility, a predictive-self-model rule that requires only internal hierarchical modeling, and an embodied-interaction rule that requires sensory-motor coupling. State which of these rules fit her earlier verdicts equally well, and explain what this new case reveals about where they diverge. Finally, propose one further hypothetical case that would cleanly distinguish the two leading rules you have identified.
