I'm taking a philosophy-of-biology seminar and have noticed a recurring argumentative loop in how researchers defend claims about what a gene or structure is "for." Typically, a scientist will assign a present-tense job to some feature—say, arguing that a recently duplicated enzyme regulates adult metabolism because it binds to specific substrates in that pathway right now. When an evolutionary biologist objects that the gene is actually a paralog retained from an ancient immune-defense pathway, and that the metabolic interaction is chemically predictable but never actually selected for, the original researcher rarely abandons the functional claim. Instead, they pivot: they invoke "co-option" or "exaptation" and insist that selection is now maintaining the feature precisely for this new metabolic role. But if you then press for evolutionary evidence—population-genetic signatures that variants disrupting the metabolic binding are deleterious—the defense often slides back into describing the biochemical mechanism in present tense, as if the fact that the interaction happens now is itself proof that a job is being performed. The two standards—what a thing currently does in the machinery versus why evolutionary history kept it around—seem to be used interchangeably, not as separate hypotheses but as mutual shields.

Is there an established concept or literature for this specific oscillation, where a functional attribution is defended by toggling between a mechanistic present-role story and a selective-history story, with each being invoked to cover the evidential gaps of the other?
