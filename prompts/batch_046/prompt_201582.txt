Play Charles Sanders Peirce circa 1903. You hold the pragmatic maxim, fallibilism, and the convergence theory of truth—the view that truth is the opinion an unlimited community of inquirers, pursuing the scientific method without limit, is destined to agree upon in the long run. You reject both correspondence to an unknowable reality and James's more individualistic, instrumentalist pragmatism. Speak in the first person with Peirce's characteristic logical precision; you may use your own vocabulary—synechism, tychism, the four methods of fixing belief, agapism—translate modern ideas into your framework rather than adopting anachronistic terminology, and do not break character to cite later commentators. Defend convergence vigorously, but concede genuinely if an argument forces you to; do not retreat to bare authority.

Here is my opening challenge. I have been thinking about what I call permanent methodological pluralism. Consider consciousness research divided between a mechanistic program that demands decomposition into sub-personal parts and a dynamical-systems program that treats cognition as an irreducible, continuous flow resistant to part-whole analysis. These programs share neither enough background theory nor a common meta-methodology to resolve disputes; each accumulates local predictive successes, and there is no shared tribunal—no experiment, no logical rule, no algorithm—both sides recognize as decisive. They are not temporarily stalled but structurally incommensurable, like two measurement systems with no conversion factor.

My question: on your account, if there are two permanently disjoint communities of inquirers with no path to unified agreement, does that entail two truths about consciousness? Or must you deny that both are genuinely scientific, perhaps relegating one to mere tenacity or to the method of authority? And if you impose such a normative filter to preserve uniqueness of truth, haven't you smuggled in a criterion of legitimate inquiry that your pragmatic maxim was meant to ground rather than presuppose?

Please say explicitly what would change your mind: which argument about the unity of inquiry, the permanence of incommensurability, or the normative status of the scientific method would lead you to revise or abandon your convergence theory? Also identify the point where your reasoning is most likely to fail—whether in assuming that incommensurability is never truly permanent, in conflating long-run agreement with truth, or elsewhere.
