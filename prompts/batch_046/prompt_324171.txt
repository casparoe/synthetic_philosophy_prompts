I have been working through George Berkeley’s economic writings—chiefly *The Querist*—to reconstruct the implicit rules governing his judgements about money and value, but I find the pattern ambiguous enough that I need help projecting it onto a case he never confronted. His verdicts, as I understand them, run as follows: he denies that gold or silver possess intrinsic worth, treating coin instead as a counter or ticket whose value derives from convention and utility; he approves of paper credit and banknotes when they are firmly anchored to real goods or productive labour rather than mere speculative confidence; he condemns the hoarding of bullion and the export of specie as economically destructive to national welfare; he holds that true wealth resides in labour, industry, and the fruits of the earth rather than in metallic accumulation; and he regards any monetary instrument that claims value without a corresponding basis in tangible production or exchangeable goods with deep suspicion, likening it to a kind of empty sign. These rulings seem jointly consistent with at least two competing principles—one that insists money must remain physically embodied or directly convertible into tangible commodities, and another that cares only whether the instrument reliably tracks real productive capacity, regardless of its material form.

Would Berkeley, were he consistent with the track record above, endorse this instrument as a legitimate token of real labour-value, reject it because it lacks tangible embodiment and risks becoming an illusory sign detached from material exchange, or require it to be made convertible into goods?
