Write a sestina of 39 lines using the standard end-word rotation, with the six terminal words in this opening order: rain, believe, then, now, true, false. In the first stanza, present the classic present-tense Moore-paradoxical assertion—something like “it is raining, but I do not believe it”—so that the lines close with those six words in sequence. Through the successive rotations, gradually shift the grammatical tense and temporal adverbs so that the assertion concerns the past (“it was raining then”) and the belief denial is also displaced in time, structurally enacting the standard philosophical observation that the past-tense version avoids absurdity. By the three-line envoi, gather all six end-words together with “rain” paired near “then” and “believe” paired near “now,” leaving the temporal separation visible but the underlying tension unresolved. Use unrhymed lines except for the repeated end-words; keep the diction modern, plain, and free of technical terms like “performative contradiction”; and do not cite philosophers or texts—let the rotation of the terminal words and the tense shift carry the entire argument.
