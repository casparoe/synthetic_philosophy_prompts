I am a student in an upper-level seminar on procreation ethics and population philosophy, and our instructor has asked each of us to design a runnable workshop exercise for next week’s session. The setting is a single 75-minute meeting with thirteen peers, so fourteen participants total, and the group is familiar with basic normative ethics but has not yet worked through antinatalist arguments in detail. I want an activity that makes Benatar’s asymmetry between harm and benefit in non-existence feel operational rather than diagrammatic: the claim that the absence of suffering is good even without a subject to enjoy it, while the absence of pleasure is not bad without a subject to be deprived of it.

To avoid a standard lecture-format, I want to inject a registry-simulation framing that is not drawn directly from the literature. Each participant draws one of three sealed roles by lot: Prospective Parent, Potential Child (who may or may not be instantiated), or Empty-Chair Adjudicator representing a being that will never exist. The Empty-Chair Adjudicators hold veto cards that can block any proposed birth, but they possess no claim-cards that could demand one. The group receives a fixed pool of well-being tokens to allocate across proposed lives, but the scoring rules encode the asymmetry mechanically: spending tokens to prevent suffering to an Empty-Chair Adjudicator yields positive moral credit, while spending tokens to provide pleasure to one yields exactly zero credit. Prospective Parents propose instantiations; if a Potential Child is instantiated, they receive the allocated tokens; the Adjudicators then vote. The asymmetry should operate through the token mechanics and the veto structure before any facilitator names it.

Please provide: (1) a precise 75-minute timeline with turn order, token mechanics, veto procedures, and rules for breaking ties; (2) a debrief protocol that maps the veto stage onto the good of absent harm without a subject, the missing claim-cards onto the non-badness of absent pleasure, and the token asymmetry onto the shadow of the non-identity problem; (3) an honest assessment of which feature of Benatar’s view—most likely the stronger claim that any life with possible suffering is not worth initiating—cannot be reproduced in a voluntary classroom game and must be supplied by the facilitator; and (4) a forecast of predictable failures, such as participants anthropomorphizing the empty chairs, treating the zero-credit rule as unfair, or refusing to play the Adjudicator role, together with concrete suggestions for handling each. Do not include a bibliography, reading list, or citations to specific editions; I have the primary texts available and need only the structural design.
