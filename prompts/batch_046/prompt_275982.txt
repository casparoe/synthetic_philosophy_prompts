As I have been composing my present novel—a multi-generational narrative structured as a sequence of discrete first-person chapters, each narrated by a different family member with no omniscient frame to bridge their consciousnesses—I have found myself returning to an old ethical anxiety that seems to migrate from fiction into moral philosophy without leaving its conceptual luggage behind. The argument that troubles me proceeds as follows. First, the separateness of persons objection to utilitarianism holds that aggregating benefits and burdens across distinct individuals is illegitimate because suffering and flourishing are not transferable between separate centers of consciousness; one person’s agony cannot be mathematically canceled by another’s pleasure. Second, yet the boundary of what counts as a “separate center” is far less stable than the objection assumes; in my narrative, the same biological body across sixty years houses agents with radically discontinuous values, memories, and narrative identities, a condition I have begun to call, for my own compositional purposes, the anthology self. Third, if separateness is genuinely grounded in the absence of a continuous first-person narrative rather than in mere biological separation, then intertemporal aggregation within a single life should be as impermissible as interpersonal aggregation—there is no more reason to allow the elderly protagonist’s comfort to justify the young protagonist’s sacrifice than to allow a stranger’s comfort to justify mine. Fourth, but this conclusion seems intolerable, for it would render long-term contracts, retirement planning, retrospective moral responsibility, and even the coherence of character development conceptually confused, since the agent who reaps the benefit is never identical to the agent who bore the cost. Fifth, we are therefore forced either to abandon the anti-aggregationist intuition altogether or to restrict it by an arbitrary biological criterion that the objection itself never supplies or defends. I am convinced that some subtle equivocation has slipped in between the metaphysical observation about narrative discontinuity and the moral claim about non-transferability—perhaps involving the difference between psychological continuity and moral standing—but I cannot isolate the exact step where the reasoning overreaches. Which premise should be rejected, and does rejecting it allow one to preserve both the separateness objection and the possibility of meaningful narrative continuity within a single life?
