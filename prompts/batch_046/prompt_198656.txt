I'm putting together a decision tree for a small seminar on institutional design, and I want it to sort people by their actual commitments about selecting representatives rather than by the labels they prefer. The tree should begin with a concrete choice: is the core failure mode of representative democracy an epistemic one—voters and representatives systematically misjudge policy outcomes—or a principal-agent one—representatives face structural incentives to diverge from constituent interests once in office?

From that root, each branch should pose an institutional question answerable without knowing political philosophy: does selecting representatives by lot from the citizenry solve the failure you identified; does imposing strict term limits solve it; and does allowing long-tenured career politicians solve it or worsen it?

Please include at least these five leaves, treating any missing combinations as unoccupied space to be flagged: (1) pure sortition with short, non-renewable terms and no career track; (2) election with strict term limits and post-service lobbying restrictions; (3) a career-politician defense that treats long tenure as necessary for complex policy expertise; (4) a hybrid—call it "sortition with institutional-memory retention"—in which citizens are randomly selected but serve in staggered cohorts that partially retain experienced members; and (5) whatever unnamed position results from an unexpected yes/no pattern across the three branch questions.

For each leaf, state in one sentence its most awkward practical commitment: the consequence its defenders must accept but rarely advertise. Build the tree from the logical space of answers rather than from named theories, so no node asks whether someone is a "democratic realist" or an "epistocrat." Every branch should be a claim about an institution. Please flag any pair of leaves the tree genuinely cannot separate, and briefly diagnose whether the gap is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Write the response in American English.
