As a retired experimental physicist who has only recently begun studying moral and political philosophy, I am organizing a structured workshop for fourteen participants—mostly advanced undergraduates together with a few faculty from other disciplines—to run for exactly ninety minutes. The subject is the cultural tension between celebrating exceptional achievement and suppressing it: specifically, the American Dream’s meritocratic ethos versus the Scandinavian concept of Jantelagen and the related phenomenon known as tall poppy syndrome. I do not want a lecture or a debate; I want a runnable simulation in which the philosophical problem is encountered through action before it is ever named.

Design a game with real but small stakes. Each participant should accumulate visibility points through individual problem-solving tasks, while the group as a whole controls a collective mechanism—call it the solidarity switch—that can either amplify the highest scorer’s visibility or redistribute those points downward across the group. The payoff structure should depend on a hidden matrix that the facilitator reveals only after several rounds, so that participants discover through strategic interaction that suppressing the top performer often maximizes collective stability while destroying individual motivation. The rules themselves, not the facilitator’s commentary, should generate the paradox.

Please specify: the physical or procedural setup; the exact rules of point accumulation and the solidarity switch; a minute-by-minute timeline for the ninety minutes; any assigned roles and how they constrain choice; the debrief that maps specific rounds onto the American Dream, Jantelagen, and tall poppy dynamics; an honest prediction of the strategic failure likely in round two or three and how the facilitator should intervene without breaking the frame; and, finally, a frank admission of which feature of actual social norms—for example, the internalized emotional weight of Jantelagen or the intergenerational transmission of meritocratic belief—the simulation structurally cannot reproduce and must therefore be supplied in the closing discussion. The participants should leave having felt, rather than merely heard, why societies both need and resent their most visible successes.
