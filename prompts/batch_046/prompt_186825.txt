You are a professional philosopher with expertise in welfare economics, social choice theory, and the metaphysics of value. Approach the following with analytical rigor: think carefully, start by briefly brainstorming the core commitments and obvious escape routes of the position described, then proceed systematically.

Position: Interpersonal utility comparisons are not merely epistemically difficult but conceptually malformed outside of a shared “metric convention” that is always normative rather than descriptive. To say that person A is twice as well off as person B is, on this view, like asserting one poem is twice as beautiful as another without a shared critical framework—it sounds quantitative but smuggles in a contested standard of evaluation. Even apparent successes of comparison—willingness-to-pay measures, revealed preference aggregation—depend on background institutional conditions that artificially homogenize preferences.

Now consider a modified, more specific variant I will call frame-relative comparability. This holds that comparisons are valid within but not across welfare frames, where a frame is defined not by subjective preference but by the structure of reasons a person can offer for ranking states of affairs. So two agents reasoning within a eudaimonist frame (flourishing as the ultimate good) are comparable with one another; a eudaimonist and a hedonist are not, not because we lack data, but because there is no fact of the matter about whether flourishing in one domain outweighs pleasure in another. The view treats cross-frame comparison failure not as ignorance but as category error.

What would count against frame-relative comparability? I want an honest map of its exposure—what observations, arguments, formal results, or practical necessities would genuinely threaten it, not a generic list of objections to anti-comparabilism.
