I'm working through the literature on global workspace theory for a graduate seminar on consciousness, and I keep encountering the broadcasting analogy: consciousness is said to work like a radio station that sends signals out to anyone with a receiver, where the brain's global workspace makes representations available to all downstream cognitive modules and that availability is taken to constitute, or fully explain, conscious awareness. Some authors in our reading extend this further, arguing that phenomenal consciousness -- the subjective character of experience -- is nothing over and above this global broadcasting, so that explaining the broadcast mechanism exhausts the explanation of awareness itself. Others invoke the familiar challenge of phenomenal overflow, suggesting that subjective experience can be far richer than whatever actually achieves global accessibility, which makes me wonder whether the broadcasting picture is doing hidden work.

Audit the analogy in detail. First, build an explicit correspondence table: what plays the role of the transmitter, the signal, the receivers, the audience, and the publicness condition? Then identify the precise fractures -- for instance, whether broadcasting presupposes a central station that neural architecture lacks, whether radio signals carry intrinsic qualitative properties analogous to qualia, and whether the analogy quietly imports a functionalist commitment that being received by cognitive modules is sufficient for being experienced. Next, evaluate whether the conclusion that phenomenal consciousness collapses into access consciousness survives once these disanalogies are on the table, or whether the analogy was carrying that conclusion all along by smuggling in a non-phenomenal picture of awareness. Finally, if the broadcasting model breaks down, suggest whether a different analogy -- perhaps drawn from distributed computing or cryptography -- could capture the relation between access and phenomenal consciousness without importing the same misleading commitments, and whether any such alternative would still license reducing experience to accessibility.
