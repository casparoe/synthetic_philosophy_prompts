I'm working on a paper about substrate-independent consciousness, and I'm trying to think through the well-known dialogue in The Mind's I (Hofstadter and Dennett, 1981) in which Einstein's brain is preserved and engaged in conversation. Most treatments of this scenario treat it as a variant of the brain-in-a-vat problem applied to personal identity, but I'm interested in a different target: could the physical book itself—the printed object that contains and frames that dialogue—ever count as conscious?

To work this out, I want to compare Hofstadter's strange-loop theory of consciousness with Dennett's multiple-drafts model. For each theory, please lay out (i) the core mechanism by which consciousness is supposed to arise, (ii) whether that mechanism requires a specific causal or running computational implementation, or whether a static organizational pattern is sufficient, and (iii) whether the printed pages of a book, arranged exactly as they are in The Mind's I, could instantiate that mechanism. In particular: does Hofstadter's appeal to self-referential symbolic patterns require that those patterns be actively interpreted or executed, or can a fixed inscription carry them? And does Dennett's rejection of a Cartesian Theater allow consciousness to be distributed across a non-dynamic text, or does the absence of real-time revision, competition, and publication among drafts rule the book out?

Then, evaluate which of the two theories gives the more coherent answer to whether the book—not the brain inside the fiction—could be conscious, and whether either answer depends on an unstated assumption about the boundary between representation and computation. I am not looking for a bibliography or historical survey; I want a careful conceptual comparison that treats the book as the system under analysis and tells me where each theory's machinery actually applies and where it breaks.
