I am currently working through some material for an independent study group in philosophy of mathematics, and a disagreement from our last meeting has been lingering with me. We had been discussing Hilbert's hotel as an illustration that an actually infinite collection—say, a fully occupied hotel with rooms numbered 1, 2, 3, and so on—can consistently accommodate additional guests without contradiction, which seemed to us to establish that actual infinity is at least mathematically coherent. From there we moved to Cantor's diagonal argument, which demonstrates that the power set of the natural numbers has a strictly greater cardinality, a result that appears to require treating these infinite collections as completed totalities over which one can quantify.

More recently, however, I encountered potentialism, the view that infinity is never an actual completed totality but only an unending potentiality, as well as stricter finitist positions that deny the existence of arbitrarily large finite collections altogether. This leads me to a confusion: if Hilbert's hotel and Cantor's theorem are both formally valid within standard mathematics, does that not show that actual infinities of different magnitudes are unavoidable structural features of the discipline? Furthermore, if potentialists accept these proofs as correct derivations, are they not being inconsistent in denying that the completed infinite sets these arguments invoke actually exist? I cannot see how one can consistently affirm Cantor's result regarding uncountable sets while maintaining that no infinite collection is ever fully completed, and I would appreciate help in seeing where my reasoning goes wrong.
