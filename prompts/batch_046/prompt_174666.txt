I have noticed a recurring structure in arguments about deductive justification that I do not have a name for, and I want to know whether it is recognized in the literature or whether it needs a new label. Carroll's tortoise shows that applying modus ponens can always be made into an explicit premise, generating an infinite regress; but what I keep seeing is not an infinite loop but a layered retreat. First, a particular inference is defended by citing an explicit logical rule. Next, that rule is defended by appealing to the coherence or completeness of the formal system it belongs to. Finally, when that system is questioned, the defender stops appealing to logic altogether and treats the framework as a conventional or practical stopping point—sometimes saying it is simply 'how reasoning works,' or, in debates about artificial intelligence, that the pattern was learned from statistically reliable training data. Each layer is different in kind, and the chain terminates rather than circling back, yet none of the layers feels like an ordinary auxiliary hypothesis added to save a theory. Does this specific pattern—this stratified termination of justification—have an established name? If there is one, how does it differ from Carroll's regress, from Wittgenstein's idea that justification comes to an end in action, and from a bare refusal to answer? If there is no name, I would like a precise carving: what exactly counts as this phenomenon, where its boundaries lie, and what distinguishes a legitimate halting point from a mere change of subject.
