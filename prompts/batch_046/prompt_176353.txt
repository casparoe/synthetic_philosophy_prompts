I've been thinking about Quine's take on moral values lately, and I think people usually undersell how weird it gets if you push his ideas all the way. Most summaries say he's basically a naturalist who thinks ethics lacks the empirical backing science has, so moral beliefs are just conventions or habits of mind. But if you take the indeterminacy of translation seriously, it seems like normative terms — 'wrong', 'justice', whatever — might not even have stable extensions across different linguistic communities. So moral disagreement wouldn't be a conflict over shared properties; it might be closer to talking past each other with sounds that don't determinately map onto the same things. I'm calling this the 'semantic collapse' reading of Quinean ethics, and I think it's more consistent with his broader philosophy than the softer conventionalist version people usually attribute to him.

What would actually count against this? I'm not looking for the standard 'but people feel strongly about morality' line — I want the serious defeaters. Does Quine's own writing about values leave any room for normative terms being anchored well enough to avoid indeterminacy? Would a Quinean say that moral language is fixed by shared behavioral dispositions or practical reasoning in a way that 'gavagai' isn't? And then there's the cross-cultural argument problem: if this view is right, why do people across cultures seem to genuinely argue about morality instead of just misunderstanding each other? Is that a fatal blow, or can the view absorb it by saying we project shared reference? Also, does this collapse Quine's position into a kind of moral relativism he might reject, or is it actually stricter than relativism? I'd like the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward, and I'd especially like to know wheather a committed Quinean would concede any of these or dismiss them all.
