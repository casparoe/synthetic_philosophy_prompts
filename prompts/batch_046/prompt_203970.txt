I am currently taking an advanced course in comparative metaphysics and have been working through the Buddhist doctrine of anatta, not as a simple negation of selfhood but as a systematic claim about the ontological status of the mental stream. The standard pedagogical move is to contrast anatta with an enduring substance—whether the Hindu atman or a Cartesian ego—and then to explain karmic continuity through analogies like a flame transferring from one candle to another. Yet when I try to press this analogy conceptually, I run into a tension that introductory treatments rarely address with precision: if the mental aggregates are ultimately reducible to discrete, momentary dharmas lacking any hidden substrate, and if there is no persisting entity that owns them, then in what precise sense can the agent who performed an action at time t1 be said to bear the karmic consequences at time t2?

I am not asking for a doctrinal history of Theravada versus Sarvastivada positions, nor for a bibliography. Rather, I want to understand how the framework of conventional truth (sammuti-sacca) and ultimate truth (paramattha-sacca) is meant to resolve this tension without either dissolving moral responsibility into pure fiction or implicitly reintroducing a substantial self under another name. Specifically, how does dependent origination (pratītyasamutpāda) function as the conceptual bridge that allows continuity to be real enough for karma to operate, yet insubstantial enough to preserve anatta? And where, conceptually, does this bridge remain unstable—particularly if one insists that moral accountability requires more than a causally linked sequence of impersonal events? Please explain the conceptual machinery here, and indicate whether the resolution ultimately depends on treating agency itself as conventionally designated rather than ultimately grounded.
