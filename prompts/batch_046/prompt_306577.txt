I am a PhD student in philosophy of mind preparing a seminar on Gilbert Ryle's distinction between knowing-how and knowing-that, and I am trying to use The Matrix (1999) as a pedagogical case study without letting students conclude that the film simply endorses the intellectualist reduction of practical skill to stored propositional content. The neural-upload sequence—where Neo acquires martial-arts proficiency through direct data transfer—seems to literalize exactly the view Ryle criticizes, treating knowing-how as nothing more than knowing-that arranged in memory. At the same time, the narrative offers countervailing moments: Morpheus distinguishes knowing the path from walking it, and the spoon-bending scene implies that mastery involves a transformation of embodied engagement rather than the acquisition of beliefs. I would like a critical reading that tracks both directions with equal care: where the film genuinely dramatizes Ryle's anti-intellectualism, where it depends on the confusion between representational access and practical mastery, and where specific scenes—the upload, the dojo sparring, the spoon—resist being read as mere illustrations of philosophy. In particular, I am interested in whether Neo's initial physical failures and later successes in the construct can be interpreted, even within the film's own science-fiction premises, as showing that knowing-how requires an embodied history or dispositional training that informational transfer alone cannot supply. The goal is not to reduce the film to an example of Ryle, but to ask what the cinematic dramatization of skill acquisition adds to the philosophical debate—especially regarding the role of embodied practice—that abstract argument alone tends to miss. Please begin with your bottom line in the first sentence, then develop the reasoning with no preamble and without restating the question.
