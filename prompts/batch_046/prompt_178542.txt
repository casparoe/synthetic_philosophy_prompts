i've been trying to work out Keynes's implicit rulings on the good life — not the macroeconomics, but his scattered takes on work, leisure, art and status. my reading of his essays and letters gives me four verdicts that feel consistent but might hide different principles. one: he'd back slashing the working week to about 20 hours once productivity allows, since the economic problem is temporary and the art of living is permanent. two: he'd reject a state ministry that picks which artists get salaries, because centralising taste is dangerous even if the funding is generous. three: he'd approve of a rich patron funding a local theatre anonymously, but condemn the same act if done for social prestige — the motive matters more than the money. four: he'd turn down a scheme that gives everyone a leisure grant but forces them to log 'self-improvement hours' to keep recieving it, since he thinks the real obstacle isn't lack of time but the psychology of relative needs and status competition.

now here's the new proposal: a regional goverment introduces a 20-hour week funded by automation taxes, with every adult getting an unconditional stipend. the catch is that the stipend is loaded onto a 'culture card' that can only be spent on approved arts, education, or local craft workshops — there's no forced attendance or logging programme, just a spending fence. if we want to stay consistent with Keynes's track record, what should we predict he'd say? which of the four precedents is the closest analogue, and do they genuinely underdetermine this? if they do, what principle would we be adopting by saying yes, and what principle by saying no? and what's one further hypothetical that would actually tell the two rules apart?
