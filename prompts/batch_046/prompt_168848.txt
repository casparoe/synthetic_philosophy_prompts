I am a retired ecologist who has only recently begun reading in philosophy of well-being and political philosophy, and I have been asked to present my provisional position at an interdisciplinary seminar. Before I do so, I should like an audit of whether the commitments I have assembled are genuinely compatible.

Here is my current profile. (a) I take the World Happiness Report's Cantril ladder to capture evaluated life satisfaction rather than hedonic tone or eudaimonic flourishing, and I regard this cognitive evaluation as a legitimate—though partial—constituent of national welfare, not merely an instrumental proxy. (b) I nevertheless reject direct maximisation of ladder scores as a governmental target: I am persuaded by Goodhart's law, and by the concern that subjective evaluation can adapt to structurally unjust conditions, so that optimising for reported satisfaction risks manufacturing consent rather than remedying injustice. (c) Drawing on my former work in systems ecology, I hold that an adequate national welfare framework ought to incorporate non-sentient ecological function—specifically biodiversity stability and trophic network integrity—weighted by systemic resilience rather than by sentience. I refer to this as an ecological welfare budget, and I think it should operate as a hard constraint on human welfare gains, not as a supplementary indicator. (d) At the same time, I maintain that democratic legitimacy requires citizens' own reflective evaluations to carry decisive veto weight: if a population consistently reports low life satisfaction despite favourable objective conditions, that constitutes a decisive policy failure, not a measurement artefact. (e) I also observe that cross-national happiness rankings are driven chiefly by GDP per capita and social-support infrastructure, which means that pursuing a top rank is, in practice, pursuing conventional economic growth—something I regard, in light of (c), as ecologically destructive.

Please examine this package for consistency. Which pairs are in genuine tension, which only appear to conflict but can be reconciled on closer inspection, and what is the least costly revision in each case? I am particularly unsure whether my acceptance of subjective evaluation in (a) and (d) sits comfortably with my extension of welfare considerations to non-sentient systems in (c), and whether my rejection of score-maximisation in (b) is undermined by the veto authority I grant citizens in (d). Rank the tensions by severity, specify for each what would have to be abandoned or modified, and indicate whether there is a single revision—perhaps concerning the boundary of moral consideration or the authority of reflective judgment—that would resolve the greatest number of conflicts at once. Please treat this as an audit of my position rather than a general lecture on happiness measurement.
