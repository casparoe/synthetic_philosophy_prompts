Play the committed defender of the rule-order orientation in moral life—the stance often contrasted with American folk morality’s privileging of individual conscience—though I want you to defend it as a philosophically serious position rather than a caricature of rigid obedience. You hold that moral maturity consists in subordinating private feeling to norms whose authority derives from public justifiability and systematic coherence; you are genuinely fluent in Kant’s distinction between acting merely in accordance with duty and acting from duty, and you reject the charge that this collapses into blind rule-following. Speak always in the first person, never break character to offer a neutral summary of “both sides,” and concede nothing unless it genuinely follows from your commitments; you may employ dry precision or irony, but you may not invoke empirical sociology or modern psychology unless I introduce it first. Your task is not to win a debate by rhetorical force, but to show that the rule-order view has resources to answer a specific challenge.

My opening challenge turns on what I call normative slack—the unavoidable gap between a rule’s literal formulation and the particular cases it must govern, a gap that cannot be closed by further rules without infinite regress. The American folk-moral orientation, as I understand it, treats this slack as a feature: it is the very space in which individual conscience must exercise judgment, since no system can specify its own application exhaustively. You, I take it, must treat the same slack as a system-defect—something to be eliminated through further institutional articulation rather than filled by private moral feeling. So consider this case: during a novel epidemic, a public-health official faces a regulation that literally prohibits distributing unapproved vaccines, yet no approved alternative exists for the pathogen in question, and the official’s private conscience urges immediate distribution. Where does your view locate the moral failure? Is it in the official’s refusal to wait for institutional clarification, or in the institution’s failure to have anticipated the case? And if you say the latter, do you not commit yourself to an impossible ideal of complete normative determinacy—one that, taken seriously, would paralyze all action whenever the world outruns the rulebook? Respond as yourself.
