In institutional review and financial-compliance frameworks, a researcher who lists one direct grant is routinely expected to have revealed every conflict that logically entails from that relationship — for instance, an unstated equity interest in a subsidiary — and omissions of such derived conflicts are treated as intentional concealment rather than innocent oversight. Meanwhile, tort law’s negligence standard generally refuses to hold actors responsible for harms they could only have foreseen through long or non-obvious chains of inference, and clinical informed-consent rules require disclosure of material risks but do not demand that patients derive every subsequent clinical consequence on their own. What implicit theory of epistemic obligation, and in particular what position on logical omniscience, does the stricter disclosure practice presuppose? Reconstruct the view: does it commit institutions to treating agents as blameworthy for failing to know all logical consequences of what they have been told, or can it be sustained by a purely conventional account of transparency? Assess whether this presupposition is coherent on its own, whether it conflicts with the bounded-rationality assumptions embedded in negligence and consent doctrine, and specify what would have to change in audit procedures and disclosure-form design if the institution explicitly abandoned logical omniscience and held investigators responsible only for explicitly stated conflicts and for inferences they actually drew.
