In the decades since the replication crisis became widely acknowledged, a particular exchange has recurred with little apparent movement: frequentists contend that the crisis exposes pathologies in null-hypothesis significance testing—p-hacking, arbitrary thresholds, and underpowered studies—and call for stricter alpha levels, pre-registration, and larger samples; Bayesians contend that it exposes the incoherence of treating evidence as a binary rejection of a point null and advocate replacing p-values with Bayes factors or credible intervals that update belief continuously. Despite extensive literature, neither framework has displaced the other in practice, and both are routinely applied to the same failed replications without convergence. Is this stalemate substantive—do the camps disagree about the nature of statistical evidence, about what counts as an error, or about the ontology of the effects being estimated—or is it largely verbal, turning on different uses of terms like "evidence," "error," and "confirmation"? Alternatively, are they answering different questions under one heading: one concerned with long-run error control in institutional science, the other with rational belief revision for an individual researcher? More specifically, might a hidden disagreement be doing the work—perhaps not about statistical machinery at all, but about whether replication failures reveal that there is no single stable effect size across contexts, making both frameworks misapply a metaphysical assumption of uniformity that neither side defends explicitly? Diagnose the structure of the disagreement using what proponents of each framework actually say when they discuss replication failures, distinguishing disagreements about empirical facts, about standards of evidence, about which studies count as relevant data, and about starting intuitions that neither side can argue for without circularity. If part of the divergence stems from the frameworks being optimized for different institutional roles—regulatory decision-making versus individual belief updating—say what that implies for progress: should we expect theoretical convergence, a stable division of labor, or is the framing question "which statistics solves the replication crisis" itself ill-posed? Offer a concrete criterion—whether formal, empirical, or conceptual—that would indicate genuine progress rather than another round of the same exchange, and identify what kind of result or distinction would show that the debate has been miscast from the start.
