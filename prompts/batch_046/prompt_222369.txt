Read Hamlet through Wittgensteins language games and family resemblance, but specifically through his private-language argument and the idea that criteria are public and embedded in practices. Hamlet spends half the play trying to establish Claudius's guilt and his own grief through private soliloquys -- like he's trying to build a private language for mental states that only he can check. Meanwhile the play is packed with family-resemblance concepts: madness (Hamlets feigned vs Ophelias actual), death (Yoricks skull vs the poison cup vs the ghost), and even action versus thought. Ask whether the play actually dramatizes the failure of Hamlets private-language project: does his paralysis come from trying to apply private criteria where only public language-games -- the court rituals, the play-within-the-play -- can do the work? And does the graveyard scene show that death has no essential feature but only family traits? I want a reading that takes both the play and Wittgenstein seriously, and says whether Shakespeare gets something about language and criteria that the philosophy papers miss.
