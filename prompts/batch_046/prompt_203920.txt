Since I have recently become the primary caregiver for a parent with frontotemporal dementia, the problem of other minds has shifted from an abstract philosophical exercise to an urgent practical concern: I must determine whether the particular distress my parent exhibits is a genuine mental state or merely behavioral noise, especially when that state appears structurally alien to anything I have experienced in my own first-person life. I want you to design a precise, constrained thought experiment drawn from this caregiving context.

Construct a single realistic scenario—no longer than one paragraph, set in eldercare or clinical practice, with no science-fiction elements, no zombies, no brains in vats, and no conditions of perfect deception. In the scenario, an observer must attribute a specific mental state—call it mnestic grief, a form of distress that arises not from recognizing that a loss has occurred but from failing to recognize that anything has been lost—to a patient whose behavior consistently manifests this pattern. Engineer the case so that three philosophical accounts deliver exactly these verdicts:

First, the analogical argument for other minds, which reasons from the observer's own mental states to those of the patient, must fail to justify the attribution, because the observer lacks any first-person phenomenal analog for mnestic grief.

Second, an inference-to-the-best-explanation account must succeed: the mental state should be the best available theoretical explanation of the observed behavioral, affective, and expressive patterns, despite the absence of analogical support.

Third, a direct phenomenological account—one that requires a non-inferential, embodied resonance with the patient's expressive presence—must yield an ambiguous or intermediate verdict, neither fully endorsing nor denying the attribution.

After presenting the scenario, demonstrate rigorously that it meets each specification: state the precise belief being attributed, describe the relevant behavior and expressive markers, specify the method of attribution employed by each theory, identify the nearby possible worlds relevant to testing each condition, and show exactly why each account delivers its verdict. Then isolate the single structural feature that forces the analogical argument to peel away from the other two—being explicit about whether the divergence turns on phenomenal dissimilarity, predictive success, embodied accessibility, or something else. Next, explain what happens if that feature is removed: does the case collapse back into a standard other-minds situation in which all three accounts converge? Finally, present the strongest objection a committed defender of the analogical argument could raise—specifically, how they might argue that an attribution lacking analogical grounding is not genuinely mentalistic but merely a predictive instrument dressed in mentalistic language—and address whether answering that objection undermines the engineered divergence you have constructed.
