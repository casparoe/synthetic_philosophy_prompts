I am preparing a seminar on aesthetic theory and have been struck by how often Tolstoy’s infection metaphor from his 1897 essay What Is Art? is invoked in discussions of digital culture. A colleague recently proposed that algorithmic recommendation systems are best understood through that analogy: the content creator plays the artist, the emotional valence of a post plays the feeling to be transmitted, algorithmic amplification replaces physical proximity as the vector of spread, and infection succeeds when engagement metrics spike. From this, the colleague concludes that viral media represents the most authentic fulfillment of Tolstoy’s artistic ideal, since it achieves near-universal emotional transmission with unprecedented efficiency. Another participant objected that this reading ignores Tolstoy’s moral criterion—that genuine art must communicate a spirit of universal brotherly love—and also misses that biological infection carries no intentional structure, whereas Tolstoy insisted artistic communication is deliberately directed and morally evaluated.

Can you audit this analogy systematically? First, provide an explicit correspondence table mapping the elements of Tolstoy’s biological infection model onto the algorithmic-media version: what plays the role of the pathogen, the host, the immune system, the conditions of transmission, and the symptomatic response? Then identify the disanalogies that matter specifically for Tolstoy’s normative conclusions—where does the absence of moral evaluation in viral transmission break the mapping, and where does the algorithmic intermediary (the platform itself) correspond to nothing in Tolstoy’s original picture? Finally, assess whether the conclusion that viral content fulfills Tolstoy’s artistic ideal survives once these breaks are on the table, or whether the analogy was carrying that conclusion all along. If the analogy collapses under scrutiny, suggest a better framework for thinking about emotional transmission in algorithmic culture—one that preserves Tolstoy’s concern with moral purpose without importing the biological determinism he used to describe artistic communication.
