My study group is stuck on an argument about Dennett's cranes and greedy reductionism that seems to show his framework is self-defeating. (1) A scientific explanation must be a crane: it constructs a complex phenomenon from simpler parts without invoking an unexplained explainer dropped in from above. (2) Greedy reductionism is the mistake of treating such an explanation as eliminating the phenomenon—say, explaining consciousness away as "just neurons firing"—rather than accounting for its reality. (3) Introduce compositional inertia: parts arranged in a structure can only yield properties already latent in their interaction capacities; genuinely novel causal powers cannot emerge from arrangement alone. (4) Apply this to intentionality. A crane account explains belief and desire via evolutionary selection and neural computation. (5) If compositional inertia holds, then belief is nothing over and above neuron-arrangement-behavior, so the crane explanation eliminates belief as a real mental state—this is greedy reductionism. (6) If compositional inertia fails, then brain-arranged neurons generate genuinely novel powers—aboutness, semantic content—that no individual neuron possesses, which is precisely the unexplained leap that requires a skyhook. (7) So for intentionality, any crane explanation either collapses into greedy reductionism (if inertia holds) or secretly becomes a skyhook in disguise (if inertia fails). (8) Therefore Dennett's distinction cannot do its intended work: you cannot explain the mind with cranes without either eliminating it or smuggling in an unexplained explainer. I would most like to keep (1) and (2). Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost—especially for (3) or (6)?
