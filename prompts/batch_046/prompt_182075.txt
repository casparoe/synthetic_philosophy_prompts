Here is an argument I constructed for a seminar on personal identity that now seems suspicious because its conclusion undermines the very view it targets. (1) Origin essentialism holds that I am essentially the result of the fusion of my actual sperm S and egg E; had different gametes fused, the resulting person would not be me. (2) But monozygotic twinning shows that a single zygote formed from S and E can split at the eight-cell stage, with each totipotent blastomere lineage developing into a distinct, continuous biological individual. Since both twins share the identical fusion event and initial cell line, the essentialist must either identify them as one person or concede that origin alone does not individuate. (3) To avoid that result, the essentialist might refine the origin claim: my essential origin includes not merely the fusion event but my specific continuous trajectory of cell divisions — for instance, the left-half versus right-half blastomere lineage. (4) Yet if my identity is essentially tied to that precise microscopic trajectory, then any stochastic deviation in early embryogenesis — a single blastomere failing to divide and being replaced by a neighboring cell's progeny — yields a world in which I do not exist. (5) We do not think that such microscopic variations, nor the routine replacement of every somatic cell in my body over time, destroy my identity. (6) Therefore origin essentialism either collapses monozygotic twins into a single individual or renders personal identity fragile to the point of absurdity. Where does the reasoning fail? I am inclined to preserve (1) and blame (2) for mischaracterizing the essentialist's claim, but I want to know whether saving the view forces me to accept (4) or to abandon something else.
