Write a take-home exam prompt for an upper-level undergraduate seminar in philosophy of mind and aesthetics—allow three hours, require about 1,500 words, and weight it at 40 percent of the final grade—centered on the problem of letter identity explored through Douglas Hofstadter’s Letter Spirit project and through philosophical theories of categorization. The exam should pose this precise crux: if every token of the lowercase “a” lacks a single geometric feature shared by all other tokens, yet human observers recognize them all instantly as instances of the same letter, does this recognition depend on a hidden essential form, on a Wittgensteinian family-resemblance network of overlapping similarities, or on a learned mapping between abstract roles (e.g., bowl, stem, ascender) and variable stylistic fillers?

To keep the task from being answerable by generic summary, inject the following niche framing: ask students to evaluate a hypothetical extension called “Inverted Grid,” in which a Letter Spirit-style system is trained not on positive stroke geometry but on negative-space topology—the enclosed empty regions within and around a letterform—so that it classifies a highly stylized cursive “a” with an open bowl and a blocky geometric “a” with identical enclosed regions as the same letter, despite the two tokens sharing no positive-line features at all. Students must defend a verdict on whether this extension captures something deeper about the essence of “a”-ness or whether it severs categorical identity from the intentional stylistic choices of a type designer, and they must do so without falling back on mere intuition.

The deliverable must contain three parts. First, the exam question itself, phrased sharply and requiring students to take a stand rather than survey positions neutrally. Second, a model answer of roughly 400 words that reaches and justifies a specific conclusion—for example, that role-filler structure explains categorical identity but that negative-space topology alone collapses the distinction between a letterform and an accidental silhouette, so family resemblance must be supplemented by an intentional-design constraint. Third, a marking scheme with explicit point allocations and named errors: award marks for correctly distinguishing role-filler structure from symbolic representation; for applying family-resemblance versus essentialist arguments without conflating them; for direct engagement with the Inverted Grid hypothetical rather than treating it as an afterthought; and for identifying at least two specific mistakes that should cost marks, such as treating geometric similarity as sufficient for categorical identity, or accepting the negative-space feature as decisive without addressing how stylistic deviation functions in actual type design. Write the entire response in American English. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations, and do not rely on obscure technical details that fall outside standard coursework in philosophy of mind, cognitive science, and aesthetics.
