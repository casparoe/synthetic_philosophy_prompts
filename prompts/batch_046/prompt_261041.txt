I've been reading a bunch of rationalist-adjacent stuff about world history and I'm kind of stuck on whether the past can actually be told from everywhere at once, or if there's always some hidden Eurocentric gravity pulling the narrative back to Europe as the main character. I want to stress-test that idea with a real episode, not just another abstract debate.

Take the 1258 Mongol sack of Baghdad and the fall of the Abbasid Caliphate. European chronicles of the period mostly ignored it or folded it into vague 'Eastern chaos', while Persian and Arabic sources kept detailed court-level accounts, and Chinese chronicles of the Mongol period noted it mainly through the lens of their own imperial diplomacy rather than as an Islamic tragedy. Analyze this through the lens of polycentric historiography: can these three archives be synthesized into one narrative with no center, or are they incommensurable in ways that force any historian to pick a protagonist?

I want the analysis to run both directions. First, what does the theory say the actors and chroniclers faced -- does it require us to treat all three source-traditions as equally authoritative, and what does it do with the silences where one tradition doesn't care about what another thinks is central? Second, what does the episode show about the theory itself? Does it prove Eurocentrism is just a failure of translation, or does it reveal that any synthesis still hides someone? And be honest about where the theory is silent rather than forcing it to speak.
