I am writing a philosophical novel in which a narrator, herself a writer, tries to prove that she never truly knows anything. She reasons as follows. (1) Luminosity applies to knowledge itself: if I know p, then I am in a position to know that I know p. (2) The KK principle holds: knowing p entails knowing that one knows p. (3) Together these generate an infinite hierarchy—Kp, KKp, KKKp, and so on—and because both premises describe actual epistemic relations, each level is an actual state I must possess, not a mere potentiality. (4) But any finite mind has a bounded representational depth; there is some level n at which I lack the cognitive resources to represent K^n p, however intact the lower levels. (5) So if I ever genuinely know p, I must hold epistemic states that exceed my own architecture. (6) Hence I never genuinely know anything, which is absurd; yet denying (4) requires an infinite mental architecture, which seems worse. Where does the argument slip? I would rather preserve luminosity in (1) than abandon the finitude claim in (4), but I want to know precisely what that commitment costs. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
