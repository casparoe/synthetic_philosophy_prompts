As a novelist who has spent the last fifteen years writing historical fiction set in the interwar period, I have recently been appointed to the curatorial ethics board of the Whitmore Municipal Archive, and I must help our board deliver a binding recommendation to the city council regarding the proposed permanent exhibition of the Calder Family Papers—an unfiltered collection of private correspondence, business ledgers, and personal journals from 1912 to 1948 belonging to a locally celebrated industrialist whose fortune funded three of the city’s still-standing institutions. The papers contain explicit racialist pseudoscience, gender essentialism, and eugenicist policy proposals that were unremarkable among his social circle at the time but are now categorically rejected by every contemporary ethical framework; the dispute is not whether to preserve them, but how they should be framed for public access. One faction, led by our senior archivist, argues for what he terms hermeneutic neutrality—the materials should be digitized and displayed with only minimal chronological and biographical annotation, on the grounds that any explicit moral framing constitutes presentism, the illegitimate projection of contemporary norms onto agents who operated within an entirely different moral ecology, and that historians have a duty to escape the standards of their own time. The opposing faction, led by a public historian on our board, contends that so-called neutrality is itself presentism in reverse: by withholding explicit moral evaluation, we implicitly normalize past injustices for a contemporary audience that lacks the historical literacy to recognize them as such, and she proposes instead a layered interpretive framework—what she calls diachronic ethical indexing—in which each document is accompanied by parallel commentary mapping the writer’s moral commitments onto the normative landscape of his era without translating them into ours, yet marking the precise points where those commitments violate principles that were available, if marginalized, even within that era. A third proposal from one of our museum consultants suggests a tiered-access model, physically segregating the papers into an unrestricted academic tier and a guided public tier, on the theory that moral distance is not binary but a calibrated pedagogical instrument.

Please draft the formal policy memo that our board will submit to the city council. The memo should recommend a specific course of action—whether hermeneutic neutrality, diachronic ethical indexing, the tiered-access model, or a modified synthesis—and provide reasoning the council can act on, including clear criteria that reviewers and future curators can apply when similar disputes arise. Your recommendation must treat the strongest argument on the losing side with complete intellectual honesty rather than caricature, and because this case turns on whether historians can ever truly escape the standards of their own time, your reasoning should address that issue directly: not by invoking named schools of thought, but by working through what it actually means for an institutional body to present the past without either sanctifying it or colonizing it with present concerns. Write in a formal, polished register appropriate to a municipal advisory document, but do not include bibliographies, citations, requests for further reading, or appeals to authority the board cannot verify on its own, since we must evaluate your reasoning solely on its internal merits.
