everyone in philosophy twitter keeps using the blind-men-and-elephant story to explain jain anekāntavāda: each guy grabs a trunk or leg and thinks he knows the beast, so every view is partly right and nobody sees the whole. people lean on it to argue for a chill pluralism — don't be dogmatic, every tradition has a slice. but i want to actually audit the mapping. what's the elephant, what's the blindness, what's the 'part' each school grabs? and does the analogy smuggle in that there's one unified elephant out there that we're all just failing to see? because anekānta isn't just epistemic humility; it's tied to syādvāda and those modal maybe-predicates that say reality is literally many-sided, not just that we're limited. so where does the elephant picture break: does it miss the logical apparatus? does it turn jainism into simple relativism? lay out the correspondance table, separate the disanalogies that matter for the pluralist conclusion from the ones that don't, and tell me whether that relaxed 'everyone's right' conclusion survives once the analogy's baggage is on the table — or whether there's a better analogy for what jains actually mean.
