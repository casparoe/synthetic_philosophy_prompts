I'm a software engineer without formal training in philosophy, though I occasionally read work on ethics in technology and governance. Lately I have been thinking about the classic political situation in which a leader must take an action that is clearly morally wrong—authorizing deceptive communications, using coercive surveillance, or breaking a procedural safeguard—to prevent a catastrophe, and then carries the moral stain of that choice. I know this general territory is associated with the problem of dirty hands, but I have noticed a more specific pattern that I do not have vocabulary for, and I want to know whether it is recognized in the literature or whether I am conflating distinct things.

The pattern works like this. A leader takes an exceptional, tainted action under extreme pressure and treats it as a singular, non-repeatable breach of principle. When a later situation arises that is serious but not catastrophic, the leader does not defend the new action on its own merits; instead, they point back to the first breach and argue that the moral boundary has already been crossed, so this second step is merely extending an established exception rather than creating a new one. Over successive iterations, the category of what counts as exceptional expands outward through a chain of incremental comparisons, each one defensible in isolation because it stays close to the previous case. Crucially, the leader never repudiates the original moral framework; they repeatedly invoke the gravity of the first violation as evidence of their moral seriousness, even as they systematically widen the scope of actions covered by the exception. The result is a gradual relocation of the permissible boundary that feels structurally different from ordinary corruption or hypocrisy, because the justification at each stage depends on preserving—not denying—the wrongness of the foundational act.

Does this specific mechanism—an acknowledged moral exception being used not to excuse one act but to ratchet outward the boundary of permissible action through chained precedent—have an established name within political ethics? How precisely should it be distinguished from related phenomena such as moral licensing, the normalization of deviance, or the kind of incremental policy drift often called scope creep? And if philosophers have not named it separately, is it best understood as a sociological failure mode of the dirty-hands framework itself, or as a distinct structural risk that applies whenever exceptional moral reasoning is institutionalized?
