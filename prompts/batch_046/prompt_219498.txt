I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy background, and I've been trying to clarify my own judgments about technology-driven automation through Marx's framework—particularly his treatment of machinery, labor displacement, and the capture of surplus value. Here are five concrete cases with my gut-level verdicts, which I hope add up to something coherent, though I suspect they don't.

Case 1: A manufacturing firm installs robotic assembly arms that replace 200 line workers. The owners retain the cost savings as higher dividends; the displaced workers have no comparable jobs available. Verdict: unjust.

Case 2: A technology firm deploys an AI pair-programming system. Hiring for junior developers falls sharply; senior engineers become significantly more productive; company profits rise substantially. Verdict: acceptable—perhaps even beneficial for the industry.

Case 3: A nonprofit releases a free, open-source computer-vision model that eliminates global demand for thousands of freelance image-labeling jobs. No corporation encloses the value; reproduction costs are near zero; the freelancers simply lose income. Verdict: ambivalent—not clearly unjust, but not morally neutral either.

Case 4: A logistics firm fully automates its warehouses, cutting operating costs by roughly half. Most savings pass to consumers through lower prices; shareholders see only modest returns; displaced workers receive six months of severance but no lasting security. Verdict: largely acceptable.

Case 5: A government mandates that any factory automation above a defined threshold must grant displaced workers equity shares in the automated facility, so labor participates directly in the capital returns generated by the machinery. Verdict: if genuinely implemented, this resolves the wrong.

The tension I cannot resolve is between Case 1 and Case 2: in both, capital substitutes machinery for labor and the surplus accrues to owners or to a smaller, higher-skilled labor pool, yet I judge them differently. Case 3 complicates matters further because there is no enclosing owner—only an automated commons—while Case 4 distributes surplus broadly to consumers rather than concentrating it.

Is there a single precise principle—perhaps involving enclosure, the distribution of surplus, control over the means of production, or the near-zero reproduction cost of digital automation—that delivers all five verdicts? If not, which verdict should I overturn, and why? Please state the principle exactly, specify which clause is motivated by which case, and be explicit about where it fails to match my judgments. Then apply it to a case I have not listed: automation eliminates 500 factory jobs but creates 50 highly paid robot-maintenance technician positions, with the net surplus going primarily to shareholders and the original workers receiving only standard severance. What does the principle predict?
