I am a high school senior preparing a submission for an independent student philosophy journal, and I have drafted an essay on what is often called the dogmatism paradox—the tension that seems to arise if knowing one proposition entails being in a position to know everything it logically implies, since that would license retaining belief in the original proposition without investigating its consequences, yet investigating every consequence seems cognitively impossible. Rather than treating the paradox as a simple clash between closure principles and epistemic responsibility, my essay proposes a distinction I call dormant versus activated entailment-justification. On my view, an agent can possess a justification for p that implicitly covers q (where p entails q) without that justification being epistemically available for q until the agent performs a specific cognitive act of unfolding the entailment—something like recognizing the logical relation and integrating it into her evidential structure. I argue that knowledge requires not merely that a justification exists in some logical space, but that it is activated with respect to the specific proposition believed. This means the dogmatist is wrong not because closure fails, but because closure applies only to activated justifications; an agent who never activates the entailment from p to q genuinely lacks knowledge of q, and therefore the failure to know q does not undermine her knowledge of p, since p’s justification was never required to cover q in an activated form.

The essay then considers three objections. First, that my view merely relocates the problem: if activation is required for knowledge of q, then knowing p should also require having activated all of p’s entailments, which seems to reproduce the original paradox at a higher level. Second, that the distinction is psychologically unrealistic, since entailment recognition is not always a discrete act but often a gradual or tacit process. Third, that the view makes knowledge too fragile, because an agent might lose knowledge of q simply by forgetting she had activated the entailment, even though her original justification for p remains intact.

I am writing to ask for critical comments and suggestions, specifically on the following points: whether the dormant/activated distinction is philosophically coherent or whether it collapses into a familiar internalist/externalist divide that I have mislcharacterized; whether my response to the first objection—namely, that p can be known with only locally activated justifications, and that global activation is not required—avoids infinite regress or merely postpones it; and whether there is a more straightforward counterexample that shows knowledge can survive the deactivation of an entailment-justification without thereby becoming dogmatic. I am not looking for bibliographic recommendations or citation checks, but rather for an assessment of whether the argument holds together, which of the three objections is most damaging, and whether the central distinction introduces hidden costs—perhaps in how we understand testimony, memory, or the social transmission of knowledge—that I have not addressed. If the essay’s framing seems unclear or the niche terminology obstructs rather than clarifies the underlying idea, I would also welcome suggestions for how to present the same point without relying on that particular vocabulary.
