Below is a draft essay of roughly 650 words on responsibility for autonomous weapon systems. It avoids bibliographic references and assumes only general familiarity with the debate. I distinguish two failures usually run together: a causal gap, in which no individual human choice is the proximate cause of harm, and a normative-attribution gap, in which a human present in the causal chain—say, an operator who confirms a recommendation—lacks the kind of practical identity-reflection required to be a proper object of reactive attitudes such as moral blame. I argue that inserting a human-in-the-loop may close the first gap while leaving the second intact, because loop-operators can function as rubber stamps whose approvals are procedural rather than substantive expressions of agency. I then note that collective or institutional responsibility might fill the second gap, and that deployment decisions could themselves be seen as expressions of practical identity, but I treat these as responses that need separate evaluation rather than as dissolutions of the distinction.

Please comment on the following three points.

First, accuracy and coherence: Is the separation between a causal gap and a normative-attribution gap genuinely stable, or does it collapse once we examine concrete cases—say, in medical diagnostic AI or autonomous vehicles—where one gap appears without the other? Are there institutional conditions under which an operator's approval becomes a substantive expression of practical identity rather than a rubber stamp?

Second, the rubber-stamp claim: Is it fair to say that a loop-operator is present in the causal chain but absent from the normative structure? What qualifications—training, authority to override, time for deliberation—would be necessary and sufficient to make such an operator genuinely attributability-eligible?

Third, interest and framing: Does this distinction advance the debate in a substantive way, or is it merely taxonomical? If you think the essay misses something deeper—perhaps about collective agency, distributed cognition, or the ontology of artificial action—please identify the omission and suggest how the argument might be restructured. I am especially interested in whether the essay's claim about policy—namely, that requiring loop-presence risks a category mistake—holds up under scrutiny, or whether it overstates the disconnect between causal and normative responsibility.
