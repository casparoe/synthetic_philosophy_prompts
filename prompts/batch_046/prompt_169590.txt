I am a novelist developing a work of historical fiction about the emergence of mercantile rationality in a seventeenth-century port city, and I am applying for a six-month writing fellowship that requires a concise philosophical research proposal—no more than two pages—to ground the narrative logic of the book. The conceptual problem I need resolved: can culture operate as a genuine cause of institutional change, or can it only rationalize changes driven by material conditions? Specifically, I want the proposal to take Weber's Protestant Ethic thesis not as settled history but as an open philosophical problem: does Weber's notion of an 'elective affinity' between Calvinist asceticism and early capitalist practice describe an efficient causal mechanism, a normative scaffold that renders certain economic actions intelligible without mechanically producing them, or a retrospective rationalization that obscures underlying material drivers? The proposal must commit to one of these three interpretations—defending it with explicit reasoning rather than surveying all three neutrally—because the narrative requires a definite model of how belief shapes action: my characters' religious convictions must influence institutional outcomes in a way that is neither deterministic psychology nor post-hoc storytelling. The proposal should state the question with enough precision to yield a determinate answer, explain why the standard deadlock between idealist and materialist readings of Weber fails to resolve it (particularly the objection that correlation is not causation, and the counter-objection that reducing values to epiphenomena eliminates explanatory depth), and lay out the actual method: what conceptual distinctions need to be sharpened, which arguments need to be reconstructed or critiqued, and how a philosopher could advance the debate without new archival evidence or empirical data. Because I have no library access and must work from conceptual analysis alone, do not include a bibliography, citation list, or literature review; reference positions and arguments as needed, but treat the deliverable as philosophically self-contained. The proposal should specify what progress would look like—perhaps a defensible account of 'value-explanation' that distinguishes cultural causation from mental causation and from rationalization—what result would count as failure (for instance, if the normative-scaffold interpretation collapses into either efficient causation or pure rationalization), and why the scope is realistic for six months. Throughout, maintain a committed stance: take a definite position on the interpretation and on the method, justify that choice, and avoid presenting the options as equally viable.
