In a discussion comparing European 35-hour-week legislation with Japanese karoshi as two divergent inheritances of the Protestant work ethic, an interlocutor defended the elimination of overtime caps by invoking the proverb that idle hands are the devil's workshop. Depending on the listener, the phrase seemed to function either as a moral warning about unstructured leisure, as an empirical claim linking unemployment to social disorder, as an ontological thesis that human beings realize value only through continuous productive activity, or as a covert normative argument that legislated leisure limits violate a deeper right to labor. Can you disambiguate the precise claims this slogan could be advancing—cataloguing at least the tautological, the empirical, the normative, and the metaphysical readings—and for each indicate whether it is trivially true, empirically defensible, false, or a category error? Then specify which of these readings actually supports the conclusion that mandatory leisure ceilings are harmful or unjust, which ones confuse the psychological need for occupation with an unlimited right to work, and which are red herrings that do no argumentative work at all. I want a menu of interpretations precise enough to deploy in rebuttal, not a single authoritative translation.
