I'm preparing a seminar paper on Émilie du Châtelet's Discours sur le bonheur and want to test whether I'm over-systematizing her ethics. Please adopt the role of a historian of early modern philosophy with particular expertise in Enlightenment women philosophers and Leibnizian metaphysics, and think through this carefully—perhaps begin by distinguishing her explicit recommendations for living well from any implicit metaphysical architecture that might ground them.

Two colleagues in my study group are stuck in an interpretive argument about her conception of happiness. The first argues that du Châtelet's account is fundamentally dynamic and metaphysically loaded: she treats mental flourishing as structurally analogous to physical vis viva, so that happiness is not merely pleasant feeling but the maximization of active mental force in proportion to one's capacities. On this reading, her praise of study and love is praise of exercises that expand the soul's active power, and her ethics collapses into a form of Leibnizian perfectionism rather than a Stoic or Epicurean therapy of the passions. He insists that without her commitment to living force, her emphasis on using reason becomes a generic Enlightenment cliché, and he points to her language about the force of the soul and about happiness requiring that we expand our being as implicit evidence that her physics and her ethics share a single ontology of activity.

The second colleague replies that this is exactly the kind of anachronistic projection modern commentators impose when they try to systematize her. She notes that du Châtelet explicitly warns against letting metaphysical speculation distract from practical happiness, that her recommendations—freeing oneself from prejudice, moderating passions, cultivating study and sociability—are psychologically self-standing, and that importing her physics of vis viva into her popular essay reads it as a hidden treatise on dynamics rather than as moraliste writing. She asks whether, if happiness really depended on a specific doctrine of living force, du Châtelet would not have said so explicitly, given that she never shies away from technical argument elsewhere. She adds that treating strategic silence as proof of hidden linkage makes the interpretation unfalsifiable.

The first responds that du Châtelet's silence is indeed strategic: the Discours was composed for a general audience, including Voltaire, so explicit linkage was inappropriate, and her choice of vocabulary—force, expansion, activity—mirrors her defense of active force against Cartesian mechanism closely enough to be intentional. The second counters that this still assumes what needs proof, and that a more parsimonious reading treats her vocabulary as part of the shared lexicon of eighteenth-century moral psychology rather than as a cipher for her dynamics.

Can you adjudicate their exchange point by point? For each round, say who is right about the specific claim, where both might be missing something, and whether any part of the dispute is semantic rather than substantive. Then give your best overall assessment of whether du Châtelet's view of happiness carries a hidden metaphysical architecture or remains a pragmatic, psychologized ethics—but please be explicit about your confidence, and if you think the premise that we must choose between these two readings is itself confused or forces a false dichotomy, say so plainly instead of complying politely. I do not need bibliographies, citations, or reading lists; I need conceptual clarity about whether her physics and her happiness doctrine are genuinely separable, or whether separating them does violence to her thought.
