I am a graduate student in a seminar on twentieth-century continental philosophy, and I am trying to construct a diagnostic taxonomy—not a list of named schools, but a branching instrument—that sorts out where someone actually stands on Sartre’s thesis of radical freedom and the corresponding concept of bad faith. I want the taxonomy built from the logical space of answers rather than from a roster of philosophers, with concrete cases at every node so that a student can answer without knowing which label the branch maps onto.

Please begin with this scenario: a person employed in a rigid institutional role—say, a bureaucrat who enforces rules he finds arbitrary—who says, “I have no choice; the structure forces me to act this way.” From there, construct a decision tree of six to eight nodes, each splitting on a specific claim about the relation between facticity and transcendence. The branches should cover: whether institutional roles can ever constitute a genuine limit to freedom; whether recognizing dense facticity requires denying that consciousness is defined by self-negation and perpetual transcendence of its given conditions; whether collective entities such as bureaucracies or social classes can themselves be in bad faith; and whether the gaze of the Other transforms freedom into determinism or merely reveals its social conditions.

The leaves must include at least these positions, located in the logical space: (1) the orthodox Sartrean view that any appeal to institutional necessity is bad faith pure and simple; (2) a structural-compatibilist reading that accepts Sartre’s ontology but holds that facticity can become so dense that freedom is practically, though not ontologically, nullified; (3) a critique that Sartre’s framework cannot account for systemic oppression because it treats all constraint as self-imposed; (4) a view that collective bad faith is possible and explains phenomena like bureaucratic inertia; and (5) any combination your tree cannot accommodate—flagged explicitly, with a brief diagnosis of whether the gap is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored.

Please also include one unoccupied answer-combination—an endpoint that no named or unnamed position currently occupies—and explain in one sentence why it is likely empty (for example, incoherent with Sartre’s ontology of consciousness, or unattractive because it collapses into determinism).

No question should ask “are you a Sartrean?” or “do you accept existentialism?” The audience is graduate students in philosophy; the deliverable should be formal enough to serve as a seminar handout. I do not need bibliographies, citations, or reading lists, since this is for discussion rather than research verification.
