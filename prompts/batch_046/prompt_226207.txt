I have been comparing structural failures of belief revision across artificial intelligence and normative ethics, and I keep returning to a pattern I do not know how to classify. In artificial reasoning, there is the familiar difficulty that an agent updates its model to reflect the direct effects of an action but fails to preserve beliefs about aspects of the world that should have remained unchanged, which gives rise to an ever-expanding list of explicit preservation rules. I see what appears to be a parallel failure in moral reasoning: when a normative framework is expanded to accommodate a new, weighty obligation—say, a long-range duty to distant future generations—the revision does not merely append an additional principle. Instead, prior commitments that are never logically contradicted by the new duty—such as obligations of contractual fidelity or immediate beneficence—seem to be retroactively weakened or dissolved, not through explicit rejection, but because the framework treats normative weights as globally interdependent. Adding substantial weight in one region appears to redistribute the representational load everywhere, so that preserving older commitments requires as much labor as deriving the new one.

This does not strike me as ordinary moral conflict, since the prior commitments are not overridden by a stronger competing claim, nor as value incommensurability, which concerns whether values share a common metric rather than this specific dynamic of representational erosion during update. Is there an established concept—whether in philosophy of mind, ethics, or the philosophy of science—that captures this precise difficulty: the problem of preserving existing commitments across framework expansions, where the failure is representational rather than logical? If an analog to the technical frame problem exists in normative theory, how exactly does it differ in its structure and in its remedies; and if no such analog exists, what boundary conditions would distinguish a genuinely normative version of this preservation failure from a mere metaphorical extension?
