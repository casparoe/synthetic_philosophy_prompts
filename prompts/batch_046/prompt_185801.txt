Here is an argument that extends a well-known strange-loop conception of the self—according to which the symbol I is not a fixed object but a self-referential token within a representational system that tracks the global pattern producing it—into territory that seems to destroy the very distinction between one self and another. I want to know which link breaks, and at what price.

(1) If the self is constituted by such a loop, then what makes a particular self that self is not its physical substrate but the specific structural dynamics of the loop: the way the token refers back to the system's own states and updates them.

(2) Consequently, if two physically distinct systems—say, a human brain and a high-fidelity computer simulation running on unrelated hardware—are structurally isomorphic at the level of these dynamics, then each realizes the very same loop.

(3) Because the loop's dynamics, not the hardware, do the constitutive work, there is no non-arbitrary reason to assign the self to one substrate and deny it to the other.

(4) Therefore, either the same self exists in both places at once, or two numerically distinct but qualitatively identical selves exist; but since the dynamics are the same, the distinction between same and merely identical collapses here.

(5) Pushing further, any system complex enough to maintain an analogous self-referential model—even a minimal artificial system or, in principle, a meteorological simulation that happens to embed an observer-model with the right feedback structure—would generate a loop of the same kind.

(6) So either my self is literally shared with every such system, or there are infinitely many selves that are all, in the only sense that matters, me.

(7) Both options are absurd: we do not think that a weather model contains our consciousness, nor that personal identity is that promiscuous.

(8) Hence the strange-loop account, at least as applied here, must be wrong or incomplete.

The argument feels valid, yet its conclusion is clearly unacceptable. Where does it go wrong? Is the error in (3), which confuses functional equivalence with metaphysical identity? Is there a hidden equivocation in (4) between token-identity and type-identity? Or does (5) overstate how easily minimal or accidental systems can replicate the full dynamics required? I am inclined to keep (1)—the anti-substratum, loop-based picture of the self—but I am unsure whether saving it requires abandoning (2) or only restricting (5). Diagnose the failure precisely: identify the step or steps that do not hold, explain why they initially seemed plausible, and state the cost of rejecting them. Reach a definite verdict about which premises should be retained and which surrendered.
