The debate over whether Kelly betting expresses a genuine rationality constraint—geometric rationality—or merely describes a particular risk attitude has stalled in decision theory for decades, at least in the literature I follow. Proponents treat maximizing expected log utility as the correct normative criterion for repeated or sequential choice, emphasizing that any strategy with a lower growth rate is eventually outgrown with probability one. Critics respond that this smuggles in an intertemporal aggregation rule that standard expected-utility theory can replicate with appropriate curvature, and that the “almost sure ruin” argument proves too much because it treats capital trajectories across time as if they were parallel worlds rather than successive stages of one life. My working hypothesis is that the disagreement is not about the mathematics—everyone accepts the growth-rate result—but about what rationality is evaluating: a property of each isolated decision, of an entire strategy considered as a plan, or of an agent’s welfare across time. Is a hidden empirical assumption doing the work, perhaps about whether agents actually face repeated identical bets, or about how to weigh near-certain ruin against high expected payoff?
