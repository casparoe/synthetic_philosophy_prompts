In management consulting and organizational-psychology writing, a familiar analogy maps the classic dollar-auction dynamics onto internal capital allocation: a division head defending a failing strategic project is said to be playing the dollar auction, where each quarterly budget defense is a fresh bid, the accumulated sunk cost is the escalating loss, and terminating the project is the equivalent of quitting—which means accepting a total loss of prior investment plus the reputational cost of admitting error. Proponents use this mapping to license a specific institutional fix: hard kill-criteria and pre-committed exit rules, on the theory that without them escalation dynamics will systematically override expected-value reasoning. Audit the analogy precisely. First, lay out the correspondence table: what plays the role of the bidder, the rival bidder, the item being auctioned, the bid increment, the rule that the second-place bidder also pays, and the act of quitting. Second, identify the structural disanalogies that matter for the institutional conclusion—including, but not limited to, the fact that in real organizations the bids are distributed micro-commitments made by different stakeholders (engineers, middle managers, board members) rather than discrete moves by a single agent, and that the project can be re-scoped mid-stream in ways that have no equivalent in the auction rules. Third, tell me which features of the auction source domain are being quietly imported—such as the assumption of a unitary rational agent, the absence of information asymmetry, or the zero-sum framing—and whether those imports help or hurt the case for kill-criteria. Finally, render a verdict: does the analogy actually support the prescription of hard exit rules, or was that conclusion riding on the emotional pull of the sunk-cost image rather than on the structural parallel? If the analogy collapses under scrutiny, suggest a better one for the organizational phenomenon.
