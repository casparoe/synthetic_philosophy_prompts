In constructing the metaphysical scaffolding for my novel—specifically the ethical rulings of Dr. Elara Voss, a neurophilosopher who advises on identity after radical neural intervention—I have established a record of her judgments in the tradition of Dennett's brain-transplant puzzle. I need her ruling, or a faithful prediction of it, in a case that fractures her pattern, together with an honest accounting of what remains unsettled.

Her prior verdicts:
1. A brain transplanted intact into a synthetic body preserves the original person, so long as internal causal structure remains continuous.
2. A brain gradually replaced, neuron by neuron, with functionally identical synthetic tissue is still the same person throughout the transition.
3. A brain kept alive ex vivo in a perfusion vat, its signals routed wirelessly to a robotic body, remains the locus of selfhood, because the biological tissue retains causal continuity.
4. A perfect digital scan of a brain, with the biological original destroyed, produces only a replica; the original does not survive.
5. If a brain is bisected and each hemisphere transplanted into a separate, functioning body, neither successor is uniquely the original; the original has divided or ceased.

The new case: A consenting patient has her cerebral cortex extracted and distributed across three separate bioreactors. No single vessel holds more than forty percent of the tissue, and none can sustain consciousness alone. Real-time synaptic relay integrates the three into a single functional system, preserving unified memory, personality coherence, and a singular first-person report—the patient states, from the network, "I am still here." Her original body survives with brainstem and autonomic function intact, but without consciousness, memory, or personality.

What should Voss conclude—survival in the distributed network, in the vegetative body, in neither, or divided—if she is to remain consistent with her record? Identify the closest analog among her five prior rulings and explain its pull. Then articulate at least two competing rules that accommodate all five judgments but diverge on this case, giving the verdict each delivers here. If her precedents genuinely underdetermine the outcome, state the principle she would be adopting with each available choice. Finally, propose one further realistic case—not reliant on exotic physics—that would best distinguish these rules, and state what each predicts.
