I am a PhD student in philosophy serving on my university's behavioral-research ethics panel, and we face a protocol that sits precisely at the boundary between libertarian paternalism and deceptive manipulation. The investigators propose enrolling student volunteers in a retirement-savings simulation in which every participant is defaulted into a high-risk portfolio. The opt-out mechanism is intentionally friction-laden—pre-populated with arguments favoring retention and requiring navigation through several screens—so that leaving demands sustained cognitive effort. Crucially, participants who score below the median on a standard cognitive-reflection measure are never told that the default was deliberately engineered for them; they are informed that assignment was random. Those scoring above the median receive full disclosure. The researchers defend this asymmetry by arguing that status-quo bias is disproportionately strong among low-reflection individuals, that transparency would trigger psychological reactance and nullify welfare gains, and that hiding the architecture therefore functions as protection rather than exploitation. One faction of the panel treats this as sophisticated choice architecture; the other treats it as an informed-consent failure that exploits cognitive vulnerability rather than accommodating it.

Draft the panel's formal decision memo—approve, reject, or approve with conditions—directed to the principal investigator. The memo should state a concrete, actionable recommendation with reasoning the researchers could substantively respond to, take the welfare-based justification seriously, and address the strongest autonomy-based objection without reducing it to a legal formality. End with a line beginning exactly "Verdict:" followed by your recommendation, and then a final line giving a one-sentence summary of the bottom line.
