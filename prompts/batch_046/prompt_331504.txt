I have been thinking on my own about whether an entire nation can bear guilt for wrongs committed long before most of its present citizens were born. I came across Karl Jaspers’s idea—developed in the aftermath of the Second World War—that guilt is not one thing but splits into criminal, political, moral and metaphysical kinds. I am not an academic, and I do not have access to specialised archives, but I would like to test whether his framework actually illuminates a real moment rather than just sorting concepts on paper.

The moment I have in mind is Willy Brandt’s kneeling at the Warsaw Ghetto Uprising memorial in December 1970. Brandt was personally a resister, not a perpetrator, yet as Chancellor of West Germany he performed a public act of atonement for Nazi crimes. I want you to analyse this episode through Jaspers’s four categories. For each kind of guilt, say whether Brandt’s gesture expresses it, whether it applies to the West German state he represented, and whether it can extend to Germans born after 1945 who had no part in the crimes. Then tell me where the framework explains the kneeling well and where it seems strained or silent—particularly whether Brandt accidentally collapsed distinctions Jaspers wanted to keep separate, and whether the idea of collective guilt across generations survives this case. I am especially interested in whether metaphysical guilt, if it exists at all, can be shared by people who never chose to belong to the nation in question, or whether that renders the concept empty.

Please write your answer in British English, keep the analysis disciplined to the actual episode and to Jaspers’s distinctions as I have outlined them, and reach a clear verdict: does this case support the usefulness of dividing collective guilt into these four types, or does it show that the division breaks down when confronted with a real act of national atonement?
