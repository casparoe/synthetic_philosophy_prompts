I have been working through the practical judgments of a colleague who advises municipalities on commemorative policy, trying to map the implicit framework behind her recommendations on remembrance and forgetting. Her record is consistent but not obvious. She endorsed preserving a derelict industrial site—where a catastrophic worker safety failure killed dozens—as an unrestored ruin with restricted guided access, arguing that museum-quality restoration would falsify the material reality of suffering; yet she also insisted on removing a heroic bronze statue of the site's founder and replacing it with an empty plinth inscribed only with his birth and death years, with no explanatory text whatsoever. She rejected a proposal to seal worker testimonies in a digital archive after fifty years, restricting access thereafter to direct descendants, and she has consistently defended maintaining those testimonies in a publicly accessible, meticulously curated repository; simultaneously, she opposed a mandatory secondary-school curriculum requiring students to memorize victim lists and chronological timelines, maintaining that remembrance must not be coercive. She approved a small ground-level plaque listing the dead at the site entrance despite local merchants' objections about property values, but she vetoed an annual state-led ceremony of collective mourning, warning that official grief risks performative appropriation of private loss.

The municipality now proposes an ephemeral memorial at the ruin: on each anniversary, the victims' names are projected onto the wall for exactly one hour after sunset via a system that automatically deletes its data file afterward, requiring a fresh community vote to renew the display for the following year. There is no permanent physical marker, no link to the archive, and no official ceremony—only this conditional, technologically mediated appearance and disappearance. Predict her verdict. Then identify at least two distinct principles—perhaps one closer to an ethics of enduring moral accountability and one closer to a politics of structured social forgetting—that could each explain her previous five judgments, state what each principle predicts about this ephemeral projection, and say which principle you think she is actually running and why. Finally, describe one further hypothetical case—different in kind from the ones above—that would clearly distinguish between these two principles and show which one governs her view.
