In that conversation someone repeatedly invoked the line "if you've done nothing wrong, you have nothing to hide," using it to dismiss concerns about both expansive state surveillance and about citizens actively withholding information. I want to understand precisely what that sentence is doing in this comparative context.

Can you lay out the distinct precise theses the slogan could be expressing, arranged as a menu rather than resolved into one interpretation? In particular, distinguish versions where "wrong" means legal violation, moral failure, deviation from local social norms, or any behavior that lowers one's score in an administrative system; and distinguish what "hide" covers—active deception, passive refusal to disclose, or the prior refusal to generate data at all. For each combination, say whether it genuinely licenses welcoming total visibility, and identify which reading someone is probably relying on when they use the phrase to shut down cross-national comparison rather than to address a specific institutional design. I am not asking for historical attribution or for a literature review; I want the conceptual disambiguation I can take back into the argument.
