You are an expert in comparative political ethics, with deep familiarity with anthropological critiques of universalist anti-corruption frameworks and with the design of experiential philosophy seminars. Before you draft anything, think carefully and brainstorm: identify the precise social moment in a patronage or gift exchange where a Western legal-rational observer sees corruption, but a local participant experiences an unavoidable relational obligation. Then list three concrete decision scenarios in which a formal merit-based rule and a social reciprocity norm issue directly contradictory commands.

Design a runnable, 75-minute classroom activity for 16 upper-level undergraduates seated in a circle, using only paper, index cards, and two-column score sheets. The scenario is the fictional municipality of Valle del Norte, which operates a dual-accountability system: every public decision must be recorded in Ledger A (transparent, rule-bound, conflict-of-interest prohibitions modeled on Western administrative law) and Ledger B (tracking kinship obligations, prior gifts, patronage debts, and democratic-access expectations that bind officials to constituents). Assign roles: eight officials (each with hidden family and patronage ties printed on role cards), six citizens (each carrying an obligation card representing a prior gift or service rendered to an official), and two rotating auditors—one trained in Western procedural audit, one in local customary practice.

The activity runs in three 15-minute decision rounds:
1. Awarding a construction contract through competitive bid.
2. Distributing scarce medical supplies during an emergency.
3. Appointing a school principal.

In each round, every official must enter a decision in both ledgers simultaneously. The mechanics should make it structurally impossible to maximize both: Ledger A rewards procedural purity with reputation points and penalizes any deviation; Ledger B rewards fulfillment of social obligations with community-standing points and penalizes neglect with exclusion penalties. Use drawn obligation cards (e.g., “Your cousin’s cooperative submitted the lowest bid,” “The elder who secured your family’s land now needs medicine for his nephew”) to force the conflict into the open. Citizens vote or appeal after each round based on which ledger they prioritize.

After the rounds, hold a 15-minute debrief that maps each decision onto three conceptual distinctions: (i) corruption as private appropriation of public goods, (ii) corruption as confusion of institutional roles, and (iii) corruption as misalignment between formal rules and the social functions those rules are meant to serve. The debrief should ask which ledger captures which conception, and whether a practice can be non-corrupt under one framing and corrupt under another without anyone being dishonest.
