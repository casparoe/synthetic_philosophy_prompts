I'm running a philosophy-adjacent book club and we're tackling Ursula K. Le Guin's "The Ones Who Walk Away from Omelas" next month. I want to read the story through the seperateness-of-persons objection to utilitarianism — the idea that people aren't fungible containers of welfare and you can't trade one person's crushing suffering for the happiness of thousands. The story is basically the thought experiment made narrative: a child is tortured in a basement so the rest of the city thrives. But I keep wondering whether Le Guin is actually endorsing that objection or subtly undermining it. The citizens who stay seem to perform a kind of aggregation calculus with their eyes open, while the ones who walk away refuse to participate — but they don't save the kid either. Is walking away an acknowledgment of the child's seperateness, or is it just moral evacuation? And the citizens' joy depends on knowing but not knowing, on a kind of structured ignorance — does that map onto anything in the literature about epistemic complicity or moral distance, or am I forcing it?

I want a reading that takes both the story and the philosophy seriously: where does the narrative illuminate the Rawls/Nozick objection in ways that formal arguments don't, and where does it resist that framing? For instance, does the story suggest that recognizing seperateness without acting is just another luxury good? I'd also like to know if there's a scene or character choice that a philosopher of ethics would point to as getting something right that the papers miss — maybe the way the child's suffering is made invisible but necessary. No need for citations or a reading list; I'm just trying to figure out if the story actually advances the debate or just dresses it up, and where an honest critic has to admit the reading breaks down.
