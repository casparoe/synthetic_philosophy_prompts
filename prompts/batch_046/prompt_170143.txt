I'm taking a seminar on Nietzsche's philosophy of history, and I'm trying to apply his triad of monumental, antiquarian, and critical history to concrete institutional practices rather than to nations or individuals in the abstract. Here are my verdicts on whether each practice serves life or undermines it.

A sports federation that constantly invokes its 1960s championship era to motivate current athletes who have never won: monumental history, and I judge it life-denying, because it substitutes an unrecoverable past for present capacity.

A municipal archive that digitizes every record from the past century with no interpretive framework or selection criteria: antiquarian history, life-denying, since preservation itself replaces present action.

A research collective that systematically demolishes the foundational myths of its own discipline but refuses to construct replacements: critical history, life-denying, because pure demolition leaves no ground for decision.

An immigrant community that deliberately suppresses trauma documentation from its homeland so that children grow up with an unburdened sense of belonging: this is selective critical forgetting, and I judge it life-affirming, even though it destroys historical knowledge.

My unsettled case: a therapist guides a patient to write an exhaustive, unflattering family memoir that demolishes every comforting narrative they grew up with, leaving accurate memory but no emotional ground. This also seems like critical history, yet I keep oscillating between seeing it as liberation and as paralysis.

My working principle is that history serves life when it expands present agency—whether by inspiring, preserving usable resources, or clearing obstacles—and harms life when it either paralyzes action or substitutes for it. But this makes both the research collective and the memoir case life-denying for essentially the same reason, and they still feel structurally different: one is intellectual and collective, the other emotional and personal. Can a sharper principle—perhaps distinguishing demolition followed by reconstruction from demolition that is not, or distinguishing collective from individual applications—accommodate all four verdicts? If not, which verdict should be overturned? Please also apply your best principle to a case I have not listed: a corporation that maintains a public museum celebrating its founder's original ethical vision while actively suppressing internal records of recent violations.
