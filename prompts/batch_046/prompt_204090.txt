I run a 90-minute postgraduate philosophy-of-mind seminar with eight participants. I would like you to design a structured in-class simulation that makes the students feel, through their own coordinated actions and subsequent failures, the conceptual pressure behind rejecting the Cartesian theatre in favour of something closer to a multiple drafts model of consciousness. The exercise should assign specific roles, impose restricted information flows, and culminate in a forced collective decision, arranged so that everyone initially assumes there must be a central observational space where all reports converge, and then discovers through the mechanics of play that no such convergence point exists and that apparent unity is only achieved through competitive, distributed editorial revision. To keep the prompt from collapsing into a familiar rehearsal of standard split-brain anecdotes, please inject the following somewhat artificial but rigorous constraint: every participant must simultaneously maintain two temporally incompatible orderings of an identical perceptual sequence — an anterior draft in which event A precedes event B, and a posterior draft in which B precedes A — and the group must reach a unanimous verdict on causation without ever being permitted to bring both drafts into the same shared communicative space or to appoint any individual as an authoritative integrating observer. Please specify the exact roles, for instance sensory reporters, editorial nodes, and a silent arbitrator capable only of veto, the information architecture governing who receives which draft through which channel at each stage, the precise crisis point at which coordination breaks down, and the debrief that maps each experiential failure onto Dennett’s rejection of the Cartesian theatre and onto the editorial dynamics of the multiple drafts account. In addition, include an honest prediction of what will predictably go wrong during the first running and how a facilitator should manage it, together with a frank admission of which feature of the multiple drafts model — particularly the absence of subjective qualitative experience within the editorial process itself — the simulation necessarily falsifies and therefore must be supplied afterwards by explanation rather than by experience. The response should be composed entirely in continuous prose, without headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, written in the manner of a formal letter or email, and should be rendered in British English. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; do not ask students to quote from specific texts; and avoid depending on obscure theoretical references that an informed but offline model could not reasonably be expected to recall.
