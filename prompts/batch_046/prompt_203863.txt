I'm moderating a colloquium Q&A in philosophy of mind, and my guest is an unnamed philosopher who defends what she calls "reconstructive phenomenalism." She accepts Dennett's argument in "Quining Qualia" that intrinsic, ineffable, private qualia are incoherent—she takes the Chase-and-Sanborn taste-comparison intuition pump seriously rather than treating it as a mere rhetorical flourish—but she denies that eliminating those properties removes phenomenal character altogether. On her view, phenomenal properties survive only as high-dimensional relational structures anchored to intersubjective calibration protocols: essentially, a functionalist recovery of "what it's like" that is explicitly modeled on comparative taste-mapping rather than on inverted-spectrum or zombie thought experiments. She claims this avoids both the incoherence Dennett targets and the explanatory gap left open by standard representationalism, because phenomenal facts are grounded in stable cross-subjective mapping relations rather than in behavior or in intrinsic monadic qualities. Write me a sequence of eight to ten interview questions that go beyond having her restate the position. I want at least one question pressing how her calibration protocol handles radical private-language scenarios without collapsing into behaviorism; one on whether her relational reconstruction can genuinely accommodate the intuition—exploited by Dennett—that phenomenal properties seem essentially non-relational and intrinsic; one that distinguishes her view from contemporary representationalist theories of consciousness; and, for each of these, a follow-up keyed specifically to her likely reply that "inter-subjective stability is sufficient to ground phenomenal facts." End with one question asking what neurophenomenological or psychophysical finding could, in principle, count against her reconstructed phenomenal properties. The questions should be rigorous enough for an audience familiar with the literature but should not ask for bibliographies, reading lists, or specific citations, and none should depend on obscure technical results I would need to look up.
