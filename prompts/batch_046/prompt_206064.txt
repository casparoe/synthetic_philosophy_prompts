Me and my mate have been arguing absolutley ages about Keynes’s take on the good life after I went back to that 1930 essay where he imagines we’ll solve the economic problem within a century and then face the real task — living wisely and well. My friend reckons Keynes was just naieve: people in rich countries still grind themselves to dust, so clearly they don’t want leisure, they want status and accumulation. But I think that misses the subtle architecture in Keynes’s framework — he separates absolute needs from relative needs, and he treats the love of money as a kind of mental morbidity rather than ordinary greed. What strikes me as genuinely underdeveloped is whether he thinks relative needs are satisfiable once abundance hits a certain threshold, or whether they are socially constructed in a way that makes them structurally insatiable regardless of wealth. If modern “hustle culture” and performative busyness are actually a form of relative need — status productivity rather than material accumulation — then Keynes’s prediction might fail for reasons he never anticipated, without us having to say people simply suffer from the love of money.

So I’d like you to draft a final-exam essay question for an upper-level history of economic thought seminar — 90 minutes, 20 marks, closed-book — that forces students to do more than summarise Keynes and actually diagnose this tension. The prompt should ask candidates to apply Keynes’s own distinctions — absolute versus relative needs, purposiveness versus the love of money, and the economic problem versus the art of life itself — to the phenomenon of persistent overwork in post-scarcity societies. Specifically, they should evaluate whether this overwork is best explained as (a) the economic problem remaining unsolved, (b) the persistence of the love of money, or (c) a socially constructed relative need that Keynes’s binary framework doesn’t fully capture. Then provide a model answer and a marking scheme built around five must-hit points, with the marks each point carries (total 20), and a one-line note on how candidates typically get each wrong.
