In my metaphysics seminar we are working through the problem of material constitution, specifically the case of a statue and the lump of clay that composes it. One argument circulating in our discussion analogizes the statue's relation to the clay to the relation between a person and their living body: just as a person remains the same individual despite the constant replacement of cells, the statue might remain the same object despite the gradual replacement or reshaping of clay particles, and just as we do not say the person is literally identical to the aggregate of cells at any moment, we should not say the statue is literally identical to the lump. The analogy is being used to support a constitution view over strict identity. Could you lay out the correspondence table explicitly—what plays the role of the person, the body, the cells, and the persistence conditions—then identify where the mapping breaks, especially regarding whether psychological continuity has any counterpart in artifacts, and finally assess whether the conclusion that constitution is the correct relation survives once these disanalogies are exposed, or whether the analogy was carrying the argumentative weight all along?
