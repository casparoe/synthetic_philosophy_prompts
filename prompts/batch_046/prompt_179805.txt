'Newton hangs a bucket of water from a twisted cord and lets it go. In the first stage the bucket spins but the water is flat, even though there's relative motion between them. In the second stage they spin together, so relative motion is zero, but now the water curves up at the sides. Newton says this shows true circular motion is absolute: the water is rotating with respect to space itself, and the concavity is a direct effect of that absolute motion. A relationist like Mach should deny this, but she faces a problem — once the bucket and water share no relative motion, there's no relational difference left to explain the curve. So relationism seems forced to say the water should be flat in stage two, which it isn't.

Mach's reply is that the water is rotating relative to the distant fixed stars and galaxies. The curvature tracks that cosmic relation, not an invisible space. So Newton hasn't proved absolute space; he's only shown that local dynamics depend on the distribution of matter in the universe — Mach's principle. Newton's other thought experiment with two globes tied by a cord in empty space makes the same point: the taut cord proves absolute rotation even with no other bodies around. The relationist has to bite the bullet and say that in a truly empty universe the globes wouldn't rotate, which sounds strange but saves the relational view.'
