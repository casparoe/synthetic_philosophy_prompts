I chair a quarterly allocation panel for a regional disaster-relief fund. Every cycle we face a fixed budget and must choose among competing proposals—immediate food and shelter distribution, long-term legal and housing advocacy for displaced families, ecological remediation of irreversibly damaged watersheds, and preservation of non-substitutable cultural practices such as endangered craft transmission or community archives with no copies. The goods served are genuinely incommensurable: no common metric, whether lives saved, preference satisfaction, quality-adjusted life years, or monetary equivalent, applies across all four categories without systematically misrepresenting what is at stake. I need a reusable decision procedure that my panel, composed of members with mixed expertise, can run within a single three-hour meeting, with the final sign-off resting on me.

Ground the procedure in the philosophical analysis of value pluralism, structural incomparability, and hard choices in which goods are on a par rather than on a scale, but do not provide a bibliography, reading list, or citations. In particular, build in a step I call the "shadow-value audit": a proposal may appear dominated on every articulated criterion yet carry a value—precedent-setting, symbolic repair, or the preservation of a practice that cannot be recreated elsewhere—that only becomes visible once forced comparison on a shared scale is suspended. The audit must include explicit conditions for when it succeeds and when it should be rejected as special pleading.

Please organize your response in the following sections, in this order, with approximate lengths indicated:

1. The Practitioner's Constraints (about 80 words): specify the role, the frequency and stakes of decisions, time and expertise limits, and the sign-off requirement.

2. The Procedure (about 450 words): present the ordered questions or tests to apply to any proposal or pair of proposals; for each, state what counts as a sufficient answer, identify where the procedure returns a definite "approve," "reject," or "defer"; mark precisely where it halts and transfers authority to human judgment because the remaining conflict is one of values rather than facts; and flag which steps are genuine procedural rules and which are judgment calls dressed up as rules.

3. Three Worked Cases (about 250 words each): apply the procedure to (a) rapid food distribution versus rebuilding a destroyed community archive that holds irreplaceable records; (b) funding a long-term legal-advocacy network for displaced families versus constructing temporary climate-resilient shelters for the same population; and (c) a watershed-remediation proposal that scores poorly on every immediate humanitarian axis but invokes shadow-value through irreversible ecological loss. Include at least one case in which the procedure yields an answer the asker is likely to find unwelcome, and explain why the procedure produces it despite that discomfort.

4. Limits and Misfire Modes (about 200 words): state explicitly what the procedure cannot settle, where it will systematically fail or be gamed, and how a panel chair should document a decision that ultimately rests on unweighable commitments.

The procedure must be usable repeatedly without redesign, and it must distinguish the points where an editor—or here, a panel chair—must simply decide.
