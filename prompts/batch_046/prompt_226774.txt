Construct a thought experiment—realistic, confined to one paragraph, situated in a clinical or public-health workplace—engineered to produce the following divergence: an agent who maximizes aggregate moral good comes out intuitively less admirable than an agent who achieves marginally less good but preserves a particular non-instrumental commitment. Specify the commitments of both agents precisely. Constraints: (i) the maximally good agent (Agent A) must subordinate every personal project, including intimate relationships and aesthetic pursuits, to continuous moral optimization, without being self-destructive or causing direct harm; (ii) the second agent (Agent B) must treat one specific non-moral ritual—for example, an unscheduled weekly obligation to a disabled sibling—as a non-derogable constraint rather than as recuperation or as a means to greater future productivity; (iii) neither scenario may invoke lotteries, counterfactual demons, futuristic technology, or exotic physics; (iv) the welfare gap between A and B must be real but modest. Present the scenario. Then, for each agent, state the evaluation that a critic of moral sainthood (in the tradition that treats personal projects as constitutive of a good life) would deliver, identify the belief being evaluated, map the nearby worlds in which the evaluation reverses, and isolate the feature of A's conduct that generates the negative verdict. Conclude with the defense a strict consequentialist would mount: specifically, whether assigning derivative moral weight to B's sibling ritual avoids collapsing back into A's optimization strategy, and what this reveals about whether the distinction between maximization and integrity can survive institutional pressure. Do not provide bibliographies or citation lists; the task is conceptual design and analysis.
