Two colleagues, Mira and Leo, were debating whether the intermediate representations in a large predictive model of protein folding—states that encode causal mechanisms rather than mere correlations—constitute Dennett-style real patterns, and if so, which stance explains them. Mira granted that the patterns are real, but insisted they belong solely to the design stance: they are functional adaptations for minimizing prediction error, like a thermostat tracking temperature. She argued that treating them as beliefs about molecular structure is unwarranted anthropomorphism, because the system lacks normative commitments; apparent misrepresentation is just functional error. Leo replied that these representations carry satisfaction conditions independent of their causal history, and that selective pressure has made them genuinely content-bearing about specific configurations. He claimed that once states can be false in a non-derivative way, the intentional stance becomes indispensable for prediction, not merely heuristic. Mira countered that Leo confuses predictive independence with semantic content—robust counterfactual correlations need not be beliefs—and that the absence of rational agency means the intentional stance misapplies. Leo answered that he requires only representational content, not full agency, and that refusing this attribution misses structurally important generalizations only the intentional stance captures. They asked me to referee. Please adjudicate point by point: which claims hold, where each overreaches, and whether a distinction—say, between derivative and original intentionality, or between the design and intentional stances as Dennett frames them—resolves or sharpens the disagreement.
