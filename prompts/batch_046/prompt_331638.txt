As a member of my university’s museum restitution advisory board, I have been trying to formalize the implicit logic behind our recommendations so that we can apply it consistently to new petitions. Our record to date is as follows.

First, regarding the Benin Bronzes seized during the 1897 British punitive expedition against the Kingdom of Benin: we recommended unconditional physical restitution to Nigerian custodians, specifically recognizing the Oba’s palace as the appropriate receiving institution.

Second, regarding the Parthenon Marbles acquired between 1801 and 1812 through an Ottoman firman: we recommended retention in the present holding institution, coupled with a perpetual rotating exhibition agreement and an open-access digital archive.

Third, regarding a group of sacred masks removed in 1923 by a private colonial expedition—not by state military forces—in what is now Cameroon, where the claimant community continues active initiation rites that employ analogous objects: we recommended physical restitution under a co-curation agreement, allowing our museum to retain study rights over high-fidelity replicas.

Fourth, regarding a collection of pottery legally exported in 1965 from an independent post-colonial state, originally excavated in 1925 without documented community consent, but where the claimant group no longer maintains the associated ceramic tradition: we recommended retention, accompanied by formal provenance acknowledgment and an endowment for a community cultural center.

We now face a new proposal. In 1975, an academic researcher obtained formal export permits from the sovereign government of an independent, post-colonial nation to remove a ritual object still used in local worship. The permits were legally valid, and the state overruled objections raised by the local religious community, which continues to use analogous objects in active practice today. The community now petitions for restitution, arguing that state-level consent did not constitute meaningful community consent. Our museum acquired the object through a bequest in 1998.

If we wish to remain consistent with our own precedents, what should we recommend—restitution, retention with conditions, or some intermediate arrangement? Which of our prior cases is the closest analog, and do our precedents genuinely underdetermine the outcome? If they do, what principle would we be adopting with each possible decision?

More generally, identify at least two distinct rules or frameworks that could jointly explain our four prior verdicts, state what each predicts about this 1975 case, offer your best-guess recommendation with reasoning, and specify one further hypothetical case that would distinguish the two frameworks.

Please state how confident you are in each part of your answer, and explicitly identify the point about which you are least certain.
