After retiring from four decades of work in evolutionary biology—where I spent my time measuring selection pressures, stochastic adaptation, and the predictive modeling that even simple organisms use to navigate environments—I have recently turned to philosophy of action, and I find the standard grid of compatibilism, libertarianism, and hard determinism unsatisfying because it sorts people by the labels they prefer rather than by what they actually believe about concrete cases. I want you to construct a decision tree, not a list of named theories, that maps the varieties of free will genuinely worth wanting, organized entirely by diagnostic commitments a reader can answer without knowing any philosophical terminology. Begin with this concrete scenario: an experienced surgeon performs a routine operation largely on autopilot, later reporting that she could have stopped at any moment, though she never actually formed an intention to do so. The first branching question should ask whether this counts as exercising the relevant freedom—answer yes if the capacity in question is predictive control (counterfactual readiness without conscious deliberation), and no if it requires explicit narrative authorship (the kind of self-constitution in which an action must be endorsed as genuinely mine). For those who answer yes, the second branch should ask whether evolutionary continuity with non-human animals undermines, leaves unchanged, or actually grounds this capacity—specifically, does the fact that a hunting raptor exhibits similar predictive readiness diminish the surgeon’s freedom, enhance its biological legitimacy, or bear no relevance? The third branch should split by where the value of this capacity lies: in its causal efficacy, in its social recognizability, or in its resistance to covert manipulation (for example, by a neurostimulator that preserves predictive control but severs the surgeon’s awareness of the source). For those who answered no at the root, branch instead on whether narrative authorship requires metaphysical openness—indeterminism at the neural level—or only functional autonomy within deterministic processes. Then split that branch by whether evolutionary continuity undermines, supports, or is irrelevant to such authorship, and split again by whether its value is causal, social, or anti-manipulative. Every leaf must state the full combination of answers; if a named position genuinely occupies that space, label it, otherwise mark it unnamed, and in every case include exactly one sentence describing its most awkward practical commitment—something it forces us to say about moral responsibility, addiction, or democratic participation that sounds counterintuitive. The tree should contain at least ten leaves. You must also identify any logical combinations that remain unoccupied and briefly diagnose whether the gap reflects incoherence, unattractiveness, or mere neglect. Keep the language accessible to scientifically literate lay readers; do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations, and do not ask respondents to identify themselves with standard labels. The purpose is to sort people by what they believe, not by what they call themselves.
