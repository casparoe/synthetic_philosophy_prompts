Read Macbeth through Aquinas's criteria for legitimate tyrannicide. Aquinas says you can only kill a ruler if he's a usurper, not a legit authority, if there's genuinely no other recourse, and if it won't just plunge everything into worse chaos — there's also this wierd distinction he draws between private individuals and some form of public authority acting. Macbeth kills Duncan, who's clearly legit, and Macduff eventually kills Macbeth, who's become a tyrant. So: is Macbeth ever in the right according to Aquinas? Does Macduff count as public authority? And here's the more niche part — Aquinas cares about whether the tyrant's rule violates natural law in a specific structural way, not just that he's cruel. The play is full of nature going haywire (the owl killing the falcon, horses eating eachother). Does that map onto the idea that a tyrant disrupts the natural order? Or is Shakespeare just using gothic vibes? I want a reading that takes both the play and the philosophy seriously: don't just say Macbeth is evil and Aquinas agrees, but ask whether the play dramatizes the conditions for just tyrannicide failing, or whether it shows private violence always corrupts regardless of the target. Where does the work resist a Thomist reading?
