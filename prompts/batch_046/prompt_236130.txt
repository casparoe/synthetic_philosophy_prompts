I am a doctoral student in political economy assisting with the design of the final assessment for a graduate seminar on economic philosophy and institutional design. I need a single take-home essay prompt—intended to be answered in approximately 90 minutes at a length of about 1,500 words—that examines students’ command of the socialist calculation debate, specifically the conceptual distinction between Mises’s argument that rational economic calculation requires private property and genuine market prices, and Hayek’s argument that the central obstacle is the dispersion and tacit character of local knowledge rather than a mere computational bottleneck. The question must not be answerable by summarizing Lange’s competitive-solution model; instead, it should present a hypothetical institutional mechanism that forces students to apply both critiques precisely.

The hypothetical mechanism is this: a decentralized, non-market planning system in which production units do not face a central price board, but instead receive continuous input signals generated by peer-to-peer preference revelation across local nodes, with an algorithm aggregating these signals into social-need weights that guide resource allocation. There is no profit motive, no private ownership of capital goods, and democratic oversight only of the aggregation parameters, not of individual production decisions. Students should be asked to determine whether this arrangement escapes Mises’s calculation problem, Hayek’s knowledge problem, both, or neither, and to identify exactly where in the signal-generation, aggregation, and production-response chain each classic critique would apply.

Please provide:
1. The exam question itself, clearly describing the mechanism and specifying the analytical task.
2. A model answer that distinguishes static allocation from dynamic discovery, explains why tacit local knowledge cannot be fully articulated into peer-to-peer signals, addresses the incentive problem for truthful revelation of local conditions, and clarifies why Lange’s iterative price-adjustment framework does not map onto this distributed model.
3. A marking scheme structured around five must-hit analytical moves, with marks allocated to each (total 25), and a one-line note for each on the characteristic misconception or error that should cost marks—such as treating Hayek’s critique as merely about insufficient computing power, or assuming that democratic oversight of aggregation weights solves the incentive-to-reveal problem.

The prompt should not request bibliographies, citations, reading lists, or specific page references, and it should be answerable by a student who understands the conceptual architecture of the debate without having memorized particular editions or publication dates. Write in a formal, polished register appropriate to a PhD-level assessment committee.
