In a seminar I have been running for several years on the idea of the good life in twentieth-century economic thought, one argument has stalled completely. The dispute concerns John Maynard Keynes’s vision—famously articulated in his essay imagining the economic conditions of future generations—of what a good human life looks like once basic material scarcity is largely overcome. One faction, call them the leisure interpreters, holds that Keynes was advancing a normative thesis: the purpose of economic growth is eventually to free human beings for art, friendship, and contemplation, and that a society failing to move toward drastically shorter working hours despite rising productivity is failing to realize the good life he described. The other faction, the adaptationists, holds that Keynes was offering a predictive thesis about human psychology that turned out false: people do not naturally shift toward leisure and culture once their absolute needs are met, because human wants are structurally insatiable and largely positional; therefore Keynes’s vision is not a normative failure but a psychological miscalculation, and the good life for actual humans includes competitive consumption and status-seeking that Keynes underestimated.

Everyone in the room agrees on the relevant historical and economic facts: Keynes valued the arts, he anticipated a future of very short working hours, and modern wealthy societies have not adopted them. Everyone agrees that suffering from absolute deprivation is bad. Yet for years the two sides have talked past each other without identifying a decisive argument either way.

My working hypothesis is that this is not primarily a disagreement about Keynes’s intentions or about empirical psychology, but a hidden disagreement about what counts as a need that economic organization should satisfy—specifically, whether relative or positional wants are needs that a just society should accommodate, or whether accommodating them is precisely the institutional trap that prevents reaching Keynes’s good life.
