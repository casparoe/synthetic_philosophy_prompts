I am working through a passage in Book I of Jean-Jacques Rousseau’s The Social Contract (1762) that describes the formation of the sovereign body through the social pact. Rendered in my own words, the text argues that each person, in entering the agreement, alienates his natural liberty to the collective and receives civil liberty in return; by this act, the contracting parties become a single moral body—the sovereign—composed of citizens who are simultaneously members of the general will and subjects bound by the laws it issues. Rousseau notes that anyone who refuses the pact stands outside this body, yet he supplies no explicit rule for deciding who counts as having entered, so the boundary of the contracting group appears to be fixed prior to the contract itself.

What puzzles me is a tension in the passage’s logic. On one hand, the sovereign is constituted only by those who have actually consented, which seems to presuppose an already-delimited group of contractors. On the other hand, the state’s legitimacy—including its authority to determine membership—derives from this very sovereign. This suggests a recursive membership condition: the demos must be defined in order to constitute itself, yet its constitution is supposed to justify the boundary. I am trying to understand whether Rousseau’s distinction between sovereign and government, or his conception of the general will as applying only to those already inside the association, offers any resources to break this circle. Does the passage implicitly rely on a pre-political boundary—perhaps territorial presence or natural association—that is not itself generated by democratic decision? Or does the circularity remain unresolved, indicating that the contractarian framework cannot ground the demos without assuming it?

Please help me understand the internal structure of this argument without appealing to modern secondary literature or offering a bibliography. I am especially interested in whether introducing the idea of recursive membership clarifies the difficulty, and in whether the boundary problem is here dissolved, relocated, or left genuinely open within Rousseau’s own conceptual framework.
