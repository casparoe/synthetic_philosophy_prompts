Over the past term I have been recording the judgments of a colleague—an art historian with a background in moral philosophy—on whether immoral origins or content can diminish a work's aesthetic value. Her verdicts so far: (1) a celebrated novel written by an author who privately held virulent racist views, but which depicts multiracial friendship with sympathy and contains no racist content, is an unqualified masterpiece and should be taught without biographical caveat; (2) a contemporary film that graphically depicts sexual violence and invites the audience to take aesthetic pleasure in domination, directed by a filmmaker later convicted of assaulting cast members in ways that mirror the on-screen dynamics, is aesthetically defective—its formal choices are corrupted by the moral vision they serve; (3) a monumental sculpture made from marble illegally quarried from a protected site, whose form and meaning are entirely unrelated to archaeology or theft, is beautiful and conceptually successful, yet exhibiting it without explicit acknowledgment of the theft is ethically impermissible; (4) an abstract symphony by a composer who decades later advocated totalitarian politics, with no programmatic or lyrical content, retains its full aesthetic value and should be performed without reservation. I now want to ask her about a hypothetical installation, "The Empathy Machine," created by an anonymous collective. The piece uses biometric sensors—calibrated using data extracted without consent from participants in a deceptive psychology study conducted by one collective member—to detect viewers' emotional states and weave them into a resolving soundscape. Its explicit theme is genuine emotional transparency and informed consent; it contains no reference to the study. The fraudulent calibration is technically essential to the installation's functioning but conceptually invisible.

Predict her verdict on whether "The Empathy Machine" is good art, identify the implicit rule governing her judgments—distinguishing, for instance, internal ethical criticism that evaluates only the work's represented attitudes and invited responses from production-integrated evaluation that treats the moral character of the making as aesthetically relevant when thematically echoed—note at least one competing rule that accommodates all four prior verdicts equally well, and propose a further hypothetical case that would clearly separate those rules. Please answer in American English. State how confident you are in your interpretation as a whole, and identify the specific point—the classification of this case, the characterization of the competing rules, or the proposed distinguishing case—about which you are least certain.
