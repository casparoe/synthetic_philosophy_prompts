As a retired physical chemist who has only recently begun formal study of philosophy, I find standard textbook treatments of value conflict unsatisfying because they tend to collapse qualitative differences into quantitative trade-offs. I am auditing an advanced seminar on practical reason, and I want to set an examination problem that cannot be answered by reciting familiar distinctions alone. The domain is incommensurable values and hard choices, but I want you to embed the analysis in a constructed institutional scenario rather than a canonical classroom example. Specifically: a regional water authority must decide between Project A, which installs filtration that eliminates a chronic neurotoxin but permanently alters a watershed’s hydrology, and Project B, which preserves the watershed intact but maintains sub-acute neurotoxin exposure across a generation. The goods at stake—population-level neurological security versus ecological integrity—are not reducible to a shared metric such as QALYs, willingness-to-pay, or disability-adjusted life years.

Write an exam essay question, appropriate for a 90-minute response at the upper-division level, that requires the student to do three things: first, explain why this case exemplifies incommensurability rather than mere difficulty in weighing values; second, articulate the difference between comparative evaluation and the hard-choice structure in which one option is neither better than, worse than, nor equal to the other, and in which rational choice requires a non-comparative reason rather than a heavier weight; and third, evaluate whether the authority’s obligation is to render a decisive choice now or to design a procedural framework that preserves the conditions for future revision without resolving the conflict.

Provide a model answer of approximately 700 words. Then provide a marking scheme totaling 20 marks, divided among five specific conceptual moves that a strong answer must contain; for each move, state the marks assigned and a one-line note on the characteristic error that costs those marks—such as conflating incommensurability with incomparability, treating hard choices as merely high-stakes trade-offs, or assuming that institutional rationality requires a single metric. The prompt should be formal and precise, should not request bibliographies, citations, or reading lists, and should assume only familiarity with the conceptual terrain rather than with obscure secondary literature.
