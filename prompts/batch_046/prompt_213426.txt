A philosopher colleague gives these survival verdicts: (1) standard teletransporter—scan, destroy the original, build a psychologically identical replica on Mars—survival holds; (2) same procedure but the original is not destroyed—only the Earth-bound original survives, the copy is a different person despite perfect psychological overlap; (3) gradual neuronal replacement with synthetic equivalents preserving all psychology—survival continues uninterrupted; (4) branching destruction that produces two perfect replicas—neither is the original, which is closer to death than to double survival; (5) cryogenic suspension and later revival of the intact brain—survival, but storing psychology as inert digital data with no causal interaction for a century then instantiating it in a new body—no survival.

Consider a new scenario: for twenty years, a person's psychology is maintained continuously in a distributed, non-biological substrate, never residing in a single body but migrating every few months between temporary robotic hosts with perfect informational fidelity and no branching, after which it is downloaded into one permanent biological body. Predict her verdict. Identify at least two competing principles that accommodate her earlier judgments—one allowing substrate-independent continuity so long as causal-informational uniqueness and an unbroken chain are preserved, another requiring that survival be mediated through a singular, uninterrupted embodiment or direct causal pathway that is broken by serial disembodied hosting across disconnected carriers. State what each principle predicts for this case. Give your best-guess answer with reasoning, name the further hypothetical case that would best discriminate between these principles, and specify what philosophical commitment she adopts by answering yes versus no.
