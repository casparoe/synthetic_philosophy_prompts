I just finished a long argument with a friend about Chesterton's fence and Burkean conservatism—specifically whether the principle survives once bounded rationality is taken seriously. She claimed that if investigating an institution's purpose is genuinely costly and the answer is obscure, then preserving it out of ignorance is no more rational than tearing it down; the fence collapses into mere status-quo bias. I want participants to feel the pull of preservation as an epistemic cost, not hear it as a slogan. Design a 75-minute workshop activity for about 20 philosophy or politics graduate students: they serve on reform committees facing a detailed fictional institutional "fence"—a complex practice whose original rationale has been lost to organizational amnesia. Give them strict budgets of "inquiry tokens" that can be spent to recover fragments of the original purpose, but investigating consumes collective time and resources, while voting to abolish is free and immediate. Arrange the payoff structure so that free-riding or skipping straight to abolition is individually rational, yet groups that do so suffer a hidden systemic failure revealed only in the debrief. Include specific roles, rules, a timeline, and materials. The debrief should map moments in the activity onto Chesterton's epistemic humility and Burke's defense of prescription, and should be frank about what the simulation cannot reproduce—especially that real institutional knowledge is often distributed across users rather than buried in recoverable archives. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it.
