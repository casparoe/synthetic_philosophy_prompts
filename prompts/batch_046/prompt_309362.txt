I've been working through my own position on whether being able to imagine something is a good reason to think it could actually exist, particularly when it comes to minds and consciousness. I want to lay out where I've landed and see if the pieces fit, because individually they all seem plausible to me, but I have a nagging sense that I'm borrowing from incompatible motivations. Here is the package as it stands. First, I think conceivability is a defeasible but genuine guide to metaphysical possibility, but only in what I think of as "structurally transparent" cases—where I can clearly see how all the components are arranged and how they relate, with no hidden machinery I don't understand. Second, I think the zombie scenario counts as exactly that kind of case: I can conceive of a being physically arranged exactly like me, down to every neuron and behavior, but with no inner experience whatsoever, and I don't see any contradiction hiding in that picture; so I take this to show that consciousness is not reducible to physical structure. Third, I don't think the water-is-H2O objection undermines this, because my ordinary concept of water is "porous"—it points outward to whatever fills the lakes and quenches thirst, hiding chemical structure I don't grasp—so conceiving water without H2O is really just conceiving a lookalike, not a transparent counter-possibility.

There are a few more commitments that complicate the picture. Fourth, I think mathematics shows that structural transparency can fail even without hidden empirical facts: I can conceive, in a structurally clear way, of a geometry with five parallel lines through a point, yet I accept that this is impossible because it violates axiomatic constraints. Fifth, despite that, I want to maintain that consciousness is special because phenomenal concepts involve a direct kind of acquaintance that mathematical and empirical concepts lack, which is why the zombie case survives where mathematical falsehoods don't. Sixth, I hold that if a concept is genuinely transparent, then even an ideally rational agent with complete knowledge should still be able to conceive its absence—otherwise the concept wasn't transparent, just unknown to me. Seventh, I also think that for practical modal judgment, actual conceivability should be enough; I shouldn't have to become an ideal agent to license a possibility claim. I'm not asking for a lecture on the literature—just an audit of my own set. Which pairs are genuinely in tension, not just apparently so? How severe is each tension, and what would be the least costly revision? Specifically, does my appeal to direct acquaintance in consciousness conflict with my claim that zombie conception is structurally transparent? Does the mathematical failure mean I need an ad hoc exemption for mind, and if so, does that undermine the guide's reliability? And is there only an apparent tension between my view that water-conceptions are porous and my view about ideal agents, since an ideal knower wouldn't be able to conceive false necessities anyway? Rank the real problems by severity, tell me what each revision costs, and indicate whether there's a single change that cleans up the most.
