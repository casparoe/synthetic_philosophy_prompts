You sit on an institutional review board that must decide, for each social-insect research proposal, whether the colony counts as possessing a collective mind—thereby triggering welfare-protection protocols—before any cognitive-intervention study may proceed. Operationalize the theoretical criterion of reciprocal corrigibility: a distributed system qualifies as minded if and only if subgroups can alter global behavior in ways not reducible to the summed responses of individual agents, and if those alterations can be erroneous (the system can be misled) without every node receiving the misleading signal.

Write a reusable decision procedure your board can run in thirty minutes using only observable field data—trail geometry, response latency, error-correction patterns, and brief isolation tests. Specify the ordered questions to ask; what counts as sufficient evidence at each stage; the exact outputs (treat as minded, treat as non-minded, or indeterminate—escalate to full board); and the judgment calls that cannot be eliminated, particularly how to distinguish collective error from local malfunction.

Then apply the procedure to three proposals: (1) vaccinating a mature leaf-cutter colony against a fungal pathogen that disrupts pheromone signaling; (2) culling foragers in an overpopulated harvester-ant colony to prevent a winter die-off; (3) introducing an artificial pheromone trail to redirect a colony away from a protected crop zone, where the colony repeatedly returns to the old route despite the new trail being chemically dominant.

In at least one case the procedure should deliver a verdict that conflicts with intuitive welfare concern; flag precisely which normative premise within the criterion drives that result, and state what the procedure cannot settle on its own.
