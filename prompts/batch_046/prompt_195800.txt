Please construct a decision tree that classifies philosophical positions on whether Gödel's first and second incompleteness theorems carry any non-mathematical significance—specifically for theories of mind, physical law, or artificial intelligence. Build the tree from logical commitments rather than from named authors, with every internal node phrased as a concrete yes/no or either/or question that an advanced undergraduate could answer without knowing which interpretation it leads to.

Use four successive branching distinctions as the backbone:
1. Is the target domain (mind, physics, or AI) in principle capturable by a single, consistent, recursively enumerable formal system?
2. If yes, can self-reference be eliminated by stratification—typed hierarchies, meta-language separation, or modular architecture—so that Gödel's diagonal construction is blocked?
3. If self-reference is unavoidable, does the system's inability to prove its own consistency constitute an epistemic limitation for that domain, or is it merely a technical feature with no philosophical spillover?
4. For the domain in question, is there an independent, non-formal access to truth—such as direct intuition, empirical measurement, or external verification—that allows the system to recognize its own consistency from the outside?

Each leaf must carry a named position (or be labeled "unnamed / unexplored" if no prominent view occupies it) together with one sentence on its most awkward commitment—the counter-intuitive consequence its defenders must accept. You must include at least the following positions somewhere in the tree: the strict mathematical-isolationist view (Gödel applies only within formal arithmetic and has no philosophical spillover); the Penrose-style argument that human mathematical insight transcends any formal system; a distributed or plural-system response that blocks Gödel by denying there is one system describing the mind; and a "probabilistic" or "fuzzy-logic" escape that denies the bivalence required for the first theorem's proof.

In addition, flag any pair of leaves your tree cannot genuinely separate, and include at least one logically possible but unoccupied branch—an answer combination that nobody seems to defend—together with a brief diagnosis of why it is vacant (incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored). The audience is philosophy-of-science students who have seen Gödel's theorems in outline but have not studied technical logic texts in depth. Do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations, and avoid framing any question so that answering it requires knowing an author's name in advance.
