I cover cognitive science and philosophy of mind for a magazine, and I'm putting together a workshop session for next month—about 10 people, mostly gradute students and a couple of artists, 75 minutes, paper and pens only, no screens. I've been obsessed lately with Hofstadter's old knob-twiddling picture: creativity isn't some ex nihilo lightning bolt, it's just turning hidden dials—mood, scale, tempo, relational structure—on a mental template you didn't know you were carrying. I want them to feel that pull in their bones, not just hear me describe it. Could you design a structured game or exercise where they start with one bare-bones "theme"—say, a three-line interaction between two strangers in a doorway—and then generate what they genuinely believe are wildly different creative spin-offs? The rules should secretly restrict them to adjusting only specific parameter dimensions (like formality, physical proximity, temporal urgency, emotional valence, and one "locked" negative knob that can't be turned up, only down), so that every output lands on the same hidden grid. They should compare results, argue about which ones are "truly original," and only then see the grid revealed.

The debrief needs to map those moments—especially the defensive insistence that one version breaks the mold—onto Hofstadter's claim about analogical mapping versus genuine invention from nothing. Be honest about what the game can't capture: for instance, the emergence of a brand-new axis that wasn't on the original template, which is probably where the knob-twiddling model actually gets shaky. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong—like one participant deliberately breaking the parameter rules to invent a new category and ruining the statistical pattern—and how to handle it without shutting them down. Don't assign any pre-reading or ask for citations or reading lists; some folks won't have touched Hofstadter's stuff and I don't want anyone guessing bibliographies. Keep instructions practical and runnable, and make sure they come away seeing creativity as navigation inside a parameter space they didn't design, rather than magic.
