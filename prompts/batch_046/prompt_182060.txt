I'm preparing a feature on the ethics of dramatizing real, deceased figures in historical fiction and biopics. For the piece, I want to compare two conceptual frameworks that address what we owe the dead. On one side is posthumous autonomy: the position that deceased persons retain a dignity-based interest against misrepresentation, especially regarding unverifiable private mental states, which imposes strict limits on artistic invention. On the other side is narrative stewardship: the view that dramatists hold a fiduciary-like obligation to the historical record, one that permits imaginative reconstruction of interior life—specifically, what I term counterfactual interiority—provided that such invention illuminates documented actions and relationships rather than distorting them.

Please compare these frameworks with particular attention to counterfactual interiority: the attribution of specific, unrecorded thoughts and emotional responses to real dead people. Which framework provides the more defensible boundary between legitimate artistic license and wrongful misrepresentation? In your comparison, evaluate whether either account can accommodate cases in which invented interiority is historically plausible and emotionally resonant yet factually unverified, and identify any important conceptual overlap or tension between the two positions that my readers should understand.
