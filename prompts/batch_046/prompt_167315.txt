I'm preparing a long-form feature for a science-and-ideas magazine on the conceptual architecture of synthetic biology, and I've become stuck on a philosophical distinction that my editors think readers will find unsettling. In philosophy of biology, etiological accounts hold that a trait's function is determined by its evolutionary history of natural selection—hearts pump blood because selection shaped them for that—while causal-role accounts define function by a trait's present contribution to a system-level capacity, regardless of origin. The textbook examples are familiar—hearts, kidneys, eyes—but I'm writing about engineered carbon-capture microbes and de-extinct organisms that have no evolutionary lineage whatsoever, yet are designed precisely to perform ecological roles like nitrogen fixation or atmospheric carbon sequestration. Given this, what are the most important implications of the etiological versus causal-role divide for whether we can coherently attribute biological functions to such entities? Specifically, I want to know whether the etiological view forces us to deny functional language to all synthetic life, reducing it to mere mechanism, and whether the causal-role view risks dissolving the boundary between biological function and engineered artifact—say, between a synthetic microbe and a carbon-scrubbing factory filter. I am not looking for a literature survey or reading list; I need conceptual mapping that a reader without specialist access could follow. Please push back plainly if the premise is confused—if the distinction genuinely collapses in synthetic contexts, or if asking for "implications" here presupposes a false dichotomy—rather than politely accommodating the framing.
