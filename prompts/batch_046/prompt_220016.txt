In my philosophy of mind seminar this term, two classmates—Maya and Leo—had a sustained disagreement about whether Douglas Hofstadter's pattern-based account of the self allows an "I" to be distributed across separate brains, and they asked me to help adjudicate since I was only listening. Maya began by arguing that if the self is a strange loop—an abstract pattern of self-reference—then it should be substrate-independent, much like software running on different hardware. She proposed a hypothetical: if every neural correlation of my episodic and emotional patterns were copied into a second brain, while my original brain continued functioning, there would now be two instances of the same loop. She claimed both should count as me, not because they share atoms, but because they instantiate the identical causal-structural pattern that Hofstadter identifies with the self. Leo objected that this ignores a constraint built into the loop idea itself: self-reference is not just structural but historically anchored. A loop, he said, requires a single causal thread in which earlier states influence later ones; if you copy the pattern to a new substrate, the copy lacks that continuous causal history with my past. He went further and introduced a condition he called substrate coupling: for a distributed self to exist, the separate substrates must remain causally coupled—exchanging information in real time—otherwise they are not one distributed "I" but two diverging branches, each with its own loop. Maya replied that Leo was smuggling substance metaphysics back in through the language of causal threads and ownership. She insisted that the historical anchoring is itself just another pattern—specifically, a narrative pattern of continuity—and that both copies could inherit it. She added a twist: suppose only my emotional and episodic patterns are copied, not my semantic or linguistic ones. Then we have a partial distribution. She argued that this partial "I" is still genuinely me, just incomplete, like a symphony played with missing instruments. Leo countered that partial copying undermines the very idea of a loop, because self-reference requires the full set of patterns that recognize themselves as such; a fragment that remembers feeling but cannot recognize itself as remembering is not a self at all, but a relic. He also argued that if Maya is right, then practical concerns—such as whether I should care about the copy's welfare—collapse into ambiguity, since there is no principled way to decide which instance is the original deserving priority. Maya's final response was that this practical ambiguity is not a metaphysical problem but a feature: if the self is genuinely distributed, then our moral and prudential obligations should also distribute, and clinging to uniqueness is just an evolutionary leftover.

In particular, address whether Leo's substrate coupling condition is consistent with Hofstadter's view or an ad hoc addition, and whether Maya's partial-distribution case actually preserves self-reference or destroys it.
