I am a doctoral student in aesthetics and the philosophy of literature, currently preparing a seminar on Tolstoy's late theory of artistic communication in What Is Art? (1897). I want to read Oscar Wilde's The Picture of Dorian Gray (1890) through this framework, but I am concerned not to treat the novel as a mere illustration of Tolstoy's moralism. Tolstoy argues that genuine art communicates feeling with sincerity and infectiousness, uniting an audience in shared perception, whereas counterfeit art stimulates lower faculties—vanity, sensual curiosity, or elite exclusivity—without achieving that union. I am particularly drawn to a tension that standard accounts of Tolstoy overlook: his claim that art must be immediately accessible to ordinary people, and his corresponding suspicion of works whose formal brilliance obscures the feeling being communicated.

Read the novel through this lens, paying close attention to Lord Henry Wotton's aphorisms, the progressive disfigurement of the portrait, Sibyl Vane's suicide, and Dorian's final confrontation with the canvas. Where does the text seem to embody the counterfeit art Tolstoy condemns—where its aesthetic surface might actually obstruct the sincere moral feeling that the plot ostensibly conveys? Conversely, is there any scene or image, particularly in the portrait's transformation, that functions as genuinely infectious art by Tolstoy's criteria, communicating a universal moral perception despite the decadent framing? I would also like you to analyse whether the novel's historical reception as immoral, rather than as a sincere warning against corruption, can be explained by this formal tension. I am writing this for an interdisciplinary audience, so I want the analysis to remain faithful to Wilde's text—treating it neither as pure aestheticist doctrine nor as a hidden Tolstoyan sermon—while showing exactly where Tolstoy's framework illuminates the novel's structure and where the novel exposes limits in his distinction between sincere feeling and artificial form.
