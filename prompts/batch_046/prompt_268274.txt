My opening move introduces a twist on the standard Rawlsian separateness objection. Suppose we accept a reductionist account of personal identity—the view that a single life is a series of psychologically connected stages lacking any further metaphysical unity. On that picture, intrapersonal prudence is already a form of aggregation across stages that are, in the relevant metaphysical sense, "separate." If the separateness objection blocks interpersonal aggregation because the units involved are not sufficiently unified, then it should also block rational prudence across one's own life—which is absurd. Conversely, if intrapersonal aggregation is permissible despite this reductionism, then the moral barrier cannot be metaphysical separateness itself, but must be something else—perhaps a normative status that the objection never explicitly defends.
