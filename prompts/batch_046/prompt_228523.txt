I've been getting into Bernard Williams's take on opera — specifcally his idea that opera's power comes from it not even pretending to be realistic, so the emotional stuff lands differently than in film or regular theater. But I think he's missing a layer. To me, opera works not just because it's artifcial, but because there's this specific kind of structural transparency: the music and the plot are often deliberatly out of sync in a way that forces you, the audience, to actively build the emotional meaning yourself. Like, you hear a gorgeous aria about joy while the character is actually being betrayed, and because the artifice is so obvious, you can't just passively absorb the feeling — you have to decide what the emotion actually means in context. That active construction is why opera can handle moral ambiguity without being manipulative; you're always aware you're constructing the response, so you don't confuse feeling with moral judgment.

So I'm holding this modified Williams-style view: opera's artifciality isn't just absence of realism, it's a kind of engineered misallignment that makes the audience co-construct emotional meaning, which protects it from being ethically dubious in the way realistic tragedy might be. What would actually count against this? Not just "opera is unrealistic and therefore silly" — I want the serious defeaters. Which empirical observations about how audiences actually process opera would hurt it? What if psychological studies showed people don't actively construct meaning but just absorb the music's emotional valence regardless of plot? Or what if the best opera productions actually minimize misallignment and maximize coherence, and that's what critics praise? Also, could there be a philosophical objection that if emotional response is always mediated by conscious construction, then opera isn't really moving us genuinely, it's just an intellectual exercise?
