A colleague recently summarized for me an argument—drawn, I believe, from a recent essay in comparative pragmatics and moral philosophy—concerning Dutch communicative directness, Japanese indirectness, and the ethical status of white lies. The summary runs approximately as follows.

Dutch directness is not, on this account, a failure of politeness but rather an expression of low negative-face concern in the Brown-Levinson framework: the speaker treats the interlocutor as an autonomous agent who requires no protective buffering, so explicitness functions itself as a form of respect. By contrast, Japanese indirectness, mediated through the distinction between tatemae (public stance) and honne (private feeling), is said not to concern negative face at all, but rather to preserve positive face and the relational value of wa (harmony). From this it is inferred that a white lie in a Dutch context violates Grice’s Maxim of Quality and thereby undermines the interlocutor’s epistemic autonomy, constituting a genuine moral failure. In Japanese contexts, however, the same utterance should not be classified as a lie in the moral sense; instead it is an instance of assertive force modulation, whereby the speaker adjusts the epistemic commitment of the statement to maintain social equilibrium, analogous in function to a hedge or performative disclaimer. The essay concludes that the moral wrongness of white lies is therefore indexed to the operative face-system—breaching autonomy in negative-face cultures, expressing care in positive-face cultures—which entails that universalist condemnations of dishonesty are culturally parochial.

This strikes me as largely coherent, yet I am unsure whether it fairly represents the pragmatics literature or whether the philosophical inferences genuinely follow. Could you audit this summary with some care? I should like you to distinguish, point by point, what is accurate about Brown and Levinson’s politeness theory and about Japanese communicative practice, what are common simplifications or subtle conflations—particularly regarding the relation between tatemae/honne and face theory, and between Gricean maxims and adjustments of speech-act force—and whether the move from observed cultural variation in communicative norms to the parochialism of moral universalism is warranted or instead commits an error of inference. I am especially interested in whether the notion of assertive force modulation, as applied here to Japanese indirectness, is a legitimate extension of speech-act theory or rather a category mistake that treats a social-relational practice as a technical manipulation of propositional content.
