A professor in my philosophy of language seminar offered the following summary of Richard Rorty’s antirepresentationalism: Rorty holds that because mental states are not mirrors of nature, beliefs are best understood as habits of action rather than representations of reality. This means, she said, that there is no substantive distinction between being epistemically justified in holding a belief and that belief being true of the world—justification is all there is, and truth adds nothing explanatory. She went on to claim that scientific progress is therefore never rationally compelled by evidence but is always just a shift in vocabulary driven by social utility, so a well-confirmed theory like evolutionary biology has no more epistemic authority than a myth that happens to help a community cope. Truth, on this reading, is nothing but whatever vocabulary achieves solidarity, and antirepresentationalism consequently undermines the special authority of empirical science. I suspect this is unfair but I lack the background to pinpoint where. Could you audit this summary? Please separate what accurately reflects Rorty’s rejection of the mirror-of-nature metaphor and his pragmatist account of belief, what subtly misrepresents his view by collapsing the distinction between causal interaction with the environment and justification within a vocabulary, and what overstates his position into a linguistic idealism or relativism he explicitly resisted. Also, does her conclusion—that antirepresentationalism strips science of its authority—actually follow from the corrected parts, or does Rorty’s framework allow empirical inquiry to retain a kind of practical authority that the summary obscures?
