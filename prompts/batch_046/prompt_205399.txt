I sit on our city's Community Wellness Allocation Board — we divy up about $14M a year to neighborhood clinics based on health-need scores. For the past five years we've used a weighted formula: clinics serving populations with lower baseline health outcomes get more per resident. Next Tuesday we vote on Proposal 7B, which would switch us to flat per-capita grants.

Here's the thing: because our total budget is capped and there's a transition-cost clause in 7B, the flat rate wouldn't actually raise funding for the worst-off clinics at all. The savings from cutting the well-funded clinics get eaten by administative audits and compliance checks. So we're literally talking about making the better-served neighborhoods worse off without helping anyone else — classic leveling-down, except the people proposing it say that's exactly why we should do it, because our current weighted system "institutionalizes status hierarchy" even if it improves absolute outcomes. They aren't calling it prioritarianism; they're calling it "relational fairness." Meanwhile half our board thinks the charter's language about "priority to the most disadvantaged" is basically prioritarian and that leveling-down is a reason to reject 7B, not a reason to accept it. The other half thinks prioritarianism is vulnerable to the leveling-down objection and that 7B fixes that by removing the hierarchy altogether.

Can you draft the short decision memo I'll circulate before Tuesday's meeting? It should recommend whether to approve, reject, or approve with conditions — with a concrete time frame if conditions are involved — and it needs to treat the relational-fairness argument seriously instead of just quoting the charter. I'm especially stuck on whether prioritarian weighting actually committs us to reject a pure leveling-down move, or whether that's a confusion about what the leveling-down objection targets. Don't just list both sides; take a position I can defend in the room, but be honest about the strongest point the losing side has.
