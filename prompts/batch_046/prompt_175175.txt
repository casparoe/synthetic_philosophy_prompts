You are a senior philosopher of practical reason with particular expertise in decision theory, value theory, and the philosophy of personal identity. Before constructing your answer, take a few minutes to think carefully and to brainstorm the conceptual terrain: what is genuinely distinctive about experiences said to be "transformative," and why do standard models of rational choice seem to fail for them?

Write the full specification—the desiderata—that any adequate theory of transformative experience must meet in order to count as genuinely explanatory rather than merely descriptive or taxonomic. I want the criteria themselves and the work of justification, not a literature review or a bibliography.

Structure your response as follows:

1. The canonical cases and phenomena the theory must accommodate. Include at least the decision to become a parent, the decision to undergo a radical religious or vocational conversion, and the so-called "vampire" scenario of choosing an experience one currently cannot imagine valuing. 2. The non-negotiable conceptual distinctions the theory must preserve without collapse. 3. The consistency conditions with adjacent domains. State which results from psychology of identity continuity, from normative decision theory, and from evolutionary or biological accounts of preference formation constitute hard constraints that a philosophical theory may not contradict without argument, and which are merely preferences of particular theoretical camps (e.g., a commitment to full transparency of reasons to the pre-transformation self).

4. The joint-unsatisfiability problem. Identify any subset of your desiderata that appears to be jointly unsatisfiable given standard assumptions—particularly any tension between preserving the rational autonomy of the pre-transformation agent and granting authoritative weight to the preferences or judgments of the post-transformation self. Articulate this tension precisely; if you believe a subset of requirements is genuinely incompatible, say so explicitly, since that incompatibility is itself a philosophical result. This is the part I want developed most carefully.

5. The heaviest exclusionary criterion. Flag which single desideratum eliminates the largest number of intuitive or popular theoretical responses—whether it is the demand for first-person authority, the demand for intertemporal rational coherence, or something else—and explain why.

A brief scorecard—no more than a short paragraph for each—indicating how three broad theoretical orientations fare against your list: a cognitive-modeling orientation (treating transformation primarily as the rational anticipation of new experiential knowledge), an authenticity-or-self-creation orientation (treating transformation as the construction or revelation of a truer self), and a deflationary or skeptical orientation (denying that transformative choices present a distinctive problem for rational decision theory).
