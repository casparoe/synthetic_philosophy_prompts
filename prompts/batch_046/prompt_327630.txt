You serve on the Joint Epistemic Standards Board of the National Strategic Forecasting Directorate. The Board must issue a binding decision memo by Friday regarding Protocol 7-C, which governs how intelligence analysts revise credences when they receive both a direct conditional assessment and independent evidence about its antecedent.

The immediate case concerns an analytical unit that received two inputs: first, a high-confidence conditional report stating that, if a specific regime collapses within the next quarter, widespread civil conflict is highly probable; second, separate intelligence that lowers the probability of that regime’s collapse. The analysts applied standard Bayesian conditioning to the antecedent and left their credence in the conditional intact, which produced a counterintuitive result: their belief in widespread conflict remained essentially unchanged despite the diminished likelihood of the triggering condition. A senior reviewer has flagged this as an instance of the Judy Benjamin problem in belief updating—where treating a conditional as a fixed constraint on joint probabilities can block the expected transmission of evidential change to the consequent, even when the antecedent’s probability shifts markedly.

The Board is divided. One faction argues that analysts should adopt a “joint-probability-first” protocol: decompose every conditional report into its underlying joint distribution and revise all components together, so that lowering the antecedent automatically pulls down the consequent in line with the conditional probability. The other faction supports a “conditional-lock” protocol: treat authoritative conditional assessments as invariant evidential constraints that survive revision of the antecedent, on the grounds that the conditional captures a structural or causal relation that should not be weakened merely because the triggering scenario becomes less likely.

Complicating the matter, an internal methodological working paper—circulated only within the Directorate—has proposed a hybrid “symmetry-breaking” rule: analysts should distinguish between indicative conditionals (which are subject to Judy Benjamin-style stability and should be locked) and counterfactual screening reports (which represent modal comparisons rather than evidential constraints and should be revised downward if the antecedent becomes improbable). The working paper offers no practical decision procedure for classifying a given report into one category or the other.

Your task is to draft the Board’s formal decision memo. It should recommend a concrete course of action—adopt one protocol, reject both in favour of a modified alternative, or approve a phased trial—with a clear time frame for implementation and review. The memo must engage seriously with the strongest argument for the losing side, specifically addressing whether preserving structural relations justifies insulating conditionals from antecedent revision. It should also address the hybrid proposal: either incorporate it with practical criteria for classification, or explain why the Board should set it aside despite its theoretical appeal. The reasoning should be accessible to analysts without requiring citation of specific philosophical texts.

Please write the memo in British English, with a formal institutional tone, and ensure it contains an explicit recommendation, the reasoning for it, an honest treatment of the strongest opposing consideration, and a practical implementation timeline.
