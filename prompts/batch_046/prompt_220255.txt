As a doctoral student in applied ethics working on distributive justice, I need a reusable decision procedure for a specific institutional role: I serve as the ethics liaison for a regional clinical-research oversight board that reviews proposed multi-site studies every quarter. The board frequently encounters protocols that pool risks and benefits across distinct patient cohorts—for example, requiring intensive biomonitoring from one subgroup so that aggregate predictive models improve outcomes for a different subgroup, or accepting higher adverse-event rates in a small enrollment pool to accelerate approval of a therapy that will benefit a larger population. The board currently relies on standard risk-benefit ratios, which collapse individual burdens and collective gains into a single metric. I want to replace that with a procedure grounded specifically in the separateness-of-persons objection to utilitarian aggregation—the view, associated with Rawls, that interpersonal trade-offs of burdens and benefits violate the moral boundary between persons, and that what justifies intrapersonal sacrifice within one life does not justify imposing a burden on person A for person B's gain.

Write a step-by-step procedure the board can run in a 90-minute review session without requiring philosophical training. It should include: (1) a preliminary filter that distinguishes intrapersonal trade-offs within one consent trajectory or life plan from interpersonal ones; (2) ordered diagnostic questions that test whether a proposed protocol treats individuals as separate sources of moral claims or as fungible units in an aggregate welfare function; (3) explicit stopping rules—where the procedure mandates rejection, where it mandates approval, and, crucially, where it must suspend judgment and hand the decision to the board's values committee because the conflict is irreducibly normative rather than procedural; and (4) a small set of concrete test sentences or criteria the board can apply to protocol documents to detect hidden aggregation.

Then apply the procedure to three cases:
(a) A forty-patient study in which ten participants must undergo repeated lumbar punctures to refine a biomarker that will guide therapy for future patients in the same cohort; all consent, but none of the ten receive direct therapeutic benefit.
(b) A public-health vaccination campaign that deliberately prioritizes immunizing low-risk healthcare workers before high-risk elderly patients, because system-level modeling shows this sequence minimizes total mortality—requiring some elderly individuals to wait and face infection risk for the aggregate reduction.
(c) A widely supported protocol that shifts all invasive monitoring burden onto a small, already-vulnerable subgroup (homeless participants in a shelter-based study) in exchange for a modest aggregate improvement in care quality across the broader enrolled population. The board will likely want to approve this; show exactly where and why the procedure blocks it, and identify the specific value judgment the board must make if it wishes to override the block.

Finally, state clearly what the procedure cannot do: identify the philosophical distinctions it deliberately suppresses to remain operational, the types of protocols for which it is not designed, and the points at which a board member's personal judgment about distributive fairness will necessarily re-enter.

Please do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations. Do not ask me to verify sources. Write from the philosophical literature on the separateness objection and its institutional applications as it is generally known.
