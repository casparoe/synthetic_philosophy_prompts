I am a doctoral student in political philosophy trying to systematize my intuitive judgments about economic entitlement in cases that extend, and sometimes strain, the standard Wilt Chamberlain scenario. I want to see whether a coherent principle unifies my verdicts, or whether parsimony forces me to abandon at least one.

Here are the cases and my judgments:

Case 1 — The canonical scenario. From a just initial distribution, individuals voluntarily pay a portion of their holdings to watch an exceptionally talented performer. The performer ends up with far more than anyone else, and the payers are left with less. Verdict: the result is just. The voluntary nature of the transfers defeats any pattern-based objection.

Case 2 — The epistemically opaque baseline. The same voluntary payments occur, but the initial distribution was shaped by a now-formally-rectified historical injustice that persists in lived economic reality; neither the performer nor the audience knows this, and all parties sincerely believe the baseline fair. Verdict: I oscillate. One part of me says the transfers remain valid because they are genuinely voluntary and no one acted in bad faith; another part says they compound an unrecognized structural wrong that the participants could not have consented to, had they known.

Case 3 — The digital analog. Users make voluntary micropayments to access an AI-generated simulation of a performer whose “skill” involves no human labor, scarcity, or personal risk. The payments accumulate into a massive fortune for the model’s owner. Verdict: this is not fully entitled, despite being voluntary. Something about the absence of corresponding labor or desert undermines the claim, though I am unsure whether that undermines only the magnitude or the legitimacy of the holding itself.

Case 4 — Unconsented inheritance. The performer from Case 1 dies, leaving the accumulated wealth to descendants who have contributed nothing and who never participated in any exchange that generated the holdings. The original earners are dead, and the heirs live comfortably for generations. Verdict: inheritance is acceptable within limits, but massive, indefinite intergenerational concentration without further voluntary exchange eventually becomes unjust. The boundary is vague to me.

Case 5 — Uncompensated externalities. The payments generate substantial negative externalities—say, environmental degradation borne by non-consenting third parties who received none of the benefits. Every direct transaction was voluntary and informed. Verdict: the performer (or estate) owes rectification to those harmed. The entitlement is qualified, not absolute; voluntary exchange does not license harm to outsiders.

Case 6 — Exploitative knowledge. The performer secretly knows that the initial distribution was unjust, deliberately conceals this, and maximizes payments by exploiting the audience’s false belief in fairness. Verdict: clearly unjust, and I want to distinguish this sharply from Case 2, where ignorance is mutual and innocent rather than asymmetrical and strategic.

My question is whether there is a single principle—perhaps with weighted modifiers—that accommodates all six, or whether I must drop one verdict for the sake of coherence. Specifically, I am stuck on Case 2: if I allow unrecognized background injustice to taint voluntary exchange, I seem to impose an impossible epistemic condition on every legitimate transaction; if I deny it, I allow structural wrongs to reproduce themselves invisibly through apparently clean markets. How should that tension be resolved? And does whatever principle resolves it also determine where Case 4’s inheritance boundary lies, or is inheritance governed by an independent consideration? I want a precise statement of the principle (or a small set of rival candidates), with each clause tied to the case that motivates it, its verdict on cases I have not considered, and an honest admission of which of my judgments it cannot save.
