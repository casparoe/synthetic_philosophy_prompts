I just finished a long argument with a friend about the Pasadena paradox. She contends that the standard resolution—observing that the expected value of switching envelopes is undefined because the implicit prior over the amounts is improper—is little more than a technical evasion: it tells us the calculation breaks, but not whether we should switch. I countered that an undefined expectation genuinely dissolves the paradox, since there is no rational pressure to switch if no well-defined expectation favors it. Write an essay evaluating whether the undefined-expected-value resolution is philosophically satisfactory. Specifically, compare its explanatory force to the way we typically resolve the St. Petersburg paradox, and reach a justified conclusion on whether Pasadena represents a distinct failure of expected-utility reasoning or merely the same underlying problem in a different form.
