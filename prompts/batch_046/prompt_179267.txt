My dad was just diagnossed with early-onset dementia, and now I'm weirdly stuck on whether his decision to sign the DNR was actually caused by him or just by his neurons. A friend in philosphy of mind sent me this argument she says proves mental causation is impossible, but it feels like it proves way too much and I can't find the exit.

(1) The causal exclusion principle: if a physical event P has a complete sufficient cause C at the physical level, then no non-physical event M can also cause P, unless we allow constant overdetermination.
(2) Every physical movement that looks mentaly caused — like my dad's hand signing the form — has a complete sufficient cause in prior brain states.
(3) So mental states don't cause physical events.
(4) The same logic applies mental-to-mental: deciding to sign is realized in neural transitions that are already sufficient for the next mental state.
(5) So mental states cause nothing whatsoever; they are epiphenomenal.
(6) But this doesn't just kill minds. The bridge collapsing is fully caused by atoms, the hospital canceling visiting hours is fully caused by administrator neurons, and every macro-level explanation gets pushed out.
(7) If we apply exclusion consistently, science should only cite fundamental physics, never psychology, medicine, or sociology.
(8) Nobody thinks medicine should be replaced by particle physics.
(9) So either exclusion applies selectively (which is ad hoc), or mental causation is no more mysterious than bridge-collapse causation, and steps 1-5 fail somewhere.

She says the only escape is to deny supervenience and become a dualist, which I don't want to do. Where is the weak step? Is it the jump from physical-to-physical sufficiency to mental exclusion?
