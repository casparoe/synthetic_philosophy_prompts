As a novelist who has long maintained a private cipher—invented graphemes and compressed metaphors for emotional textures and sensory states that I record in notebooks with no intended reader—I find myself drawn into debates about Wittgenstein's private language argument. In these discussions, people often invoke my own practice as an analogy: constructing a private language, they say, is like a writer inventing an encrypted lexicon of inner life, a personal system of signs for qualia that requires no external decoder. The analogy is used to argue that private language is not a philosopher's abstraction but an ordinary feature of artistic cognition, and that Wittgenstein's critique must therefore be attacking a straw man or denying an evident autonomy of internal signification.

Audit this analogy with precision. Provide the correspondence table: what plays the role of the private-language speaker, the private sign, the rule for correct use, the check against error, and the absent linguistic community? Then identify where the mapping breaks—specifically, whether my cipher necessarily borrows syntactic scaffolding, narrative coherence, or referential anchoring from public language; whether the absence of a reader eliminates criteria of correct application or merely displaces them onto my own memory and later interpretation; and whether treating sensation as manuscript-entry smuggles in an unjustified assumption about the determinacy and repeatability of inner states. Separate the disanalogies that undermine the intended defense from those that do not. Finally, say whether the conclusion—that artistic practice vindicates the possibility of private language—survives once the breaks are visible, and if it collapses, propose what a more defensible analogy for the defender of private language might actually be.
