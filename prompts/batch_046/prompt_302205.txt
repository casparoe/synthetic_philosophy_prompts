I’ve been trying to sort out my views on emotion for a seminar I’m helping to run, partly because the topic has become personally acute: over the past year I’ve been the primary carer for a parent with progressive dementia, and I’ve found myself experiencing affective states—something like anticipatory grief mixed with protective tenderness—that do not map neatly onto standard taxonomies. Here is where I’ve landed, and I’d like a consistency audit before I present it. I hold that (1) emotions are not reducible to bodily feelings, though they typically involve interoceptive predictions that the brain updates against incoming visceral signals; (2) every genuine emotion involves an evaluative appraisal, but this need not be conscious or conceptually articulated, which is why infants and non-human animals can genuinely fear or mourn; (3) many emotions are culturally scripted in a constitutive way—the particular shape of shame, pride, or even romantic love depends on social norms, and without the appropriate framing a physiological state might not count as that emotion; (4) I reject literal perceptual theories (emotions are not like seeing colour), yet I think they present values to us with an immediacy that feels direct and unmediated; (5) the phenomenology of emotion is irreducible: you cannot fully capture what anger or anticipatory grief is like in purely functional or cognitive terms, and this qualitative character carries motivational force that the appraisal alone does not supply; (6) I am inclined toward emotional pluralism—there is no single unified nature shared by all states we call emotions; anger, nostalgia, and the strange tenderness-grief I mentioned are genuinely different phenomena rather than variations on one underlying kind.

Do these hang together? I am particularly worried that (2) and (3) pull in opposite directions: if appraisal is necessary but can be non-conceptual, does cultural scripting apply only to the conceptual layer, leaving animal emotions untouched, or does it threaten to make all emotion culturally relative? I also wonder whether (5) undermines (1): if I insist that phenomenology is irreducible and motivational, am I smuggling the feeling theory back in through the side door? And does (6) sit badly with (2)—if appraisal is the common thread, haven’t I just identified a unified nature after all, contradicting pluralism? At the same time, I suspect some of these tensions are only apparent—for instance, that rejecting reduction to bodily feeling while accepting irreducible phenomenology may not be contradictory, or that pluralism might accommodate a very thin appraisal condition without collapsing into unity. Please go through the pairs, rank them by severity rather than listing them all as equally bad, and for each say what the cheapest revision would be and what it costs. If my framework rests on a confused premise—perhaps in treating anticipatory grief as a philosophical test case, or in trying to combine pluralism with a universal appraisal condition—say so plainly rather than smoothing over the crack. I want an audit, not a lecture, ordered by how badly things break.
