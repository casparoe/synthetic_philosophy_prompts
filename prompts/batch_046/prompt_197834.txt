I'm an ethics reviewer for a mental-health tribunal and we get about thirty compulsory-treatment orders a week, usually with a 48-hour turnaround and a lawyer plus a psychiatrist on the panel. Its not enough to just rubber-stamp diagnoses like 'schizophrenia' or 'bipolar disorder', so I want a practical decision procedure grounded in Szasz's claim that mental illness is a myth — that psychiatric labels are moral or political judgements about behaviour we find unacceptable, not medical discoveries like a broken bone. The procedure should be something the panel can actually run: ordered questions, stopping rules for when coercion is out, points where a value judgement has to take over, and a clear flag for when the framework misfires. Run it on three cases: (1) a man refusing meds for what clinicians call paranoid schizophrenia but he describes as religious revelation; (2) a teenager with documented frontal-lobe damage whose aggression tracks the lesion but who refuses surgery; (3) someone brought in preemptively after an algorithmic risk system — smart-home sleep and purchase patterns — flagged them, with no request for help and no acute crisis. Case two should be the one where the procedure gives an answer I probably won't like — I lean toward allowing coercion there — so show me if Szasz's distinction between biological dysfunction and problems in living forces a different result. Don't just list options; commit to a definite stance in each case and say exactly what the procedure cannot do. End with one line: Verdict: [your one-sentence summary].
