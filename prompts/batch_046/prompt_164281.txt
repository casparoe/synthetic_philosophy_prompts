You serve as the senior evaluation officer for an independent philanthropic fund that receives approximately twenty-five grant proposals per quarter across direct service delivery, policy advocacy, and research infrastructure. Each proposal is allotted sixty minutes of your analysis, with one fifteen-minute consultation available from a subject-matter specialist; your recommendation, together with its documented reasoning, is submitted to a board that requires reproducible evaluation criteria and explicit notation of every point at which a value judgment substitutes for measurement. You require a reusable decision procedure—ordered as a flowchart in words, with explicit questions, conditions for a sufficient answer, termination points that yield a provisional rejection, pause points that hand the decision to a human arbiter of values rather than facts, and honest flags indicating where the procedure will misfire or where reasonable evaluators can diverge despite following identical rules.

The procedure must be grounded in cause prioritization through importance, tractability, and neglectedness, but it must also apply a binding modification: the reversibility-risk gate. This gate does not ask merely whether an outcome is good if achieved, but asks how much of that good persists if funding is withdrawn after three years, and whether the deployed resources become irreversibly sunk in ways that crowd out more adaptable interventions. Within tractability, you must distinguish epistemic tractability—the confidence with which we can learn whether the intervention works—from execution tractability—the reliability with which we can deliver it at scale—because this distinction must change the evaluation in at least one branch of the procedure. The framework should be executable without external literature access and should not depend on precise quantitative inputs when none are available; where numbers are absent, the procedure must specify what qualitative threshold counts as adequate and must label that step as an irreducible judgment call.

Write the procedure in full. Then apply it to three proposals:

1. A vaccination campaign against a painful but non-fatal livestock disease in a low-income region, requiring annual booster administration with no local manufacturing capacity.
2. A permanent endowment funding a dedicated advocacy office to pursue long-term regulatory reform on air-quality standards, with no directly measurable health outcomes within five years.
3. A capital build for a specialized clinic treating a condition for which a curative vaccine is in Phase III trials with probable regulatory approval within four years; the clinic requires a ten-year facility lease and multi-year specialist staff contracts.

In your applications, include one case in which the procedure produces a recommendation that conflicts with the intuitive humanitarian appeal of the proposal—identify the exact gate that generates the conflict and explain why. Additionally, specify two distinct points in the procedure at which two reasonable evaluators, both adhering to the rules, could legitimately diverge, describing the nature of each disagreement (e.g., empirical versus normative). Finally, state clearly what the procedure cannot do—what class of proposals or values it is structurally incapable of resolving.
