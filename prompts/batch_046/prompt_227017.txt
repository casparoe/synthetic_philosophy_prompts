Read Fritz Lang's Metropolis through the philosophy of labor and leisure rights—specifically the Protestant work ethic's moralization of toil, the institutional claim of the 35-hour week to bounded labor, and karoshi as the pathological endpoint where work consumes life itself. Focus on the film's visual economy: the synchronized underground shifts, the Eternal Gardens as leisure reserved for the elite, the flood that collapses labor and catastrophe, and the mediator figure intended to bridge the planners and the laborers. Where does the film treat labor as redemptive vocation, and where does it reveal leisure as a class privilege externalized onto disposable worker-bodies? Examine whether the resolution—reconciling capital and labor through sentiment—distinguishes between granting workers time away from labor and granting them authority over its conditions, or whether it collapses that distinction. If the collapse is present, determine whether it is a philosophical confusion or an ideological move. Conclude by saying what the film adds to the debate about work and leisure that normative argument alone cannot provide: does its imagery of mechanized bodies and inundated machines reveal something about the phenomenology of exploited labor that conceptual analysis misses, or does it ultimately aestheticize the very exploitation it depicts?
