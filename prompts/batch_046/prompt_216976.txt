Below is a draft of a short essay I intend to include in a larger project on metaontology. The central claim is that the standard deflationary diagnosis of ontological disputes as "merely verbal" works for descriptive domains but systematically fails for normative ontology, because the pragmatic criteria that license translation into a shared base language are themselves normatively loaded.

(1) In descriptive ontology—say, the debate over whether composite objects exist—the deflationist can often show that disputants agree on all the underlying spatiotemporal and causal facts, and differ only in whether they apply the term "object" to certain arrangements. Once both sides are translated into a neutral vocabulary (regions, relations, properties), the disagreement dissolves into a question about which linguistic framework is more useful for prediction and explanation. The metaphysical neutrality of the base language makes this maneuver plausible.

(2) In normative ontology—whether moral rights, obligations, or values "really exist"—the same maneuver breaks down. The pragmatic virtues that are supposed to adjudicate between competing frameworks (simplicity, explanatory power, coherence with practical reasoning) are not metaphysically neutral. To claim that a realist moral vocabulary is "less useful" than an error-theoretic or expressivist replacement, the deflationist must already take a stand on what counts as successful moral explanation, which obligations we have reason to recognize, and whether practical reasoning aims at tracking independent normative facts. These are precisely the questions the ontological dispute was about.

(3) Therefore, applying the verbal-dispute diagnosis to normative ontology requires smuggling in substantive normative premises. The deflationist cannot remain agnostic about moral reality while using pragmatic criteria to dissolve the debate, because selecting those criteria presupposes an answer to the debate itself. This does not mean normative disputes are always substantive; it means that if they are verbal, that verdict cannot be delivered from a metaphysically neutral standpoint.

(4) One might object that this only shows normative disputes are harder to deflate, not that they resist deflation in principle. I think the stronger point is that the entanglement runs both ways: in the normative case, semantic choices are partly constitutive of the subject matter, so there is no "underlying fact" layer independent of the frameworks being compared. If that is right, then metaontological deflationism in ethics is not a relocation of the problem into language, but a concealed first-order normative argument.

Please comment on the following: first, whether the distinction I draw between descriptive and normative ontology for deflationary purposes is coherent, or whether it collapses once we recognize that all pragmatic criteria involve value-laden choices about explanatory goals; second, whether I am conflating metaphysical neutrality with normative neutrality in a way that unfairly privileges the realist; and third, whether there is a specific counterexample—perhaps in the ontology of institutional facts or legal norms—where the verbal-dispute diagnosis genuinely succeeds without normative presupposition. I am especially interested in suggestions for sharpening the thesis so that it does not overreach into claiming that all normative ontology is substantive, but rather specifies the conditions under which deflation becomes question-begging. I do not need references or a bibliography; I want conceptual pressure on the argument's structure.
