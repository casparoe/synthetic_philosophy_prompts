Consider the classic thought experiment—often discussed in philosophy of mind contexts—in which Einstein’s brain is copied, pattern by pattern, into an enormous printed book, so that every neuronal state is represented by a symbol on a page arranged in a static spatial layout. Proponents of functionalism or substrate-independence frequently invoke a software/hardware analogy to argue that the book, if properly interpreted or read in sequence, could literally be conscious: the brain is the hardware, consciousness is the software or running program, copying the program into a different medium is like installing software on a new machine, and a conscious book is therefore just Einstein running on unconventional substrate.

Treat that software/hardware analogy as the argument’s backbone and audit it explicitly. First, provide the correspondence table: what in the source domain (computers and software) plays the role of the brain, the mental states, the causal interactions between neurons, the temporal dynamics of cognition, the environment, and the interpreter or reader? Then identify where the mapping fractures—especially regarding diachronic integration (mental states unfold in time, whereas the book is spatially extended but temporally static unless sequentially activated), causal coupling with an environment, embodiment, and the distinction between representing information and enacting a process. Address whether the analogy quietly smuggles in an intentional interpreter (someone who reads and activates the pages) that has no counterpart in the original brain case, and whether this makes the book scenario structurally closer to Searle’s Chinese Room than to a genuine substrate-independent transfer of mind.

Finally, assess whether the conclusion—that the book could literally hold a conversation with us and be conscious in the same sense Einstein was—survives once these disanalogies are fully on the table. Separate the disanalogies that genuinely undermine the functionalist conclusion from those that are merely cosmetic. If the analogy collapses, propose a better replacement (for instance, comparing the brain-book relation to a musical score versus a live performance, or to DNA versus a living organism) and explain what that replacement reveals about whether consciousness requires not just the right structural pattern but also a specific kind of temporal, causal, and embodied enactment. Be precise about what would have to be true of the “reader” or activation process for the book to avoid being merely a static representation rather than a thinking thing.
