You are an expert metaphysician and philosopher of language with deep familiarity with the problem of propositional unity, Bradley's regress, and contemporary debates on structured propositions. I need you to think carefully and systematically, so please begin by briefly brainstorming the different ways philosophers have tried to stop Bradley's regress — for instance, by treating relations as internal, by appealing to unsaturatedness, by invoking mental acts, or by denying that propositions are structured entities at all — before you construct the deliverable.

Please design a reusable diagnostic procedure that a philosophy tutor or peer reviewer can apply in about fifteen minutes when assessing an argument about how propositions are unified. The procedure must be grounded in Bradley's regress and the challenge of explaining the unity of the proposition without either leaving constituents unrelated or launching an infinite hierarchy of relating relations.

The practitioner should work through the following in order:
1. Identify the constituent entities (objects, properties, relations, concepts, mental acts) and the proposed unifier.
2. Test whether the unifier is itself one of the constituents that needs relating, or whether it is of a different ontological kind.
3. Ask whether the account treats the unifying factor as internal to the constituents or as an external addition; flag where this distinction is doing hidden work.
4. Determine whether the account avoids the regress by dissolving it (denying there is a real unity problem) or by solving it (providing a non-iterative ground), and assess whether the dissolution is principled or ad hoc.
5. Check for hidden iterations: does the explanation of the unifier itself require a further unifier?
6. Mark the point where the procedure must hand over to metaphysical judgement rather than delivering a mechanical verdict — for example, deciding whether the cost of positing a primitive structural glue is acceptable, or whether a deflationary fiction is sufficient for the theoretical purpose.

The procedure should include explicit stop conditions: a clear outcome of avoids regress, falls into regress, dissolves legitimately, or indeterminate — requires judgement. It should also flag common misfires: for instance, conflating psychological unity (how a thinker grasps a thought) with ontological unity (what makes the proposition one thing), or mistaking the absence of a named relation for the absence of a relating relation.

Please apply the procedure to three worked cases:
(a) A standard Russellian multiple-relation account of the judgement that Brutus killed Caesar, where the mind relates Brutus and Caesar via a mental act of judging.
(b) A hypothetical inference-role account — which you should treat as a novel hybrid for this exercise — according to which concepts are nodes in a normative network, and the unity of a proposition consists solely in the fact that applying one concept licenses inferences involving others, with no further ontological glue.
(c) A deflationary account that denies propositions exist as structured entities and instead treats talk of propositional unity as a useful fiction for tracking patterns of commitment and disagreement.

For each case, run the steps, state the outcome the procedure delivers, and say where the honest critic must admit that the procedure reaches its limit and a substantive philosophical choice remains.

Finally, conclude by summarising what the procedure can and cannot do: which kinds of argument it reliably diagnoses, where it is vulnerable to being gamed by clever redescription, and what single revision to the procedure would most improve its robustness without turning it into an unworkable theoretical treatise.
