My sister, a clinical psychologist with firmly held taxonomic views about self-deception, has delivered consistent verdicts across a range of cases. She classifies the husband who dismisses unmistakable evidence of spousal infidelity as genuinely self-deceiving; she denies that status to the student who procrastinates and insists she thrives under pressure, treating that as ordinary rationalization rather than deception of the self; she counts sustained implicit bias—systematic behavioral prejudice coupled with sincere egalitarian avowals—as self-deception, but excludes isolated slips; she rejects motivated forgetting of an unwanted medical diagnosis as self-deception, assigning it to a distinct mnemonic category; and she distinguishes the politician who privately acknowledges falsehoods yet repeats them publicly as a liar, not as someone deceiving himself. These judgments have become urgently personal to me as I examine my own persistent refusal to accept that a long-planned career trajectory is unviable, despite repeated objective failures and direct counsel from mentors.

The new case for discussion involves a patient who maintains, in detailed private journals, a fully articulated belief that her chronic physical symptoms are psychogenic in origin, yet in every practical domain—medical consultations, workplace accommodation requests, and descriptions to friends—she operates with complete sincerity within an organic-disease framework. Crucially, she reports neither anxiety nor cognitive dissonance when the two frameworks are juxtaposed; she describes them as belonging to separate domains of life, and she does not suppress awareness of either. Predict my sister's verdict on whether this constitutes self-deception. Identify at least two competing interpretive rules—such as one requiring a divided or partitioned self, one requiring motivated belief-formation with partial awareness, and one requiring a failure of phenomenological integration—that are jointly consistent with her earlier rulings, state what each predicts about this patient, offer your best-guess prediction with reasons, and specify the further hypothetical case that would best distinguish between the rules.
