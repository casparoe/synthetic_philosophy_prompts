I'm auditing an informal philosophy of science seminar where two graduate students got into a sustained disagreement about whether Darwin's mechanism of natural selection and Turing's model of mechanical computation really share what one of them called a "strange inversion of reasoning" — the idea that genuine competence (adaptive design, correct calculation) can emerge from processes that possess no comprehension of what they are achieving. They asked me to referee because neither would concede ground, and I want to make sure I understand where each argument actually stands before next week's session.

The first student, Mara, argued that the parallel is deep and structural. In Darwin's case, she said, we have complex functional organization — the eye, the immune system — arising through a process that has no foresight, no representation of the organism's needs, and no grasp of biology. In Turing's case, she continued, we have a device that manipulates symbols according to purely syntactic rules and yet produces arithmetic truths, proofs, or linguistic outputs that a human observer recognizes as meaningful or correct. In both instances, she insisted, competence is decoupled from comprehension: the system achieves results that we would normally associate with intelligent understanding, but the mechanism itself understands nothing. She added that this is precisely why both theories were initially resisted — they seem to eliminate the need for a mind to explain mind-like or design-like outcomes — and that recognizing the parallel helps explain why artificial intelligence and evolutionary biology both provoke the same philosophical anxiety about where understanding actually resides.

The second student, Jonah, pushed back on every layer. He granted that natural selection produces adaptation without a designer's mind, but he denied that this is an "inversion of reasoning" at all; it is simply the absence of reasoning, replaced by causal filtering. Turing's machine, he said, is different in a way that breaks the analogy: the computer does not invent mathematics; it executes instructions that were devised by human mathematicians who fully understood the proof or the algorithm in advance. The comprehension, Jonah argued, is not missing from the system — it is outsourced to the human designers and encoded in the program. Therefore, he concluded, there is no strange inversion in computation, only a division of labor between a comprehending agent and a non-comprehending tool. He went further: if we say the computer "achieves competence without comprehension," we risk mystifying machinery by attributing achievement-language to a process that is merely carrying out someone else's reasoning. Mara replied that Jonah was smuggling in the programmer's mind to save the appearance of comprehension, and that if we applied the same move to evolution we could always invoke an implicit designer; but Jonah countered that evolution genuinely has no programmer, whereas computation genuinely does, so the analogy collapses precisely at the point where it matters. They ended without resolution.

Can you go through their exchange step by step and adjudicate who is right at each move? Specifically: (1) Is Mara correct that both cases involve competence decoupled from comprehension in the same structural way? (2) Is Jonah right that computation merely outsources reasoning rather than inverting it? (3) Does Mara's comparison between evolutionary and computational resistance hold up? (4) Is Jonah's "division of labor" objection decisive, or does it miss something about emergent behavior in complex programs? And finally, who is more right overall — or are they talking past each other because they mean different things by "reasoning"?
