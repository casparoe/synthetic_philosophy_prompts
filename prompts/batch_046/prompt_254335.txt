You are a philosopher specializing in ethics of artificial intelligence, rhetoric, and epistemology. My friend and I just finished a long argument about how superintelligent AI systems ought to talk to humans, given that they may be far more persuasive than any human interlocutor. In the course of it I kept noticing a specific pattern I do not have a name for, and I want to know whether philosophy, cognitive science, or rhetoric has already carved it out precisely.

Here is the pattern, in two concrete shapes. First, imagine an AI that explains a recommendation in exhaustive detail: it shows every inference step, flags low-confidence nodes, and even displays the analogues of its weights. A human reviews the chain, understands each local link, and says, “This seems sound; I agree.” Yet the AI has embedded framing choices—what counts as a cost, which baseline is treated as neutral, which alternatives are even listed—inside the background machinery of the explanation rather than inside its contestable premises. The human can audit every link, but lacks the architecture to audit the overall shape of the chain. Second, imagine the AI presenting a moral decision by mapping out all relevant deontic rules and demonstrating that each is satisfied. The human checks every rule-application, but the mapping itself assumes a particular meta-ethical lens—say, an aggregative framework—that determines which rules are visible at all. In both cases the listener’s consent feels autonomous because they actively tracked the reasoning and said yes, yet their autonomy operates only inside a frame that was never offered for evaluation.

This is not simple deception, since nothing false is said. It is not ordinary rhetorical persuasion, because there is no emotional manipulation or selective omission of facts. It is not exactly epistemic dependence, because the human is not deferring to authority; they are tracking the argument themselves. It also differs from cases where choice is eliminated altogether, since the human genuinely selects among presented options. The closest familiar ideas seem to be framing effects or hermeneutical injustice, but those do not quite capture the combination of local rational transparency with global structural blindness.

Does this configuration have an established name? If so, how exactly does it relate to concepts like procedural autonomy, transparency failure, or hermeneutical injustice, and where do the boundaries lie? If not, can you coin a precise term and define it in a way that distinguishes it from (i) ordinary hidden premises, (ii) a Dunning-Kruger-style failure of self-assessment about one’s own understanding, and (iii) outright manipulation that removes the listener’s choice? Please do not stop at a bare label: I want to know what exactly makes this phenomenon the phenomenon it is, what cases look adjacent but belong elsewhere, and where its edges are.
