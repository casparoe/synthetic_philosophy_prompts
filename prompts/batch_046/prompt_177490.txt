I'm trying to identify whether there is an established concept—perhaps in philosophy of causation or in legal theory on omission liability—for the following pattern. Take a case where someone stops performing an action they had long performed, not because they actively do something harmful, but because they withdraw an ongoing intervention that had been keeping a situation in an artificial or non-default state. For example, imagine a remote research station that has run desalination pumps for decades so that a small settlement can exist in an arid region. A new administrator halts the pumps to cut costs. The settlement does not immediately collapse; but over the following months, the natural salinity of the groundwater gradually returns, and eventually the water supply becomes unusable. Now compare this with a more familiar omission case: a lifeguard who is supposed to watch a specific swimmer and looks away, allowing that swimmer to drown. In the lifeguard case, almost everyone treats the failure to act as a direct cause of the specific harm. In the desalination case, opinions split: some treat the administrator's decision as the cause of the settlement's eventual failure, because the settlement's survival was causally dependent on her continued action; others treat the natural salinity as the cause and her withdrawal as merely the removal of a counterfactual shield, not a genuine causal contribution to the harm. The difference seems to turn on whether the omitted action was sustaining a state that ran counter to background tendencies, rather than blocking a discrete threat. I have noticed this split in arguments about environmental stewardship, medical life-support withdrawal, and even historical debates about colonial infrastructure abandonment, but I do not know whether philosophers have isolated it as its own category within causation by omission. Does this configuration have a specific name or recognized boundary within the literature? If there is no distinct concept, how should we understand the disagreement—does it reflect a real distinction in causal structure, or is it just a psychological difference in how we assign blame when an artificial system collapses? Please do not just offer a label; I want to understand exactly what the phenomenon includes, what standard omission cases it excludes, and where the line should be drawn.
