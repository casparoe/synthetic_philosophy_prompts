As a novelist structuring a multi-generational saga spanning 1350 to 1650, I need to decide whether my narrative architecture should honor sharp historical breaks or deny them. Compare the historiographical rupture model—which treats the labels "Renaissance," "Scientific Revolution," and the end of the "Middle Ages" as discrete, identifiable transitions with new subjectivities and institutional breaks—with the revisionist continuity thesis that regards these categories as largely retrospective impositions upon a far messier, overlapping reality. Specifically, examine how each framework accounts for the simultaneous practice of scholastic disputation and empirical instrument-making within the same 16th-century courtly and monastic spaces, and whether adopting the continuity model requires abandoning epochal chapter divisions in favor of thematic or genealogical narrative structures. Which framework better accommodates characters who inhabit what we now call "medieval" and "modern" mentalities without anachronism, and what specific feature of the rupture model—if removed—would cause its explanatory advantages over continuity to collapse?
