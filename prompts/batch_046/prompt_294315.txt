ok so me and my roommate were arguing about deplatforming and cancel culture, specifically whether its actually punishment or just like social market consequences or whatever. but then we noticed this weird pattern on big platforms - they have these formal strike systems where violations accumulate and never really expire, but the actual ban almost never comes from hitting a strike limit. instead it comes from a viral thread where people dig up old tweets or clips, and then the platform treats it like immediate danger even though the content is years old. so you've got this bureaucratic, cumulative record-keeping on one side, and on the other this spontaneous collective ritual where old speech gets treated as present-tense and the person needs expelled right now.

what philosophical commitments about time, moral responsibility, and community authority does that hybrid presuppose? like, does it require a view where wrongness doesn't decay, or where digital archives make past actions permanently present? and is it smuggling in a theory of punishment closer to exile, to bankruptcy without discharge, or to a distributed jury with no rules of evedence? reconstruct the implicit theory, tell me whether the bureaucratic part and the viral part are actually philosophically consistent or in tension, and say what platform design and social norms would have to change if we replaced the underlying view with one that treated harmful speech more like a civil tort - something that can be remedied, compensated, and then actually finished. also, can any coherent view of moral agency hold both that old posts should stay visible for accountability and that people shouldn't face permanent exclusion for them?
