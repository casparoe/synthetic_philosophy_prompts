I've been trying to understand how Bayesian updating works by reading some popular philosophy of science, and I got caught in an argument between two friends — Mara and Leo — about a case that seems to sit right on the boundary of what's called the "old evidence" problem. I want an outside adjudication of their actual exchange, not just a general essay.

The background is this. Both Mara and Leo have known for years about a robust result in sleep and memory research: after learning a list of words, people who sleep eight hours recall about twenty percent more the next day than people who stay awake. Recently they both encountered a new neurobiological theory — call it Theory S — that explains memory consolidation through a specific mechanism of synaptic downscaling during slow-wave sleep. The theory was apparently built without direct reference to the word-list studies, but its mathematical structure strictly implies the twenty-percent improvement should occur. Leo said that learning this should make him more confident in Theory S. Mara said it should not change his confidence at all, since the study results were already part of his background knowledge.

Here is how their exchange actually went.

(1) Leo said: "If I compare Theory S to a rival theory that doesn't predict the sleep-memory effect, and I already know the effect is real, then discovering that S gets it right should shift my belief toward S. The result is still evidence; I just happened to possess it before I knew about the theories."

(2) Mara replied: "Bayesian updating conditions on new information about the world. The world hasn't changed — the studies weren't rerun. What's new is only my knowledge of Theory S's internal structure. If I already treat the effect as certain, then conditioning on it again is double-counting. The factor is the likelihood of the effect given S divided by its prior probability, which is roughly one divided by one — essentially no movement."

(3) Leo came back: "You're treating the theory like a black box. What's new isn't the world; it's my recognition that S is structurally tied to a fact I take seriously. That's information about S, not about sleep. If I learn that a detective's model necessarily includes the victim being left-handed, and I already knew the victim was left-handed, I've learned something about the quality of the detective's model, even though I already knew about the handedness."

(4) Mara said: "That analogy hides the problem. My prior over theories should already have accounted for how likely each one is to generate that feature, given everything I know. If I failed to do that, it's a flaw in my prior, not a license to update now. You're proposing a second-order update on my own ignorance — which might be psychologically real, but it isn't the same as confirming S with old data."

(5) Leo added: "There's a further distinction you're ignoring. Theory S doesn't merely allow the twenty-percent figure; it derives it from a parameter I can measure independently — the rate of synaptic pruning in rodent models. So the old result isn't just sitting there; it's being anchored to a new measurable pathway. That anchoring is the real belief-revision, and your flat conditioning formula misses it because it evaluates the likelihood in isolation from these structural connections."

(6) Mara answered: "Now you've changed the subject. You're talking about new rodent measurements, which are genuinely fresh evidence, and I'm not disputing those. I'm disputing the part where you claim that the match with the old word-list studies itself, once noticed, should boost confidence in S. If that match matters separately, then every theory that happens to line up with well-known facts gets an automatic credibility bonus, which rewards post-hoc storytelling. You need a principled line between your 'anchoring' and simply noticing a convenient fit after the fact — and I don't see one."

Can you adjudicate this move by move: rate each of the six moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with brief justification for each rating? Then say who is more right overall, and specifically identify what both Mara and Leo are missing. In particular, I want to know whether Leo's "anchoring to a measurable pathway" point can be separated from Mara's charge that he is smuggling new evidence in under the guise of old-evidence confirmation. Please explain everything in the terms Mara and Leo are actually using, without just naming standard philosophers or dropping references I wouldn't recognize. I am not looking for a reading list or citations; I want a point-by-point verdict.
