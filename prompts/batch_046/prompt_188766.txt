I am writing this immediately after a long, somewhat circular argument with a friend about how to read artifacts that deliberately blur the line between their container and their content—works in which a framing convention (genre signals, presentation format, material medium, or explicit meta-commentary) is not merely decorative but structurally essential. I kept appealing to the distinction among frame messages, outer messages, and inner messages—the framework that separates the conditions of delivery, the explicit propositional content, and the deeper self-referential or intentional meaning that the framing makes possible. My friend countered that this tripartite division is unstable: once the frame is doing substantive work, it is part of the outer message, and any supposed inner layer is either redundant or a projection of the reader's own expectations.

This disagreement led me to notice a specific interpretive failure mode that I have seen repeatedly but cannot name. It is not simple literalism, nor is it the intentional fallacy, nor is it the familiar error of mistaking the medium for the message. Rather, it is a precise cascade. The interpreter correctly registers the framing mechanism—the archaic diction in a legal text that contains a clause disclaiming its own enforceability, or the visible synthetic-origin watermark on an AI-generated image that depicts its own generation process. But instead of using that frame as a ladder to a self-referential inner message—say, about the symbolic versus functional authority of law, or about authenticity in generative media—the interpreter absorbs the frame entirely into the explicit propositional content. The result is that the outer message is inflated (the text is about archaism, the image is about falsehood) while the inner message is never reached, not because it was missed but because the frame was never allowed to function as a frame.

Is there an established term for this exact configuration: correct frame recognition followed by frame-outer conflation followed by inner-message occlusion? How does it differ from related failures—such as treating a satirical frame as part of the satire's target rather than its vehicle, or from legitimate interpretations that genuinely conclude no inner message exists? If the literature offers no precise name, I would like a rigorous definition that specifies the boundary conditions—particularly how to tell whether an interpreter has collapsed the levels or has correctly judged them to be collapsed by the work itself—together with a brief taxonomy of the neighboring errors from which it must be distinguished.
