I'm messing around with typeface design and there's this thing I keep noticing but don't have a word for. You can render an a in like a hundred ways — double-story, single-story, blackletter, graffiti, some futuristic thing where the loop is almost a square — and people still go yep, that's an a. But then nudge one curve a little and suddenly it's that's not an a, that's just garbage or some other symbol. What's weird is there's no fixed checklist of features every a has — some miss the top hook, some don't close the bowl — so it's not template matching. And it doesn't feel like pure family resemblance either, cause there's a real cliff, not just a gradient of similarity. I know there's that old Letter Spirit idea about the essence of a form surviving style changes, but I'm not asking about that directly — I'm asking about the perceptual boundary itself, where huge stylistic variation is tolerated but then there's a hard drop-off. I'm not sure wether psychology or design theory has a specific name for this kind of category. How is it different from prototype theory or feature extraction? If there's no exact term, what would you call it — maybe categorical invariance with a stylistic cliff? Also, be honest: what evidence or argument would change your mind about where that cliff actually is, and where is your reasoning here most likely to fall apart?
