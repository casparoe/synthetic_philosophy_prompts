I'm designing a 75-minute graduate seminar session (10 philosophy PhD students) on Adam Smith's Theory of Moral Sentiments, and I need a runnable in-class activity—not a lecture—that makes participants feel the tension between sympathetic projection and the impartial spectator's normative judgment of propriety, particularly under informational asymmetry. Design a structured role-play: split the group into "agents" and rotating "spectator panels." Each agent receives the same brief clinical-ethics scenario (a resident physician withholds a terminal prognosis at family request) plus one hidden contextual detail unknown to the panel (e.g., the patient previously demanded full disclosure, or the family is acting within a specific cultural framework of non-disclosure). Spectators must judge the propriety of the agent's emotional response—its proportionality and situational fit—using only Smithian criteria, without access to the hidden detail. Include explicit rules that prevent retreat to consequentialist or deontological reasoning, a three-phase timeline (initial judgment / revelation of hidden detail / defense or revision), and small stakes such as mandatory public justification. In the debrief, map each phase onto Smith's distinctions—sympathy as imaginative exchange, direct versus indirect sympathy, the spectator as an internalized normative standard—explain the predictable failure modes (participants conflating sympathy with approval, spectators becoming punitive rather than impartial) and how to correct them in real time. Finally, be explicit about which feature of Smith's account—likely the universalizability of the spectator or the historical variability of custom—this exercise cannot reproduce and that I must supply verbally at the end.
