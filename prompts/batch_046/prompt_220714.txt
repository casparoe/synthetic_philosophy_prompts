I run a philosophy-of-mind and aesthetics reading group — eight people, ninety minutes, mostly grad students with rationalist leanings. Design a structured activity that forces participants to feel the conflict between causal-authenticity and functional-identity values in art, but avoid the tired fake-Vermeer examples. Instead, inject a niche scenario: "resurrected works" — objects reconstructed by generative models from fragmented training-data traces of physically destroyed originals, presented alongside exact physical forgeries and untouched originals. The rules should require the group to assign ownership rights, insurance values, and display privileges to the objects before a reveal shows which is which; include roles (e.g., collector, insurer, curator, ethicist) and a simple point-based payoff scheme tied to post-reveal consistency. The debrief should map each valuation decision onto the relevant philosophical distinctions (causal history, aesthetic properties, artist intentionality), include an honest admission of which feature of original-value — specifically the direct causal link to the artist's hand — the activity structurally cannot reproduce and the facilitator must supply, and list the predictable failure mode (participants conflating epistemic uncertainty about provenance with metaphysical judgments about authenticity) plus how to correct it live. No up-front lecture; they should construct the problem through the incentives and roles themselves.
