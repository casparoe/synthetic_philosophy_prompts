I write about science and ideas for a general audience, and in recent conversations with colleagues I have been trying to pin down my own moral logic about voting—not the legal right, but the conditions under which one has a duty to vote, a license to vote expressively, or an obligation to abstain. I am particularly interested in the tension between voting as an instrumental act to prevent harm and voting as an expressive affirmation of one's values, especially when the "lesser evil" is genuinely distasteful. To that end, I have sorted my intuitions about five concrete situations, and I want to know whether they cohere around a single principle or whether one of them must be sacrificed for consistency.

Here are my verdicts. First, in a high-stakes election where one candidate advocates policies that I believe would cause serious and widespread harm, while the other is flawed but safe, I feel I have a positive duty to vote for the safe candidate—even though I disagree with them on most issues—rather than abstaining or protesting. Second, in my own congressional district, which is structurally safe for a party I oppose, I vote for a long-shot independent candidate who has no realistic path to victory; this feels not merely permitted but commendable, as an honest expression of preference rather than strategic capitulation. Third, in a local referendum on a technical zoning ordinance that I have not studied and do not understand, I leave that portion of the ballot blank; voting blindly strikes me as negligent, perhaps worse than not participating at all. Fourth, as a small shareholder receiving a proxy ballot on an executive-compensation resolution I barely comprehend, I vote with the board's recommendation, treating my vote as ratification of delegated expertise rather than as a personal judgment; this seems acceptable, with no failure of civic or moral duty. Fifth, returning to the high-stakes election in the first case, if I were to write in a protest candidate—knowing that in a closer race this could weaken the safe option—I would regard that as wrong, something like free-riding on others' willingness to stop the dangerous outcome.

Is there a coherent principle—perhaps one that distinguishes instrumental weight from expressive value, and epistemic responsibility from legitimate delegation—that delivers all five verdicts? If not, which of my judgments should I overturn, and what does the simpler principle predict for a case I have not listed, such as a closely divided referendum on a moral issue where I am genuinely undecided but my vote might break the tie? Please state any principle precisely, identify which case motivates each clause, and be honest about which intuition it cannot save. I also ask that you say what would change your mind: which argument or observation about these cases would lead you to revise the principle you propose, and where your reasoning is most likely to fail.
