I'm drafting a proposal for a three-month independent research workshop on historiographic epistemology. The conventional framing pits Ranke's program of recovering the past as it actually happened against Carr's insistence on the historian's present-minded selection and Hayden White's claim that historical narratives are rhetorical constructions rather than transparent representations. I believe that framing has reached a stalemate because it treats the disagreement as purely conceptual. I want to inject a different question: do these metaphysical commitments about what history is produce measurably different predictions about the evidentiary weight of as-yet-unexamined archival materials?

The core idea is to design an adversarial-forecasting protocol in which paired historians, each self-identified with a Rankean, Carr-style, or Whitean orientation, generate explicit, time-stamped forecasts about which documents in a designated unopened archive will prove load-bearing for a specific contested event. Once the materials are digitized or declassified, the predictions are scored against an agreed resolution criterion. The philosophical work lies not in running the study but in constructing the protocol so that it genuinely distinguishes the positions: defining what counts as a Rankean versus Whitean prediction, identifying confounders such as selection bias in archive formation and narrative smoothing in report-writing, and analyzing what a null result — predictive convergence across camps — implies for whether the disagreement is empirical or merely stylistic.

This approach has not been taken because existing literature remains at the level of conceptual comparison or close-reading of finished narratives, never operationalizing the disagreement in a falsifiable form. The obvious alternative is a standard comparative essay, but that only rehearses familiar positions. The risk is that the protocol collapses into a sociology of belief rather than an epistemology of history, so the proposal must defend the philosophical payoff: if predictions diverge, we identify which archival features are theory-laden in practice-relevant ways; if they converge, we learn whether objectivity functions as a regulative ideal with no behavioral signature.
