I'm giving the toast tomorrow at my aunt Rita's retirement dinner. She spent 35 years on the floor of a car-parts plant, and over that time they swapped out almost every hand-tool for robotic arms. Half the room is family who just know she was "the one who never missed a shift"; the other half are the engineers and techs she actually worked with, who know she was the only one who'd hit the emergency stop when the sensors lied.

I've been reading about this wasp — sphex? sphexis? — that digs a burrow, checks it, and if you move the pebble it starts over from scratch every single time. People use it to say animals are just robots with no wiggle room. But Rita wasn't like that; she had routines that looked robotic but she could break them exactly when she needed to. At the same time she wasn't just "flexible" — she was rigid about the right things, like never letting a bad part through just because the line was behind schedule.

I want the toast to honor that without sounding like I'm giving a philosophy lecture. The idea should shape what I can and can't say: I can't claim she was "free" in some big abstract way, and I can't pretend she was just a cog who learned to adapt. I need to say something true about how her kind of rigidity — the kind that knows exactly when to break — is different from machine rigidity, and different from just being easygoing or flexable.

There's also this thing I've been calling "loop blindness" — when you automate a step you don't even see the decision that used to live inside it. I want to work that in, not as jargon, but as the reason her job got harder, not easier, as the robots came in.

Please write a five-minute toast that lands for both halves of the room. After it, give me a brief structured summary in JSON with these fields: "core_tension" (what the speech balances), "sphex_reference" (how the wasp is used), "loop_blindness" (how it's explained), "audience_split" (how both halves are addressed), and "what_i_avoid_saying" (the false comforts the speech rejects).
