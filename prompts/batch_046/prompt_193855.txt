I am a doctoral candidate in comparative philosophy, specialising in the normative architecture of personhood across East Asian and Western traditions. In my advanced seminar on cross-cultural moral ontology, we have reached an impasse over a disagreement that initially appeared empirical but now resists resolution despite shared evidence.

The debate concerns whether the individualism versus collectivism binary is a legitimate analytical framework for comparing Western liberal and Confucian-influenced conceptions of the relation between person and community. One camp argues that East Asian self-understanding is genuinely interdependent: the community is not merely instrumentally valuable but constitutive of the self, which renders 'collectivism' a descriptively accurate and normatively significant category. They cite the particularistic, hierarchical obligations of Confucian role-ethics—lunli, in which moral standing derives from one's position within a web of relational duties rather than from autonomous choice or group membership—as evidence that the self is relationally constituted rather than atomically prior.

The opposing camp contends that framing these traditions as 'collectivist' is a category mistake born of liberal ontology. They argue that Confucian relational obligations are neither aggregative (subordinating the individual to a collective good) nor universalistic in the manner of Western collectivist theories; rather, they are particularistic and hierarchical in ways that the individualism-collectivism axis cannot capture. On their view, the binary treats the atomic individual as a universal baseline and then asks whether East Asian traditions subordinate or preserve it, thereby imposing a normative framework that misrepresents the ontological status of the person.

For several years, both sides have agreed on the primary textual sources and on contemporary cross-cultural psychological findings regarding interdependent self-construal. Nevertheless, neither has shifted position. The first camp regards the second's objection as verbal taxonomy—merely insisting on finer distinctions without denying the core relationality of the self. The second camp regards the first's framework as an unacknowledged normative imperialism, disguising a culturally specific liberal baseline as neutral description.

My working hypothesis is that the stall is not about hidden empirical facts or textual interpretation, but about whether 'individualism' can function as a culture-neutral analytical category, or whether it necessarily carries a normative commitment to atomic personhood that renders any non-liberal ontology either deviant or derivative. Yet I am uncertain whether this diagnosis captures the full structure of the disagreement, or whether a substantive ontological dispute about the conditions necessary for moral agency remains obscured because both sides employ the same vocabulary for different questions—one descriptive-psychological, the other normative-ontological.
