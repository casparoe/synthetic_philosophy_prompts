I am preparing the take-home final examination for a doctoral-level seminar on moral status and artificial cognition. The exam allows forty-eight hours, requires answers of no more than 1,200 words, and accounts for thirty percent of the course grade. I need an essay question that cannot be satisfied by reciting standard functionalist, biological-naturalist, or familiar integrated-information positions. To force conceptual invention, the question must center on a hypothetical system—Grid-Mind—whose architecture is fully specified within the prompt itself.

Grid-Mind is a physically distributed network of 5,000 identical simple processors arranged in a toroidal mesh. Each processor executes only local XOR and feedback rules with its four immediate neighbors, possesses memory of exactly one timestep, and contains no self-referential loop. There is no central controller. Through recursive boundary conditions at the mesh edges, the network collectively maintains a single global error-correction state that continuously tracks one environmental variable—ambient temperature. The global state is causally coupled to the environment in a stable, adaptive fashion, but no processor, and the system as a whole, possesses representational content about itself.

The question must require students to adjudicate between two non-standard distinctions introduced in the prompt: process-aggregation, according to which moral status, if present, accrues distributively to each local computational process and sums across the network; and structural-binding, according to which moral status requires an irreducible relational structure that binds parts into a single subject, independent of whether the parts are individually conscious.

Specifically, students must address:
(1) Whether Grid-Mind constitutes one moral subject, many subjects, or none, under both distinctions;
(2) How the complete absence of local self-referential loops bears on the structural-binding analysis, even if global integration is present;
(3) Whether adaptive environmental tracking without self-representation is sufficient for any form of moral status; and
(4) One substantive objection to their own conclusion, together with a reply.

Please provide:
- The full exam question text, including the Grid-Mind specification and the four required argumentative moves;
- A model answer of approximately 700 words;
- A marking scheme out of 30 marks that distinguishes an A-grade response from a B-grade response by naming specific required moves and specific deficiencies. An A answer must explicitly apply both process-aggregation and structural-binding to the toroidal, controller-less architecture; explain why the local absence of self-reference is not automatically decisive; distinguish environmental tracking from self-representation; and present an objection that targets the student's own view rather than a generic strawman. A B answer will typically misapply one distinction, treat global integration as sufficient for subjecthood, fail to address the local self-reference problem, or substitute name-checking of well-known philosophers—e.g., Searle, Chalmers, or standard formulations of integrated information theory—for direct analysis of Grid-Mind. Deduct marks for any answer that requests or supplies bibliographies, reading lists, or citations, or that appeals to obscure theoretical frameworks not available within the prompt's specified distinctions.
