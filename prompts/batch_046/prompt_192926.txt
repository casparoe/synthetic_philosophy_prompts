In debates over whether the Exodus, the Trojan War, or the King Arthur legends should be treated as history rather than legend, people often invoke the line “absence of evidence is not evidence of absence”—for instance, to excuse the lack of archaeological traces of a large-scale departure from Egypt, or to dismiss the silence of early British records about a commander named Arthur. Can you lay out the distinct precise theses this slogan might express—distinguishing, say, an epistemic claim about our current ignorance from a claim about preservation bias for ephemeral events from a claim about burden of proof—and for each say whether it is plausible in general and whether it actually supports upgrading these particular cases from legend to history? In particular, address how the principle interacts with the partial positive evidence we do have, such as the Merneptah Stele’s reference to “Israel” in Canaan or the Hittite texts mentioning “Wilusa” in connection with Troy, and say whether it helps adjudicate which elements, if any, should be granted historical status rather than remaining purely legendary.
