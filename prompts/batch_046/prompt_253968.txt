I am preparing remarks for a seminar on medieval philosophy and contemporary ethics, and I want to test a counterfactual that standard surveys rarely address. Maimonides, in the Guide for the Perplexed, treats the sacrificial laws as a pedagogical concession: God permitted animal slaughter in worship not because blood-offering is intrinsically valuable, but because the Israelites were habituated to idolatrous rites and could not be weaned all at once to purely contemplative prayer. This is the familiar reading. But I want to press the reasoning into territory he never encountered.

Consider three modern developments he could not have anticipated: first, industrial factory farming in which billions of animals are raised and killed not for ritual purpose but for commodified protein consumption, with the suffering structurally invisible to the consumer; second, lab-cultivated meat that eliminates slaughter entirely yet replicates the sensory experience of flesh; and third, algorithmic welfare-monitoring systems in which AI vision models continuously assess stress indicators in livestock, allowing suffering to be minimized without ending the practice of use.

Given his concession theory—if sacrifice was permitted only to redirect an existing, hard-to-eradicate human tendency toward blood-ritual—would he view factory farming as a worse corruption (because it severs even the ritual context and multiplies suffering without theological purpose), or as a logical continuation of the concession (because human appetite for meat is as intractable as appetite for ritual)? Would he regard cultivated meat as the true fulfillment of the concession—preserving the sensory form while removing the moral cost of slaughter—much as prayer replaced sacrifice? And would algorithmic welfare monitoring strike him as a kind of false piety, optimizing the conditions of an institution he already considered second-best?

I am not asking for a bibliography or a survey of secondary literature. I want you to construct the most coherent Maimonidean position across these three cases, identify which of his underlying commitments—about human psychology, about the hierarchy of worship, about whether concessions should eventually be transcended or merely managed—do the real work, and point to the modern development that would most sharply divide a Maimonides who thinks concessions are temporary scaffolding from one who thinks they are permanent fixtures of human nature.
