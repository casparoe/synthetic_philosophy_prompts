I've recently become personally invested in Jain epistemology after a debate about religious pluralism left me dissatisfied with the standard framing. The analogy everyone reaches for is the blind men and the elephant: each touches one part—the tusk, the leg, the tail—and reports a partial truth, with the implicit promise that a wise synthesizer can assemble the full beast. Popular writers extend this to claim that conflicting doctrines are merely local perspectives on one unified reality. Before I adopt this as my working model, I want to audit it precisely. What corresponds to the elephant, to the blind men, to the sense of touch, and to the synthesis? Where does the mapping break? In particular, does the analogy smuggle in a monistic ontology—one whole object behind all views—that conflicts with anekāntavāda's possible commitment to ontological multiplicity rather than mere epistemic limitation? How does it relate to the technical apparatus of syādvāda and saptabhaṅgī, where predications like "the jar exists" are always conditional (syāt) and can be affirmed, denied, or declared inexpressible in different respects? Does the analogy survive once we recognize that Jain logic allows genuinely incompatible descriptions to be simultaneously true of the same object from different standpoints (nayas), not just incomplete ones? Finally, if we substitute a modern analogy—say, a distributed ledger in which nodes record non-contradictory but non-aggregable transactions—does it capture the doctrine better, or does it import its own misleading commitments about consensus, consistency, and a single canonical record?
