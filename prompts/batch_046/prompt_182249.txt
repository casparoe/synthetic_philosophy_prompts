In bioethical debates over advance directives, the standard framing treats the competent patient as a principal who binds a future agent — the later, incapacitated self — through a written instrument, with the moral force of the arrangement drawn from the authority of contracts or promises across time. Defenders use this to claim that overriding a directive is not merely unwise but a breach of obligation to one's earlier self, carrying the same weight as breaking a promise to a distinct person. Lay out the correspondence table in detail: what plays the role of the principal, the agent, the consideration, the enforcement mechanism, and the conditions under which the contract can be voided? Then identify the fractures: does the future self possess the standing to renegotiate, is there a meaningful injury to a second party when personal identity is continuous, and does psychological continuity supply normative authority or merely descriptive connection? Next, examine what the contract analogy quietly imports about autonomy — specifically, whether it treats temporal parts as legally separate entities rather than stages of one life — and ask whether the conclusion that directives should always be honored survives once these disanalogies are made explicit. If the contractual frame collapses, propose a replacement analogy — perhaps to constitutional provisions that a polity binds itself to under conditions of anticipated ignorance, or to epistemic deference granted when one's future judgment is expected to be impaired — and determine whether that substitute strengthens, weakens, or transforms the authority of the earlier self.
