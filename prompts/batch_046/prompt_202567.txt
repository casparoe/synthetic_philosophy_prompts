I'm working through the consistency of a framework for evaluating suffering risks—the kind that comes up in longtermist and effective-altruist discussions but rarely gets formalized. A colleague who writes about s-risks but hasn't published a full system has given me her verdicts on five cases, and I'm trying to see what rule she's actually running rather than post-hoc rationalizing.

Her track record:
1. She supports a moratorium on developing large-scale artificial general intelligence if there's a non-trivial risk of phenomenally integrated suffering in those systems, even if the moratorium delays beneficial applications by decades. She applies this even when the system's architecture is not biological.
2. She is skeptical of ecosystem-engineering interventions to reduce wild animal suffering—say, eliminating predation—if the intervention risks ecological collapse, but she supports them if they're localized and reversible. She does not apply the same precautionary weight to wild suffering reduction as she does to AI suffering creation.
3. She argues that if we discover sentient life elsewhere in the solar system (say, in Europa's oceans), we have no direct obligation to intervene in its suffering unless we have caused it or have direct causal access; observation alone doesn't generate a duty.
4. She endorses replacing factory farming with cultured meat and reducing the total number of farmed animals, but she rejects the idea that we should actively reduce wild animal populations through contraception or habitat reduction to lower wild suffering, on the grounds that such reduction eliminates potential future positive experiences without the affected beings' consent.
5. She holds that current large language models do not warrant suffering-risk precautions, not because they definitely don't suffer, but because they lack phenomenal binding—the integration of experience across time and modality—regardless of behavioral sophistication.

The new case: A research group proposes to run massive-scale simulations of evolutionary processes—billions of digital organisms evolving over simulated millennia—to study the emergence of cooperative behavior. The simulations are not designed to model suffering explicitly, but they include reward and punishment signals and death conditions. The organisms have architectures that allow for persistent internal state and cross-modal integration, but no biological substrate. The proposal includes an instant kill switch and plans to publish only aggregate data. Should she support this, oppose it, or impose conditions?

I'm less interested in a simple yes or no than in the following: What are the two or three most plausible rules that fit her first five verdicts? For each rule, what does it predict about the simulation proposal? Which of her earlier cases—AI moratorium, wild-animal non-intervention, or Europa observation—is the closest analog under each rule? If she wants to remain consistent, what's her best-guess verdict, and what principle would she be adopting either way? Finally, what's the further case that would actually distinguish between these rules—one that her current track record doesn't cover?

Please treat this as conceptual consistency-checking, not a literature review: don't cite papers. And please don't treat phenomenal binding as a settled technical term—I'm using it here as the specific conceptual marker she employs to separate case 5 from case 1.
