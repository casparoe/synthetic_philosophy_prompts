As a doctoral student working at the intersection of metaphysics and information science, I have been invited to deliver the opening remarks at a workshop titled "Ontological Status and Distributed Archives." The audience is genuinely mixed: on one side, philosophers well-versed in Berkeley's idealism and the master argument—particularly the conceptual pressure of claiming that something exists while being necessarily unperceived—will attend and will catch any sloppy framing; on the other side, digital archivists and preservation engineers will be present, for whom existence is a practical problem of redundant server storage, cryptographic checksums, and the absence of any canonical physical artifact. I want the address to treat Berkeley's master argument not as historical ornament but as a live structural constraint: I need to articulate what it means for a culturally significant object—say, a digitized manuscript that survives only as dispersed, verified data fragments with no unique material instantiation—to exist without invoking an unperceived material substrate, without reducing the archive to a mere fiction or social convention, and without offering the room a bibliography or scholarly apparatus they do not need.

The remarks should be appropriate for a formal academic setting, running approximately eight to ten minutes, and must navigate the tension between philosophical precision and accessibility for the non-philosophers present. In my discussions with my advisor, we agreed that the central difficulty is avoiding both naive realism about the underlying server hardware and a collapse into conventionalism about the archive's content; the speech should therefore shape the workshop's framing rather than decorate it. Please write this address so that the philosophical idea does real work in determining what can and cannot be said about these distributed objects, keeping in mind that some listeners will recognize every move in the dialectic while others will need the conceptual stakes made plain without jargon. Do not include citation lists, reading recommendations, or references to specific secondary literature.
