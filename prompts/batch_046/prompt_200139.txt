Consider four distinct temptations that lead historians to write the past as progress toward the present: (1) teleological projection, treating contingent outcomes as inevitable endpoints; (2) conceptual assimilation, translating older categories into modern ones so that differences vanish; (3) moral gradient mapping, arranging past positions on a scale that culminates in present moral consensus; and (4) institutional isomorphism, assuming that similar-looking institutions serve identical functions across eras. Rank these four by how insidiously they corrupt historical interpretation when applied to the development of political liberty from the medieval period through the early modern era—assign each a tier from most to least damaging, with the top tier reserved for the distortion that is both pervasive and hardest to self-correct. For each tier, provide a one-sentence justification that references a concrete historical pattern (for instance, the reading of Magna Carta, the interpretation of conciliarism, or the treatment of early modern censorship debates) rather than an abstract principle alone. Finally, identify which single temptation a careful historian is most likely to commit unintentionally, and explain in two sentences why its invisibility makes it more dangerous than the others.
