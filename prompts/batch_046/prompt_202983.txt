I am preparing a briefing for a municipal archive board that must choose between two competing accounts of a six-month anomaly in its records. During that window, three administratively separate departments—tax assessment, building permits, and public health—each experienced abrupt, temporary surges in data-entry errors. The errors involved different software systems, different staff rosters, and different mistake categories, with no shared procedural link. One archivist proposes a unified explanation: an undetected, transient malware infection crossed departmental networks and subtly corrupted input fields before being automatically purged, leaving no recoverable forensic trace. This account renders the simultaneity and cross-departmental scope highly unsurprising, since one mechanism produces all three spikes. The competing explanation holds that each department independently suffered a documented staffing shortage during the same fiscal quarter, leading to rushed manual entry; this requires accepting three coincidentally timed events, but each is corroborated by HR logs, and targeted staffing adjustments have already eliminated errors in two of the three departments. A board member argues that rejecting the malware theory merely because we lack direct detection abandons the principle that we should prefer the explanation making the observed pattern least surprising. Another counters that adopting the unified theory commits us to an unobservable entity, severs explanation from practical intervention—since we cannot design a malware-specific safeguard without knowing its properties—and risks treating explanatory elegance as more important than predictive control. I am not asking which account is factually correct; I want to know how to evaluate the competing reasoning. Specifically: does the criterion of making the data least surprising genuinely favor the unified malware explanation over the coincidental-staffing account, and is there a hidden philosophical cost—particularly in how explanation relates to causal intervention or empirical risk—if we privilege unity in this way? Please identify where each side's reasoning slips, if it does, and articulate the underlying commitments about explanation, evidence, and practical reasoning that each position implicitly assumes.
