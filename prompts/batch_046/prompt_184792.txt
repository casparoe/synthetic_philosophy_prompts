I am drafting an essay for a philosophy-of-religion seminar on whether the Buddhist doctrine of anatta (no-self) can be reconciled with the practical requirements of personal identity and moral accountability without collapsing into either Humean reductionism or metaphysical dualism. Rather than treating anatta as a blanket denial of diachronic unity, I argue that the Pali Canon’s decomposition of experience into the five khandhas (aggregates) targets specifically the fiction of a permanent, autonomous atman, not every form of continuity. I then propose that the Abhidhammic analysis of citta (mind-moments) and cetasika (mental factors) supports what I call a “process-continuity” interpretation: personal identity is neither a substance nor a static bundle, but a causally sustained, relationally structured pattern of intentional and affective streams that is sufficient to ground karmic responsibility across rebirth without positing an enduring substrate.

To test this interpretation, I inject an unconventional comparison with process ontology, asking whether treating identity as constituted by narrative-causal relations rather than intrinsic properties resolves the tension between anatta and rebirth more effectively than standard two-truths or reductionist accounts. I am particularly concerned that I may be overstating the coherence of the Abhidhammic framework, and I wonder whether my framing ignores a more damaging internal critique—perhaps the Madhyamaka rejection of svabhava (inherent existence)—which might undermine even the minimal continuity my argument requires.

I would welcome detailed comments on the following points. First, is my reading of the Abhidhammic material philosophically defensible, or does it impose a modern process-theoretic vocabulary onto texts that resist such systematisation? Second, does the “process-continuity” view genuinely solve the problem of moral responsibility across lives, or does it merely rename the difficulty of explaining how karma attaches to a non-substantial subject? Third, have I overlooked a stronger objection—whether from within Buddhist philosophy or from contemporary personal-identity theory—that renders the reconciliation I propose unstable? Please treat the essay as a work in progress: point out where the reasoning feels strained, suggest conceptual clarifications, and indicate whether there is a more compelling way to articulate the central tension between anatta and practical identity.
