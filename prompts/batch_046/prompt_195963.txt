In discussions of Buddhist anatta, I keep encountering a particular analogy used to defend the coherence of moral responsibility without a substantial self. The comparison runs that a person is like a corporation: it can enter contracts, bear legal liability, and pursue long-term strategies, yet it possesses no "corporate soul" over and above its shareholders, employees, assets, and governing charter. Proponents use this to argue that karma, rebirth, and ethical agency need not presuppose an atman, just as corporate action needs no metaphysical person behind the legal fiction. I want to audit how far this mapping actually holds, because it seems to do a lot of work that I am not convinced it can carry.

Please provide an explicit correspondence table—what plays the role of the shareholders, the board of directors, the charter or bylaws, bankruptcy, and dissolution in the Buddhist framework of the five skandhas? Then identify the precise points of breakdown: does the corporation's legal recognition as a unified agent smuggle in exactly the kind of persistent decision-maker that orthodox anatta denies? Does the analogy accidentally align with the Pudgalavada position—that there is a "person" neither identical to nor different from the aggregates—rather than with strict reductionism? I also want to know whether the conclusion—that moral responsibility survives intact—actually follows, or whether it depends on importing organizational unity that the skandhas lack. Finally, compare this with an alternative analogy, such as a river delta with no essential channel but continuous flow: does that avoid the smuggled unity, and if so, why do defenders prefer the corporation? Give your verdict on whether either analogy can genuinely support anatta, or whether the analogy itself reveals that the doctrine is either incoherent or far less radical than its defenders claim.
