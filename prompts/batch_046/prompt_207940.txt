I recently found myself arguing with friends after a philosophy-of-mind seminar about whether a navigation algorithm can genuinely represent a route through terrain it has never traversed—terrain that did not exist during its training—without possessing the evolutionary history that teleosemantic theories typically require for representational content. That discussion convinced me that the standard literature on teleosemantics and mental content has concentrated so heavily on misrepresentation within actual-world tracking that it leaves almost untouched the question of whether the framework can naturalize representations whose proper function is essentially counterfactual or modal. For a one-semester graduate seminar, I am proposing a research project of roughly 6,000 words that asks precisely this: can Millikan-style direct and derived proper functions be assigned to representations that stabilize around merely possible planning targets—scenarios never selected for tracking—without collapsing into interpretationism, or does Papineau’s learning-based selectionism fare better at grounding such modal content?

The proposal must state the question with enough precision to have a determinate answer, explain why existing treatments of misrepresentation and derived social functions do not settle the counterfactual case, and set out a month-by-month method comparing biological and artificial planning systems to see whether counterfactual tracking can be treated as a derived proper function. It should specify what success looks like—demonstrating that modal representations acquire determinate content through functional stabilization—versus failure—showing that teleosemantic accounts must restrict content to actual-world tracking histories—and it should include an honest assessment of why the project might prove too large for one semester.
