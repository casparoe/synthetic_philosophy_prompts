I am preparing a session for an advanced philosophy-of-probability seminar—fourteen students, ninety minutes—who have read basic decision theory but have little exposure to geometric measure. I want them to feel, through physical action and small real stakes, that Bertrand's paradox is not merely a puzzle yielding three answers (1/3, 1/2, and 1/4) but a demonstration that the principle of indifference collapses because there is no procedure-independent notion of selecting a chord "uniformly at random"; every physical ritual for generating randomness already parameterizes the space of chords. To keep this from being a standard lecture demonstration, inject a structural twist: before running any trials, each team must publicly defend why their assigned physical ritual is genuinely unbiased, and only afterwards do they discover that each ritual corresponds to exactly one of the classic parameterizations, with appeals to symmetry or physical invariance merely selecting one parameterization rather than resolving the ambiguity.

Design a complete runnable activity specifying rules, roles, materials, timeline, and debrief. Split the group into three teams of four or five, each given an identical large circular board (radius about 30 cm) and a different apparatus: Team A spins the board and drops a small ring onto it, then draws the chord perpendicular to the radius through the ring's center; Team B presses a tack randomly near the circumference and spins a pointer to select a second endpoint; Team C lays two parallel rulers across the board at randomly chosen positions and draws the chord midway between them according to a uniform distance rule. Before trials begin, every student writes a private prediction—"If all three teams are really selecting uniformly at random, the proportion of chords longer than the inscribed equilateral triangle's side should be..."—and wagers betting chips (redeemable for snacks) both on that prediction and on whether the teams will agree. Each team then conducts twenty measured trials. After results are pooled and the three frequencies diverge, the debrief should map each apparatus to its implicit sample space (midpoint-area, endpoint-arc, parallel-distance), show why each team experienced their ritual as fair, and demonstrate that arguments from rotational or scale invariance do not adjudicate among them but merely restate one parameterization's privileges. Predict what will go wrong the first time you run this—likely one team will accidentally standardize their procedure into another team's parameterization, or students will insist one ritual is "obviously" correct—and say how to handle it. Include an explicit, honest note on which feature of the paradox the activity cannot reproduce: probably the pure measure-theoretic fact that the set of all chords carries no canonical probability measure absent a generating process, and tell me exactly how I should supply that verbally in the debrief. Finally, provide a committed response: take definite positions on why these three apparatuses are the right ones, defend your specific rules and payoff scheme, and do not present the design as a menu of interchangeable options for me to choose among.
