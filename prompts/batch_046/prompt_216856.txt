Consider the following argument about diachronic identity, built from the Ship of Theseus but extended in directions that seem to collapse every distinction between persistence and mere succession. Define “structural essentialism” for artifacts: an artifact at time t2 is identical to one at t1 if and only if there is an unbroken chain of spatiotemporal continuity among the parts that realize its design function at each intermediate moment, regardless of material replacement. (1) The ship continuously repaired plank-by-plank in the water maintains this chain, so it is the original ship. (2) The ship rebuilt from the stored old planks does not maintain it, since the planks were removed and stored separately, breaking continuity; therefore it is not the original. (3) Now apply the same principle to a running software kernel gradually migrated across servers: every bit is replaced, yet the functional process is never interrupted. By the reasoning in (1), the migrated kernel is identical to the original. (4) Apply it to a corporation that replaces every employee, board member, and physical office over decades, yet never suspends its legal charter: it remains the same corporation. (5) Apply it analogously to a human being, in whom every cell is replaced over a lifetime but psychological-functional continuity is maintained: the person persists. (6) But now imagine a corporation whose charter is amended to an entirely different purpose, whose staff is replaced all at once in a coordinated transition, and whose assets are liquidated and replaced, yet whose legal existence is technically preserved by one continuous notary signature chain. By structural essentialism, this is still the original corporation, even though nothing about its function, membership, or purpose survives. (7) Generalizing, the view implies that any entity possessing a formal continuity token—legal, causal, or procedural—survives every internal change, which erases the line between genuine persistence and historical bookkeeping. (8) That conclusion is absurd: we clearly distinguish an evolved corporation from a hollow shell that retains only a file number. So one step must fail. Which step do you reject—the initial application to the repaired ship in (1), the analogical extension to non-physical entities in (3)–(5), or the framing in (6) that treats simultaneous total replacement as compatible with continuity? And whatever you reject, what does it cost? Do you lose a unified account of artifact identity, must you deny that people and digital files persist in the same sense, or do you need a hidden criterion that distinguishes “genuine” functional continuity from formal record-keeping?
