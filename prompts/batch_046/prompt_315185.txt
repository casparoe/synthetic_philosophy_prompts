I'm putting together a feature on how legal instutions quietly take sides in metaphysics, and I keep running into this weird asymmetry. Courts in most jurisdictions throw out lawsuits brought on behalf of dead ancestors — the reasoning is usually that the harm is past and the victims no longer exist to have standing. But the same courts, especially in climate litigation, grant standing to minors suing for future generations who haven't been born yet. Meanwhile carbon budgets treat emissions in 2050 as real liabilities constraining present policy, like those future harms are already sitting in the ledger. Even reparations debates frame historical wrongs as present-day injustices to living descendants, not as debts owed to people who are gone.

What theory of time — presentism, eternalism, or maybe that middle growing-block view where the past is fixed but the future isn't real yet — does this patchwork actually presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit ontology: does the practice strictly need a view where only present entities have moral status, or can an eternalist explain why dead people lack standing while unborn people have it? And if we replaced the hidden theory with a consistent one — say, a flat block-universe view where all times are equally real — what would have to change in court rules on standing, carbon accounting, or how we talk about repairing historical occurences? I'm also wondering if the practice is philosophically underdetermined: maybe it smuggles in a tensed 'now' for legal responsibility but uses tenseless relations for climate targets, fitting neither presentism nor eternalism cleanly. Write it like you're unpacking this for a smart reader who knows the law feels inconsistent but hasn't named the metaphysics yet.
