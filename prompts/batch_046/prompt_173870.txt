As a journalist covering the philosophy and governance of emerging technology, I spent part of last week at a workshop where researchers presented formalizations of so-called low-impact AI—systems constrained by causal influence measures or reachability metrics to avoid altering their environments in ways that go beyond a narrowly defined task. The technical discussion turned on whether such constraints can be defined without first specifying a full account of value: if a side effect is measured as deviation from a baseline world-state, that baseline already seems to embed normative judgments about which environmental changes count as damage and which are merely neutral perturbation. I kept returning to the conceptual tension between formal precision and value-ladenness, and I found myself wondering how a classical liberal thinker might have parsed the distinction.

If John Stuart Mill—who formulated the harm principle in On Liberty (1859) and died in 1873, long before computation became a philosophical subject—were alive to read the current literature on impact measurement, what do you think he would say about the claim that avoid side effects can be rigorously separated from specify what is good? Given his view that social coercion is only justified to prevent harm to others, and his broader utilitarian commitment to welfare, would he regard formal impact bounds as genuinely value-neutral instruments that protect liberty without smuggling in prior judgments about desirable world-states? Or would he argue that selecting a reference baseline, weighting different kinds of causal influence, and deciding which system-environment interactions fall within the scope of moral concern necessarily imports normative commitments that make value-neutrality an illusion? Please separate what is settled in the philosophy of low-impact AI—such as the formal existence of influence metrics—from what remains genuinely contested among specialists, particularly whether these metrics avoid value specification or merely disguise it within mathematical notation; and do not present any contested view about the inevitability of value-ladenness as though it were the consensus. I am especially curious whether Mill's distinction between self-regarding and other-regarding conduct could survive translation into these computational frameworks, or whether the very concept of a side effect collapses that boundary by treating all unanticipated environmental changes as potential harms.
