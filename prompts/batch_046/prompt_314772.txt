Analyze the documented prisoner abuse committed by members of the 372nd Military Police Company at Abu Ghraib in 2003–2004 through the competing explanatory lenses developed in Christopher Browning's Ordinary Men and Daniel Goldhagen's Hitler's Willing Executioners. Construct the mapping with discipline: specify which actors correspond to Browning's battalion members, identify the situational mechanisms he isolates—peer conformity, bureaucratic compartmentalization, authority diffusion, and incremental escalation—and assess whether they are present in this case, noting where the structural absence of a state-sponsored dehumanization ideology changes their operation. Then examine whether Goldhagen's thesis of a culturally embedded eliminationist worldview can be applied without forcing the analogy: is there a specifically American ideological substrate comparable to his account of German antisemitism, or does the framework require positing a parallel eliminationist culture that the documented evidence—training protocols, command climate, and the soldiers' own statements—does not support? Finally, identify what Abu Ghraib reveals about the explanatory limits of both models, particularly whether the perpetrators' photographic self-documentation and the subsequent court-martial dynamics constitute anomalies neither framework anticipated, and whether the collapse of professional military identity under reserve mobilization explains residual features that both situationist and culturalist accounts leave silent. Be explicit about where the theory is silent rather than forcing it to speak.
