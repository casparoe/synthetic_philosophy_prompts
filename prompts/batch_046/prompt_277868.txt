For my metaphysics seminar, I am examining whether the development of quantum statistics in the 1920s—when physicists recognized that photons and electrons of the same kind are in principle indistinguishable, forcing statistical treatments that abandon individual tracking—constitutes a real-world analogue to Max Black's counterexample to Leibniz's principle of the identity of indiscernibles. Take this scientific episode as your case and analyze it through the lens of Black's two qualitatively identical spheres in an otherwise empty symmetric universe. Does the quantum situation genuinely mirror Black's spheres, or does the absence of spatiotemporal individuation in quantum mechanics break the analogy? Specifically: does the statistical behavior of identical particles show they lack individual identity, or merely that we lack epistemic access to distinguishing properties; does Black's argument depend on an implicit classical metaphysics of spatial relation that quantum mechanics undermines; and what does the episode reveal about whether the identity principle was always restricted to macroscopic objects, or whether quantum mechanics forces a revision of numerical identity itself?
