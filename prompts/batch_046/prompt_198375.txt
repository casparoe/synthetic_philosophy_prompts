I'm working on a novel with a Victorian naturalist arguing with a clergyman, so I've been skimming Darwin's Descent of Man and some letters. There's a pattern I keep running into that I can't name, and it's not just the naturalistic fallacy in general. Darwin explains moral feelings as evolved social instincts — sympahy for the group, loyalty, that kind of thing — and traces how they spread. But then he also talks about the standard of morality rising over time, and about some instincts being more noble or higher than others. The evolutionary story explains where the feelings come from, but it doesnt seem to justify why the higher ones should win; that judgment looks like it's coming from outside the account. So people use Darwin to say morality is just biolgy, yet Darwin himself keeps importing value judgments into the biolgy. I see the same move in other evolutionary ethics: the description smuggles in a normative ranking that the mechanism itself doesnt produce. Does this specific circularity have an established name — where evolutionary history is treated as both cause and authority for moral progress? Or does Darwin actually resolve it in a way I'm missing? I dont need a bibliography, just whether there's a concept for the move and where its edges are.
