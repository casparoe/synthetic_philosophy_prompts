You are a professional philosopher of biology with expertise in taxonomy and metaphysics. I am preparing material for an advanced seminar on the species problem, and I need you to construct a formal diagnostic taxonomy—a decision tree whose branches are labeled by the specific conceptual claims that split positions, not by the names of the positions themselves. Before you draw the tree, please spend a moment brainstorming: what are the genuinely divisive judgments about boundary cases, and which metaphysical commitments (realism about kinds versus individuals, monism versus pluralism, essentialism versus cluster thinking) actually manifest in answers about those cases?

The root node should ask whether reproductive isolation is necessary for species membership. From there, branch through three concrete problem cases: ring species, in which geographically arranged populations interbreed with immediate neighbors but not with distant ends of the loop; obligate asexual lineages that reproduce clonally yet cluster ecologically; and a synthetic scenario I want included—engineered microbial consortia deliberately designed to exchange genetic modules horizontally across otherwise isolated lineages, so that any boundary drawn is a design choice rather than a discovered natural joint. At each internal node, phrase the split as a concrete yes/no or either/or question a student could answer without knowing which camp it belongs to—for instance, “In the ring-species loop, do the two terminal populations belong to one species or two despite the absence of direct gene flow?”—and make sure the path through all three cases determines the leaf.

The leaves should carry named positions where they are established: the Mayr-style biological species concept, the phylogenetic species concept, the ecological species concept, species pluralism, nominalism, and the view of species as spatiotemporally restricted individuals associated with Ghiselin and Hull. Where the logical space permits combinations that no standard view occupies, label them “unnamed” and provide one sentence on why they are unoccupied—whether incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. For every named leaf, state in one sentence its most awkward commitment, the price it pays for consistency across the three cases. Finally, flag any pair of leaves that this particular tree cannot genuinely separate, and briefly diagnose whether the gap is rhetorical, metaphysical but invisible here, or due to shared judgments on all three boundary cases. Keep the taxonomy rigorous and self-contained; do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations, since the task is conceptual mapping rather than source tracking.
