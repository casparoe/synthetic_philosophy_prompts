I am trying to work out whether lying and merely misleading someone differ in moral weight, using my own judgments on concrete cases as a guide.

My verdicts are these. Telling my parents I finished my homework when I have not done so is plainly wrong. If they ask generally about my schoolwork and I reply that I have been working all evening, deliberately omitting the unfinished assignment, that also feels wrong, but less like a direct betrayal of trust. When a classmate asks whether I liked their presentation and I answer that it was "really unique," intending them to hear praise while I actually think it was poor, this strikes me as nearly as bad as an explicit lie, despite the literal truth of my words. If I remain silent while a friend proceeds on the false assumption that I support their plan to skip an exam, allowing them to maintain that misconception without correction, I judge this wrong but somewhat less blameworthy than speaking a falsehood. Yet on a college application, describing myself as having "led a team project" when I was only one member among several, using technically accurate but selectively framed language, feels to me closer to the explicit lie in the first case than to the silent omission in the fourth. The case that keeps dividing me is this: if I send a message reading "I'll be there," knowing I will arrive late but also knowing the recipient will understand it as a commitment to punctuality, have I lied, misled, or done something that falls between?

Is there a unified principle—perhaps centered on the speaker's communicative intention, the listener's reasonable interpretive expectations, or the distinction between asserting and merely implying—that can accommodate all of these judgments? Which of my verdicts must I revise to maintain consistency, and what does the principle say about the unresolved message case?
