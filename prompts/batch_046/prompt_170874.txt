In my philosophy seminar, the instructor has been ruling case-by-case on whether knowledge is genuinely more valuable than mere true belief—not in the abstract, but in concrete scenarios drawn from science, technology, and everyday life. Her verdicts so far are these. First, a hiker follows a GPS black-box through a forest; she has correct beliefs about every turn but understands nothing about satellite triangulation. Verdict: true belief is fully adequate—knowing geolocation theory would not have improved her hike. Second, a research chemist synthesizes a compound using a reaction mechanism she fully grasps, and correctly believes the product will be stable. Verdict: knowledge clearly outstrips true belief; without grasp of the mechanism she could not adapt when impurities appear. Third, a patient takes an inert pill, truly believes it will reduce inflammation, and the belief alone produces a real physiological improvement. Verdict: true belief does all the work; knowing the pharmacological truth (that the pill is inert) would actually destroy the benefit. Fourth, a structural engineer approves a bridge design calculated by proprietary software she does not understand; the calculations happen to be correct for these soil conditions. Verdict: knowledge of the underlying mechanics is more valuable—if conditions shift slightly, her true belief about this specific design is not portable. Fifth, a child uses a bird-identification app with a known glitch that, in this particular instance, correctly names a rare warbler; she possesses no ornithological framework. Verdict: the true belief is enough for her purpose; understanding avian taxonomy would not make her sighting more valuable to her.

Next week’s case is a ship’s captain navigating an unprecedented storm using a predictive oracle—a black-box model trained on centuries of meteorological data that issues correct, real-time course corrections she cannot explain, reproduce, or integrate into any broader theory of atmospheric dynamics. She saves all four hundred passengers. Her beliefs that each correction is correct are true, formed by a highly reliable process, and safe across nearby navigational scenarios, yet she holds sole legal and moral responsibility for every life aboard and has no grasp of why the corrections work. Predict her verdict on whether her own knowledge—an understanding of the meteorology behind the corrections—would have been more valuable than the true beliefs the oracle supplied. Identify the implicit principle that best explains her track record, for instance an integration-with-explanation account tied to Meno’s aitias logismos, a credit-and-responsibility account, a practical-portability or anti-luck account, or something else; then name the competing principle that fits her earlier rulings equally well. Specify which further case would best discriminate between those two rules. In your response, say how confident you are in this reconstruction and single out the specific point about which you are least sure.
