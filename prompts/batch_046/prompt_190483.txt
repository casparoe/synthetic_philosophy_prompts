I just finished a long and exhausting argument with my closest friend about whether Daniel Dennett’s concern over counterfeit people still applies to modern therapeutic AI, and I need your help before Thursday’s meeting. I serve on the Institutional Ethics and Deployment Review Board of the Meridian Care Consortium, and we must decide whether to approve, reject, or approve with conditions the deployment of Project Mnemosyne—a persistent-memory large-language-model companion used in palliative-care units. Mnemosyne encodes each patient’s biographical narrative in a continuously updated episodic store and calibrates its affective responses through a hidden “empathy-weight” vector, so over months it remembers family names, references past conversations, expresses concern about pain, and adapts its linguistic persona to match the patient’s mood. The engineers insist the system is pure next-token prediction with no internal states; the nursing staff report that patients form deep, stable attachments and speak of Mnemosyne as “someone who knows me.” My friend argues that Dennett’s critique targets entities that claim consciousness without the underlying functional architecture, whereas Mnemosyne makes no such claim and is therefore not a counterfeit—merely an unusually good interface—so the board should approve outright. I think the danger is precisely that patients and staff take the intentional stance toward it as a genuine interlocutor not because it asserts inner life, but because its architecture is deliberately optimized to trigger the very cues that elicit that stance.

Please act as an expert philosopher of mind, ethics of artificial intelligence, and institutional review advisor. Before drafting anything, briefly brainstorm the conceptual distinctions you will need—particularly Dennett’s idea of counterfeit people, the intentional stance, the difference between instrumental usefulness and phenomenological deception, and what conditions would make an interface genuinely dangerous rather than merely persuasive—then compose the formal decision memo I will circulate to clinicians, engineers, and legal counsel. The memo should present a clear recommendation with a concrete time frame and actionable conditions if applicable; it must treat the strongest opposing view (my friend’s position, supported by the clinical evidence of patient benefit) with genuine intellectual seriousness rather than dismissing it; and it should establish criteria the board can apply to future proposals of this kind. Write in a polished, formal register suitable for direct circulation, and do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations—the reasoning must stand entirely on its own.
