After a long argument last night with a friend about Alfred North Whitehead's philosophy of education, I realized she applies a consistent but unstated framework to pedagogical designs, judging them against his well-known triad of romance, precision, and generalization. Her verdicts are these. She considers a classic Montessori primary classroom an exemplary realization of the romantic stage, precisely because it cultivates wonder without premature systematization. She rejects standardized test-preparation curricula as violations of the rhythm, arguing that they collapse precision into a false generalization—students learn to apply rules they have not genuinely disciplined within a domain. She regards a traditional graduate seminar devoted to close textual exegesis as achieving genuine precision but judges it educationally stunted because it never moves through romance to reach generalization within its own disciplinary frame. Finally, she approves an interdisciplinary capstone course, but only conditionally: she insists it achieves true generalization solely when students have already completed the prior rhythms with discipline-specific content, not merely with generic skills.

We now disagree about a newer model she has not yet evaluated: a "flipped mastery" secondary program in which students watch structured lecture videos at home (which she and I both take to supply precision), then spend all class time on open-ended, cross-disciplinary collaborative projects with no predetermined domain boundary (which supplies generalization). The program has no explicit curricular stage for romance—relying instead on student self-selection for initial motivation—and it never requires students to achieve disciplined precision within a single domain before applying knowledge across domains. Given her four verdicts, should she approve this model, reject it, or approve it only with conditions? I am less interested in the final label than in the competing interpretive rules that could generate her track record—perhaps she requires the rhythms to occur in strict sequential order within each domain, or perhaps she requires only that all three be genuinely present somewhere in the student's experience regardless of order and location—what each rule predicts about the flipped-mastery case, which rule you think best coheres with her pattern, and what further pedagogical case would definitively distinguish the two.
