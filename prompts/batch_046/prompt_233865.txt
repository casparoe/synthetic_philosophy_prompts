I've been reading on my own about the so-called Boltzmann brain problem—the worry that in a sufficiently large or eternal universe, random thermal fluctuations should produce conscious observers with fully formed false memories far more often than evolutionary or cosmological processes produce observers like me with genuinely continuous pasts. I don't want a reading list or a scorecard of cosmological models; I want the specification first. What are the desiderata—the actual requirements—that any adequate theory of cosmic history, statistical mechanics, or observer selection must meet if it is to justify my treating my childhood memories as causally grounded rather than as accidental structural duplicates? For each requirement, explain what distinction or case it protects, and mark whether it is non-negotiable or merely a preference of one camp. I am especially interested in three specific demands: first, a principled distinction between causally continuous memory formation and mere thermal duplication of mental-state structures; second, consistency with our observed low-entropy past that does not rely on an ad hoc or unjustified prior over initial conditions; and third, some criterion—whether quantitative or qualitative—by which the prevalence of "real" observers can be shown to exceed that of fluctuation-observers, if such a comparison is even required. Please also address whether any subset of these desiderata is jointly unsatisfiable given established results in statistical physics or cosmology; that tension is precisely what I want to understand. Keep the focus on the criteria and their motivating cases, not on naming specific theories.
