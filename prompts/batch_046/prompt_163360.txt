I've been trying to work out for myself whether being blunt is morally better than being polite, especially when politeness requires saying things that aren't literally true, and I keep running into the same trap. Here is the chain, laid out as clearly as I can manage it.

(1) If saying something false is wrong, it is wrong because it manipulates another person's beliefs in a way that undermines their autonomy.

(2) A white lie—telling my aunt I love the sweater she knit me—does exactly this: I say something I believe is false, knowing it will shape her belief about my opinion.

(3) The usual defense is that intent matters: I meant kindness, not harm, and the falsehood doesn't affect any important practical decision of hers.

(4) But Japanese indirect communication works the same way. When a colleague says that a proposal might be a bit difficult, meaning that it is unacceptable, they also say something that is literally false or misleading, knowing it will shape my belief about the situation, and they do it with kind intent—to preserve harmony and face.

(5) So if kind intent excuses the white lie in (3), it should also excuse Japanese indirectness.

(6) Dutch directness, by contrast, rejects both practices. It holds that you should state your true belief plainly, regardless of emotional cost, because any other course treats the listener as unable to handle reality.

(7) If Dutch directness is correct, then (3) is wrong—intent does not excuse falsehood.

(8) But if (7) is true, then Japanese indirectness is not merely different but systematically wrong, not in one case but as a general practice built on conventional misstatement.

(9) That seems like an absurd result: it implies that an entire culture's ordinary, respectful mode of interaction is morally defective.

(10) To avoid that, we might reject (7) and say that kind intent does excuse falsehood.

(11) But then there is no special moral wrongness in lying itself—there is only wrongness in malicious intent.

(12) So a malicious lie and an honest mistake of fact are morally on a par, as long as neither intends harm, which feels wrong.

(13) Therefore either we accept that Japanese indirectness is immoral (keeping directness), or we accept that lying is never specially wrong (giving up directness). I cannot accept either horn.

Which step should I actually reject, and what does rejecting it cost me? I would prefer to keep both the idea that kind intent matters and the idea that respectful indirectness is not immoral, but I cannot see how to hold both without contradiction. Please separate what is settled here from what is genuinely contested among people who study ethics and communication, and do not present a contested view as if it were the consensus.
