I serve on the State Criminal Justice Reform Commission, a statutory advisory body that must submit a recommendation to the legislature by the end of the fiscal year on whether to retain, structurally reform, or sunset the capital sentencing statute. Our hearings have covered the standard terrain—retributive proportionality, econometric claims about deterrence, and the irreversibility of erroneous execution—but our internal working papers have also flagged a structural concern that rarely surfaces in public briefs: that the death penalty functions as a high-value bargaining chip in plea negotiations, which may systematically inflate the rate of false convictions in capital-eligible cases and thereby compound the risk of executing factually innocent defendants whose appeals eventually turn on procedural rather than substantive error.

Please draft the formal decision memo I will circulate to the full commission before our Thursday vote. It should recommend one concrete course of action—either retention with specified procedural safeguards, replacement with mandatory life without parole coupled with a 15-year appellate audit mechanism, or an immediate legislative moratorium—together with a precise 90-day implementation timeline. The reasoning must engage the strongest retributivist claim seriously rather than reducing it to vengeance: specifically, address whether the expressive and moral-equilibrium-restoring functions of capital punishment for aggravated murder can be fulfilled by any non-lethal sentence, and whether those functions depend on actual execution or merely on the sentence's symbolic availability. On deterrence, analyze whether the conditions required for capital punishment to deter—high certainty, swift celerity, and perceptible severity—are structurally undermined by mandatory appellate delay and geographic variance in application, without invoking named empirical studies. Crucially, address the plea-bargaining corruption pathway described above: either propose a concrete procedural or institutional remedy that severs the link between capital charging authority and conviction risk, or argue explicitly why no remedy can be adequate given the asymmetry between false positives and false negatives in capital cases. The memo should conclude with an unambiguous bottom line, treat the strongest counter-argument with intellectual honesty, and be written as a document the legislature could act on rather than as an abstract philosophical essay.
