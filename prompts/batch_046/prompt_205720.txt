Construct a one-paragraph thought experiment in which an agent makes a conscious, deliberative decision — say, to sign a document after weighing a moral reason — and then performs the corresponding intentional movement, but during the brief interval between decision and action the neural substrates that originally realised the mental state are replaced, region by region, by physically distinct yet functionally equivalent components, so that the properties causally driving the movement are not those that initially realised the decision. The replacement must obey physical law, proceed without redundant or overdetermined causation, and leave the agent sincerely reporting that the act felt fully willed rather than alien.

Then analyse the case to specification: show why Kim’s causal exclusion argument forces the non-reductive physicalist to deny that the mental decision caused the movement; show why an emergentist dualist can consistently retain mental causation; identify the structural feature doing the work (the diachronic separation between the mental state’s original realiser and the physical properties that actually drive the effect); explain whether removing that separation — so the original neural substrate remains intact throughout — collapses the case back into a standard exclusion problem; and state how a defender of non-reductive physicalism might attack the scenario, for instance by broadening the supervenience base to include the replacement substrates or by denying that token-identity across time is required. Write in British English, and do not include bibliographies, citations, or reading lists.
