In a reading group on classical Chinese ethics, I have gradually adopted what I take to be a stricter variant of Confucian role ethics: not merely that moral cultivation proceeds through perfecting one's relational roles—parent, child, ruler, minister, friend—but that the self is exhaustively constituted by the performance of those roles, leaving no residual "inner" identity that could stand apart and evaluate them. I am calling this position inter-role constitutionalism, and I want to treat it as a live metaphysical and ethical thesis rather than a purely interpretive claim about the Analects or Mencius. Before I commit further, I need an honest map of its exposure: what observations, conceptual arguments, or results from adjacent fields—moral psychology, theories of personal identity, cross-cultural ethics, or even formal work on social ontology—would actually count against it?

Please distinguish the defeaters that a committed defender of inter-role constitutionalism would themselves concede as damaging from those they would dismiss as resting on a misunderstanding of the view; rank them from fatal to merely awkward; and include both external critiques and internal tensions—for example, whether the view can coherently account for moral reformers who reject corrupt roles, whether it collapses into a form of social determinism it was designed to avoid, or whether it can explain the phenomenology of moral conflict without smuggling in a non-relational self. I also want an assessment of whether the position has been qualified into unfalsifiability, and if so whether that insulation is a vice or an acceptable feature for a thesis of this kind. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then lay out the reasoning with no preamble and without restating my question.
