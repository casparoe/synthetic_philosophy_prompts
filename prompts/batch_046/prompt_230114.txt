I am organizing a small interdisciplinary seminar on advance directives and the authority of the earlier self, and I want to move past the familiar dialectic between personal-identity-based objections and clinical-utilitarian defenses. I see four structurally distinct open questions that could orient serious work over the next several years.

First, the problem of graded authority in intermediate competence: progressive conditions often produce a prolonged zone in which a patient retains partial decisional capacity—enough to revise some preferences but not enough to meet full legal thresholds—while an advance directive was drafted for binary incompetence. Does the directive's authority diminish continuously with retained capacity, or does it remain fully binding until a discrete threshold is crossed?

Second, the divergence between proxy interpretation and current expression: when an appointed surrogate interprets the directive in a way that conflicts with the patient's present, albeit compromised, expressed wishes, we face a conflict not between two temporal selves but between two present authorities—representational and direct. Which form of authority should prevail, and does the answer depend on whether we conceive the directive as a continuing command or as evidence of stable values?

Third, the moral-status-transition question: honoring a directive can be justified either by attributing continuing moral authority to the earlier self, or by treating the directive merely as a high-quality instrument for determining the patient's current best interests. These justifications diverge sharply in cases where the patient's present welfare clearly conflicts with the directive's instructions. Which justification is more defensible, and what does each imply for cases of psychiatric advance directives where the patient's present suffering might be relieved by violating the prior instruction?

Fourth, the institutional-design question: should legal frameworks distinguish between revocable Ulysses contracts—binding only if the patient at the time of potential revocation lacks sufficient insight to revoke—and permanently binding directives? And does the answer depend on whether we view the earlier self as exercising sovereignty over future states or as delegating decision-making to a trusted proxy?

Given limited resources—a single research group, a three-year grant, and an audience of clinical ethicists and legal theorists—how would you prioritize these four lines? I want a defended agenda, not a survey. Specify the criteria you are using—tractability with current empirical and conceptual tools, dependence relations among the questions, downstream impact on law and clinical practice, and the risk that resolving one renders the others moot. Identify which question is upstream of the others, which is most likely to change practice regardless of theoretical resolution, and which you would deprioritize because its answer would have the narrowest ripple effects. Also indicate whether any of these four should be reconceived or replaced by a different open question that you think is currently neglected.
