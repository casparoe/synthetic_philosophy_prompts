I'm giving the retirement toast at my doctoral advisor's department dinner. She spent three decades applying Foucault's analytics of power, knowledge, and disciplinary institutions—specifically on how archival practices in the human sciences construct the objects they claim merely to study—and about half the room are historians of science and critical theorists who will catch every allusion; the other half are family, staff, and administrators with no background in the work. Write a five-minute toast that treats Foucault's framework as a structural constraint on what can be said: it must be impossible within the speech to celebrate her as a self-authoring genius, to suggest she will watch over the department from retirement, or to frame knowledge as pure discovery outside institutional conditions. Instead, the toast should locate her achievement inside the disciplinary networks she analyzed—treating retirement not as leaving the field but as a redistribution of her position within the archive—and it should include one concrete, affectionate anecdote illustrating reverse discourse, the phenomenon in which a marginalized position inside a discipline becomes a source of critical insight. The speech needs to land precisely for scholars without becoming a lecture, and it must comfort non-specialists without asserting anything she would reject as false metaphysics. Do not include citations, a bibliography, or a reading list. End with a one-sentence summary of the toast's core message as the last line.
