Analyze the Wolfgang Beltracchi forgery affair—specifically the 2011 exposure of his modernist forgeries, including works attributed to Heinrich Campendonk and Max Ernst, through the detection of anachronistic titanium-white pigment—as a test case for the causal-intentional theory of artistic value, according to which an original's worth derives essentially from being a direct material trace of its attributed artist's intentional act. Map the affair onto this framework: identify the relevant agents (Beltracchi, expert authenticators, collectors, museums), state what the theory predicts about the value of the forgeries both before disclosure and after, and explain how the fact that leading experts endorsed the works on purely stylistic grounds relates to the claim that causal origin is constitutive rather than merely incidental. Then examine a less-discussed complication: once revealed, should these objects be reconceived not as nullities but as "parasitic originals"—works that acquire a derivative but genuine artistic value tied to Beltracchi's own intentional act of deceptive creation and to their meta-commentary on the institution of attribution—thereby suggesting that artistic value can be transferred rather than annihilated by misattribution? Finally, say what the episode reveals about the theory itself: does it confirm that causal history is indispensable to value, or does it show that the value we call "authenticity" is actually sustained by institutional practices of certification that do not reliably track causal history? Be disciplined about where the theory explains the post-revelation collapse in market value, where it is silent on the continued aesthetic and curatorial interest in the revealed fakes, and whether the case forces a sharper separation between artistic value and provenance than the causal-intentional account allows.
