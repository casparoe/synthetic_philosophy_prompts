The debate over Hofstadter's Gödelian analogy in Gödel, Escher, Bach—between self-referential formal sentences and the emergence of self-aware minds—seems stuck in a loop of its own. Logicians emphasize that Gödel numbering is pure syntactic encoding: a formula asserts properties of its own Gödel number, with no semantics, no evaluation, and no intentional aboutness directed at itself as a subject. Philosophers of mind reply that human self-awareness also lacks a Cartesian spectator; it is just recursive processing monitoring its own outputs, structurally analogous to a strange loop. Both sides have rehearsed these moves for decades, neither denies the formal results, neither dismisses the phenomenology, and yet nobody shifts position. My working theory is that the stall hides a category mistake about levels of representation: critics attack the analogy for lacking semantic ascent—minds represent themselves as representing—while defenders offer only object-level syntactic recursion. So is this disagreement substantive (about whether syntax can ground intentionality), verbal (about whether "strange loop" means structural isomorphism or causal explanation), or are two different questions—one about necessary conditions for consciousness, one about illuminating metaphors—being forced under one heading? Diagnose it using what each side actually commits to, and tell me whether anything—say, a formal result linking specific recursive architectures to self-modeling capacities, or a conceptual distinction separating encoding from evaluation—could actually resolve it, or whether the impasse signals that the analogy is permanently heuristic rather than explanatory.
