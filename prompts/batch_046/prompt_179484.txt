A philosophy podcast I recently listened to offered the following summary of disjunctivism in the philosophy of perception. According to the summary, disjunctivists maintain that veridical perception involves direct acquaintance with an external object, whereas in hallucination there is no mental object present whatsoever—not even an internal representation—so that the two states share no phenomenal property in common. The argument from illusion, on this account, wrongly assumes that subjective indistinguishability between a veridical case and a hallucination entails a shared mental component, such as a sense-datum or representational content. The disjunctivist response is that indistinguishability only establishes a shared disposition to judge, not a shared experiential content. The podcast then drew two further conclusions: first, that disjunctivism eliminates mental representations from perception entirely; and second, that because experience requires direct world-contact, a brain in a vat with neural states identical to those of a normal perceiver would have no phenomenal experience at all, which means disjunctivism is incompatible with any internalist thesis that phenomenal character supervenes solely on brain states. Is this summary fair? Please distinguish the parts that are broadly accurate, the parts that are common but imprecise simplifications, and the parts that genuinely misrepresent the disjunctivist view—especially with respect to mental representation, the structure of the argument from illusion, and the brain-in-a-vat implication. Finally, please assess whether the podcast's inference from disjunctivism to the rejection of internalism actually follows, or whether it depends on additional premises that a disjunctivist could deny.
