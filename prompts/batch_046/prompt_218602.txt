I'm working through a problem in poetic translation theory that has come up in a course on formal constraint and cross-linguistic preservation, specifically with Clément Marot's rondeaux. In Marot's practice, the rondeau's architecture—typically rendered with the rhyme scheme AbaAabAB—requires not only a fixed refrain that returns with minor variation but also that the refrain's lexical anchors sit in rhyme positions that are structurally predetermined. The difficulty is that these rhyme sounds do not merely frame the content; they seem to generate it. I want to explore what I will call the refrain-variation dependency: the idea that in Marot's original, the specific phonemes of the rhyme scheme determine which words can serve as stable refrain anchors, so that the form operates as a generative constraint rather than a neutral container. When an English translator substitutes rhymes that obey the same pattern, the lexical items available for the refrain shift, which in turn alters the ironic echo or tonal resonance that the repetition was designed to produce.

Could you explain, at a conceptual rather than practical level, whether preserving the formal architecture of the rondeau and preserving the semantic or ironic effect of its refrain are genuinely competing desiderata, or whether they are ultimately inseparable because the form itself functions as part of the meaning? I am particularly interested in whether a "functional equivalence" strategy—replicating the effect of the refrain through different wording—actually resolves the tension or merely relocates it, and whether there is a principled distinction between altering the refrain's wording to maintain rhyme and altering it to maintain tonal irony. If the form and content are inseparable in the way I am suggesting, does that imply that any translation of Marot's rondeaux must choose between being formally faithful and being semantically faithful, or is there a deeper sense in which one kind of fidelity entails the other?
