Consider the analogy commonly used to contrast Descartes’s Cartesian theater with Dennett’s multiple drafts model of consciousness. The Cartesian theater is often described as a private movie house inside the skull: sensory streams converge onto a single screen, projected for an inner audience—the self—and the moment of conscious experience is precisely the moment that audience watches the film. Against this, the multiple drafts model is likened to a large newsroom with no editor-in-chief: reporters in different departments file competing drafts of the same event, no single version is canonically “the” story, and when a query arrives—a verbal report, a memory probe—one draft is promoted to “publication” without ever having been ratified by a central authority. This analogy is frequently invoked to argue that the unity of consciousness is an illusion generated by retrospective narration, and that there is therefore no Cartesian theater requiring a homunculus.

Audit the analogy systematically.

First, provide an explicit correspondence table: in the theater model, what plays the role of the projector, the screen, the audience, the film reel, and the theater building? In the newsroom model, what corresponds to the reporters, the competing drafts, the absence of an editor, the publication mechanism, and the “query” that selects a draft?

Second, identify where the mapping breaks down. For instance, a movie theater implies a single spatial location and a unified temporal sequence, whereas neural processes are distributed in space and time; does this disanalogy undermine the critique of the theater, or does it merely redescribe it? In the newsroom, “publication” suggests a discrete, irreversible act, but Dennett’s framework allows for both “Orwellian” revisions (a draft rewritten after the fact) and “Stalinesque” revisions (a draft suppressed before it reaches awareness). Does the newsroom analogy have the resources to distinguish these two mechanisms, or does it collapse them into a single editorial decision?

Third, examine what each side of the analogy quietly imports about the value of the whole versus the parts. The theater analogy imports a privileged observer whose experience is the final arbiter of reality; the newsroom analogy imports a democratic or anarchic pluralism that may underestimate the functional integration required for coherent action. Which of these imported features actually belong to the target theories, and which are artifacts of the source domain?

Finally, assess whether the conclusion—that there is no central self or unified conscious moment—survives once the disanalogies are on the table. If the newsroom cannot accommodate Orwellian and Stalinesque revisions separately, does that weaken the multiple drafts model, or only the analogy? And if the theater analogy’s spatial localization is literally false of neural architecture, is the Cartesian theater defeated, or merely relocated?
