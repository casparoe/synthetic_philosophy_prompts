I’ve been working through the combination problem for panpsychism in a philosophy of mind seminar, and I’ve built an argument that uses cosmopsychism—the view that only the cosmos is fundamentally conscious and human minds are derivative parts—to show that downward derivation actually makes the combination problem worse. The steps are as follows. (1) Panpsychism holds that phenomenal properties are fundamental and present in basic physical entities. (2) The combination problem asks how micro-subjects combine into unified macro-subjects like us. (3) Cosmopsychists solve this by denying upward combination; instead, macro-subjects derive their consciousness downward from one cosmic subject. (4) But if consciousness can be derived downward from a larger subject to its parts, then derivation should also apply horizontally or across arbitrary partitions: any subsystem of the cosmos is, in principle, a part that could derive its own unified phenomenal field from the whole. (5) So not only am I conscious, but my left cerebral hemisphere, my left hand together with the Andromeda galaxy, and every other arbitrary subset of the universe, each derives a subject from the cosmic mind. (6) To block this proliferation, the cosmopsychist must restrict derivation to parts that stand in a special relation—say, maximal causal integration or informational closure—to the whole. (7) But once consciousness is tied to such physical-structural relations, it is no longer a fundamental property independent of physical organization; it becomes structurally or functionally determined, which is precisely the physicalist picture panpsychism was meant to replace. (8) Therefore cosmopsychism either allows an absurd infinity of arbitrary subjects or collapses into a form of functionalism about consciousness.

The conclusion strikes me as wrong—I do not think cosmopsychism is self-refuting in this way—but I cannot locate the exit. Which step should I reject? I would prefer to keep (3) and (6), since I think downward derivation is the distinctive cosmopsychist move and that some boundary condition on parts is genuinely necessary. But if keeping both forces me into the absurd conclusion, I am willing to pay that cost and want to know exactly what I am giving up. Please diagnose whether the slip is an equivocation on “part,” a modal slide from “can derive” to “actually derives,” a hidden physicalist assumption about what counts as a boundary, or whether the argument is actually valid and one of my premises is just implausible. If my framing rests on a confused premise or the task as posed cannot be answered well—say, if downward derivation was never meant to apply across arbitrary partitions in the first place—say so plainly rather than complying politely. I want an honest account of what each rejection costs, and whether I am smuggling in assumptions that a panpsychist can consistently deny.
