Exam question: In Republic Book II, Glaucon introduces the myth of Gyges’ ring to press a challenge about the value of justice. First, reconstruct the challenge precisely, distinguishing Glaucon’s position from Thrasymachus’s claim that justice is the interest of the stronger. Second, evaluate whether Glaucon’s thought experiment succeeds in establishing that justice is valued only for its consequences. In your evaluation, address whether the invisibility granted by the ring undermines what we may call the psychological-transparency defense—the view that just and unjust characters remain distinguishable even when unobserved, because internal harmony or discord manifests in action independently of social reward. Conclude by stating whether Socrates’s appeal to the tripartite soul in Book IV adequately answers this specific challenge.

Student answer: Glaucon recounts how Gyges finds a ring that renders him invisible, uses it to seduce the queen and kill the king, and asks what either a just or an unjust person would do with such power. He concludes that both would act unjustly, since justice is practiced only out of fear of punishment and desire for reputation. Thus justice has no intrinsic worth; it is purely instrumental. Glaucon’s position is essentially Thrasymachus’s view that justice serves the stronger, except Glaucon frames it through mutual vulnerability rather than political domination. The challenge succeeds because it establishes behavioral equivalence: once social surveillance is removed, the just and unjust agents are indistinguishable in their actions, which proves that justice lacks any internal structure. The ring also destroys psychological transparency—because the agent is unseen, there is no internal witness to generate guilt, so the soul’s harmony cannot operate as an independent check. Socrates answers this in Book IV by introducing the tripartite soul and using the chariot analogy to show that reason must govern the appetites; this demonstrates that justice is internal harmony regardless of visibility. Socrates also invokes the myth of Er in Book X, which functions as an invisible audience that restores transparency by guaranteeing posthumous judgment. Therefore Glaucon is mistaken, because justice remains visible to the gods even when concealed by the ring, and the soul’s order persists irrespective of external observation.
