I am currently writing an extended essay for my philosophy of science course, and I have become particularly interested in whether Thomas Malthus's An Essay on the Principle of Population ought to be understood not as a straightforwardly falsified theory, but as one that was right yet premature—that is, structurally sound in its long-run predictions yet apparently disconfirmed by the agricultural and demographic transitions that followed shortly after its publication. My tutor suggested that there is a live debate over whether such rescue strategies genuinely preserve scientific status or merely render a theory immune to refutation, but I find myself unsure which of the related conceptual problems are genuinely upstream of the others.

Specifically, I have been trying to map out three interrelated lines of enquiry that seem somewhat underexplored. First, whether the distinction between a progressive and a degenerating research programme, as characterised by Imre Lakatos, can be coherently applied to theories whose predictive failures are confined to a particular historical window rather than being systematically recurring. Second, whether philosophers can defend a distinct epistemic category—perhaps that of being temporarily falsified yet structurally vindicated—and, if so, which criteria, such as predictive novelty, explanatory coherence, or heuristic fertility, ought to govern admission to it. Third, whether accepting such a category would weaken the normative force of Popperian falsificationism in domains of long-run scientific forecasting, particularly in climate science or resource economics, where predictions span centuries.

As I have only one term to develop this into a substantial argument, I should be grateful for a defended agenda rather than a survey. Which of these lines of enquiry, or which others you consider more fundamental, deserves priority for my limited time? I am especially interested in your reasoning: which question is conceptually upstream of the others, which is most tractable for a student working without specialised archival resources, and which, if resolved, would most significantly alter how philosophers assess theories that are initially disconfirmed but later appear vindicated by long-run trends? Please respond in British English, and I do not require a bibliography or reading list—only a prioritisation supported by criteria such as importance, tractability, and downstream influence.
