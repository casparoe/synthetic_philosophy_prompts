At a philosophy discussion group, two members—Sam and Riley—disagreed over what Descartes's cogito actually proves. Sam argued that it establishes only the bare occurrence of a present thought, not an enduring thinker: one can genuinely say that thinking is happening now without committing to an "I," and Descartes illicitly moves from that impersonal fact to "I am a thinking thing" by letting grammar do metaphysical work. Riley replied that first-person awareness is structurally irreducible—without a subject to whom the thought belongs there is no way to distinguish this occurrence of thinking from any other—so the "I" is not smuggled in but is constitutive of the experience itself. Sam then pressed that even if one grants an instantaneous subject, the cogito offers no basis for attributing past or future thoughts to that same subject, so it fails to prove any temporal continuity and therefore cannot support the personal identity Descartes seems to assume. Riley countered that doubting is not instantaneous but a temporally extended mental act; the thinker who begins the doubt is already present through its completion, which gives internal duration without requiring a separate substantial self. Sam objected that this assumes exactly what needs proving—namely, that one continuous mental process requires one continuous owner rather than being a sequence of discrete mental events.
