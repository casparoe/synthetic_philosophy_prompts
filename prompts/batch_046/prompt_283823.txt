A conceptual research program has three years and funding for roughly four philosophers or formal epistemologists—not empirical scientists—to work on open problems surrounding the common prior assumption. The program is specifically interested in what happens when the assumption is relaxed in structured but non-idealized settings. Three candidate projects are under consideration, but resources are limited and the ordering matters.

Project A asks whether approximate agreement can still be derived when agents share an algorithmic prior (in the Solomonoff induction tradition) but face different computational resource bounds that prevent them from computing the same posterior approximations.

Project B asks for a dependency map: which results in epistemic game theory—particularly around bargaining, belief merging, and disagreement—genuinely require a common prior versus only requiring mutual knowledge of rationality or shared logical closure, and which results survive its relaxation.

Project C asks for normative criteria to distinguish cases where rational agents ought to treat divergent priors as errors in updating from a genuinely shared base, versus cases where the priors are non-commensurable in a way that makes shared prior talk conceptually inappropriate.

Your task: provide a defended prioritization across these three lines. Specify the criteria you are using—such as tractability without ML expertise, how many downstream conceptual problems depend on resolving each, and whether success would change practice in philosophy, economics, or AI alignment—defend the ranking, and identify the trade-offs between them. Further, indicate what specific theoretical result, formal counterexample, or conceptual distinction over the next two years would cause you to reorder the priorities. The deliverable is an agenda, not a survey, and should not include bibliographies, reading lists, or requests for citations.
