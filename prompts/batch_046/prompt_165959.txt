student answer:
St. Petersburg gives an infinite payout, which you can fix with a bounded utility curve like log(x). The Pasadena game is worse: you win 2^n/n on odd flips and lose it on even flips, and the series never converges because the terms blow up in magnitude. It's conditionally convergent, just like the alternating harmonic series, except the partial sums oscillate without settling, so rearranging terms can make the sum equal any real number you want.

The restricted summation fix separates the odd terms and even terms. The odd domain sums to positive infinity, the even domain to negative infinity. Then you apply a relative expectation measure across domains and they cancel, yielding a neutral value of zero. Colyvan proposed this domain-restriction strategy in his reply to Nover and Hájek, and it saves expected utility theory because once the domains are isolated, standard averaging works inside each one.

There's still a leftover problem: even after separation, both domains are infinite with opposite signs, so the theory can't rank the game against a safe bet. That means formal decision theory needs an extra normative rule for cross-domain comparison, not just a mathematical fix.
