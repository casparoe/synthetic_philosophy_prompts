I'm taking a seminar on infinity and physical possibility, and we've been working through Thomson's lamp. Rather than asking for the standard resolution, I want to test whether my verdicts across several modified supertasks hang together.

Case A: The classic lamp—toggled on and off at intervals 1/2, 1/4, 1/8, ...—after two minutes. I think its state is genuinely indeterminate: there is no fact of the matter whether it is on.

Case B: A countdown supertask that utters n, then n-1, ..., reaching "zero" exactly at the limit. I think this is not merely indeterminate but conceptually impossible—there is no coherent limit state.

Case C: A stochastic lamp, where each toggle is determined by an independent fair coin flip executed in half the remaining time. At the limit, I think there is a well-defined 50/50 probability distribution over on and off, not ambiguity.

Case D: A reward-structured lamp, where being on at the limit yields utility 1 and being off yields 0, but each toggle costs utility 2^{-(k+1)}, so the net expected utility converges. I think an agent should rationally undertake the sequence, and that the convergent utility calculus resolves the ambiguity.

Case E: A computational lamp that writes one deterministic bit per step according to a fixed algorithm. At the limit, I think there is a specific infinite binary output—determinacy holds—even though I deny determinacy in Case A.

Is there a single principle—say, about whether a well-defined evaluation function or topological limit exists for the state-space—that delivers verdicts A through E? If not, which of my verdicts should I overturn, and why? Please also apply your account to a variant I haven't seen discussed: a self-referential toggle rule in which step k toggles the lamp if and only if the lamp will be on at the limit. Does this generate a contradiction analogous to the liar paradox, or can it be assigned consistently?
