Exam question: In debates about vagueness, epistemicism (associated with Timothy Williamson) and supervaluationism (associated with Kit Fine) both deny that borderline cases are genuinely indeterminate in reality, but they do so in opposite directions. Explain (a) what epistemicism claims about the ontological and epistemic status of borderline cases for a predicate like "bald," including the role of the margin-for-error principle; (b) how supervaluationism uses admissible precisifications and the notion of supertruth to treat the same borderline cases; and (c) whether supervaluationism genuinely avoids sharp boundaries or merely relocates them, with attention to how each view treats bivalence and the law of excluded middle.

Student answer:
"Epistemicism treats vagueness as a kind of ignorance rather than a feature of language or reality. For any precise hair count, there is a fact of the matter about whether someone with that count is bald; the boundary is sharp but unknown to us because our knowledge is inexact. Williamson's margin-for-error principle says that if you know someone with n hairs is not bald, then anyone with n+1 hairs is also not bald according to your knowledge, since adjacent cases fall within your margin of error. This blocks the sorites reasoning not by denying any premise, but by showing that we are never in a position to know the premises that drive the paradox. So epistemicism preserves full classical logic: bivalence holds everywhere, and the law of excluded middle is never violated, even for borderline cases.

Supervaluationism, by contrast, denies that there is a hidden precise line. It introduces admissible precisifications—ways of sharpening "bald" that respect the clear cases. A statement is supertrue if true on every admissible precisification, superfalse if false on all, and in borderline cases it receives an intermediate, indeterminate truth value. Because of this, supervaluationism rejects bivalence for borderline statements and also rejects the law of excluded middle, since neither "John is bald" nor "John is not bald" comes out true.

On the relocation worry: supervaluationism does push sharpness into the metalanguage, because the set of admissible precisifications must itself be precisely delimited. If the criteria for admissibility are vague, we apply supervaluationism at a higher level, generating a benign hierarchy of supervaluations that Fine accepts. The epistemicist responds that this hierarchy is vicious: each level requires an unknown sharp boundary, confirming that vagueness is ultimately epistemic. So supervaluationism translates the sorites into a problem about admissibility conditions without solving it, whereas epistemicism dissolves the paradox by treating all vagueness as inexact knowledge."
