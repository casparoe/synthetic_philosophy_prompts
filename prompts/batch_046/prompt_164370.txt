I just came out of a long, heated argument with a friend about whether transformative experiences—like becoming a parent or undergoing a profound religious conversion—can be rationally chosen at all. I ended up defending a position stronger than the standard epistemic-limit view: I claimed that in genuinely transformative cases, the post-experience agent doesn't just lack information about a future utility state, but operates with an evaluative framework that is categorically incommensurable with the pre-experience agent's, which means standard expected-utility reasoning doesn't merely need supplementation—it structurally fails, and rational choice in these domains requires an agent-relative normativity that allows commitment without comparative evaluation. I want to stress-test this. What would actually count against it? I need the defeaters sorted by severity: empirical results in decision psychology that show people do successfully forecast post-transformation preferences; conceptual distinctions that dissolve the alleged incommensurability without collapsing into simple ignorance; formal results in decision theory showing that agent-relative norms can be reconstructed within a unified framework; and any consequence of my view—perhaps about moral responsibility for transformative choices—that turns out unacceptable. I also want to know which objections I should concede as genuinely damaging versus which I can dismiss as misreading the claim, and whether my position has been qualified into unfalsifiability—if so, is that a fatal flaw for a normative thesis about rational agency, or can it be defended?
