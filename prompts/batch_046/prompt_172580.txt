In a philosophy-of-language reading group focused on Twin Earth cases and semantic externalism, a colleague kept dismissing internalist objections with the slogan "meanings just ain't in the head," using it both to defend the Twin Earth argument for natural-kind terms and to deny that psychological states ever determine content. I want to know exactly what work that sentence can do. Can you separate the precise theses it might express—distinguishing, for example, claims about reference-fixing mechanisms, about conceptual or narrow content, about phenomenal character, about neural realization, and about social transmission—and for each say whether it is a near-truism, a defensible philosophical position, or false? In particular, identify which readings actually depend on the Twin Earth thought experiment (where molecular duplicates differ in water-thoughts), which extend to full causal or social externalism for all mental content, and which amount to empirical claims about brain states that cognitive science could reject without touching semantics. Also address whether there is a coherent moderate position—say, that meanings involve both narrow functional roles or modes of presentation and wide environmental relations—and whether that saves the slogan's intuition or betrays it. Finally, say which of these readings actually support the argumentative move she was making, and which are just rhetorical padding. I need a menu of disambiguated claims with verdicts, not a bibliography or a lecture on the history of externalism.
