I have come to hold a particular dissolution of the dogmatism paradox: rather than treating justification as a binary credential that is either present or forfeited, I think rational belief-retention should be modelled as a stake-indexed, continuous entitlement. On this view, an agent is permitted to maintain belief P despite apparently defeating evidence E when the expected epistemic cost of full revision—including cascading updates through an interconnected doxastic network, disruption to action-plans, and the risk of error in the revision procedure itself—exceeds the calibrated evidential weight of E, where the calibration coefficient varies by domain (e.g. theoretical physics versus social coordination). This is not a retreat to pragmatic encroachment in the usual sense; it is the claim that rationality for bounded agents with networked beliefs is structurally analogous to conservative portfolio management, and that the paradox only arises if we mistakenly model belief as an isolated, atomistic state that can be revised costlessly.

What would genuinely count against this position? I want the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward. Specifically:

- Which observations about actual human belief-revision behaviour, or which formal results from epistemic logic or belief-revision theory, would undermine the claim that revision costs are structurally relevant to entitlement?
- Are there possible cases—perhaps involving high-stakes moral beliefs or predictive models in engineering—where the framework predicts retention but intuition demands immediate abandonment, or vice versa?
- Does the position risk being qualified into unfalsifiability by allowing the agent to inflate revision costs or adjust domain stakes post hoc? If so, is that insulation a genuine defect for a normative account of this kind, or an unavoidable feature of any bounded-rationality framework?
- Which of these defeaters would defenders of the view themselves concede as damaging, and which would they dismiss as relying on the very atomistic model of belief the view rejects?
- Finally, is there a hidden tension between this conservative, network-sensitive approach and the rationalist emphasis on rapid belief updating in response to strong evidence—particularly in domains where being wrong carries existential or collective risk?

Please keep the analysis focused on the structural commitments rather than on surveying the historical literature.
