Having retired from three decades of experimental condensed-matter physics, I have spent the past several years in philosophy seminars, and I am now drafting the final examination for an upper-level graduate epistemology course. I want a single essay question on the Gettier problem that students cannot answer by reciting the standard counterexamples or by cataloguing post-Gettier conditions—reliabilism, safety, sensitivity, virtue epistemology, and so on. Instead, the question should present a constructed laboratory scenario: a researcher measures a fundamental constant using two redundant detectors. Detector A is calibrated according to an obsolete theoretical model that, by coincidence, applies the correct numerical adjustment factor; Detector B carries an opposing systematic error that exactly cancels A’s deviation. The researcher aggregates the outputs, obtains the true value, and holds a well-justified belief in it, yet neither her individual cognitive state nor the distributed instrumental process tracks truth in the manner that standard epistemologies require. The exam prompt should ask students to determine whether this constitutes a genuine Gettier case, whether the distribution of justification across instruments alters the analysis of knowledge, and whether any standard fourth condition can accommodate the scenario without collapsing into triviality or self-undermining circularity. Please provide: (1) the exam question itself, with explicit instructions, a recommended structure, and a word limit appropriate for a 90-minute response; (2) a model answer of approximately 600 words; (3) a marking scheme totaling 25 marks that names five specific philosophical moves an A-range answer must contain, assigns point values to each, and specifies the characteristic partial-credit error or misconception associated with each move. The assessment must not request bibliographies, author citations, page references, or reading lists; it should be fully answerable through conceptual reasoning alone.
