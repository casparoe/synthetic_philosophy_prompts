I'm preparing a feature on democratic legitimacy and want to audit my own premises before I commit to them in print. Here is my working profile on populism and the will of the people.

First, I treat "the will of the people" as a rhetorical fiction: there is no collective mental state or aggregated preference set that exists prior to political articulation. Second, I think populist challenges to institutional gatekeeping are democratically healthy when they expand participation, but pathological when they claim unmediated, direct access to a unitary popular will. Third, I hold that democratic legitimacy is purely procedural: an outcome is legitimate if produced by fair deliberation and voting, regardless of whether it tracks an antecedent desire or objective justice. Fourth, I also believe there are objective, non-procedural standards of justice, and that a democratically produced result can be illegitimate if it violates them—though I deny that experts have standing to override democratic outcomes on these grounds. Fifth, I reject any prerequisite of cultural homogeneity or national character for democracy; moreover, I think "the people" functions indexically in political discourse—its referent shifts with the speaker's position—so there is no cross-contextual identity condition for populist claims. Sixth, I believe empirical social science can determine, for any specific populist claim, whether it genuinely embodies the will of the people, yielding an objective fact about representation accuracy.
