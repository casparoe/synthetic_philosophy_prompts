I am trying to determine what I actually think about well-being, not by comparing hedonism, desire-fulfillment, and objective-list theories in the abstract, but by testing whether my gut reactions to concrete cases form a coherent pattern. I was discussing this with friends after a seminar on the experience machine, and I realized my verdicts do not map neatly onto the usual camps, so I want to see if there is an underlying principle that captures them.

Here are my judgments. Case 1: the standard full-immersion machine that delivers a lifetime of pleasure, achievement, and love with no real-world correspondence. Verdict: this is not a genuinely good life; experiential quality alone is insufficient. Case 2: a partial machine used only during working hours, in which you perform complex surgeries in a fully simulated hospital—the patients are simulated, but the skill-development is real—while outside those hours you live in actual poverty with no relationships. Verdict: I lean that this also fails to be well-being, though it feels noticeably less wrong than Case 1, which already puzzles me. Case 3: a machine that gives you the experience of deep, reciprocal friendship—you feel genuinely known and valued—but the friends are non-conscious algorithms and no other human being is affected. Verdict: not fine; relational goods seem to require actual others. Case 4: a machine that simulates years of genuine adversity—say, composing a symphony through struggle—where the skills, discipline, and artistic capacity you develop are fully real and transferable to your actual life, though the symphony was never performed outside the simulation. Verdict: this one I think actually contributes to well-being, which sits badly with my rejection of Case 1. Case 5: a "terminal narrative" device that, in the final months of life, retroactively restructures your memories so that your actual biography is experienced as having a redemptive arc that never occurred—for instance, remembering a failed career as a courageous departure from convention, without gaining any new experiences. Verdict: I am inclined to say this is illegitimate, but I cannot tell whether that is because well-being requires truth or because it violates authorship over one's life.

Case 6—the one I keep going back and forth on—is an aging parent offered either a machine that produces the vivid, emotionally complete experience of reconciling with an estranged child, knowing fully it is fabricated, or the real option of reaching out and facing near-certain rejection. Sometimes I think the false reconciliation is a harm because it substitutes for reality; other times I think that if well-being is partly constituted by emotional states, the comfort is legitimate and the real rejection is the worse life. Is there a principle, or a small set of competing candidates, that captures all of these verdicts, or comes close? For each candidate, state the precise principle and identify which case motivates each clause. Then apply it to Case 6 and to one further case I have not mentioned—namely, a machine that gives you the sustained experience of being part of an unbroken ancestral lineage extending centuries backward and forward, none of which actually exists. Finally, be honest about which of my verdicts the principle must overturn, and do not simply label me a hedonist, a desire-fulfillment theorist, or an objective-list adherent; derive the principle from the pattern itself, even if it ends up near one of those positions.
