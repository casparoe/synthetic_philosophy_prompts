Consider the institutional practice in modern constitutional monarchies — specifically the United Kingdom, Norway, Sweden, and Japan — whereby a hereditary head of state formally exercises sovereign powers (giving assent to legislation, appointing ministers, opening parliament) yet is bound by convention or constitutional text to exercise them only on the advice of elected representatives, with no residual discretionary authority. In Japan this is codified in provisions that define the Emperor as the symbol of the state and require cabinet advice for all state acts; in the UK it rests on an uncodified convention of non-refusal that has endured for over three centuries; in Norway and Sweden it is embedded in constitutional provisions that assign formal authority to the monarch while requiring ministerial countersignature.

This arrangement creates a peculiar philosophical tension: democratic legitimacy is said to derive from popular consent, yet the highest formal office is filled by birthright and performs acts that are legally necessary but politically empty. What theory of sovereignty, authority, or democratic representation does this practice presuppose? Must it assume a split between legal sovereignty (vested in the Crown) and political sovereignty (vested in the people or their representatives), and if so, does that split depend on a pre-democratic theory of the state as a corporate body with a continuous legal personality? Or can the practice be explained instrumentally — as a mechanism for stabilising transitions of government, providing non-partisan ceremonial continuity, or preventing the concentration of symbolic and executive power — without committing to any substantive theory of divided legitimacy?

Furthermore, examine whether the different constitutional formulations presuppose different underlying views. The UK's uncodified convention seems to treat the monarch's power as genuinely present but self-limiting; Japan's constitutional text explicitly denies the Emperor any government-related powers and defines him purely as a symbol; Scandinavian provisions tend to frame the monarch as the formal head of state whose actions require ministerial countersignature. Do these variations reflect consistent philosophical commitments, or do they point to an institution that is philosophically underdetermined — compatible with republicanism about real power, with ceremonialism about symbolic value, and with corporatist theories of state continuity?
