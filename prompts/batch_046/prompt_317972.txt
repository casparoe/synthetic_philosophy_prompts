For my graduate seminar on early modern political thought, I want a conceptual comparison of two doctrines in Pascal’s Pensées that are usually treated in isolation. First, the familiar synthesis of justice and force: because justice is disputable and therefore vulnerable, while force is undeniable and therefore dangerous when unchecked, the two must be joined—so that either justice is made strong or strength is made just. Second, the doctrine of the raison des effets: since human reason is too blunt an instrument to grasp true justice without crushing it, people in practice obey not justice itself but custom, authority, and the established order, which serves as a necessary functional substitute. Compare these two strands not as biographical curiosities but as competing accounts of political legitimacy. Is the justice-force synthesis best read as a normative prescription—that we ought to align institutional power with justice—or as a descriptive claim about how social order is actually stabilized? And if the raison des effets entails that true justice is epistemically inaccessible, does that undermine the normative aspiration of the synthesis, reducing it to the claim that we should accept whatever forceful order custom has made stable? Specifically, address whether Pascal can consistently maintain both that justice is genuinely disputable and that there remains a meaningful distinction between making the just strong and merely making the strong appear just. I want no survey of secondary literature or citation list; I want an analysis that traces the conceptual tension between epistemic humility about justice and the normative demand to marry justice to power.
