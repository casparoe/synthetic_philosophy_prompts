Consider the argument that the very framework used to blame Imperial Germany for the First World War proves far too much. (1) To hold a nation responsible for initiating a war, we must be able to attribute a culpable collective intention to it. (2) A collective intention requires either shared mental content among the relevant members or a democratic decision procedure that binds them; without either, we are merely distributing individual blame across a population that did not share the plan. (3) The Fischer controversy shows that Germany's decision for war in 1914 was made by a narrow elite—guided by strategic documents outlining expansive war aims drafted shortly after mobilization—without popular authorization or shared popular intent; the German people at large neither knew of nor endorsed these aims. (4) Therefore, if we insist on a voluntarist standard for collective blame, "Germany" is not responsible—only specific officials are. (5) But if we relax that standard and blame the nation regardless of democratic authorization—treating the state's actions as the nation's actions—then the same applies to any modern state with a hierarchical foreign-policy apparatus, including democracies where citizens never vote on specific military interventions. (6) Consequently, we either abandon national blame entirely (reducing all responsibility to individuals) or apply it indiscriminately across regime types, destroying the moral distinction between authoritarian and democratic war-making that the historiographical debate implicitly relies upon. (7) The argument seems to show that "holding a nation responsible" is either incoherent or so universally applicable that it becomes useless. Which step should be rejected? Is the error in the voluntarist premise (2), in the historical claim about elite decision-making (3), or in the extension to modern democracies (5)? Explain why that step looked plausible and what giving it up costs—does saving collective responsibility require embracing a non-voluntarist conception that makes democratic citizens routinely liable for wars they never chose, or does it depend on a regime-type distinction that ends up exculpating authoritarian states?
