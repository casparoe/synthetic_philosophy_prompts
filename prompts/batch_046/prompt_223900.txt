In debates over metaphysical fundamentality, philosophers often invoke a geological-layer analogy: bedrock entities—fundamental particles, properties, or structures—lie at the bottom, and everything else, from tables to moral facts, rests upon them through relations of metaphysical grounding, just as sedimentary rock rests upon older strata through processes of deposition. Proponents of a “thin” ontology use this image to license the claim that derivative entities are ontologically parasitic: they exist only in virtue of the bedrock, carry no independent explanatory weight, and could in principle be omitted from a complete inventory of reality, just as a geologist describing the bedrock need not separately catalog every sedimentary layer if she grasps the deposition processes. How far does this analogy actually hold? Provide an explicit correspondence table—what plays the role of bedrock, sediment, deposition, the geologist’s complete inventory, erosion, and so forth—then identify where the mapping breaks. In particular, consider whether grounding permits circular or non-well-founded dependence (the analog of sediment that somehow supports its own bedrock), whether derivative entities can exert downward influence on their grounds (the analog of erosion reshaping bedrock), and whether the analogy quietly imports a temporal or causal dimension that metaphysical grounding is usually said to lack. Separate the disanalogies that genuinely threaten the deflationary conclusion from those that do not. Does the claim that derivative entities can be omitted from a complete ontology survive once these breaks are on the table, or was the analogy carrying that conclusion all along? If there is a more accurate structural analogy for grounding relations, propose one and briefly defend its superiority.
