Write opening remarks (maximum 400 words) for a workshop titled "Choice Architecture in Plural Societies." You are the director addressing a split audience: social-choice theorists on one side, municipal practitioners and activists on the other, with little shared vocabulary between them.

Your remarks must do structural work with Sen's liberal paradox—the tension between minimal liberalism (each group decides over its own local domain) and Pareto efficiency (when all parties genuinely prefer cooperation to conflict, the collective outcome should reflect that). Do not present the paradox as an abstract puzzle to be solved, and do not resolve it. Instead, use it to undermine the practitioner intuition that "if we just decentralize authority and let each community choose its own rules, autonomy and harmony will naturally align." Construct a concrete two-group, two-option framing—say, local control over school curricula or neighborhood zoning—so that the theorists recognize the formal impossibility (no social decision rule can honor both minimal liberal rights and unanimous preference) while the practitioners feel the warning in institutional terms rather than as a philosophy lecture.

The practitioners should leave understanding that decentralization is not automatically self-implementing, even when every group sincerely wants peace. The theorists should hear the structure without being patronized. Mention Amartya Sen only if it arises naturally; do not cite papers, do not invoke other authors, and do not provide a bibliography or reading list. Keep the tone welcoming but intellectually serious, and close with a single framing question that turns the paradox into the workshop's practical task: how should we design institutions when the two commitments we value most—respecting local choice and honoring collective preference—can pull in opposite directions?
