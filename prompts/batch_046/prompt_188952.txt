As a journalist who writes about the architecture of scientific reasoning, I have been invited to lead a forty-minute guided discussion for a small group of urban-planning professionals and longtime readers of my newsletter—people accustomed to navigating conflicting regulations, but with no formal background in logic beyond what they remember from high school. The topic I want them to genuinely understand, rather than merely tolerate as an eccentricity, is dialetheism together with paraconsistent logic: specifically, the idea that some contradictions might be true in the world rather than merely apparent in our descriptions of it, and that reasoning need not collapse into triviality when it encounters them. I am not asking you to lecture me on the subject itself; I am asking for a teaching plan that maps the dependency structure of the explanation—which conceptual pieces must be laid down before dialetheism can sound like a philosophical position rather than a simple mistake—together with the single concrete scenario that should carry the entire session.

For the carrying example, I am thinking of constructing a hypothetical but realistic case I will call the Meridian Block registry: a single parcel of urban land that, through two independently valid and non-revocable municipal amendments, is simultaneously designated as exclusively residential and exclusively commercial, with no hierarchical authority available to break the tie and no hidden ambiguity in the language of either ordinance. I need to know what this example must possess—and what it must deliberately lack—in order to serve as a genuine illustration of a true contradiction rather than a clerical error, a vague predicate, or a resolvable conflict of norms. Alongside the arc, I want a misconception map: the audience will likely arrive thinking that contradiction is always a signal of error, that accepting both P and not-P automatically licenses belief in every proposition, and that paraconsistency is merely a fancy word for being sloppy; I need to know exactly where in the sequence each of these should be intercepted and dissolved. I also need a precise check for understanding—a single question or brief exercise at the end—that would reveal whether they have grasped that the point is controlling the explosion of inference, not celebrating confusion. Finally, please say explicitly what must be omitted at this level—formal semantics of LP logic, Gödel numbering, detailed comparisons with relevance logic—and how I should acknowledge that omission without making it sound like a failure of rigor.

The session should be built so that consistency is first introduced as a practical norm they already respect in zoning law, then shown to be strained by the Meridian case, then suspended in a controlled way; only after they feel the pressure of the contradiction should the distinction between contradiction-as-world-feature and contradiction-as-reasoning-failure be drawn. Please lay out the order in which these moves must occur, why each depends on the previous, how the example should be presented step by step, and what I should refuse to explain even if someone asks, together with the language I should use to refuse it gracefully.
