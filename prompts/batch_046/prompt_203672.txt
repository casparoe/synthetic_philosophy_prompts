You are serving as a consultant in the philosophy of history and comparative sociology, with particular expertise in the logic of counterfactual and contrastive explanation. Before offering your assessment, please take a few moments to unpack any hidden presuppositions embedded in the framing below, and consider whether the difficulty lies in the historical evidence or in the conceptual architecture of the question itself. I am especially interested in whether certain demands we place on historical explanations—demands that might be appropriate in experimental contexts—are being illegitimately imported into a domain where only actual-sequence counterfactuals are available.

I have been working through debates on the Great Divergence and what is sometimes called the Needham question—namely, why sustained, self-reinforcing industrial modernity consolidated in parts of Northwestern Europe rather than in the economically and technologically advanced regions of late imperial China. Many prominent accounts point to asymmetrical difference-makers: the spatial coincidence of coal and iron near British manufacturing centers, the particular architecture of British patent and capital-market institutions, or the political fragmentation of Europe versus the unified imperial state of Qing China. What puzzles me is whether these can genuinely answer a contrastive question—“why Britain and not China?”—unless they satisfy a stricter modal condition. It seems to me that for such a contrast to be well-posed, the cited factor must not merely be present in Britain and absent in China, but must be genuinely decisive in both directions: had Shanxi coal been geographically repositioned near the Yangtze manufacturing belt, Qing China should have industrialized, and had British coal been absent or inaccessible, Britain should have stagnated rather than adapting through trade or alternative energy regimes. Since historians often grant that Britain might have imported coal or developed different technological paths, and that Chinese coal was present but differently situated, I wonder whether the problem is that we are demanding a form of counterfactual symmetry—something like an interventionist test of causal relevance—that historical explanation cannot legitimately provide. Is my insistence on this bidirectional symmetry a subtle confusion about what contrastive explanation requires in history, or does the Great Divergence literature genuinely fail to answer its own framing question?
