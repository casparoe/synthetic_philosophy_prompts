Please construct a defense of the acts-and-omissions distinction as it applies to autonomous decision systems, without grounding the distinction in human bodily movement or conscious intent. Argue that "allowing" is best understood structurally: it occurs whenever an agent—whether human or artificial—fails to redirect a causal pathway that is running independently of its present intervention, whereas "doing" requires the agent to become a necessary node in the pathway that produces the outcome. Apply this specifically to a fully automated system that continues along its pre-programmed trajectory without human override: defend the claim that its failure to prevent harm should be classified as allowing rather than doing, even when the system's design makes the harmful outcome highly probable and real-time human intervention is practically impossible. Then address the strongest objection, namely that because every parameter of the system was originally set by human designers, all outputs ultimately trace back to human doing and the distinction collapses; show why this genealogical point does not erase the structural difference at the operational moment, and clarify what conditions, if any, would turn the system's inaction into genuine commission.
