Next week I present my paper, "When Myth Becomes Object: A Criterion for the Legend-to-History Transition," at the state interscholastic humanities symposium. My thesis is that a legend becomes history not upon archaeological corroboration, but upon the loss of what I term "mythic efficacy"—that is, the narrative's active functioning as a living framework for collective identity, ethics, or political legitimacy—such that it becomes available exclusively for critical historiographic treatment rather than for devotional, legal, or communal deployment. I apply this to three comparative cases. First, the Trojan War: once an epic explanatory framework for Greek civic identity, it was transformed—particularly after Schliemann's excavations and subsequent Bronze Age archaeology—into a contested historical horizon evaluated by material correlation and stratigraphic evidence. Second, the Arthurian corpus: migrating from Welsh resistance mythology through medieval romance and pseudo-chronicle into archaeological speculation, it has now lost mythic efficacy for all but fringe nationalist groups, and thus operates purely as historiographic and literary object. Third, the Exodus: despite comparable archaeological ambiguity, I argue it remains in a liminal state because it retains active mythic efficacy within living religious and ethical communities, meaning its evidentiary negotiation still occurs partly within theological rather than purely historiographic frameworks.

The audience will be a three-person judging panel: a university ancient historian specializing in the Eastern Mediterranean Bronze Age, who treats absence of material evidence as methodologically decisive; a philosopher of religion familiar with narrative-constructivist historiography, who will likely claim my distinction between "mythic efficacy" and "historical objecthood" collapses because all history is narratively constructed; and a high school literature teacher who regards every foundational text—epic, romance, or scripture—as equally fictive and therefore resists any framework that appears to grant religious narratives special epistemic status. There will also be a brief open question period with fellow student presenters, including one peer who has read the minimalist-maximalist debate on Exodus and will press me on whether my framework implicitly privileges secular historiography.

Prepare me for the objections in the order they are most likely to arrive, phrased precisely as each panelist or peer would phrase them.
