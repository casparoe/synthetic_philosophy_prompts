I serve on the editorial board of a mathematics journal that has recently required all submissions using logical argumentation to declare their constructive status, and because our reviewers come from different traditions — Bishop-style constructive mathematics, Brouwer-style intuitionism that permits choice sequences and bar induction, and the Russian recursive school that accepts Markov's principle — I need a single reusable decision procedure that I can run on any paper's central proof without having to master every author's background. Please turn the core commitments of intuitionistic logic into an ordered sequence of practical tests that a non-specialist referee can apply: first, check whether every existence claim is accompanied by an explicit construction or only by a proof that non-existence is absurd; second, check whether every disjunction specifies which disjunct holds or merely excludes a middle option; third, check for hidden uses of the law of excluded middle embedded in case distinctions, particularly where a proof divides into cases that are not constructively exhaustive; fourth, check whether any infinite search is justified by an explicit algorithm or instead smuggles in Markov's principle, and if the latter is present, flag whether the author's declared school permits it; fifth, identify points where the argument relies on impredicative definitions that assume the totality of a collection being defined, and mark these as requiring human judgment about whether the circularity is benign or vicious. For each test, describe what counts as a clear pass, what counts as a clear failure that stops the procedure with a rejection of constructive validity, and what must be handed back to a senior reviewer because it involves a value choice about which constructivist school is authoritative rather than a formal mistake. Write the entire procedure in continuous prose, without headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, as if you were explaining the workflow in a long letter to a colleague who will actually use it. Then run the procedure on three hypothetical submissions: one offering a bisection-based proof that a continuous real function on a closed interval attains a root, constructing the point at each stage; one offering a number-theoretic result proved solely by showing that its negation leads to contradiction, followed by an implicit appeal to double-negation elimination to extract the object; and one that applies Markov's principle to a computable property of natural numbers in order to claim that either the property holds everywhere or there is a specific counterexample, without the author having declared whether they work in Bishop's framework or in the Russian school. Make sure that at least one of these applications produces an answer that a strict Bishop-style reviewer would reject but that the procedure itself cannot resolve without a human decision about school affiliation, and say exactly where that breakdown occurs. Finally, be explicit about what the procedure cannot accomplish — for example, whether it can adjudicate between schools, whether it can detect every hidden non-constructive step, or whether it replaces rather than supplements expert judgment — and do not include any bibliography, reading list, or citations.
