Two fellow doctoral students in my distributive-justice seminar argued over coffee about whether prioritarianism genuinely evades the leveling-down objection. (1) One claimed the standard reply succeeds: prioritarian weights apply only to benefits, so destroying goods enjoyed solely by the well-off—without benefiting anyone—cannot be required, and the objection confuses welfare equality with resource equality. (2) The other replied that this misses a structural feature of continuous prioritarian welfare functions with diminishing marginal weights: for any sufficiently high welfare level, there exists a local range in which reducing the well-off person’s welfare by a small amount epsilon, with no compensating gain elsewhere, raises aggregate social value, because the weight-reduction effect dominates the welfare-loss effect. (3) The first student objected that prioritarianism orders complete outcomes, not isolated local perturbations; without a beneficiary, the social ordering should not shift, and her opponent is illicitly applying calculus to a discrete ethical framework. (4) The second student countered that unless prioritarianism builds in an explicit “no harm without benefit” side-constraint, the weighting function itself generates leveling-down incentives at the margin, and that appealing to “complete outcomes” merely asserts non-separability without defending it. Please adjudicate move by move: rate each of the four moves from clearly mistaken to clearly correct and briefly justify each rating. In particular, does the mathematical form of standard prioritarian weighting entail local leveling-down incentives, or does the appeal to outcome-level comparison block them? And what are both parties missing about how this tension interacts with population-ethical extensions of prioritarianism?
