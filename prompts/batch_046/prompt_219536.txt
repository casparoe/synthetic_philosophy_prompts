Design a 90-minute classroom activity for a graduate seminar of 12–15 students who have read only a brief primer on Leibniz, not the Theodicy itself. The goal is to make participants feel why the claim “this world is the best of all possible worlds” functions less as an optimistic slogan and more as a structural design constraint they keep violating when they try to improve things locally. Inject a slightly niche framing: treat each possible world not as a static description but as a “modal package” in which every feature carries a “compossibility price”—if you add or remove X, you must list every other feature that becomes impossible in that same package.

Divide students into four “World Councils” of 3–4 members. Give each council an identical design brief: construct one possible world that maximizes some unified value (they choose whether this is aggregate welfare, virtue, knowledge, or aesthetic richness) under three hard rules. First, the world must contain free rational agents capable of genuine error, conflict, and suffering; second, it must run on a single, exceptionless system of natural laws with no ad hoc miracles; third, every proposed feature must be listed with its “compossibility set”—the other features it can and cannot coexist with in the same world.

Phase 1 (20 minutes): Each council builds its world, then writes down three “local repairs”—small, targeted removals of suffering or injustice that seem to leave everything else intact.

Phase 2 (35 minutes): Introduce the “Adjacency Challenge.” Councils exchange worlds. Each council receives another council’s world and must apply one of that world’s local repairs. But they must do so formally: they erase the targeted suffering, then, using the compossibility lists, strike out every feature that can no longer exist in that package, and identify which natural laws must be rewritten or broken to make the repair work. They score the modified world on the same value metric they originally chose. Crucially, they may not invent hidden miracles or change the value metric mid-game.

Phase 3 (15 minutes): Councils present one modified world to the room. The group votes, not on which world is “best” in some absolute sense, but on whether any single local repair produced a world that was unambiguously better on the council’s own metric. They must defend their vote by citing only internal features and ripple effects, not appeals to divine intention.

Debrief (20 minutes): Map Phase 1 onto the intuitive pull of local improvement; Phase 2 onto Leibniz’s claim that goods and evils are law-entangled and that “compossibility” is a binding constraint, not a decorative one; Phase 3 onto the problem that comparing total worlds requires a shared metric that may not exist across different value frameworks. Be explicit about what the activity reproduces well—the experience that fixing one evil seems to unravel a network of goods—and what it cannot reproduce, which the facilitator must supply: Leibniz’s specific argument that a global maximum exists and is unique, and the theological premise that God’s choice is constrained by reason alone rather than by arbitrary will. Note what will predictably fail: some groups will try to smuggle in hidden miracles; others will collapse into incommensurable value disputes; the facilitator should have a “no miracles” enforcement card ready, and should be prepared to stop a vote deadlock by asking whether the disagreement is about the world or about the metric.

Finally, say what would change your assessment of whether this activity succeeds: which specific failure in the debrief—say, if participants consistently report that the “ripple effect” felt contrived rather than discovered—would convince you that the compossibility framing obscures rather than illuminates Leibniz’s point, and identify the weakest link in the reasoning that connects the game mechanics to the metaphysical claim.
