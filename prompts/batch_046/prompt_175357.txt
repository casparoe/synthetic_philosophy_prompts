Please explain the conceptual tension at the heart of Amartya Sen's liberal paradox, with particular attention to a refinement that is not part of the standard exposition. The paradox, in its canonical form, demonstrates that a minimal liberal condition—granting each individual decisive sovereignty over a personal pair of alternatives—can be incompatible with the Pareto principle, even when every individual respects liberal values. Now consider a conceptual modification: suppose that sovereignty is not binary but graded by preference intensity, so that an individual's claim over a choice within their personal sphere is weighted by how strongly they hold that preference, and collective aggregation can override a weak personal claim if a sufficient weight of contrary preference exists elsewhere. Does this intensity-sensitive formulation dissolve the logical contradiction that Sen identifies, or does it merely displace the conflict from the level of rights assignment to the level of preference aggregation? In addressing this, distinguish the formal structure of Sen's proof—which depends on the discrete, non-comparable nature of rights—from the normative coherence of treating liberty as a continuous, quantifiable good. Conclude by assessing whether such a graded conception of personal sovereignty preserves the normative force of liberalism or collapses it into a form of utilitarian calculus.
