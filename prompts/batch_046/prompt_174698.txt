Make the case that the dominant explanations of why we enjoy tragic art—catharsis, the safety or control afforded by fictionality, and meta-emotional satisfaction—rest on a false dichotomy: they assume that the negative affect tragedy produces must either be purged, bracketed, or sublimated into a separate positive response. Argue instead that tragic enjoyment arises because the sorrow and the pleasure are not two competing responses but a single unified experience structured by what you should call retroactive inevitability tracking. On this view, the audience continuously updates its model of the characters' practical reasoning during the drama; when the catastrophe arrives, the viewer experiences grief at the outcome simultaneously with a felt epistemic closure—the recognition that the outcome was necessary given the commitments already established. Crucially, claim that this closure is not an intellectual add-on but is phenomenologically fused with the grief itself, so that weakening either component destroys the enjoyment. Defend this against the charge that it merely rebrands the meta-response account, and show that it yields a distinctive prediction: tragedies with highly predictable plots but thin characterization should fail to move us, while tragedies whose outcomes are surprising in prospect but rigorously justified in retrospect should succeed, which runs contrary to both catharsis and control-based explanations. Do not invoke bibliographies or citation lists; develop the argument conceptually.
