I am trying to formalize an objection to the standard concessionary interpretation of Maimonides’ philosophy of worship, and I have ended up with an argument that seems to prove far too much. Here are the steps.

(1) On the concessionary reading, animal sacrifice has no intrinsic religious value; it was permitted solely as a pedagogical accommodation to the Israelites’ psychological dependence on concrete, sensory worship and on practices structurally similar to those of surrounding idolatrous peoples.

(2) The principle underlying this accommodation is that whenever a religious practice is justified solely by reference to human cognitive, affective, or imaginative limitations—rather than by its direct contribution to intellectual perfection or to knowledge of God—it is provisional and ought to be superseded once those limitations are addressed.

(3) Prayer, as Maimonides describes it, involves bodily posture, vocal articulation, directed attention, and an imaginative orientation toward a personal deity; these are precisely the kinds of sensory and corporeal mediations that characterize concessionary practices, since pure intellectual worship requires neither speech nor physical orientation.

(4) The study and transmission of Torah also depend essentially on material texts, sensory perception of written characters, and the physical operations of memory and cognition—mediations that are, on the intellectualist account, external to the essence of knowing God.

(5) So, by (1) and (2), both prayer and Torah study should be classified as concessionary practices that ought to be abandoned in favor of uninterrupted intellectual contemplation, just as sacrifice was meant to be superseded by prayer and study themselves.

(6) Yet Maimonides treats prayer as a permanent, biblically commanded obligation and regards Torah study not as a temporary scaffold but as the highest form of worship.

(7) Therefore, either Maimonides applies the concessionary principle inconsistently, or the principle, if applied consistently, dissolves the entire framework of commanded religious practice into an unattainable pure intellectualism that eliminates Judaism as a lived tradition.

(8) The latter conclusion is clearly not Maimonides’ view, so the argument must fail somewhere.

My suspicion is that the overreach occurs between (2) and (3)—specifically, in treating any psychological or sensory mediation as sufficient for concessionary status, without distinguishing between practices that are merely instrumental to intellectual ends and those that are constitutive of them. But I am unsure whether repairing the slide saves prayer without also saving sacrifice, or whether I must instead deny (4) by arguing that Torah study is uniquely non-mediational in a way that prayer is not. Which step should be rejected, why did it look plausible, and what does giving it up cost the concessionary reading? If I must choose, I would prefer to preserve (1) and (6) over (2) or (4).
