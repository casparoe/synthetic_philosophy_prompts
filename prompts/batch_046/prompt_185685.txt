Next month I defend, in a bioethics seminar, the thesis that John Taurek's argument in Should the Numbers Count? — that in direct rescue conflicts the fact that more lives are at stake does not by itself justify saving the larger group — undermines not merely the conclusion but the conceptual coherence of QALY-maximizing pandemic triage protocols, because such protocols treat individual lives as fungible units summed across a population rather than as separate, non-aggregable claims. The audience is split: utilitarian public-health ethicists who designed COVID-19 allocation frameworks; contractualist moral philosophers who accept Scanlon-style criticisms of Taurek; and clinicians who view philosophical abstraction as an obstacle to saving lives. Give me the objections in the order they are likely to come, each phrased as that kind of interlocutor would actually phrase it — not as generic philosophical counterarguments — with a note on what I should concede, what I should hold firm on, and where my reply is weakest. Identify the single weakest point in my own position that I should prepare to defend rather than hope they overlook. Finally, include the objection I am hoping nobody raises — specifically, whether my view allows a single influential patient to block resources for hundreds — and sketch a two-minute reply that avoids sounding callous.
