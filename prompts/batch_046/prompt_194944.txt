I am preparing a seminar on testimony and the epistemology of narrative, and I should like to use Yann Martel's Life of Pi as a case study that complicates the standard reading of Hume's argument against miracles. Most treatments of Hume's essay Of Miracles concentrate on the claim that uniform experience establishing a law of nature always outweighs testimony for its violation; yet Martel's novel presents a situation in which the miraculous account — Pi's survival with a Bengal tiger — is not rejected on evidential grounds but is actively preferred by some listeners as the more satisfying narrative, despite violating the investigators' uniform expectations about shipwreck behaviour.

I would like a reading that takes both Hume's probabilistic calculus and the novel's narrative architecture seriously: does Pi's dual account expose a limitation in Hume's framework, particularly his assumption that belief in extraordinary events is governed solely by weighing testimony against prior probabilities? Please examine the scene in which Pi asks the Japanese officials which version they prefer, and consider whether the appeal to the story with the tiger introduces criteria — aesthetic coherence, moral palatability, or narrative completeness — that Hume's account of testimony cannot accommodate without distortion. At the same time, I want the reading to remain faithful to the novel's own ambivalence: it should not reduce the text to a mere illustration of epistemology, but should ask whether Martel dramatises something about human credulity that Hume describes yet fails to explain — namely, why people are sometimes drawn to the very accounts that their experience should lead them to reject. If you find that particular scenes resist a Humean interpretation, say so explicitly, and indicate what the work adds to the philosophical debate that a purely argumentative treatment of miracles and testimony misses.
