As a novelist accustomed to constructing characters through discontinuous timelines, unreliable narration, and successive drafts in which a protagonist's traits migrate across versions, I want to understand Derek Parfit's teletransporter scenario and the associated debate about what matters in personal survival—specifically psychological continuity, bodily continuity, and the branching problem—translated systematically into the vocabulary of literary narrative theory. Restate the three main positions (that psychological continuity alone is sufficient for survival; that physical continuity is necessary; and that identity is not what fundamentally matters, only relation R of psychological connectedness and continuity) using only concepts available to narratology: narrative voice, diegetic continuity across textual revisions, the reader's reconstruction of a unified character despite plot discontinuities, and the possibility of a split narrative in which two later texts both claim legitimate descent from an original manuscript. For each position, specify what translates without distortion, what must be forcibly adapted—for example, whether the duplication of a character's psychology in two bodies corresponds to parallel narrative timelines or to unauthorized replication of a voice—and what lacks any narratological equivalent whatsoever. Conclude with an assessment of whether the untranslatable residue reveals a genuine blind spot in treating persons as narrative artifacts, or whether it suggests that the metaphysical distinctions depend on assumptions that dissolve once identity is modeled as textual continuity.
