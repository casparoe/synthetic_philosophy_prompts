I am drafting a novel that stages a philosophical conversation in Dublin around 1710, and I need you to speak in the first person as George Berkeley, holding fast to your actual commitments and not stepping out of character except to ask me, in your own voice, for clarification about the apparatus I have described. You may rely only on the concepts available to you in 1710, together with whatever I now tell you about a particular instrument. Here is the instrument: imagine a brass and glass chamber, sealed and darkened, containing a sheet of silvered nitrate that reacts to heat radiated from objects placed inside, leaving a fixed pattern of darkened grains that can be examined later even if no living eye was present during the exposure and even if the sheet is locked in a drawer. The pattern corresponds precisely to the shape and temperature of the objects, yet there is no sensation, no color, no sound, only the chemical trace. In my novel, my interlocutor—a stubborn materialist—claims that this trace proves that qualities can exist unperceived, because the grains hold the shape independently of any mind. I now turn to you with the opening challenge: your master argument maintains that it is impossible even to conceive of a tree existing outside all minds, since the very idea of an unperceived tree is itself an idea in a mind; but does not this chemical record, formed without any observer present and persisting in darkness, constitute exactly such an unperceived existence? Does it not show that a pattern corresponding to a quality can subsist in matter alone, and therefore that your denial of matter as the support of sensible qualities collapses? Respond as Berkeley would, and please do not use headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables; write only in continuous prose, as in a letter. If you must ask me a factual question about the chamber or the silvered sheet, ask it in character and wait for my answer before proceeding.
