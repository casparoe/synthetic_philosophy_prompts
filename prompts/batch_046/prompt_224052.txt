For a consistency audit before a seminar, I hold that (1) personal identity is constituted by psychological continuity—causal connections of memory, intention, and rational agency—and does not require bodily or phenomenal continuity; (2) gradual replacement of neural tissue with functionally equivalent synthetic substrates preserves this continuity step-by-step, so a fully uploaded mind could be identical to the original; (3) phenomenal consciousness depends on a specific biological substrate mechanism, and synthetic equivalents, however behaviorally and cognitively perfect, are phenomenally blank; (4) a phenomenally blank being lacks the unified subjective perspective required to be the subject of psychological continuity, so once synthetic tissue exceeds a critical proportion, identity is broken; and (5) a sufficiently gradual replacement, never exceeding a small annual fraction, can reach one hundred percent synthetic tissue without ever crossing that critical proportion, thereby preserving identity throughout. Which commitments genuinely conflict? In particular, does (5) implicitly require that a fully synthetic system remain below the threshold, contradicting (4)? Does (4) undermine (1)'s claim that phenomenal continuity is unnecessary for identity? Is the tension between (2) and (3) only apparent, since functional equivalence need not entail phenomenal equivalence? Rank the tensions by severity, identify for each the least costly revision, and say which single change would clean up the most problems at once.
