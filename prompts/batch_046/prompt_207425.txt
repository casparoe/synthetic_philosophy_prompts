I have spent the past year supporting a partner through progressive cognitive decline, which has left me unusually preoccupied with whether an identity can survive being fragmented or partially hosted elsewhere. Recently I returned to Douglas Hofstadter's speculation that an "I" might persist in distributed form across other brains, and I began to wonder how a philosopher who grounded personal identity in consciousness and memory—rather than in any fixed substance—would respond to technologies that make such distribution concrete. Consider a near-future neural bridge that links two subjects so that their working-memory buffers and episodic traces become bidirectionally accessible in real time, generating an emergent joint-attention module that neither brain fully contains on its own. If one subject dies, the bridge preserves a subset of those shared traces within the survivor's augmented cortex, allowing the survivor to sincerely recall experiences that originated in the deceased partner—without claiming to be that partner.

Given John Locke's account that personal identity consists in the sameness of consciousness extended over time through appropriated memory, rather than in the continuity of an immaterial soul, how do you think he would classify the survivor's relation to the deceased? Would he regard the pair during life as a genuinely distributed single person, as two persons with an unusually porous boundary, or as two distinct identities that merely exchanged mental contents? Please indicate which elements of Locke's framework—his distinction between human being and person, his criterion of consciousness-continuity, or his treatment of memory ownership—he would likely invoke, and state how confident you are in each attribution; specifically, identify the point about which you are least sure.
