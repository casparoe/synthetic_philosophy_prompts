I'm preparing a podcast interview with an unnamed philosopher who defends a Strawsonian reactive-attitudes account of moral responsibility, but with a specific bifurcation: she separates first-person interpersonal reactive attitudes—resentment, gratitude—from third-person institutional reactive attitudes—systemic indignation, collective approval—and argues that the latter can be fully appropriate even when no single individual is personally blameworthy. She applies this distinction to structural injustice, treating institutional blame as directed at social arrangements rather than at interchangeable agents.

Draft eight to ten interview questions that avoid asking her to restate the basic framework. Include at least one question pressing how the boundary between interpersonal and institutional reactive attitudes can be drawn without circular reliance on prior judgments of blame; one on how her model handles moral-luck cases in which serious harm results from collectively produced but individually innocent actions; and one on whether the objective attitude Strawson describes is ever fitting toward powerful individuals in virtue of the institutional roles they occupy rather than in virtue of personal character. For each of these three, provide a follow-up keyed to the reply she is most likely to give—specifically, that the distinction tracks differences in social practice rather than in underlying metaphysics. Also include a question that raises the standard cultural-variability objection to reactive-attitudes theories and anticipates her defense that variation in practices reveals different norms of accountability rather than undermining them. End with one question asking what empirical finding from social psychology or neuroscience would genuinely threaten her bifurcated model, as opposed to merely complicating its application.
