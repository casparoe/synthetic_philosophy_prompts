I am preparing a workshop contribution on moral psychology and normative ethics, and I want an internal consistency audit of the framework I plan to defend. My commitments are as follows.

First, I accept the situationist critique in its strongest empirical form: broad, cross-situational character traits—such as global dispositions to honesty or courage—lack predictive validity, and the personality-psychology literature undermines the characterological reading of virtue ethics.

Second, despite this, I remain a normative virtue ethicist centered on eudaimonia, and I retain a modified unity-of-virtues thesis: within any specific domain of social practice, practical wisdom integrates the virtues relevant to that domain, so that genuine possession of one virtue there requires possession of the others that belong there.

Third, I propose that virtue should be reconstructed not as a global personality structure but as embedded practical expertise: a stable, domain-specific competence that integrates perception, affect, and action within a particular practice-world, such as clinical judgment, judicial reasoning, or craft apprenticeship.

Fourth, moral evaluation should stay aretaic rather than deontic or consequentialist, but its object should be the quality of an agent's embedded practical expertise in the domains they actually inhabit, not a global moral rating.

Fifth, moral education should therefore take the form of situated apprenticeship within specific practices, rather than abstract universal principle-learning or generic character formation.

Sixth, I maintain that an agent may possess full embedded practical expertise in one domain while lacking it in another, and that this boundary does not necessarily indicate hypocrisy or moral fragmentation, but rather the normal limits of cultivated skill.

These commitments seem to me largely compatible, but I see three potential fault lines. One: if global traits are psychologically unreal, it is unclear how practical wisdom can integrate virtues within a domain in a way that preserves any substantive unity thesis rather than collapsing into a mere cluster of local habits. Two: if evaluation is aretaic but there are no global traits, the object of evaluation risks reducing to the discrete, context-driven actions that situationists emphasize, which would defeat the purpose of an aretaic framework. Three: the apprenticeship model in (fifth) assumes that stable dispositions can be cultivated through repeated practice in a community, yet if situationist dynamics are as powerful as (first) suggests, it is unclear why situated training should be more reliable than general education in producing stable expertise.

I suspect that at least one of these worries dissolves upon inspection—for instance, that the absence of global traits does not eliminate a unified object of evaluation so long as the domain itself provides structural integration. But I am unsure which tensions are real and which are illusory. Please audit the set: identify which pairs are genuinely in tension, which only appear to be, and rank the real tensions by severity. For each, specify the least costly revision—whether surrendering the modified unity thesis, the domain-specific reconstruction of virtue, the aretaic structure of evaluation, or the apprenticeship model—and state precisely what that surrender costs the overall framework. I am not asking for a literature survey; I want the logical architecture of my own position traced.
