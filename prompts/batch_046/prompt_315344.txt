I'm drafting an argument for my history seminar that historians are inescapably presentist: the conceptual categories required to explain past societies—such as structural injustice or embodied agency—are irreducibly modern, so any attempt to evaluate the past solely through its own vocabulary either collapses into incoherence or abandons critical judgment. I also claim that present moral frameworks can sometimes disclose normative commitments that historical actors enacted but could not articulate themselves, which I call normative transparency. What would genuinely count against this combined view? I want more than the standard objections about anachronism or empathy-based recovery of past meanings. Specifically: which well-known historical interpretations or documented cases would undermine the claim that modern categories are necessary for coherent explanation? Which distinctions within historiography—say, between descriptive framing and normative evaluation, or between weak and strong hermeneutic asymmetry—would have to collapse for my position to fail? Could theoretical results in the philosophy of history, such as arguments for diachronic conceptual continuity or for the autonomy of past moral worlds, serve as decisive defeaters, or only awkward ones? Finally, has my view risked unfalsifiability by treating all conceptual framing as inevitable presentism, and if so, is that insulation a theoretical virtue or a fatal weakness? Please rank the potential defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and indicate which ones a committed defender of this position would concede as damaging versus which they would dismiss.
