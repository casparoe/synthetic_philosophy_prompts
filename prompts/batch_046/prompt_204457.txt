I am preparing a handout for a graduate seminar in Indian epistemology, and I need a decision tree—structured as a branching taxonomy with yes/no or choose-one nodes—that classifies stances within the Nyāya tradition and its modern reconstructions on how many pramāṇas (valid means of cognition) there are and whether they are irreducible.

The tree must begin with a root node framed around a concrete synthetic scenario rather than a label: a subject knows that an object is absent not by inferring it from positive marks, but through what we might call a counterfactual perceptual gap—the subject perceives every condition that would necessarily accompany the object if present, notices that one such condition is missing, and thereby cognizes absence without performing an explicit inference. The root question should ask whether such absence-cognition (anupalabdhi) can ever be direct and non-inferential in this way.

From there, the branches should cover, in order: whether this cognition is reducible to anumāna (inference), to śabda (testimony), or to an expanded pratyakṣa (perception); whether comparison (upamāna) is an independent pramāṇa or reducible to perception plus testimony; whether presumption (arthāpatti) is admitted and, if so, whether it is reducible to inference; and whether the classic four-fold scheme is exhaustive.

Every internal node must be phrased as a concrete epistemic case or claim that a student can answer without already knowing the traditional names of the positions. The tree should have roughly eight to ten decision nodes.

The leaves must include at least: classical Nyāya (four irreducible pramāṇas, absence reduced to inference); Bhāṭṭa Mīmāṃsā (six, with anupalabdhi and arthāpatti as independent); Advaita Vedānta (six, but with anupalabdhi grounded differently); a modern reductionist view that collapses upamāna and eliminates arthāpatti; and any other named or unnamed combination your logical space allows. For every leaf, provide one sentence describing its most awkward commitment specifically with respect to the counterfactual perceptual gap scenario.

In addition, flag any pair of leaves that the tree genuinely cannot distinguish—positions that yield identical answers at every node despite being conceptually different elsewhere. Note also any logically possible combination of answers that remains unoccupied, with a brief diagnosis of whether it is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored.

The audience is graduate students familiar with the basic Nyāya Sūtras and the commentarial debates on pramāṇa, but the instrument should not require knowledge of secondary literature to navigate. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations.
