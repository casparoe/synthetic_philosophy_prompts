As a journalist covering the philosophy of science and statistical learning theory, I am drafting a six-month fellowship proposal for an interdisciplinary research nonprofit. The project addresses a tension between the No-Free-Lunch theorems in learning theory and the classical problem of induction that, in my view, the existing literature treats too abstractly.

The precise question is this: when inductive bias is not merely a scientist's subjective prior but is materially embedded in the calibration architecture of a measurement instrument—say, the regularization constraints in a spectrometer's inverse-problem solver or the architectural priors in a neural network used for particle-track reconstruction—does the NFL result that all algorithms perform identically over all possible targets generate a form of inductive skepticism distinct from Hume's? Specifically, does this "instrumental prior" undermine scientific realism in a way that traditional underdetermination arguments do not, or does it instead support a structural realist claim that successful instruments must converge on shared inductive commitments regardless of the theoretical framework that designed them?

Existing philosophical responses to NFL results—typically the domain-restriction defense, which argues that science never averages over all possible worlds, and the Bayesian response, which treats priors as rationally updatable beliefs—leave the instrumental case open because they treat the prior as belonging to the agent rather than to the apparatus. I propose an analytical, not empirical, methodology: a conceptual reconstruction of instrument calibration protocols coupled with a formal mapping of their embedded biases onto the algorithmic structures that NFL theorems compare. This approach is preferable to an empirical survey of instruments, which would only catalog biases without resolving their epistemic status, and to a purely historical study of scientific practice, which would miss the formal structure that makes the bias unavoidable.

A useful deliverable would be a framework paper—without bibliography or citation list, which I will supply separately—that allows scientists and philosophers to classify whether a given instrument's inductive commitment is epistemically neutral (a feature of measurement per se) or theory-laden (a hidden theoretical assumption). Failure would be shown if the instrumental prior collapses entirely into the scientist's subjective belief, leaving no independent epistemic role for the apparatus itself. The six-month scope is realistic because the work is conceptual reconstruction rather than data collection; the risk is that the formal mapping proves trivial, which the proposal should acknowledge and guard against by selecting one concrete calibration domain—such as inverse problems in imaging—to anchor the analysis. The framing should avoid reducing the issue to the standard induction-versus-deduction debate, and should treat the instrument itself as an inductive agent whose biases are subject to philosophical evaluation.
