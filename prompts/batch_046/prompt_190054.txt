I am a novelist working on a historical fiction manuscript that reimagines the interior life of a real, long-deceased composer, and I have become preoccupied with the ethics of assigning motives and suffering to the dead. Peter Shaffer's Amadeus — in both its theatrical form and the well-known 1984 screen adaptation directed by Miloš Forman — presents an almost laboratory-pure case: it invents a poisoning narrative for Mozart's death, fabricates Salieri's deathbed confession, and transforms both men into symbolic figures of genius and mediocrity. I should like to read the work through the philosophical question of what we owe the real dead when we dramatise them, but I want to move beyond the usual concerns of historical accuracy or respectful representation. Specifically, I am interested in whether there exists a distinct harm when fiction assigns a false interior life — false guilt, false envy, false religious terror — to someone who can no longer correct the record, and whether this harm is best analysed as an injury to posthumous narrative autonomy, a corruption of collective moral memory, or something analogous to defamation without legal remedy. Does the play or film itself dramatise this tension, perhaps through Salieri's unreliable narration and his desire to become the sole author of Mozart's legacy? And where must the philosophical account be refined once we acknowledge that the dead cannot experience indignation, yet the living audience is permanently shaped by the fiction? I should welcome an answer in British English that treats both Shaffer's text and the conceptual problem with equal seriousness, indicating precisely where the philosophical lens illuminates a scene and where the work resists reduction, and concluding with what Amadeus contributes to the debate that pure philosophical argument alone cannot provide.
