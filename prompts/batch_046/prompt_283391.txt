I serve on the Municipal Constitutional Reform Panel charged with designing the membership rules for a new Participatory Deliberative Assembly that will hold binding authority over zoning, environmental regulation, and the allocation of a dedicated public-works fund. Our mandate requires us to submit a formal recommendation memo to the City Council by the end of the quarter, and the Council expects a document it can vote on without further philosophical arbitration. The central difficulty we have reached is the boundary problem—who properly belongs to the demos?—made concrete by three contested groups whose relationship to the assembly's jurisdiction is ambiguous.

First, there are "prospective residents": individuals who have executed binding lease agreements or accepted employment offers with start dates within eighteen months, meaning they will live with the consequences of our decisions but currently hold no municipal domicile. Second, there are "commuter stakeholders": workers who spend the majority of their working hours within the city and depend on its commercial infrastructure, yet maintain legal residence in adjacent jurisdictions and pay taxes elsewhere. Third, there are "treaty-rights holders": members of an indigenous nation with recognized consultation rights over land-use decisions affecting ancestral territories that overlap with the municipality, who do not seek full voting membership but whose exclusion from deliberation would violate standing agreements.

The panel is split between two conceptual orientations. One group defends an all-affected-interests principle: democratic legitimacy is undermined whenever anyone substantially impacted by collective decisions lacks a corresponding voice in making them. The other group insists that the demos must be constituted by mutual dependency and shared fate—those who actually inhabit the same space, depend on the same services, and must coordinate their lives over time—warning that the affected-interests criterion, taken seriously, dissolves the boundary into an ungovernable and infinitely expandable constituency.

Please draft the memo I will circulate before Thursday's meeting. It should recommend a concrete membership framework—specifying which groups receive full voting rights, which receive structured consultative or observer status with defined speaking rights, and which are addressed through external treaty mechanisms—together with a phased implementation timeline across two budget cycles. The memo must treat the all-affected-interests position with genuine philosophical seriousness, acknowledging the moral weight of including those whose lives will be shaped by the assembly's outputs, but it must ultimately defend a bounded demos grounded in shared residence and mutual dependency. Include an honest account of the strongest consideration on the losing side—specifically, the risk that our boundary arbitrarily privileges present inhabitants over future and peripheral stakeholders who will bear real costs. The document should be actionable: the Council should be able to adopt, amend, or reject the framework as written.
