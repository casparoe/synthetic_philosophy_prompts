I am a retired experimental physicist who, since leaving the laboratory, has been working systematically through philosophy of science and quantitative genetics. Next month I present to an interdisciplinary university seminar—attended by behavioral geneticists who routinely compute heritability coefficients, philosophers of biology skeptical of variance-partitioning frameworks, and bioethicists eager to apply genetic findings to educational policy—where I defend the thesis that heritability (h²) is not an intrinsic property of a trait but a relational index tied to a specific population-environment system. I will argue that cross-population comparisons of heritability and direct policy inferences drawn from h² values are therefore conceptually malformed rather than merely empirically risky; I grant that, within a fixed environment, genetic differences can explain observed phenotypic variance, but I maintain that framing this as the trait being “X percent genetic” commits a category error by reifying a statistical artifact of one particular variance structure. To move beyond this framing, I propose replacing heritability talk with what I call counterfactual perturbation profiles—tracking how a trait’s distribution shifts under specified, realizable environmental changes—while conceding that this does not by itself resolve deep measurement problems.

The audience includes two committed quantitative geneticists who defend h² as indispensable for medicine and breeding, several philosophers who regard the concept as incoherent, and a policy researcher who wishes to use heritability estimates to justify genetically tailored education programs. Please prepare me for the objections in roughly the order they will come, phrased as each faction would phrase them: the geneticists’ defense of predictive utility, the philosophers’ charge that I am merely renaming interaction effects, the policy researcher’s demand for actionable numbers, and the hidden objection I am hoping nobody raises—namely, that my counterfactual framework implicitly requires prior knowledge of all relevant gene-environment interactions, which seems to presuppose exactly the omniscient variance-partitioning I reject. For each objection, indicate whether I should concede, hold firm, or partially qualify my claim; note the weakest reply I might be tempted to offer; and identify the point in my argument that is most vulnerable under cross-examination.
