The author summarizes the higher-order evidence debate like this: "Higher-order evidence is just evidence about your first-order reasoning—like learning you're sleep-deprived or that a peer with similar training disagrees with you. Epistemic akrasia is the state where you still believe P even though you have strong higher-order evidence that your belief in P is unreliable. Most philosophers think this is always irrational, because rationality demands coherence between your first-order and meta-level attitudes. The level-splitting defenders try to save akrasia by saying you can rationally believe P at the object-level while rationally believing you're irrational in believing P, but they only manage this by treating the two levels as psychologically isolated mental files, which is implausable. So higher-order evidence should always defeat the first-order belief, and akrasia is basically just stubbornness or a failure of Bayesian updating."
