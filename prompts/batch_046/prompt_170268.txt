For my seminar on consciousness and self-reference, I am comparing different formalizations of Hofstadter's idea that the self is a strange loop—specifically, that the symbol 'I' is a self-referential pattern within a causal system rather than a direct pointer to an entity. To sharpen the comparison, I am using a distinction I have developed between strong strange loops, in which the self-model causally modifies the substrate that generates it, and weak strange loops, in which the reference is purely descriptive and leaves the substrate unchanged. Against this background, please rank the following four accounts on a scale of 1 (purely weak) to 10 (fully strong): (1) Gödelian syntactic self-reference in a formal system, where a formula asserts its own unprovability but does not alter the system's rules; (2) Hofstadter's neural strange loop, in which brain-level symbols model the brain itself; (3) a distributed connectionist self-model lacking any central symbolic node; and (4) what I call an 'epistemic void loop,' in which the symbol 'I' refers not to any substrate but precisely to the absence of a stable referent, generating self-reference through the gap between model and modeled. For each, assign a numerical score and justify it briefly in terms of causal closure and the preservation of subjective unity. I am not asking for bibliographic references—only conceptual evaluation.
