In a recent seminar on political rhetoric, a fellow student dismissed my proposal that institutional language guidelines could improve democratic deliberation by invoking what she called Orwell's feedback-loop thesis: that degraded political thought produces degraded language, and degraded language in turn further degrades thought, so any attempt to fix language without first fixing the underlying thought is futile. She treated this as a single obvious claim drawn from Orwell's writings on propaganda and political jargon, but I think the slogan is doing ambiguous work across several distinct theses. Could you disentangle the precise claims that might hide inside this two-way corruption formula? I want a menu rather than a single interpretation. Specifically, distinguish at least (i) a weak causal-psychological claim about individual cognition, (ii) a strong linguistic-determinist claim about collective belief-formation, (iii) a conceptual or semantic claim about how political meanings are constituted by usage, (iv) a normative claim linking linguistic clarity to moral or political clarity, and (v) a social-epistemic claim about how euphemism and jargon insulate communities from counter-evidence. For each reading, say whether it amounts to a platitude, a substantive and defensible thesis, or an actually false claim; and then identify which of these readings, if any, genuinely licenses her conclusion that language reform is pointless because thought will simply re-corrupt the words. I am particularly interested in whether her argument depends on an unnoticed equivocation between corrupt as a causal-psychological process and corrupt as a normative failure of transparency, since that slide seems to be what makes the dismissal feel compelling.
