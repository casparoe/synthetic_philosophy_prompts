I'm the risk lead for a small municipal flood-preparedness team, and every quarter we have to decide whether to push for barrier upgrades in specific neighborhoods. We get engineering reports with inside-view stuff — hydrology models, soil saturation projections, climate scenarios — and we have outside-view stats on failure rates for similar barriers in comparable regions over the last fifty years. But my staff are mostly generalists, not statistians, and we have maybe an afternoon to make the call, then it goes to the city council for sign-off. I need a reusable decision procedre — a checklist in order, what counts as a passing answer at each step, where we stop with a clear yes/no, and where we have to flag epistimic modesty requires we stop here and say we don't actually know. It should be grounded in what the inside-view vs outside-view distinction actually demands, not just consider both sides — especially how to handle direct conflict, and how to avoid overconfidence when the inside model is really complex. Include a kind of modesty discount: the more free parameters in the engineering model, the wider we have to make our confidence range before it can override the outside base rate. Apply it to two cases: one where inside view predicts severe flooding but outside view shows barriers almost never reduce damage in neighborhoods with this soil type, and one where both point the same way but there's an anomalous historical event that breaks the comparison. Also tell me exactly where the procedre hands the decision to human judgment because it's a values question, not a knowledge gap.
