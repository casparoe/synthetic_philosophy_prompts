Write a sonnet of exactly fourteen lines in iambic pentameter that stages the separateness-of-persons objection to utilitarian aggregation. In the octave, use a single speaker—the utilitarian accountant—who treats pleasures and pains as fungible entries in one social ledger, with rhyme scheme ABAB CDCD. At the volta in line 9, fracture the form: the sestet must alternate line by line between two distinct speakers, A and B, each anchored to a separate life that contributed to the accountant’s sum. Preserve the rhyme scheme EFEF GG, but place the rhymes across the speakers rather than inside either voice, so that corresponding sounds echo without allowing syntax or meaning to merge; no line should grammatically depend on its partner, forcing the rhyme to ring hollow of any unified subject. In the closing couplet, both speakers should invoke the identical “total welfare” figure—one treating it as belonging to a collective, the other denying that it belongs to anyone—so that the poem ends in unresolved conflict over whether there is a single “person” to receive the good. Do not cite texts, name specific chapters, or provide bibliographic references; let the objection live in the formal break between the unified octave and the irreconcilably divided sestet, accurate enough that a philosopher of ethics recognizes the Rawlsian point and clear enough that a general reader feels the impossibility of summing two lives into one grammatical subject.
