In my high school philosophy seminar, the debate over Dennett's critique of the Cartesian theater and his multiple drafts model has stalled completely. One side argues that distributing processing across parallel temporal streams eliminates any central observer and solves consciousness without a homunculus; the other insists that this only addresses access consciousness, leaving phenomenal consciousness untouched, because without a unified point of presentation there is no subject for whom the drafts are present. We keep returning to the same illustrations—the color phi phenomenon, the cutaneous rabbit, and the gap between reportability and experience—without movement. My working theory is that we are not divided by empirical neuroscience but by what counts as an explanation: specifically, whether accounting for functional information integration also accounts for subjective presence, or whether we are using different standards—functional versus phenomenological—under one word. At the same time, I suspect a hidden architectural question may be driving the stall: whether predictive processing in the brain requires a global workspace for conscious access, or whether purely local, draft-like predictions can do the work. Is the disagreement verbal, substantive, or a case of answering different questions—one about cognition, one about phenomenology—under the same name? And given that diagnosis, could a conceptual distinction—perhaps between narrative coherence and phenomenal coherence—or a formal result about distributed architectures actually break the impasse, or should we accept that the stall is permanent because our starting intuitions about presence and absence are bedrock?
