I'm in this course on comparative philosophy and we hit Ubuntu last week — specifically the idea that a person is a person through other persons, not just in a psychological sense but as a metaphysical claim about constitution. My prof said it's not just that people are social animals but something stronger about what identity is actually made of. Anyway, I was reading about restorative justice programs, like the family conference model used in New Zealand where offenders, victims, and community members meet and the goal is repairing the social fabric rather than just punishing an individual. The way it's run treats the offender as inseperable from their web of relationships and the victim as part of a damaged collective, not just an individual rights-holder.

What view of personhood does this practice actually presuppose? Does it need the strong Ubuntu claim that individual identity is literally constituted by relations, or could a thinner social-psychological view — that people just have deep relational needs — justify the same structure? If we swapped in a liberal individualist framework, which parts of the practice become incoherent and which just get a different rationale? I'm also wondering about this distinction people draw between being shaped by relations and being nothing but relations — does the conference model smuggle in collective responsibility that only makes sense under the thicker view, and is there a hidden assumption that harm to the victim is harm to the community in a literal rather than metaphorical sense?
