In drafting a novel that imagines a first-person consciousness distributed across three interconnected brains sharing predictive models but retaining separate autonomic centers, I have been grappling with Douglas Hofstadter's argument that the self is not skull-bound but can persist—through strange loops of representation and what he calls symbolic immortality—in the minds of others who have thoroughly internalized one's patterns. To test the philosophical architecture of the fiction, I have been debating with a philosopher friend whether this describes a literal distribution of the first-person perspective or merely an elegant metaphor for influence and posthumous reputation. Our disagreement has stalled in precisely the way I have observed in the personal-identity literature: we share the same neuroscience, reject substance dualism, agree that autobiographical narrative can be reconstructed elsewhere, and yet we cannot converge on whether the pronoun 'I' can be genuinely shared. In an attempt to break the deadlock, I introduced a distinction between indexical presence—the de re awareness of being this particular subject situated here—and narrative continuity—the structural preservation of memory, values, and predictive patterns across biological substrates. Rather than resolving the dispute, this distinction has only made the stalemate more precise. One side maintains that without shared indexical access there is only causation, not distributed selfhood; the other maintains that requiring such access begs the question by assuming consciousness needs spatiotemporal contiguity. Given this structure, is our impasse verbal, turning on ambiguous uses of 'persistence' and 'self'? Is there a hidden normative disagreement about bodily integrity disguised as metaphysics? Or are we running two different explanatory projects—one phenomenological, one metaphysical—under a single heading? Furthermore, if it is not merely verbal, what kind of progress is conceivable: a formal model of self-reference that permits distributed instantiation, an empirical finding about integrated information that either permits or forbids it, or a conceptual revision that renders the original question obsolete? I am not seeking a literature survey or citation list; I want a diagnosis of the disagreement's anatomy, supported by what each position actually commits to, and an honest judgment about whether the stall indicates an ill-posed question or merely an unusually hard one.
