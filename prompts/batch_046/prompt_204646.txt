Two colleagues—an architectural historian and a philosopher of law—have been arguing about a heritage-registration case. In 2015 a 19th-century covered market, the Municipal Market Hall built in 1892 and listed in the national heritage register, burned down. The foundations and about thirty percent of the original timber framing survived; on the same footprint, using matching materials and the original dimensions, a new structure was erected with modern internal systems. The rebuilt hall applies to retain the register entry “Municipal Market Hall, 1892.” The historian says this is wrong: the original building was destroyed, and what stands now is a reconstruction, not the same object. The legal philosopher says the entry should remain, because heritage law tracks a socially recognized artifact whose identity conditions include design, location, and institutional acknowledgment, not mere material continuity.

The historian’s first reply is that if we allow full replacement of parts, then the Ship of Theseus never perishes, and heritage protection loses its point—we would be protecting an idea, not a physical structure that can be damaged or destroyed.

The philosopher answers that this is not a reductio but an accurate description: heritage status protects a design and a social function embedded in a place, not a specific aggregation of bricks; the register never claimed to protect atoms.

The historian then points to a clause in the heritage statute that gives special protection to “original fabric,” arguing that the law itself presupposes material continuity, so the philosopher is importing an external metaphysics that contradicts the text.

The philosopher concedes the clause exists but argues it governs repair standards, not identity conditions: “original fabric” means “fabric consistent with the original specification,” not “the very same pieces of wood,” and reading it otherwise makes the statute self-defeating, since any repaired heritage building would lose its status.

At this point they turn metaontological. The historian claims their disagreement is substantive: there is a real fact about whether the 1892 object still exists, and the philosopher is trying to dissolve it by redefining words. The philosopher claims it is largely verbal: “the same building” means different things in engineering talk (material continuity) and in institutional talk (registered heritage object), and once we see that, the dispute is about which framework the law should adopt, not about a hidden metaphysical fact.

The historian objects that even if frameworks differ, one can still ask which framework gets the ontology right—buildings are not social constructions all the way down; they are physical objects that persist or do not.

The philosopher replies that this begs the question: it assumes “artifact realism,” the view that artifacts have mind-independent persistence conditions. An alternative, “institutional dependence” view holds that an artifact’s existence and identity are partly constituted by the practices that recognize it; on that view, the rebuilt hall can literally be the same heritage object because the recognizing institution never withdrew its acknowledgment, and there is no deeper material fact that overrides this.

The historian’s final move is that if institutional recognition is sufficient, then a government could declare a demolished site “still protected” without rebuilding anything, which is absurd.

The philosopher’s final reply is that this is not absurd—the law could protect a site as a memorial or archaeological reserve without requiring a standing structure—but in this case there is a rebuilt structure, so the point is moot; what matters is that the historian is smuggling in an unargued metaphysical commitment and calling it common sense.

For each, rate it as clearly mistaken, mostly right, or somewhere in between, with a brief justification. Then say which party is more right overall, identify what both are missing—such as whether there might be a middle position between pure materialism and pure institutionalism, or whether the statute actually contains a definitional clause they have overlooked—and explain whether their dispute is genuinely substantive, genuinely verbal, or a mix, specifying which parts belong to each category.
