Analyse the 2008 global financial crisis and its regulatory aftermath through the competing lenses of cyclical and linear historical theory, specifically Polybius’s anacyclosis, Ibn Khaldun’s model of asabiyyah and state decay, and Spengler’s culture-to-civilisation trajectory. Rather than treating the crisis as purely economic, map its phases—credit euphoria, institutional panic, and consolidation—onto these cyclical patterns, noting where modern securitised finance, which generates a kind of disembodied group obligation lacking territorial or kin-based cohesion, forces a revision of the analogies. Then evaluate whether linear-progress narratives of institutional learning and regulatory evolution account for the recovery more persuasively. Conclude by stating what this episode demonstrates about the limits of applying pre-modern cyclical schemas to the collapse and regeneration of contemporary global institutions.
