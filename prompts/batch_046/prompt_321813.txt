In a discussion about whether accomplishing difficult goals can contribute to well-being when the process involves sustained stress and the result brings no lasting pleasure, my interlocutor ended the exchange by saying that only the absence of craving and suffering matters and everything else is ornament. They seemed to be appealing to some tranquilist view of value, but I cannot tell what precise claim is doing the work. Could you lay out, as a menu rather than a single interpretation, the distinct precise theses that one-liner could be expressing? I want these separated and evaluated: (a) a definitional claim that well-being just is tranquility; (b) a psychological claim that lasting satisfaction is always caused by the cessation of desire rather than by achievement; (c) a normative claim that positive states or accomplishments have zero intrinsic value; (d) a comparative weighting claim that any reduction in suffering always outweighs any addition of positive achievement; (e) a metaphysical claim that value itself has a purely negative structure. For each, indicate whether it is a platitude, defensible but substantive, or actually false, and say whether serious tranquilist-adjacent thinkers (for instance in negative-utilitarian or Schopenhauerian traditions) actually hold it. Crucially, tell me which of these readings—if any—would genuinely support dismissing the idea that achieving a hard goal can be good for someone even when it involves stress and no post-achievement euphoria, and which are overkill that do not actually address that case. Also address whether there is a defensible middle position—call it conative asymmetry, where the cessation of craving is intrinsically valuable but pleasure and achievement are only instrumentally so—that avoids both the triviality of (a) and the extremism of (c) through (d), and whether that middle view still undermines achievement-based well-being.
