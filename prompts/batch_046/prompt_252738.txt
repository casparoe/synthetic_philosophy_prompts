I am struck by a divergence in how software-development communities treat two kinds of norms. In many open-source projects that use distributed version control, maintainers rigorously enforce a convention that every commit message must follow a semantic taxonomy—using prefixes such as feat:, fix:, or docs:—and they reject contributions that violate it, treating the message format as a coordination problem that demands an explicit common standard. Yet these same maintainers usually impose no comparable convention on internal variable-naming styles, leaving those to emerge organically within individual modules or to be governed by loose, locally negotiated habits. By contrast, in traditional centralized development shops the pattern is often reversed: a gatekeeper enforces a uniform coding-style manual across the entire codebase, but commit logs are treated as private administrative records with no standardized taxonomy.

What view of convention, normative authority, or the ontology of norms does this divergence presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit philosophy: does the practice treat conventions as solutions to specific coordination problems that must be intentionally agreed upon, in something like David Lewis’s sense, or does it tacitly distinguish between norms that require common knowledge and norms that can be sustained by decentralized adaptation? Say whether the two halves of the practice—strict semantic commit conventions versus laissez-faire naming norms—presuppose consistent things about how norms are generated, recognized, and enforced. Then tell me what would have to change in either development culture if we replaced the implicit view with a purely evolutionary or game-theoretic account in which norms are nothing more than stable equilibria that emerge without any intentional agreement or authoritative gatekeeping. Please write your answer in American English.
