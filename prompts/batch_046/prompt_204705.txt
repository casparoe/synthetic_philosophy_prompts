Write a short story of approximately 800–1,000 words that dramatizes the metaphysical dispute between counterpart theory and transworld identity, using an original fictional framework: an archival institution called the Continuum Registry, which maintains biographical records of individuals across logically possible worlds and permits brief "convergence sessions" in which a living subject can integrate the experiential memories of a world-branch in which she died or diverged.

The protagonist, a registry analyst named Mara, discovers that her own file has bifurcated: in Branch A she is alive; in Branch B she died in infancy. The Registry’s technology allows Mara to initiate a convergence session with Branch B, but the institution’s governing protocol requires her to declare, before the session begins, whether she regards the Branch B record as (1) herself, existing in another world with different properties (the transworld-identity interpretation), or (2) a counterpart—numerically distinct but qualitatively similar—whose memories she can access only as simulated experience (the counterpart-theory interpretation). This declaration determines the legal and ethical status of any merged memories.

Embed the philosophical tension in the Registry’s operational rules rather than in expository dialogue. Specifically, introduce a novel evaluative metric invented for this setting: the "essential-property drift coefficient," which measures whether divergences between world-branches (such as survival versus death from an illness) count as accidental or essential to personal identity. In Mara’s case, the coefficient is ambiguous—her essential properties appear stable except for the single divergence of survival, yet that divergence determines whether Branch B contains "her" or only "someone like her."

The narrative should follow Mara through the procedural steps of the convergence session, showing how the physical and emotional mechanics of memory-integration change depending on which metaphysical commitment she has made. If she treats Branch B as herself, the integration should feel like recovering a lost portion of her own biography; if she treats it as a counterpart, the same sensory data should feel like inhabiting a stranger’s final moments. Do not resolve the metaphysical question. Instead, conclude with Mara making a practical decision—whether to authorize the permanent merger of the two records, to archive Branch B separately, or to suspend judgment—that forces the reader to see why the distinction between identity and counterpart-relation carries existential weight.

Avoid philosophical jargon as explanation; let the Registry’s forms, protocols, and sensory descriptions do the work. Do not include references, citations, or allusions to specific philosophers by name.
