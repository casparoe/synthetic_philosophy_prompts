I am a doctoral student in philosophy of mind with some background in cognitive science, and I am drafting a six-month dissertation-chapter proposal for a fellowship that supports conceptual work rather than empirical data collection. My question concerns whether the classic paradox of phenomenal judgment for epiphenomenalism—if phenomenal properties are causally inert, then judgments about them are not caused by them, undermining their reliability—depends on an undifferentiated treatment of phenomenal reports. Specifically, I want to ask whether partitioning phenomenal judgments into two types, which I will call indexical phenomenal registration and attributive phenomenal characterization, allows the epiphenomenalist to localize the paradox to only one type and thereby defend a restricted but non-skeptical epistemology of consciousness. Indexical registration involves the direct, first-personal tagging of a state as present-to-me-now without further descriptive content; attributive characterization involves assigning specific phenomenal qualities, comparative relations, or modal properties to that state. Existing responses to the paradox—correlationist appeals, overdetermination strategies, and outright denial of phenomenal knowledge—treat phenomenal judgment as a uniform capacity, so they either save too much or concede too much. I propose to develop the distinction rigorously, test it against constructed edge cases involving sensory substitution, lucid dreaming, and blindsight analogues where causal pathways are held constant but report-types vary, and determine whether the causal exclusion argument applies with equal force to both. The method will be conceptual analysis and structured argumentation rather than literature review: I will build the framework from the ground up, show where standard objections misapply it, and derive precise conditions under which each judgment type could remain reliable despite causal inertness. A successful result would be a precise demarcation showing that indexical registration survives the causal-exclusion challenge because its reliability conditions depend only on structural correlation rather than causal transmission, while attributive characterization falls to the paradox unless supplemented by an independent justification that the epiphenomenalist can plausibly reject. The project fails if the distinction collapses—if any attributive content necessarily infiltrates indexical registration, or if both types require the same causal story—since then the paradox either generalizes fully or dissolves trivially without illuminating the underlying mechanics. Given the scope, I will not attempt a full metaphysics of consciousness or a complete taxonomy of report-types, but I will deliver a defensible framework, three detailed case studies, and an honest assessment of which further questions the framework raises but cannot settle in six months. I am not seeking a bibliography or citation list; I will integrate references myself. What I need is the proposal turned into a precise, polished document with the question stated determinately, the gap in existing answers clearly identified, the month-by-month approach specified, the success and failure conditions made explicit, and a realistic evaluation of what could make the project too ambitious for the fellowship period.
