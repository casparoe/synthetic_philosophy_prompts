I volunteer as a peer counselor at a community drop-in center — not a therapist, just someone who meets with folks for 20 minutes once a week to talk through habits they want to change, and then I have to write a quick note for our coordinator. Lately I've been seeing the same pattern: they come in saying they really want to quit something — energy drinks, skipping the gym they paid for, doomscrolling — and we make a plan together, but then next Tuesday they show up and say "yeah i just didn't feel like it in the moment." I read a little about weakness of will (akrasia? i think thats the greek word) and hyperbolic disounting, where people don't just value the future less but value it in this weird curved way, and also this idea that your future self isn't really "you" with less willpower but more like a different agent with different priorities — like you're bargaining with a stranger who lives in your body tomorrow. I want a reusable decision procedure I can run during those 20 minutes to figure out if their plan will survive contact with their "now-self," what kind of precommitment to suggest, and when I'm actually pushing my own values on them instead of helping.

The procedure needs to go in order: first, check if they genuinely think the habit is bad or just feel guilty because someone told them; second, map out whether the failure is hyperbolic (they care when planning but not when acting) or something deeper like depression or trauma; third, match the pattern to a specific commitment device; and fourth, check if the device respects their autonomy or is coercive. For each step, tell me exactly what question to ask, what counts as enough of an answer, and flag the spots where there's no right answer and I just have to use my judgement. Then apply it to three cases: (1) a guy who wants to stop buying energy drinks but always grabs one when he's tired after work; (2) someone who signed up for a running group but keeps not going and feels guilty; (3) a case where the procedure tells me to back off and refer, even though I personally think the habit is destroying them — and explain exactly why the procedure says that. Also be clear about what this procedure absolutely cannot do, like diagnose clinical addiction or replace actual therapy.

At the end, give me a table summarizing the whole thing with these columns: Step, The Plain-Language Test, Pass / Fail / Unclear, What I Do Next, and Judgment Call? (Yes/No with a one-line reason). I don't need citations or philosophers' names, just something i can actually use with zero philosophy background. One more thing — i want the answer to be honest about which steps are just judgement calls dressed up as rules, especially around whether the "stranger-tomorrow" framing even applies to them.
