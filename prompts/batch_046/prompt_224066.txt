I'm moderating a podcast interview with an unnamed philosopher who defends a "normative anchoring" theory of valid consent. She holds that deception vitiates consent only when it corrupts the agent's second-order recognition that she is entering a normative commitment, not when it merely falsifies a first-order belief about the act; that coercion is best analyzed not as the absence of alternatives but as a specific authority-subordination that renders the consent expression performatively self-defeating; and that competence requires not exhaustive information but the stability of the agent's practical identity across the decision. Draft eight to ten interview questions that do not ask her to restate these three claims. For each condition—deception, coercion, and competence—include one question that presses the standard boundary case raised in the literature: for deception, the scenario in which the agent would have consented even with full information; for coercion, economic duress that stops short of direct command; for competence, transient cognitive impairment that leaves practical identity apparently intact. Each of these three should be paired with a follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give, namely redirecting the issue back to whether normative anchoring is preserved or disrupted. End with a single question that asks what institutional practice or empirical finding could genuinely count against her account on at least one of the three conditions.
