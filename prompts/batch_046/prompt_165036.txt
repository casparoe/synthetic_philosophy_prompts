ok so i've been playing around with a version of Scanlon's contractualism that tries to handle the aggregation problem without just giving up and summing harms. instead of the usual pairwise setup where each person asks if they can reject a principle based on their own burden, i'm using something i call 'cumulative burden clustering' — basically, when a principle spreads small costs or risks across lots of people in a structurally similar way, those people can form a joint reason to reject it that isn't just one big individual complaint added up. so in cases like the bridge or the reservoir, the group bearing tiny distributed burdens can reject collectively even though no single one outweighs the person saved. i think this keeps contractualism's anti-utilitarian core intact but stops it from always saving the one.

what i want is a straight map of what would actually count against this. not just 'Scanlon wouldn't approve' — i mean which cases, intuitions, or formal results damage it? does clustering collapse into disguised rule-utilitarianism or prioritarian aggregation? are there scenarios where it overgenerates (rejects principles that should stand) or undergenerates? also, how does this sit with Scanlon's own framework — is there anything in his account of reasonable rejection that rules out group-structured complaints? please seperate what's pretty settled about his view from what's genuinely contested among people working on this, and don't present a contested interpretation as the consensus. rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and be honest if the view is just well-insulated in a bad way — like if clustering just restates the problem with extra steps. that's the part i care about most.
