I'm giving the opening remarks at the inaugural workshop "Machine Pedagogy: Teaching Without Programming," hosted jointly by a computer-science department and a college of education. The audience is split evenly between researchers building large learning systems and educators skeptical that statistical optimization can ever constitute teaching. The workshop's founding premise—my own, which I must defend without alienating either group—is that there is a deep structural distinction between training a system and educating one, drawn from Alan Turing's analogy between a learning machine and a child's mind. On this view, education requires sustained exposure, corrective feedback, and the gradual development of judgment, rather than the direct specification of rules or the minimization of a loss function; therefore I must welcome the computer scientists without promising that scale alone produces understanding, and I must welcome the educators without conceding that current architectures are literally pupils or that values can be "taught" by dataset curation alone. The philosophical idea should do real structural work in the remarks: because I take the analogy seriously, I cannot praise the workshop as merely useful for alignment or productive for industry, and I must avoid reducing the educators' concerns to technical problems of data quality. Please write the remarks so that they set ground rules for interdisciplinary discussion, explain why the distinction between training and education matters for both fields, and land for experts without losing the non-specialists. Do not include citations, reading lists, or references to specific works by title or date.
