I'm currently serving on a clinical-ethics review board that just adopted a triage protocol, and the experience has left me uncertain about whether I actually understand deontological constraints or merely recognize them. Here is an argument I constructed to test the view; it seems to prove far too much, and I cannot locate the slip.

(1) Traditional deontology holds an absolute side-constraint: it is impermissible to harm one innocent person as a mere means, even to prevent a greater number of equivalent harms to others.
(2) The moral authority of this constraint derives from the inviolability of the person, not from the physical manner in which harm is delivered; therefore it should apply with equal force whether the harm comes from direct bodily action, from an explicit order, or from an institutional protocol that I design and authorize.
(3) Every large-scale institutional decision—clinical triage, vaccination policy, emergency resource allocation—predictably produces statistical harm to some innocent parties.
(4) In each case, those harmed are not accidental byproducts but structurally necessary conditions of the benefit. The triage protocol saves more lives precisely because it denies beds to some; the vaccination campaign achieves herd immunity precisely because a calculable subset suffers adverse effects; without those contributions, the larger good does not occur.
(5) Therefore, by (1) and (2), any institution that implements such protocols systematically violates the absolute constraint.
(6) Yet deontologists clearly do not regard hospitals, public-health authorities, or rescue organizations as systematically immoral for making these trade-offs.
(7) So deontologists must distinguish permitted statistical harms from forbidden direct harms, typically by appealing to the doctrine of double effect: the harm is foreseen but not intended, and the harmed party is not used as a means to the end.
(8) However, in institutional design the harm is not merely foreseen; it is calculated, budgeted, and incorporated into the protocol's architecture. The expected casualties are part of the design specification, just as the bridge's weight is part of its engineering.
(9) Thus either deontology condemns all modern institutional action, which is absurd, or the distinction between "intended as a means" and "foreseen side-effect" collapses once we move from personal to institutional agency.
(10) The only remaining distinction is physical immediacy: pushing a body with one's hands versus activating a bureaucratic mechanism at a distance.
(11) But physical immediacy is not a morally relevant feature of an action; it is at best a psychological feature of the agent's experience.
(12) Consequently, traditional deontology either prohibits far too much or rests on an arbitrary distinction and therefore fails to guide institutional moral life.

I would prefer to preserve the intuition that there is a genuine moral difference between physically pushing one person onto tracks and flipping a distant switch that redirects a threat, but I am afraid that (10) reduces that difference to mere psychology and that (4) smuggles in a consequentialist picture of causal contribution. Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? In particular, if I reject (2) and treat institutional agency as morally different from personal agency, do I lose the ability to apply deontological constraints to policy at all? Please separate what is settled about the doctrine of double effect and the scope of deontic constraints from what remains genuinely contested—especially regarding institutional versus personal moral agency—and do not present any contested view as the consensus.
