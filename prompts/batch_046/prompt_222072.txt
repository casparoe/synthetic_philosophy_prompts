Consider the removal and toppling of the Edward Colston statue in Bristol in June 2020, during the global protests that followed the death of George Floyd. The bronze monument commemorated a seventeenth-century merchant and slave trader; activists pulled it from its plinth and rolled it into the harbour, arguing that public space should not honour a perpetrator of mass enslavement. Defenders of retention, including several historians, responded that destroying or removing the monument risked imposing present-day moral categories upon a past in which slave-trading was legally sanctioned and economically normative, thereby failing to understand Colston's world on its own terms.

Analyse this episode through the lens of presentism in historiography — specifically the problem of whether historians, and citizens acting as public historians, can ever escape the standards and conceptual frameworks of their own time when they judge, commemorate, or condemn figures from the past.
