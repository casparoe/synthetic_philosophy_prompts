In a seminar I have been running on territorial politics, the argument over whether Scotland, Catalonia, or Quebec possesses a legitimate claim to unilateral secession has reached a peculiar standstill. On one side are what I will call the democratic-choice theorists: they hold that if a clear majority within a territorially concentrated group votes for independence through democratic procedures, that vote itself generates a moral entitlement to secede, regardless of whether the parent state has committed grave injustices. On the other side are the remedial-constitutionalists: they maintain that secession is only justified as a remedy for severe and persistent oppression, and that allowing democratic majorities to dissolve constitutional orders unilaterally undermines the rule of law, creates vulnerable minorities inside the breakaway territory, and treats citizenship as a revocable contract rather than a shared commitment. Both sides have refined their positions over years; they agree on the empirical details of each case—the referendum results, the constitutional provisions, the distribution of linguistic and economic minorities—yet neither concedes an inch. The democratic-choice side insists that denying exit treats people as subjects rather than citizens; the remedial-constitutionalists reply that treating exit as a routine democratic option dissolves the very conditions that make democracy stable.

My working suspicion is that the deadlock is not primarily about empirical facts or even about competing moral weights, but about a hidden ambiguity in the concept of 'the people' whose self-determination is at stake. The democratic-choice theorists seem to treat 'the people' as a pre-political cultural or historical community that happens to be contained within a state, so that its will can be read off directly from a regional plebiscite. The constitutionalists treat 'the people' as a legally constituted demos—an association of citizens whose boundaries and obligations are defined by the constitutional order itself—so that a subset cannot unilaterally redefine the membership rules of the whole. If this is right, then the debate is partly verbal: both sides invoke 'self-determination' but attach it to different ontologies of the political community. But I am not sure. There might also be a deeper disagreement about whether legitimacy flows upward from consent or downward from institutional stability, which would make the dispute substantive but irreducible to a single empirical test. Or the sides might simply be answering different questions—one normative question about what justice requires for a cultural nation, the other a legal-institutional question about what preserves democratic continuity—while pretending to address the same one.

Can you diagnose what kind of disagreement this actually is? I want you to examine what each side actually says—particularly how they respond to the minority-inside-the-minority problem, to the argument that constitutional exit rules should be symmetrical, and to the charge that remedial theories privilege the status quo—and tell me whether the stall is verbal, substantive but incommensurable, or a sign that the framing itself is ill-posed. And given your diagnosis, what would actually count as progress: a conceptual distinction between national and democratic self-determination, an empirical finding about how secessions affect minority rights, a formal model of nested democratic legitimacy, or the admission that no single principle can govern all three cases? Please avoid surveying the literature; I want a structural analysis of the disagreement, with an honest assessment of whether anything should be expected to move it.
