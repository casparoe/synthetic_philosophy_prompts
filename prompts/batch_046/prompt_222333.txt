Examination question: Some philosophers distinguish ontological authenticity—the actual causal and historical origin of an artwork—from expressive authenticity—the realization of the stylistic properties, aesthetic achievements, and artistic intentions characteristic of the artist to whom the work is attributed. Consider van Meegeren's forged Vermeers, which initially convinced leading experts and were in some respects praised as more typically Vermeer-like than authentic late works. Does this case show that our preference for originals over perfect forgeries is irrational? In answering, distinguish at least two distinct values originals might possess—such as aesthetic, historical, or institutional—and assess whether a perfect forgery could genuinely share each. Also address whether expressive authenticity remains coherent here, given that van Meegeren intended deception rather than expression in Vermeer's mode: does it depend on actual intentions or only on realized properties? Finally, consider whether Goodman's distinction between autographic and allographic arts supports the judgment that a forged painting is necessarily deficient.

Student answer: The student defines ontological authenticity as being actually painted by Vermeer in the seventeenth century, and expressive authenticity as embodying the luminous domestic interiors, subtle treatment of light, and compositional balance characteristic of Vermeer's oeuvre, regardless of origin. They correctly note that van Meegeren's works possessed high expressive authenticity, since experts initially valued them precisely for these properties and in some cases found them more characteristically Vermeer-like than authentic late paintings. Addressing coherence, the student argues that because van Meegeren's intentions were deceptive rather than artistic in Vermeer's mode, expressive authenticity cannot depend on actual intentions but only on objective properties realized in the work; hence the forgeries genuinely share this value with originals.

The student then distinguishes values: aesthetic value is shared if the realized properties are identical; historical value is not shared, since originals form part of Vermeer's actual artistic development while forgeries belong to the separate history of deception; institutional and market values track provenance and scarcity. They then invoke Goodman's distinction: painting is autographic, meaning each instance is unique, so any forgery automatically deviates from and is artistically deficient relative to the unique original. Finally, they cite Dutton's view that the only genuinely artistic value is expressive authenticity, concluding that once a forgery is detected, any remaining preference for the original is merely a sentimental attachment to causal origin or a market reaction to scarcity, not an aesthetic judgment.
