I'm trying to determine whether there is any coherent principle behind my judgments about rational brinkmanship and deterrence failure, and I need that principle tested against concrete cases, with an honest account of which verdict must be sacrificed if coherence demands it.

Here are my verdicts.

Case 1: Two nuclear-armed adversaries with confirmed second-strike capabilities engage in a prolonged crisis, each taking observable steps that increase the objective risk of accidental war—moving weapons closer to borders, reducing decision-time windows, issuing public ultimatums—while both genuinely prefer peace to conflict. I judge this brinkmanship rational for both sides: the risk is the price of demonstrating resolve in a situation where no credible commitment mechanism exists.

Case 2: A state constructs and deploys a fully automated retaliation system—no human authorization required—designed to launch a full second strike if sensors register an incoming attack. The sensors have a known 2 percent false-positive rate, but the state publishes the system's existence while classifying the error rate. I judge this deployment irrational, not because automation removes human fallibility but because it replaces a visible, deliberative credibility mechanism with an opaque probabilistic one that the adversary cannot calibrate against.

Case 3: A state develops and uses a weapon specifically engineered to destroy a discrete military target without triggering the adversary's automated full-scale retaliation protocol—minimizing civilian casualties, avoiding command-and-control disruption, and staying below a publicly recognized escalation threshold. The state does this not in response to an existential threat but to force a concession in a conventional conflict. I judge this more irrational than Case 1, despite the lower physical destruction, because it erodes the categorical boundary that makes mutual deterrence stable.

Case 4: During an acute crisis, State A accidentally launches a single missile, immediately broadcasts an abort command and apology, and demonstrates the abort to observers. State B detects the launch but does not observe the abort. State B's leadership waits twelve minutes—past the window for certain preemption—before deciding against retaliation. I judge State B's restraint rational and praiseworthy. At the same time, if State B had retaliated based on the observable evidence alone, I would not judge them irrational—only catastrophically unlucky.

Case 5: A state maintains a policy of strategic ambiguity, refusing to specify which actions trigger nuclear retaliation, while its leadership privately adheres to a precise, narrow trigger list unknown to adversaries. During a crisis, an adversary takes an action that falls on the private list but is not obviously covered by any public doctrine. The state retaliates according to the private list. I judge the retaliation legitimate, but I also judge that the ambiguity itself made the crisis more likely by preventing the adversary from calibrating its actions.

These verdicts feel mostly consistent, but I keep returning to the tension between Case 1 and Case 2: in both, credibility depends on removing human discretion at the critical moment, yet I approve the human-led brinkmanship and condemn the automated system. Is the difference that human discretion remains visible and therefore calibratable, or am I merely reacting to the framing of a machine? More generally, is there a principle—perhaps involving the transparency of the escalation mechanism, the preservation of categorical thresholds, and the calibratability of risk by the adversary—that captures all five verdicts without forcing me to abandon any?

If such a principle exists, state it precisely, identify which case motivates each clause, and apply it to a sixth scenario I have not resolved: a state that publicly commits to absolute non-retaliation, but secretly maintains a manually activated, hidden retaliation capability that it reveals only after suffering a first strike. Would my principle classify this as rational deterrence, as deceptive brinkmanship analogous to Case 2, or as something else? And which of my five verdicts is the weakest link—the one a simpler principle would have to overturn?
