Since retiring from an experimental physics laboratory and turning to philosophy of mind, I have been meeting regularly with a study group. One colleague, a cognitive psychologist, has offered consistent judgments about whether particular situations involve genuine self-deception. She answers yes for a senior researcher who privately wrote replication failure—check instrument in her notebook, then publicly dismissed the discrepancy as instrument drift without reopening the raw files, all while sincerely describing herself as merely conservative about anomalies. She answers yes for a division manager who received monthly reports documenting sustained losses, gradually redescribed them as temporary restructuring costs, and now reads the same figures without any felt contradiction, though he never destroyed the original documents.

She answers no for an individual who was repeatedly told in childhood that he was tone-deaf, genuinely believes he lacks any musical capacity, and has never sought an objective assessment, despite possessing the physical apparatus for singing. She answers no for a study participant who received a placebo and reported improved concentration, never having been informed about placebo effects; she lacked the relevant information to suppress. She also answers no for a split-brain patient whose left hemisphere invents a verbal rationale for an action caused by the right hemisphere, classifying this as confabulation rather than self-deception because no prior belief is being protected from awareness.

These verdicts can be accommodated by at least two different principles. One might hold that self-deception requires a motivational structure in which accepting the threatening fact would undermine a valued self-conception or practical goal. Another might hold that it requires an internal epistemic division—one cognitive subsystem representing the truth, another generating and endorsing the false belief—together with a mechanism that blocks integration. A third possibility requires that the agent once possessed explicit conscious access to the counter-evidence, which was subsequently rendered inaccessible.

Now consider a new case drawn from my own situation. Since adopting a compatibilist position on free will, I find myself constructing rapid counter-arguments whenever I read determinist texts, and I genuinely experience these rebuttals as arising from logical flaws I perceive immediately. In an informal screening, physiological measurement revealed heightened amygdala activation during these readings, yet I report no conscious emotional disturbance and have never acknowledged—nor can I recall—feeling threatened by determinism. The arousal is detectable only through instrumentation; there is no suppressed explicit memory.

What verdict should my colleague deliver if she wishes to remain consistent—yes or no? Identify the closest analog among her earlier cases, lay out the competing rules that fit her track record equally well, state what each predicts about my situation, and offer your best judgment with reasons. Then propose one further hypothetical case—perhaps involving an artificial architecture with separated predictive-error monitoring and belief-fixation modules—that would best distinguish whether her implicit criterion turns on motivational defense, on internal epistemic division, or specifically on a history of conscious access that was later blocked.
