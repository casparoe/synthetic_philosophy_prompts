Hey, so I'm on the advisory committe for my city's heritage board and we're totally stuck on a new monument policy. The draft rule says any public memorial has to represent what they call the "unbroken cultural thread" of our local community going back at least 600 years, but the kicker is that like half the "ancient" traditions these monuments celebrate were actually codified by one local historian in the 1920s and then taught in schools like they'd always existed. Some board members say who cares, social cohesion now matters more than historical accuraccy, but others are saying if the tradition was invented then funding the monument is basically endorsing a forgery. I need to figure out what theory of collective identity and historical authenticity is actually baked into this policy.

Does treating a community as having a continuous 600-year identity presuppose some kind of essentialism about groups? Or can an invented-tradition view — like Hobsbawm's idea or Anderson's imagined communities thing — handle monuments without making everything seem fake? And if we approve the funding, are we implicitly committed to the idea that for nations or local groups, the original/copy distinction just doesn't apply? I want someone to reconstruct the implicit philosophical theory, say whether the policy strictly needs one view or works with several, and explain what we'd have to change in our evaluation criteria if we dropped the "unbroken thread" framework and treated the nation as basically a recent institutional artifact with no deep past.
