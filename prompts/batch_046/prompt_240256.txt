Below are working notes for an essay I intend to write on the recent tendency in historiography and philosophy of science to treat non-human forces—pathogens, climate oscillations, and volcanic winters—as 'historical actors' possessing a form of agency comparable to that of human institutions. I want to argue that while this framing corrects an older anthropocentrism, it risks a category error that obscures causal explanation rather than clarifying it.

My central claim is that we should distinguish three modes of non-human involvement in historical change: first, catalytic perturbation, in which an external event such as the Tambora eruption destabilizes existing social equilibria without itself carrying historical meaning; second, structural conditioning, in which long-duration environmental patterns such as the Little Ice Age constrain the range of viable institutional responses without determining which response is selected; and third, narrative assimilation, in which historians retroactively assign agential properties to these forces to construct coherent causal stories.

I contend that environmental determinism becomes pernicious not when it assigns causal weight to climate or germs—this is empirically unavoidable—but when it collapses the first two modes into the third, treating physical processes as if they possessed intentional structure. The peril, on my view, is not reductionism but a misplaced teleology: we explain the fragility of a dynasty by reference to drought, yet implicitly treat the drought as 'doing' something to the dynasty, rather than treating the dynasty's fragility itself as the primary explanandum.

I am particularly concerned with whether this distinction survives in cases of co-evolutionary feedback—say, where pathogen pressure and human immunological adaptation interact over centuries. Does my framework artificially separate genuinely coupled processes? And have I underestimated the heuristic value of agential language in macro-historical synthesis?

Please comment on the coherence of this three-part taxonomy, on whether my characterization of determinism's dangers is accurate, and on whether the framework introduces more conceptual confusion than it resolves—especially regarding the feedback dynamics mentioned. I am especially interested in whether there are decisive objections I have not anticipated, and whether the distinction between structural conditioning and narrative assimilation can be maintained without collapsing into a purely semantic dispute.
