I'm prepping notes for my grad phil-of-science seminar next week and got sidetracked arguing with a friend who TA'd the course last semester. She summed up Lakatos's methodology of scientific research programmes like this: "Every research programme has a hard core of assumptions that scientists agree never to abandon, and a protective belt of auxiliary hypotheses that does all the actual testing work. If experiments come up wrong, you only ever adjust the belt, never touch the core. Lakatos said a programme is progressive when it predicts genuinely novel facts that then get confirmed, and degenerating when it just piles up ad-hoc patches to save itself from refutation. He meant this to replace Popper's falsificationism completely, because on his view no single observation can ever damage a programme—only the long-run predictive record counts. The rules that guide how you modify the belt are the negative heuristic, while the positive heuristic is basically just the hard core itself telling you what must stay fixed."

Then she applied the framework to behavioral economics. She said that when Kahneman and Tversky developed prospect theory, that wasn't a degenerating problem shift within rational-choice theory but actually a progressive one—because it predicted new, surprising violations of expected utility rather than just explaining away old anomalies after the fact. I'm not convinced, partly because I think she might have the heuristcs backwards, but also because it feels like prospect theory does undermine the hard core of rationality assumptions rather than protecting them. Does it even make sense to call that progressive?

How fair is her summary, really? Go through it sentence by sentence and tell me what's dead right, what's subtly off, and what a defender of Lakatos would object to. And then, once we correct the missteps in her framing, does her claim about prospect theory hold up, or does fixing the framework actually make behavioral economics look more like a degenerating research programme?
