I am taking a postgraduate course in environmental philosophy, and I have become perplexed by an asymmetry I keep encountering in the institutional application of the precautionary principle. In one domain—chemical regulation under frameworks such as REACH—the burden of proof is placed squarely upon manufacturers: a substance may not enter the market until its safety is demonstrated, and residual scientific uncertainty regarding long-term, low-dose, or synergistic ecological effects is treated as a legitimate basis for restriction or delay. In another domain—climate-adaptation infrastructure and geoengineering pilot schemes, including large-scale flood-defence construction, stratospheric aerosol trials, or deep-sea carbon sequestration—the very same regulatory authorities typically demand demonstrable proof of harm before they will halt or postpone a project, treating structurally similar epistemic uncertainty about unintended ecological or social consequences as an inadequate ground for inaction.

What philosophical commitments about risk, knowledge, and moral responsibility does this institutional split presuppose? Does the practice tacitly rely on a categorical distinction between 'manufactured' and 'interventionist' risks, or does it presuppose a particular theory about which agents bear the burden of proof when technological action intersects with complex systems? Reconstruct the implicit theory of precaution that licenses treating uncertainty as a decisive veto in chemical policy but not in climate engineering, say whether that theory is internally coherent or whether it collapses into ad hoc reasoning, and describe what would have to change in regulatory practice if we replaced it with a unified, domain-neutral precautionary standard.
