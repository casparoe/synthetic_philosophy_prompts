There's this weird pattern in how school boards and city councils deal with renaming buildings for people like Jefferson, Churchill, or Columbus. They always pull out the man-of-his-time defense—like, don't apply modern morals to old figures—but then they immediately turn around and say Churchill was unusually racist even for a 1940s PM, or that Columbus was especially brutal compared to other conquistadors. So the defense isn't really everyone was bad back then; it's more like if your worst actions were roughly average for your social class and era, you're protected, but if you were an outlier in cruelty, we take the name down. What philosophical commitments does that unstated rubric presuppose? Does it assume moral blame tracks statistical deviation from a peer-group baseline rather than the absolute harm you caused? Or that honoring someone requires them to have exceeded the local moral floor of thier time? Reconstruct the implicit theory, distinguish what the practice strictly needs from what just happens to fit it, and say whether it's compatible with a view that says historical judgment should just measure harm regardless of context. Also tell me what an actual renaming policy built on that pure harm-based view would have to change—would it have to rename half the buildings in the country?
