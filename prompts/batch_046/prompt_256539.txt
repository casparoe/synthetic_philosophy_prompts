The assigned concept is the rigid designator and the necessary a posteriori. Rather than opening with the familiar "water is H2O" script, which several students have already seen in popular-science contexts and will therefore treat as a slogan, I want to build the session around a less worn but equally intuitive case: comparing the proper name "Aristotle" with the definite description "the teacher of Alexander the Great," specifically with respect to modal reasoning about whether Aristotle could have failed to teach Alexander, versus whether whoever taught Alexander could have been different. I need to know exactly what properties this example must possess—what it should make salient about cross-world identity, and what it must lack—so that it illuminates rigidity without covertly importing a descriptivist theory of reference.

Because my audience knows logic but not philosophy, I want to structure the session as a dependency chain rather than a definitional list. Could you lay out the precise order in which the conceptual pieces must be introduced, and justify the ordering? In particular, what preliminary distinction—perhaps between the semantic role of an expression and the metaphysical status of a proposition, or between epistemic certainty and metaphysical necessity—must be secured before I even utter the phrase "rigid designator"? Once the Aristotle example is running, what misconceptions should I expect these particular students to form? I am especially concerned that they will conflate "necessary" with "known with certainty" because they have just learned modal logic in an epistemic framing, and that they will treat rigidity as an intrinsic feature of the word "Aristotle" rather than a feature of the reference relation across possible worlds. Finally, I need a check for understanding that forces application to a fresh case rather than repetition of the water example—something that reveals whether they genuinely grasp that a posteriori necessity involves a discovery about the world, not merely a logical deduction.
