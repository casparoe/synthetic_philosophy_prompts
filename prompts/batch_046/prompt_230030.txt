Since I recently assumed directorship of our surgical residency assessment program, I have been revisiting Ryle's distinction between knowing how and knowing that in order to redesign our competency framework. To that end, I am developing an evaluation metric—provisionally called procedural articulation density, or PAD—that measures the proportion of a clinical skill that can be rendered as explicit, ordered propositional steps without loss of operative efficacy. My working hypothesis is that Ryle's anti-intellectualist argument was essentially about cognitive compression rather than logical category: knowing-how is knowing-that under conditions of extreme propositional density that resist full verbal unpacking, not a distinct form of knowledge altogether. On this view, if a system can generate complete and accurate step-by-step surgical protocols—something current large language models can now approximate—then it possesses the corresponding clinical knowing-how in decompressed form, and our assessments ought to shift from observational competency checks to tests of propositional completeness scored by PAD. Is this interpretation of Ryle conceptually sound? Specifically, does treating knowing-how as merely dense knowing-that misrepresent the logical structure Ryle intended, and if that misrepresentation is corrected, does the further claim that AI protocol-generation amounts to genuine knowing-how still follow? I am not seeking references or a reading list; I want to know whether the conceptual move I am proposing survives philosophical scrutiny, and what adjustments to my assessment framework would be required if it does not.
