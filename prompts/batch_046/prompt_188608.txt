I'm a doctoral student in philosophy of science working on a chapter about structural realism, and I've been struck by how consistently people in seminars and recent literature reach for the subway-map analogy. The idea is that a mature scientific theory is like a subway map: what survives theory change is not the intrinsic nature of the nodes—whether a station is brick or steel, whether an electron is a point or an extended object—but the connectivity, the lines linking nodes, the overall network topology. The analogy is meant to license a specific conclusion: that we can be realists about relational structure without committing to the intrinsic nature, or even to the traditional existence, of the entities that occupy the nodes.

In trying to apply the analogy with precision, I've started distinguishing two kinds of fidelity that maps can preserve—what I have been calling topological fidelity (which connections exist, which nodes are linked) and metric fidelity (the quantitative distances, ratios, or proportional relations among nodes). Subway maps are deliberately designed to preserve the first while systematically distorting the second; geographic distance is sacrificed to legibility. This raises a question I haven't seen addressed head-on: does structural realism need both kinds of fidelity, or only topological? And does the subway analogy quietly smuggle in the assumption that metric relations are either epistemically inaccessible or scientifically unimportant?

Can you lay out the correspondence table explicitly—what plays the role of the mapmaker, the territory, the passenger, the station, the line, and the scale distortion—then identify precisely where the mapping breaks? I particularly want to know whether the analogy imports commitments about design, purpose, or human accessibility that lack analogues in scientific theorizing; whether the distinction between topological and metric fidelity strengthens structural realism or undermines it; and whether the conclusion—that realism about structure avoids ontological commitment to entities—survives once the disanalogies are on the table, or whether the analogy was carrying that conclusion all along.

Please separate what is broadly settled in the philosophy of science from what remains genuinely contested among specialists, and do not present a contested interpretation—such as whether ontic structural realism eliminates objects entirely or merely deprioritizes them—as if it were the consensus view.
