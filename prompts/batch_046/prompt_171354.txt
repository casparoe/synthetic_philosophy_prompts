Hey, I'm a retired field biologist who got into philosophy of mind after I hung up my boots. Lately I've been tinkering with an essay idea about insects and that scale argument — you know, the one where people say there are so many bugs that even a tiny chance they're sentient means we should care about them massively. I think there's a hidden statistical analogy that nobody talks about, and I want to check if I'm just confused or if there's actually something here.

Here are my rough notes:

We all know insects outnumber us by like ten to the twentieth or whatever. The scale argument says if there's even a 0.1% chance a beetle is sentient, then with 10^19 beetles you've got 10^16 moral patients. But this treats moral weight like biomass, or like counting every bacterium in a soil sample. In ecology we don't do that; we group organisms by functional roles because counting every individual cell is meaningless for the system's behavior. So I'm proposing a distinction between distributive moral weight, where each potential patient counts separately and linearly, and systemic moral weight, where what matters is whether the system as a whole exhibits properties that ground concern. Under distributive, the scale argument works. Under systemic, the vast numbers might actually dilute the case, because if sentience is sparse and randomly distributed across the population, it doesn't form a coherent system worth protecting — kind of like how background noise doesn't become a signal just by turning up the volume.

Then I try to connect this to the non-identity problem: if we change farming practices, we don't save specific bugs, we just cause different ones to exist. So the harm isn't to identifiable individuals but to a statistical distribution. I call this a "moral sampling error" — treating a population-level probability as if it applies to discrete, identifiable subjects. I think this might be different from standard person-affecting views because it's not about who exists, but about whether the ontology of the subject is individual or population-level in the first place.

Anyway, it occured to me that I might just be reinventing the old impersonal vs person-affecting debate and dressing it up in ecology jargon. Or maybe there's already a term for treating moral status as a non-linear aggregate? I'd like to recieve some feedback on whether this distributive/systemic split holds water, if the ecological analogy is misleading, and whether I've missed something basic about how philosophers usually frame this. Don't need citations — just tell me if I'm on track or if there's a name for the split I should know about. Thanks.
