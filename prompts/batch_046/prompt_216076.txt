I'm working through a philosophy of mind seminar on the nature of affect, and I've settled on a view that sits awkwardly between the standard camps. I take emotions to be neither evaluative judgments in the cognitivist sense, nor reducible to bare interoceptive feelings in the classical James-Lange tradition, but rather what I call salience-structuring commitments: transient, embodied orientations that rapidly restructure which features of one's environment count as action-relevant, without necessarily carrying full propositional content and without being identical to conscious awareness of bodily change. On the more specific framing I've adopted, they are predictive affective framings—rapid, body-informed predictions about the value-laden significance of a situation, experienced phenomenally as urgency, but functioning primarily to lock perceptual attention onto particular affordances rather than to represent the world propositionally.

Given this position—call it predictive framing if you need a label—I want an honest map of its exposure. What would actually count against it? I am not asking for a literature survey or a roll call of dissenting authors; I want the kinds of evidence, conceptual arguments, and cross-domain results that should move a defender. Which observations about emotional pathology, about development in infants or non-human animals, about the neural architecture of affect, or about phenomenological reports in deep meditation or dissociative states, would be decisive rather than merely awkward? What does this view predict that pure cognitivism or pure feeling theories do not, and does it risk anything concrete—for instance, about whether emotions can be rationally assessed, about whether they can be shared across cultures with radically different bodily practices, or about how they relate to longer-lasting moods? Has the view already been qualified into unfalsifiability—say, by allowing that any counter-case can be redescribed as a "framing failure" or a misdirected prediction rather than a failure of the theory itself—and if so, is that insulation a genuine problem for a view of this kind? Please rank the defeaters from fatal to merely uncomfortable, note which ones a committed predictive-framing theorist would likely concede as damaging and which they would dismiss as category errors, and be willing to conclude that the position is either genuinely exposed or, conversely, well-insulated in a way that may or may not be intellectually respectable.
