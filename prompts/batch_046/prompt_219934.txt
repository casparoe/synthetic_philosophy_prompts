Consider the March 2018 fatal collision in Tempe, Arizona, in which an Uber test vehicle operating in autonomous mode with a human safety driver struck and killed Elaine Herzberg, who was walking a bicycle across a roadway at night. The vehicle’s perception system reportedly classified her in shifting categories—unknown object, then vehicle, then bicycle—without initiating an emergency braking maneuver, which Uber had disabled in autonomous mode to reduce abrupt responses, leaving intervention to the distracted safety driver. In the public and regulatory aftermath, commentators widely invoked the trolley problem, framing the tragedy as a case in which an autonomous system might face a forced choice between killing a pedestrian or swerving into an obstacle, and asking what moral algorithm should govern such splits.

Analyze this episode through the theory of intuition pumps—thought experiments that persuade by isolating variables and triggering clear moral intuitions. First, map the trolley-problem framing precisely onto the technical failure: identify whether the Uber system ever encountered a discrete decision node with defined options and transparent payoffs, or whether the failure arose from cumulative probabilistic thresholds, sensor fusion delays, and an organizational protocol that removed braking authority from the algorithm altogether. Second, specify which features of the pump—binary outcomes, full causal transparency, an isolated agent, and emotional immediacy—were genuinely present in the engineering context and which were projected onto it by the framing. Third, assess whether the pump served as a useful heuristic for policy, or whether it conferred false "moral legibility" on an error that was statistical and institutional rather than deliberative. To sharpen the analysis, distinguish a "choice-structured" architecture, in which a system explicitly selects among known outcomes at an identifiable moment, from a "threshold-structured" architecture, in which behavior emerges from layered confidence scores and disabled safeguards without any singular point of decision. Apply this distinction to ask what the Tempe case reveals about the conditions under which intuition pumps can illuminate a real-world system versus when they obscure its actual failure mode, and whether the episode supports or undermines the claim that philosophical thought experiments can be reliably exported into safety-critical design ethics.
