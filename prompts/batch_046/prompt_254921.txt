For my political theory seminar I have been asked to write out my Machiavellian profile before presenting it, so I want a consistency check. Here is where I stand:

(1) Virtù is not classical moral virtue but an adaptive political capacity—the skill to read contingency and act with decisive flexibility, including violating conventional ethical norms when state preservation demands it.

(2) Fortuna behaves like a river that can be partly channeled by dikes built in calm periods, yet remains capable of overwhelming any design; I further think that such dikes can become dangerous if they generate false security, so adaptive redundancy is preferable to rigid institutional defense.

(3) The appearance of virtue—mercy, faith, integrity—is itself an instrument of rule; I take this to include strategic ambiguity in public rhetoric, allowing different constituencies to project their own values onto the ruler without committing him to any single interpretation.

(4) Republics outlast principalities because they distribute virtù across citizens and laws, reducing catastrophic dependence on one agent's judgment.

(5) Nevertheless, in moments of founding or deep corruption—when laws are either absent or thoroughly decayed—only a single ruler with exceptional personal virtù can restore order; collective deliberation is too slow for such ruptures, though once restored, authority should revert to distributed institutions.

(6) Despite this emphasis on agency, I hold that fortune sets the ultimate horizon: every ruler is eventually submerged by historical tides he could not have anticipated, and wisdom consists in accepting this finitude rather than denying it.

(7) I reject the cynical reading: there is a genuinely political morality in which preserving the state and the common good functions as a higher-order justification that can render otherwise vicious actions permissible.

Do these hang together? I can see possible friction between (1) and (7)—if virtù is purely adaptive skill with no intrinsic moral content, in what sense is state-preservation a moral justification rather than merely a pragmatic one? There may also be tension between (4) and (5), since distributing virtù across institutions seems to deny that one individual can supply it in crisis. Meanwhile (2) and (6) might look contradictory—why build dikes or maintain redundancy if the flood is inevitable?—but I am unsure whether that is a deep conflict or only apparent.

Please audit the set: rank the actual tensions by severity, indicate which pairs are only apparently in conflict and explain why they dissolve on closer inspection, and for each genuine tension specify the least costly revision—whether giving up (1), (3), (5), or (7) does the least damage to the overall view. I am not signaling which commitment I am most attached to; I want the assessment on its merits.
