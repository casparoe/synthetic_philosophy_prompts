I am writing a feature on the limits of utilitarian aggregation under conditions of radical demographic uncertainty, and I have been testing my intuitions against a sequence of stylized choice problems. I want to know whether there is a coherent principle—perhaps an adaptation of Harsanyi's aggregation theorem with an added invariance or reference-class constraint—that captures my judgments, or whether consistency forces me to abandon one of them.

Here are my verdicts.

First, when two populations of equal size share identical ordinal rankings over climate-adaptation policies but assign very different cardinal utility intervals—Group A treating the gap between best and worst as twice as large as Group B does—I feel the planner should still apply some form of weighted sum, yet I also feel that treating these raw cardinal differences as interpersonally comparable without normalization is illegitimate.

Second, in a standard risk choice between Policy X, which guarantees ten people a utility level of 5, and Policy Y, which gives nine people a level of 6 and one person a level of 0, I prefer X. That suggests I reject pure ex-post total utilitarianism, though I do not reject weighted aggregation altogether.

Third, consider a self-locating uncertainty: the planner does not know whether she governs 100 people or 10,000, and the likelihood of the larger population depends on the policy adopted—Policy Z marginally raises the probability of the larger population by enabling long-duration settlement. My instinct is that in this frame the aggregation weights should be indexed to the reference class that the policy itself makes more probable, rather than fixed by prior probabilities alone.

Fourth, if all citizens agree on the ranking of outcomes but convert them to numbers via incompatible scales—some linear, some logarithmic—I believe no aggregation is legitimate unless we first impose a transformation that respects the shared ordinal structure while stripping away arbitrary scaling. This seems to sit in tension with the standard Harsanyi result, which requires that cardinal utility differences be comparable across persons.

Is there a unified account—say, a variant of Harsanyi's theorem constrained by ordinal-invariance and endogenous reference-class adjustment—that delivers all four verdicts? If not, which judgment must I revise? And what does the corrected principle predict for a case I have not listed: a planner who must aggregate preferences across generations where the number of future individuals is not merely unknown but potentially unbounded?
