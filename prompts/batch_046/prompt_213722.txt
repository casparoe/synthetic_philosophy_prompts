As a journalist covering philosophy and conceptual science, I have been tracking a frustrating impasse in moral philosophy that I cannot quite diagnose from the outside. For decades—perhaps since the classic formulations of agent-centered ethics—the paradox of deontology has circulated in roughly the same form: theories that treat certain actions as categorically forbidden, regardless of their consequences, seem to generate obligations that are not merely costly but morally perverse, as when a principle against deception would require allowing a murderer to locate an innocent victim. Despite extensive literature, the debate appears frozen. One group treats the paradox as a reductio of non-consequentialist ethics, arguing that any theory permitting catastrophic outcomes for the sake of procedural purity is structurally self-defeating. Another group responds that the paradox only arises if we mistakenly model deontological constraints as extremely weighty pro tanto reasons that happen to be outweighed; on their view, these constraints are better understood as exclusionary reasons—considerations that do not enter a weighing calculus at all, but instead rule out certain options from consideration regardless of the balance of goods. A third group suggests the framing itself is confused, insisting that deontology is not a decision algorithm but a constraint on practical identity, so asking whether it produces bad outcomes is a category error.

My working hypothesis is that the disagreement is not about morality but about the architecture of practical reason: whether moral justification must be representable as a comparative weighing of considerations, or whether it can include categorical exclusions that function differently in rational deliberation. If I am right, then the sides are not disagreeing about the same phenomenon but answering different questions—one about normative justification, one about rational choice architecture, one about the semantics of obligation—under the single heading of the paradox. But I am genuinely unsure whether there might also be hidden empirical disagreements about moral psychology, about how actual agents experience constraint versus cost, or whether the stalemate is simply a bedrock clash of intuitions about what counts as a morally relevant reason.

Could you diagnose this impasse precisely? Is it verbal, substantive, or a case of parallel monologues answering different questions under one name? Do the sides share enough common ground for a formal distinction—perhaps between first-order reasons and second-order exclusions—to resolve it, or is the disagreement rooted in pre-theoretical commitments about the nature of moral obligation that no argument could shift? And if progress is possible, what would it look like: a conceptual clarification, a formal model of practical reasoning that accommodates both weighing and exclusion, or an acknowledgment that the question is ill-posed?
