I am a retired experimental biologist now studying philosophy, and I have been struck by a structural parallel between laboratory controls and a design choice in artificial-intelligence safety. In reinforcement-learning research, engineers often penalize an agent for “side effects”—changes to environmental states that lie outside a narrowly defined task boundary—without encoding a complete value hierarchy or even an ontology of what is worth preserving. The penalty typically measures deviation from a baseline trajectory or initial condition, treating any unmandated alteration as presumptively undesirable, much as an experimenter treats any deviation from a control condition as noise to be minimized.

What philosophical commitments does this institutional practice presuppose? Must it assume that value is localizable—that there exists a clean separation between task-relevant and task-irrelevant states—analogous to the separation of variable and control in an experiment? Or does it smuggle in an implicit negative axiology, according to which change from the status quo is bad unless specifically authorized? Can the framework cohere without a full specification of value, or is it philosophically underdetermined—compatible, say, with both a conservative status-quo bias and with a purely instrumental concern for predictability?

Please offer a committed response: take a definite position on which view (or views) the practice actually requires, argue for it with reasons rather than surveying options, and specify exactly what would have to be revised in the design framework if that underlying philosophical commitment were abandoned. I am not asking for a literature review; I want a single, well-defended reconstruction.
