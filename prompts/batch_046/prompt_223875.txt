For my sixth-form philosophy society, I am giving a brief talk on Douglas Hofstadter's idea that creativity works by knob-twiddling within a conceptual space: rather than generating ideas ex nihilo, a thinker adjusts continuous parameters — scale, emotional colour, material substrate, temporal framing, and the observer's relation — of an existing mental structure to produce variations that are novel yet recognisably related. I should like you to write a poem of six quatrains in a modified pantoum form that enacts this mechanism structurally. Begin with a single image — a brass compass lying open upon an unmarked map in an empty reading room — and in each successive quatrain alter exactly one parameter: first the material (brass to glass, then to water); second the temporal scale (a single second, then a century); third the emotional register (quiet curiosity, then dread); fourth the observer's relation (possessing the compass, then being possessed by it); fifth the acoustic environment (absolute silence, then a distant bell); and sixth the metaphysical implication (navigation through physical space, then navigation through fate). In every quatrain, two lines must remain verbatim as refrains: one naming the untouched core, the needle still seeks north, and one naming the adjustment itself, turning the hidden knob. The meaning of these refrains should shift as the surrounding lines change, and there should be a volta, preferably in the fifth quatrain, at which the listener realises that the poem is not describing the conceptual space from outside, but is itself the space being twiddled. Write in spare, precise free verse; do not quote Hofstadter directly, but paraphrase his view through the imagery if necessary. No bibliography, citation list, or reading references are needed — I require only the poem.
