I'm working through my actual commitments on intertemporal welfare comparison, not in the abstract economic sense but as a matter of moral and practical reasoning about future people. I was talking with a colleague about intergenerational ethics, and I realized my judgments don't line up neatly with either the standard exponential discount model or the pure Ramsey-style rejection of pure time preference. Instead, my reactions seem to depend on whether future welfare is tied to present agents through identity, kinship, or direct causal continuity, rather than on calendar distance alone — but I'm unsure whether that's a coherent principle or just post-hoc rationalization. Here are my verdicts on concrete cases.

First, if I can prevent moderate harm to strangers in 200 years at a small cost today, I think I should do it, and I feel no legitimate reason to weight them less than strangers in 30 years; calendar distance alone doesn't justify discounting for others. Second, if I'm deciding whether to endure a painful procedure now to avoid the same procedure in ten years versus in thirty years, I do weight the nearer avoidance more heavily — I apply something like hyperbolic discounting to my own future suffering, and this feels rational rather than merely biased. Third, if I must choose between saving resources for my own retirement and leaving them to unknown strangers two centuries hence, I feel permitted to prioritize my retirement even though both are future benefits, which suggests relational proximity matters. Fourth, I think it is morally wrong to adopt policies that impose catastrophic climate costs on people in 150 years to secure modest gains now, so for large-scale collective action I reject pure time preference entirely. Fifth, however, I also think it is permissible for me to spend surplus income on my child now rather than investing it in a fund that maximally benefits strangers in 150 years, even though in both cases I'm comparing a present relational beneficiary to distant strangers. The tension is that I want to deny calendar-based discounting for strangers generally, yet I seem to allow a form of deprioritization — or even discounting — when the comparison is between my child and distant strangers, which looks like it reintroduces proximity-based preference through the back door.

Is there a single principle that captures these verdicts — perhaps one that distinguishes structural temporal distance, which should carry no moral weight, from relational temporal anchoring, where the future party's connection to present agents via identity, kinship, or direct causal chain determines the strength of the claim?
