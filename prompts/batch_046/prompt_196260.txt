Here is an argument I developed for a seminar on well-being and now find suspicious because its conclusion seems to erase the very distinction hedonism is meant to defend. (1) To answer the heterogeneity problem—what do the pleasure of eating chocolate, solving a proof, and feeling relief have in common?—hedonism must appeal to something other than phenomenal quality, since no shared felt texture exists across all cases. (2) The best non-phenomenal account is reflexive endorsement: a mental state is a pleasure just in case it includes, as part of its own structure, a positive evaluation of its occurrence. (3) Pain, by parallel reasoning, is a state with a built-in negative evaluation of its occurrence. (4) But now imagine intense physical suffering that is reflexively endorsed—say, experienced as authentic or purifying—while lacking any positive phenomenal quality. By (2) this is a pleasure. (5) Conversely, imagine a state with all the phenomenal marks of joy that is reflexively rejected—say, experienced as undeserved or dangerous—while lacking any negative phenomenal quality. By (3) this is a pain. (6) So on this account, phenomenal character is irrelevant to whether a state is pleasure or pain; only the reflexive evaluation matters. (7) Therefore hedonism's claim that pleasure is the sole intrinsic good reduces to the claim that reflexively endorsed states are good, regardless of how they feel—which allows suffering to be intrinsically good and joy intrinsically bad, collapsing hedonism into evaluative subjectivism and abandoning the intuition that feeling good is what makes pleasure valuable. Where does the reasoning go wrong, if anywhere? I would prefer to keep (2) and (7) intact if possible, and I want to know exactly what that costs me.
