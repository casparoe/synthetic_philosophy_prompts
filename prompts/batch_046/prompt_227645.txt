I run a workshop for political philosophy grad students, usually about 16 people, and next tuesday we have a long session of about 80 minutes. I was arguing with a friend about whether tearing down a statue is actually an act of violence or just stopping an act of honoring, and she brought up this idea I've been calling mnemonic debt — the thought that keeping a monument or street name alive isn't passive at all, it's like continuously repaying an obligation to the past, which means future citizens who never consented are being drafted into that repayment. I want the group to feel the force of that, not just hear it. So design me a runnable in-room activity where they have to make real binding decisions about three commemorative objects — say, a bronze statue in a park, a university building name, and a street sign — but split them into four roles: descendants of the person commemorated, descendants of people directly harmed by that legacy, representatives of future citizens fifty years out, and an archivist who thinks physical removal is always an erasure of evidence. They should vote with colored cards, majority rules per object, no forced consensus, and then live with the collective outcome. I want rules that make every option feel like betrayal to at least one role, a timeline for the 80 minutes, social stakes rather than money (maybe they have to co-sign a one-paragraph justification for each choice?), and then a debrief that maps exactly where they felt the pull of mnemonic debt versus the "open future" objection. Be honest about what feature of the actual ethical problem this can't reproduce — probably the slow generational dynamics of forgetting — and tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time, like one role dominating or people breaking character.
