In cross-cultural philosophy and social ontology, a common analogy compares Western individualism to a marketplace of separate agents who contract for mutual benefit and can exit at will, and East Asian collectivism to a family meal where all dishes are shared, portions are not individually claimed, and leaving the table is a breach of belonging rather than a cancellation of agreement. The analogy is regularly used to argue that collectivism captures the true relation between person and community because it treats social bonds as constitutive rather than instrumental. I want a rigorous audit of its reach. Give me an explicit correspondence table — mapping agent, contract, shared dish, refusal of private portion, the late-arriving guest, and the host's authority onto the social-ontological equivalents. Then identify the disanalogies that matter for the intended conclusion: does the family image quietly import hierarchical obligations that modern East Asian societies have largely dissolved? Does the marketplace image ignore the non-contractual obligations embedded in Western welfare states? Can either image accommodate voluntary dissent or exit without collapsing? Finally, say whether the normative claim — that collectivism gets the person-community relation right — survives once these breaks are visible, or whether an alternative analogy, perhaps one drawn from ecological interdependence rather than kinship or exchange, would do the work more honestly.
