I'm preparing a presentation for my cognitive aesthetics seminar on analogical creativity, and I've been trying to formalize my advisor's implicit taxonomy of what counts as genuine artistic or conceptual innovation versus mere rearrangement. She works with a framework loosely drawn from Douglas Hofstadter's view that creative acts are fundamentally variations on an underlying theme—adjusting parameters, or knobs, of a deep conceptual structure—though she applies it through a distinction I have not encountered in the standard literature: she separates structural knobs, which alter relational architecture or logical form, from surface knobs, which change only domain, scale, sensory modality, or stylistic dressing. Her past verdicts on specific cases are these: she counts a jazz improvisation on a standard chord progression as genuinely creative, because the harmonic relationships stay intact while tempo, timbre, and instrumentation shift; she rejects a corporate logo redesign that preserves geometric relations but merely updates the color palette, since only surface knobs have turned; she denies creativity to a mathematician who proves an isomorphic theorem in a different number base using identical logical steps; but she grants it to a mathematician who inverts the quantifier order in a proof to solve an analogous problem in modal logic, because the relational structure itself has been transformed. She also judged a modern-slang parody of a canonical poem—keeping rhyme scheme and meter exactly intact—to be ambiguous: she labeled it surface-twiddling with structural mimicry and declined to give a firm verdict, suggesting that preserving architecture while changing vocabulary sits at a boundary she has not resolved.

Next week she will evaluate a case I constructed for discussion: an image-generation system takes a well-known Renaissance compositional schema—specifically, the triangular grouping and directional gaze patterns of a Madonna-and-Child painting—and produces an image in which that exact triangular architecture and those gaze relations are preserved, but every other feature is transformed: the figures are robots, the style is Japanese ukiyo-e woodblock printing, and the emotional valence is inverted from serene to menacing. The relational geometry is untouched, yet the surface domain, affective semantics, and stylistic vocabulary are all radically altered. Given her track record, what should I predict she will say about this case? I am especially interested in identifying the competing rules that could consistently generate her earlier judgments. One candidate requires transformation of structural knobs regardless of how extensive the surface and affective changes are; another permits creativity whenever high-level semantic or affective knobs are turned, even if the core relational architecture remains fixed, provided the change is not purely cosmetic like color or scale. Which principle best fits her actual pattern of verdicts, and which hypothetical further case—perhaps involving contrapuntal music or architectural spatial logic—would most sharply distinguish between these two interpretations? Please also say what principle she would be implicitly adopting if she rules in favor of the AI-generated image, and what principle she would be adopting if she rules against it.
