The central tension is this: MSRP is supposed to be normative, distinguishing progressive from degenerating programmes by their ability to generate predictions of genuinely novel empirical facts and to absorb anomalies into the protective belt rather than the hard core. But philosophical research programmes, including Lakatos’s own, do not typically yield testable empirical predictions; their “hard cores” are methodological or metaphysical commitments (e.g., that rational scientific change is best explained by competing programmes). If we therefore exempt philosophy from MSRP, we seem to undermine its claim to universal normative authority, since the defence of MSRP itself often appeals to its own status as a progressive research programme in the history of science. Conversely, if we insist on applying MSRP reflexively, we must either redefine “novel fact” so broadly that it includes conceptual clarifications or meta-methodological adjustments—which risks emptying the criterion—or we must concede that philosophical programmes are inherently degenerating, which seems self-undermining.
