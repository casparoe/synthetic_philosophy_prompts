Consider this reconstruction of Pascal's account of justice and force in the Pensées. (1) Justice has no natural or universal foundation; it is constituted entirely by custom and convention, varying from society to society. (2) At the same time, justice without force is powerless, and force without justice is tyrannical; legitimate authority therefore requires the union of both. (3) In any stable society, the customs that define justice are sustained and enforced by the prevailing coercive power; were that power withdrawn, the customs would collapse. (4) Hence, in every functioning society, the existing force is necessarily coupled with the justice-defining customs it sustains. (5) Applying Pascal's criterion from (2) to the situation described in (4), any regime that maintains enduring social order thereby possesses both justice (as sustained convention) and force (as sustaining power), and so meets the conditions for legitimacy. (6) Consequently, Pascal's framework cannot consistently identify any stable regime as tyrannical: endurance itself demonstrates the presence of justice coupled with force. (7) Yet Pascal clearly intends to condemn certain long-established regimes as unjust and tyrannical. The argument therefore proves too much, collapsing the very distinction Pascal seeks to preserve.

Which premise or step should be rejected? Explain why that step initially looks sound given Pascal's texts, and then assess what each of the three main interpretive responses must concede: the conventionalist reading, which denies that sustained custom acquires normative authority without an independent moral assessment; the institutional separation reading, which distinguishes the coercive power that preserves order from the political authority being evaluated for legitimacy; and the theological reading, which appeals to Pascal's Christian framework to supply a transcendent standard of justice independent of custom.
