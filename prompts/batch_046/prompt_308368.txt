I am currently drafting a literary novel in which one of the central figures is an anthropological linguist working in the mid-twentieth century, and I have been trying to understand the philosophical climate that might shape her private doubts about whether her translations can ever be truly faithful. Over dinner with a fellow writer, my friend offered what seemed to me a lucid but perhaps overly dramatic summary of Quine’s thesis of the indeterminacy of translation—the familiar scenario in which a field linguist hears “gavagai” as a rabbit runs past. According to my friend, Quine showed that no amount of observed native behavior can settle whether that utterance refers to a rabbit, to an undetached rabbit-part, or to a temporal stage of rabbithood, because every rival manual of translation can be made consistent with the same stimuli by adjusting auxiliary hypotheses elsewhere. From this she inferred that translation is not merely difficult but fundamentally indeterminate: there is never a single correct rendering of any sentence, not only between radically foreign languages but even between closely related ones like English and French, since the choice among competing manuals is always governed by convention and background theory rather than by fact. She went further and claimed that Quine intended this result to undermine the very idea of getting a translation right, so that all literary and philosophical translation must be regarded as an arbitrary act of invention sustained only by social habit, with no meaningful distinction between fidelity and free variation.

Because I intend for my character to wrestle with precisely this kind of skeptical crisis about her own work, I need to know whether my friend’s account respects the boundaries of Quine’s actual argument or whether it collapses distinctions that Quine preserved. Could you evaluate the fairness of her summary claim by claim—particularly the extension from radical translation to universal interlingual indeterminacy, and the further inference that fidelity in translation is therefore an illusion? I am especially interested in whether there is a principled boundary, perhaps tied to shared stimuli or prior theoretical commitments within a linguistic community, that prevents the skeptical conclusion from swallowing everyday and literary translation whole. Please do not provide a bibliography, reading list, or citations; I am working without scholarly archives at hand and need an evaluation grounded strictly in the conceptual architecture of the argument. If the summary misrepresents Quine, I want to know exactly which parts a careful defender would correct, and whether the dramatic conclusion my friend draws for my character’s profession genuinely follows from what Quine established.
