As a retired molecular biologist who has only recently begun auditing philosophy seminars in earnest, I find myself preoccupied with a problem that sits exactly at the boundary of my old discipline and my new one: whether a purely descriptive account of biological and institutional functioning can ever genuinely entail a specific prescription without concealing a normative premise within the description itself. In discussions of Hume’s is-ought gap and Moore’s open-question argument, my interlocutors keep returning to the same familiar illustrations—the definition of a good knife, or evolutionary explanations of cooperative behavior—so I would like you to construct something more precisely engineered. Please design a thought experiment that meets the following specifications.

The scenario must be realistic—set in a functioning hospital ward or research laboratory, with no science-fictional technology, perfect predictors, or hypothetical beings—and confined to a single paragraph. Within it, a senior administrator derives a specific, non-obvious moral obligation for a junior clinician or researcher purely from descriptive premises: quantitative measurements of the subordinate’s cognitive fatigue, the documented error-rate curve of the protocol being executed, and the functional architecture of the institution as a cooperative system for generating reliable knowledge. The derivation must appear genuinely plausible rather than fallacious by obvious sleight of hand; yet the hidden normative premise that allows the inference to go through must be one that is genuinely defensible in general—analogous to an optimal rule rather than an ad hoc stipulation—such as a principle linking the preservation of epistemic reliability in knowledge-producing institutions to the specific duties of their participants. After presenting the scenario, please state the derived obligation explicitly, identify the exact descriptive premises, and locate the precise point at which the normative bridge is inserted.

Then provide the analysis from three perspectives: first, a strict Humean who insists the gap is unbridgeable and pinpoints exactly where the smuggling occurs; second, a proponent of institutional functionalism who argues that the functional role itself carries intrinsic normative weight and that no illegitimate premise has been added; and third, a moral error theorist who denies that any obligation exists regardless of the functional description. Next, isolate the feature doing the philosophical work: is it the biological description of fatigue, the cooperative institutional context, or the hidden principle linking reliability to duty? Finally, explain what happens if we strip away the institutional frame—if the identical fatigue patterns and error risks occur in an isolated individual with no social or epistemic role—and say whether that collapse supports the Humean diagnosis or the functionalist defense. I want to test whether the analogy between institutional maintenance and biological homeostasis can bear genuine philosophical weight, or whether it dissolves once we recognize that only the latter involves literal survival, leaving the normative force of the former dependent on a premise we have merely failed to notice.
