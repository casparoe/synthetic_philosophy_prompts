I'm moderating a podcast interview with a philosopher who defends a modified Strawsonian view of moral responsibility. She keeps Strawson's idea that reactive attitudes—resentment, gratitude, indignation—are the core of moral responsibility, but she adds a condition she calls "co-present narrative continuity": she argues that for a reactive attitude to be genuinely appropriate, its target must share an ongoing social narrative with the person feeling it. This means, she says, that we cannot truly resent long-dead wrongdoers or thank distant ancestors in the same direct way, and that artificial systems lacking continuous experiential memory fall outside the scope of moral responsibility regardless of their behavior. She uses this to claim that moral responsibility depends not just on quality of will but on shared temporal situatedness.

Write me a sequence of interview questions that presses her on this addition. I want the questions to turn on the actual content of her position, not generic biographical ones. Specifically:

- Begin by asking how her continuity condition differs from the standard capacity-based conditions she rejects, and whether it risks reintroducing a kind of objective attitude toward anyone outside our immediate narrative circle.

- Anticipate her reply that narrative continuity is only a descriptive feature of how reactive attitudes naturally work, not a normative filter. Follow up by pressing whether that descriptive move leaves her vulnerable to the same objections Strawson himself raised against treating people as objects of social policy rather than as participants in interpersonal relations.

- Push on distributed or collective targets—corporations, states, generations—where the narrative is discontinuous and spread across many people. Ask whether she must deny that we can genuinely blame or praise such entities, or whether she has to weaken her condition so much that it loses its distinctive force.

- End with a question about what could genuinely count against her view. Ask specifically whether widespread, stable evidence that people routinely direct full-blooded reactive attitudes at non-continuous targets—such as ancestors, fictional characters, or artificial agents—would show her condition is wrong, or whether she would simply classify all such cases as misdirected feelings.

I'm not an academic; I've just been working through these ideas on my own. So please keep the questions conceptually sharp but avoid assuming I can deploy technical terminology without explanation.
