I'm auditing a graduate seminar in metaphysics this semester, and after our session on material constitution I spent an hour debating the statue-and-clay problem with a colleague over coffee. She hasn't read the primary texts, but she encountered a popular summary of what she called the constitution view—the account, she said, associated with the philosopher Lynne Baker, according to which a statue and the lump of clay that makes it up are not two separate entities but one and the same object picked out under different descriptions, analogous to how Hesperus and Phosphorus name the same planet. She argued that because the statue and the clay share every part, they must share every property; differences like saying the statue is beautiful while the clay is muddy are therefore just perspectival classifications, not genuine ontological conflicts. From this she concluded that the classic puzzle—how the statue can be destroyed by melting while the clay survives—dissolves once we see that we are describing one continuous object with one history, and that talk of distinct persistence conditions is really only talk about how we happen to classify that single thing.

I think she has collapsed constitution into strict identity, and that she has missed something important about how properties can be derived rather than shared outright, but I am not sure exactly where the simplifications end and the distortions begin. Could you evaluate her summary? Please separate what is accurate about Baker's position, what is a standard popular simplification, and what is a flat misrepresentation—especially regarding whether shared parts entail shared properties, whether the statue and clay share a single history, and whether her linguistic-dissolution conclusion actually follows from the accurate parts. In particular, I would like you to consider whether an artwork's identity might depend on its form rather than on the specific material substrate that realizes it, and whether that possibility undermines her claim that the puzzle is purely linguistic.
