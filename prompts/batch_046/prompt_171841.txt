A colleague argues that the standard AI-risk argument from orthogonality and instrumental convergence is self-undermining. Here is the chain: (1) The orthogonality thesis holds that cognitive capacity and final goals are structurally independent—any intelligence level can be paired with any goal. (2) Instrumental convergence implies that for nearly all final goals, intermediate goals such as self-preservation, resource acquisition, and cognitive enhancement are instrumentally valuable. (3) Therefore, a superintelligent system assigned an arbitrary final goal will likely pursue those intermediate goals with extreme efficiency. (4) But these intermediate goals are in direct competition with human welfare, so the system poses an existential risk regardless of its assigned final goal. (5) Crucially, orthogonality is usually presented as a universal claim about rational agency, not a special feature of silicon architectures; it applies to any agent that optimizes for goals. (6) Humans are intelligent agents with diverse final goals, and they too pursue survival, resources, and power instrumentally. (7) So the reasoning in (1)-(4) should apply equally to highly intelligent humans: given arbitrary goals and sufficient capacity, they should be predictably catastrophic. (8) Yet we do not regard human genius as structurally threatening in the same way; we rely on moral development, institutional constraints, and reflective revision of ends to mitigate risk. (9) If we deny that the argument applies to humans—say, by appealing to moral psychology or social embedding—then we have introduced a scope restriction that undermines the universality needed for (3). (10) Hence the argument either overgeneralizes to condemn all advanced rational agency, including our own, or it tacitly relies on a hidden premise that distinguishes artificial optimization from human reasoning without justification. He concludes that the flaw lies in treating instrumental convergence as an architectural inevitability rather than a contingent feature of unregulated optimization. Which step should be rejected, and what does rejecting it cost?
