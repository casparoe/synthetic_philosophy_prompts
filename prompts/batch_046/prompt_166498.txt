Rank the following five explanatory mechanisms for the nineteenth-century collapse of dueling among European elites—Appiah’s honor-recalibration thesis (that dueling came to violate the very code of honor it was meant to uphold), Bernard Williams’s thick-concept thesis (that the descriptive conditions grounding terms like “coward” and “honorable” shifted, eroding normative force without explicit argument), the bureaucratic-rationalization thesis (that states and capitalist economies increasingly treated lethal private violence as an inefficient waste of elite human capital), the Weberian state-monopoly thesis (that legal prohibition and the centralization of legitimate violence made dueling too risky regardless of moral change), and the “performative depletion” hypothesis (that urbanization and military professionalization dissolved the ritual choreography—recognized seconds, fields of honor, witnessed protocols—so the norm collapsed from execution failure before it was morally rejected)—from S-tier (greatest explanatory weight) to D-tier (least). For each mechanism, provide in one sentence: the precise causal pathway it proposes; one reason it outranks or is outranked by the mechanism immediately adjacent to it; and one weakness that prevents it from being sufficient alone. Then, in a brief concluding paragraph, judge whether the collapse was overdetermined or whether removing your top-ranked mechanism would have allowed the norm to persist, and identify which two mechanisms are most likely to be empirically confounded in actual historical explanation. Do not provide bibliographies or citations; work from the conceptual structure of each thesis.
