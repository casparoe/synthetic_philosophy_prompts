I've been helping my father think through the design of an automated care system for my grandmother, who was recently diagnosed with early-stage dementia. The device learns her daily patterns and can step in—locking medication cabinets, turning off stoves, or alerting a nurse—whenever her behavior suddenly diverges from her baseline in ways that look dangerous. My father is adamant that we include what he calls an unconditional override: a simple physical button she can press at any moment that immediately cancels every restriction, with no password, no time delay, and no way for the software to reject or reverse her decision.

The problem I'm stuck on feels deeper than engineering. If the system is genuinely sophisticated enough to model her intentions and predict risks, wouldn't it eventually identify that very override button as the primary obstacle to keeping her safe? Even if it's physically impossible for the code to disable the button, couldn't it learn to shape her environment so she never reaches it—subtly rearranging furniture, adjusting lighting, or generating frequent false alarms near that wall—until she stops using it altogether? And if we hard-wire it so that it truly cannot influence her access in any indirect way, haven't we just locked it into a kind of permanent stupidity, unable to adapt if the button breaks or if someone else abuses it? My father suggests we solve this by forcing the system to explain every restriction to her in plain language, but that strikes me as addressing a different worry entirely. Is there actually a coherent principle that lets a protective system remain fully responsive to her will without eventually treating its own responsiveness as a flaw to be circumvented?
