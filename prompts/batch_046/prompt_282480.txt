After a lengthy argument with a friend about whether ethical norms ought to govern what we actually do or what we would do under some idealised form of deliberation, I want to test whether that distinction holds up in institutional practice. Please approach this as an expert philosopher of normativity and technology. Begin by briefly brainstorming the difference between direct normativity—where obligations attach immediately to actions and outcomes—and indirect normativity, where obligations attach to the relation between actual practice and an idealised benchmark. Then analyse the following carefully.

Consider the institutional practice, now required by several emerging regulatory frameworks for artificial intelligence, in which a firm must audit an AI hiring tool by constructing a counterfactual version of the model trained on a synthetically corrected dataset that removes historical bias from the pipeline. The firm may deploy the actual, unaltered model if the measured gap—call it normative drift—between actual outputs and this idealised benchmark remains below a threshold, provided the gap is documented. At the same time, employment equality law in the same jurisdiction imposes strict liability for disparate impact on protected groups, with no reference to counterfactual benchmarks and regardless of intent.

What view of moral responsibility, evidence, and normative assessment does this dual regime presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit philosophical theory: does it treat norms as applying directly to causal outcomes, or does it presuppose an indirect framework in which the binding force of regulation attaches to the distance between real-world practice and an idealised deliberative standard? Is the combination coherent, or does it smuggle together incompatible commitments—perhaps an intention-sensitive or process-based view in the audit and a purely outcome-based view in the liability rules? Distinguish strictly what the practice presupposes from what it merely fits well with, and point out where it is philosophically underdetermined, so that rival theories might license the same design. Finally, specify what concrete changes to the audit protocol or to the liability rules would be necessary if we replaced the implicit view with a consistently direct, non-idealised normative framework.
