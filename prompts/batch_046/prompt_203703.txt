Here is an argument I constructed for a workshop on democratic inclusion, and it now strikes me as proving far more than I intended. I want to preserve the core normative premises, but I need to know where the excess is generated.

(1) A democratic decision is legitimate only if every individual whose fundamental interests are affected by it has an equal say in it—the all-affected-interests principle.
(2) The boundary of the demos—who counts as a member—is itself a decision that must be made democratically if it is not to be arbitrary.
(3) So the boundary decision must include, in its decision-making body, everyone whose fundamental interests are affected by where the line is drawn.
(4) A boundary decision does not affect only actual residents or citizens; it also determines which possible people come to exist, since rules of membership shape migration, settlement, citizenship transmission, and the social conditions under which lives are begun.
(5) For any alternative boundary, there is a counterfactual constituency—possible people who would exist only if that boundary were adopted, and who, if they existed, would have a fundamental interest in existing (assuming lives worth living).
(6) The actual boundary decision therefore determines whether the interests of these counterfactual people in coming-to-be are satisfied or frustrated.
(7) By (1) and (3), then, the actual demos must include representatives of all counterfactual constituencies whose potential existence is at stake, or else it violates the all-affected principle.
(8) But these counterfactual people do not exist and cannot vote, and the set of possible alternatives is unbounded; so no actual body can ever satisfy the inclusion requirement.
(9) Therefore democratic self-definition is impossible: either the boundary is imposed arbitrarily, or democracy can never legitimately determine its own membership.

I am inclined to keep (1) and (2), and I feel the slip lies somewhere between (4) and (7), but I cannot name whether it is an equivocation on "affected," a modal slide, or an illegitimate extension of moral consideration to non-actual beings. Which step should be rejected, and what does rejecting it cost—especially for the coherence of the all-affected principle? Please separate what is settled in the literature on democratic boundaries and on the non-identity problem from what is genuinely contested among people who know the topic, and do not present a contested view as if it were the consensus.
