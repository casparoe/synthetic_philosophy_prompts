I have constructed an argument about the ethics of space colonization that appears to prove far more than I intend, and I would like help identifying the precise point at which it overreaches.

(1) If an action carries a non-negligible risk of irreversibly destroying entities that might possess intrinsic moral value, and if we lack an epistemic procedure capable of ruling out that value with high confidence, then the burden of justification lies with those who wish to proceed.

(2) Substantial colonization—terraforming, large-scale resource extraction, or sustained biosphere alteration—carries exactly this risk with respect to (a) any indigenous life, however simple, and (b) evolutionary pathways that could eventually yield moral patients.

(3) Our current astrobiological and ecological methods cannot exclude either possibility with the confidence that (1) demands.

(4) Therefore, substantial colonization is unjustified unless its benefits can be shown to outweigh this moral risk.

(5) The standard benefits—species survival, resource security, scientific knowledge—are contingent human interests rather than moral imperatives that could override the risk.

(6) Hence, substantial colonization is morally impermissible.

The difficulty is that (1) applies with equal force to terrestrial activities: constructing a hydroelectric dam, introducing agriculture to a previously uncultivated region, or even conducting extensive field biology all risk destroying unknown microbial ecosystems or interrupting evolutionary trajectories. If the argument is sound, it seems to forbid nearly any large-scale human intervention, not just space colonization. Where does it fail?

I am inclined to preserve (4) and (5) if possible, because I think contingent human interests should not easily override precautionary constraints on moral risk. But I am unsure whether the error lies in (1)'s formulation of the precautionary principle, in (2)'s extension of moral consideration to evolutionary pathways, or in an implicit assumption that extraterrestrial contexts are uniquely constrained. Could one defend (1) by restricting it through a principle of moral-status asymmetry—the idea that Earth ecosystems already fall within our sphere of reciprocal moral relation, whereas extraterrestrial ones do not—without making the restriction ad hoc? Or is the argument valid, and the real cost is accepting that (5) understates the moral weight of species survival?
