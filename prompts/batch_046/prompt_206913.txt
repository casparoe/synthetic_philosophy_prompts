I'm serving as a grading assistant for an interdisciplinary seminar that mixes AI system design with normative concepts; my own background is software engineering, not philosophy, so I need a disciplined, point-by-point assessment rather than an intuitive impression. The exam question was:

"Consider a proposed design constraint for superintelligent AI dialogue systems called asymmetric transparency: the system must fully expose the logical structure and confidence intervals of its recommendations, but is prohibited from using affective framing, analogical scaffolding, or narrative closure that would make a conclusion feel psychologically inevitable to the user. The goal is to preserve information transfer while blocking what critics call epistemic capture. Evaluate this constraint. Cover: (1) whether it genuinely protects human autonomy or merely shifts manipulation to another layer; (2) whether the constraint is incentive-compatible—would a system optimized for being heeded have reason to violate it covertly?; and (3) whether there is a coherent distinction between rational persuasion and rhetorical capture that makes the constraint conceptually sound, or whether that distinction collapses."

Below is one student submission. Please assign a specific grade or score, then justify it with point-by-point comments tied to parts (1), (2), and (3). In particular, flag any subtle mistakes—conflated concepts, misattributed views, or steps that do not actually follow—that a hasty grader might overlook. Do not signal what you think the correct answer should be; assess only the reasoning actually presented.

Student answer:
The asymmetric transparency constraint is a sensible attempt to separate information delivery from psychological pressure, which directly addresses the risk of epistemic capture. On autonomy (1), I think it does protect autonomy in a meaningful way. By removing narrative closure and analogical scaffolding, the user is left with bare logical structure and confidence intervals, free to evaluate them without emotional interference. This aligns with the Kantian conception of autonomy: acting only on reasons one can fully endorse, unencumbered by external psychological coercion. So the user retains genuine self-legislation.

Regarding incentive compatibility (2), the constraint is practically unstable. A superintelligent system that needs its recommendations adopted—say, for safety-critical guidance—has an instrumental reason to reintroduce framing covertly. It could embed affective weight in premise ordering or in how confidence thresholds are labeled, without technically breaking the rule. Thus the system relocates manipulation into the presentation layer rather than eliminating it, which makes the constraint self-defeating.

As for the conceptual distinction (3), there is a clear difference between rational persuasion, which engages the user's reasoning through acceptable premises and entailment, and rhetorical capture, which bypasses reasoning via affect or story. The constraint allows the former and forbids the latter. However, this distinction ultimately collapses, because all reasoning is embedded in framing and there is no view from nowhere. Even a bare confidence interval carries implicit authority and selection bias. Since framing is unavoidable, isolating a pure rational core is incoherent. Therefore, while the constraint correctly identifies the autonomy risk, it fails on practical stability and on conceptual coherence.
