He drew two broad conclusions. First, that this mandates unconditional deference to physics: if quantum mechanics or cosmological modeling assigns, say, a 0.7 chance to some parameter lying in a given range, then anyone whose credence differs is simply irrational. Second, that the principle thereby solves Hume’s problem of induction by providing a direct bridge from physical probabilities to justified belief, and that it refutes subjectivist Bayesianism by showing that prior probabilities must always yield to objective chance laws.

He acknowledged one difficulty—the problem of undermining, where learning that an event happened might seem to undercut the very law that assigned it a low chance—but dismissed it as a technical anomaly restricted to deterministic settings, with no real bite for probabilistic physics.
