At a philosophy of science seminar, two colleagues—Jordan and Riley—debated the reach of I.J. Good’s result that a rational Bayesian agent should never refuse costless information before making a decision. Jordan began with the canonical argument: since the agent can always ignore information that does not change her optimal action, and since conditioning on new data can only raise or preserve expected utility relative to acting now, turning down free evidence is strictly irrational. Riley objected with a carefully constructed case: suppose acquiring a particular medical fact does not merely update beliefs about outcomes but also restructures the agent’s priorities—discovering a genetic risk might shift her values from career advancement to family care in a way she could not have anticipated or mapped onto prior outcome-states. Jordan replied that Good’s framework can absorb this via state-dependent utility: one simply encodes the post-learning preference ordering into the prior decision tree, and the non-negativity result survives intact. Riley countered that this reply treats the preference shift as just another exogenous outcome-state, whereas in her example the utility function itself changes endogenously, triggered by the epistemic act of learning, which violates the theorem’s assumption that preferences remain stable across the information-partition. Jordan then pressed that if the shift is genuinely unpredictable ex ante, it cannot rationalize refusing information—at best it reveals that ignorance might be valued as an end in itself. Riley answered that ignorance can also be instrumentally protected: acquiring certain knowledge can generate unavoidable moral obligations—learning a colleague’s confidential error, for instance, might impose disclosure burdens the agent wishes to avoid—so refusing information can safeguard other values without requiring ignorance to be a terminal good.
