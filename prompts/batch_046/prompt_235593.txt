In my philosophy-of-mind reading group—mostly people who came up through rationalist forums and are now trying to read Sartre seriously—we have hit a stall that feels structurally identical to the old free-will debates, but mapped onto existentialist ontology. We all accept Sartre’s thesis that consciousness is condemned to be free, and we all accept that people routinely disavow that freedom through what he calls bad faith (mauvaise foi). The disagreement is not about whether bad faith happens, or even about whether structural oppression, trauma, or cognitive bias constrain action; everyone grants those realities. Instead, the group has split into two camps that have not moved in months. One camp reads radical freedom as a claim about reflective, second-order endorsement: freedom is exercised only when a conscious project is explicitly chosen or ratified, and bad faith is therefore a monitoring failure—something like running a mental script that avoids acknowledging you authored it. The other camp reads freedom as an ontological feature of the for-itself at every level, including pre-reflective, System-1 habits and automatic emotional responses; on this view, you are still radically free in your anger or your avoidance, and bad faith is not a failure of oversight but a first-order self-deception in which consciousness pretends to be an in-itself precisely at the level of affect and habit. Both sides cite the same Sartrean distinctions—facticity versus transcendence, the look, the project—and both agree on the phenomenology; neither denies that people feel unfree.

My working hypothesis is that this is not a textual dispute about Sartre at all, but a disagreement about which question is being asked: one side is treating freedom as a normative concept tied to moral responsibility and reflective agency, while the other treats it as a structural claim about consciousness that holds regardless of cognitive architecture. But I am unsure whether there is a hidden empirical question doing the work—whether automatic processes are genuinely authored in any meaningful sense—or whether the sides are simply answering different questions under the single heading of radical freedom. I want a diagnosis of the disagreement’s actual structure: is it verbal, substantive, empirical, or grounded in bedrock intuitions about responsibility that neither side can argue for? And I want it supported by what each camp actually commits to, not by a survey of Sartre scholarship. Finally, given your diagnosis, what would count as progress? Would it take a conceptual distinction between synchronic and diachronic freedom, an empirical result about the architecture of cognitive control, a clarification of Sartre’s ontology of the pre-reflective cogito, or should we conclude that the question is ill-posed and move on?
