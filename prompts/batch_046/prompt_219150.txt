As a novelist constructing philosophical fiction that resists easy categorization, I ask you to adopt the dual role of professional moral philosopher and literary craftsman. Before writing anything, think carefully and proceed in two stages. First, spend a brief moment brainstorming the narrative architecture: identify a protagonist in an ordinary institutional role—perhaps a supply-chain auditor or a clinical-trial coordinator—who makes a decision that appears morally straightforward in isolation. Then conceive a hidden intermediary, such as an embedded automated protocol or an unrecognized collective decision procedure, whose independent ethical or causal states actually determine the outcome. The core concept is a subtle, under-discussed variant of moral luck—call it fragmented distributive luck—where moral desert is not merely affected by results, circumstances, constitution, or causation, but is actively redistributed across these concealed mediators, leaving the original agent's praise or blame in ambiguous suspension. Once you have outlined the protagonist's choice, the hidden system, and the retrospective moment of revelation, write a polished short story of approximately 600 to 800 words that renders this idea through concrete dramatic action rather than exposition. The narrative should ask whether the protagonist's moral standing should be diluted, transferred, or preserved intact once the distributed causal structure is exposed, and the conclusion should remain genuinely open. Please avoid any bibliographies, citations, references to specific named texts, or appeals to academic authority; let all philosophical reasoning emerge from the characters' own reflections and the situation itself.
