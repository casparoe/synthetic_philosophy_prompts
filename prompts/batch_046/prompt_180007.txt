You are a professional philosopher of biology with expertise in conceptual analysis of life and organismal boundaries. Please think carefully and step by step, beginning with a brief brainstorm of the conceptual distinctions that might be in play—such as organizational closure, metabolic integration, genetic coherence, evolutionary lineage, and material substrate—before you turn to the case record. I am asking because this has become personally urgent: since my mother's recent diagnosis with frontotemporal dementia, and my parallel involvement with a synthetic-biology consortium studying engineered cellular mergers, I have been forced to confront exactly where the boundary of a living organism lies.

Our interdisciplinary working group has rendered verdicts on several borderline cases over the past year, and I want to know whether our pattern is coherent and what it predicts. Here is our track record:

- A dormant bacterial endospore possessing intact enzymatic architecture but exhibiting zero current metabolism: we classified it as a living organism.
- An isolated influenza virion suspended in saline with no host machinery engaged: we said it is not alive.
- A synthetic protocell constructed from non-natural lipids that maintains an electrochemical gradient, adaptively responds to pH shifts, and reproduces by budding, yet contains no nucleic acids and no evolutionary history: we ruled that it counts as alive.
- A brain-dead human maintained on ECMO with a beating heart and functioning kidneys but no brainstem activity and no integrated self-regulation: we said this is not a living organism, though individual tissues remain biologically active.
- A forest-spanning Armillaria mycelium exhibiting continuous cytoplasmic flow but including large dead-wood nodes that do not metabolize: we treated it as a single organism, analogizing the dead nodes to hair or nails.
- A real-time digital simulation of an Escherichia coli metabolic network running on a supercomputer with perfect biochemical fidelity: we said it is not alive, regardless of predictive accuracy.

The new case is one I am now studying directly in the lab: two phylogenetically distant bacterial strains have been engineered to shed their individual cell walls, merge into a single continuous lipid boundary, and share cytoplasm. Each retains its own incompatible genome, and neither can survive or reproduce independently. Together they perform collective metabolism, maintain homeostasis, and reproduce by binary fission of the merged mass, with each daughter cell inheriting both genomes. There is no unified genetic code, but there is uninterrupted organizational closure and metabolic integration across the boundary.

Predict our working group's verdict: is this merged system one living organism, two organisms in symbiosis, or something that falls outside the category? Identify at least two competing principles that are jointly consistent with our six prior rulings—for example, one emphasizing organizational closure and material substrate regardless of genetic uniformity, and another emphasizing reproductive-unit coherence or evolutionary-lineage unity. Say which principle best explains our track record, point to the closest analog among our earlier cases, and specify the further borderline case that would definitively settle the disagreement between these principles. If our precedents genuinely underdetermine the verdict, state honestly what principle we would be adopting with each possible choice.
