For my philosophy of history seminar I am drafting a position statement on presentism and want a consistency audit before I present it. Here is where I stand: (a) historians ought to avoid anachronistic moral judgment, evaluating past societies by standards they could not have held; (b) nevertheless, some practices—chattel slavery, genocide—are wrong in ways that transcend historical context, and historians should not sanitize them through excessive contextualism; (c) all historical inquiry is ultimately shaped by present concerns, since the selection of archives, problems, and narratives has no "view from nowhere"; (d) genuinely understanding past actors requires conceptual empathy, a disciplined suspension of our own categories to grasp how they understood themselves; (e) yet historical explanation is impossible without comparative categories—"economy," "power," "gender"—that we import from the present, and refusing all comparison collapses into mere antiquarianism; (f) the ultimate purpose of studying history includes furnishing resources for present moral and political reflection. I do not know which of these is the outlier. Go through the pairs and tell me which commitments genuinely pull against each other, which only appear to conflict but can be reconciled, and for each real tension how severe it is and what the cheapest revision would cost. Rank the tensions by severity rather than treating them as equal, and then identify the single commitment whose revision or abandonment would restore the most coherence to the rest of the package. Be specific about whether the problem lies between evaluation and explanation, between present motivation and past understanding, or elsewhere.
