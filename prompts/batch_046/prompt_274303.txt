I have constructed an argument about Bernard Williams's philosophy of opera, and I am concerned that it proves too much. (1) Williams maintains that opera can communicate serious moral and emotional truths precisely because of, rather than despite, its extreme artificiality—characters singing at length, implausible dramatic coincidences, and deaths expressed through arias rather than realistic action. (2) He further maintains that this artificiality is not a defect to be minimized but an enabling condition that defines the medium's distinctive access to truth. (3) If (1) and (2) hold, then the criteria of credibility and moral plausibility that govern artistic representation are not universal but are relative to the conventions of each medium; what would be a damaging implausibility in realist prose may be neutral or even necessary in opera. (4) Yet if credibility conditions are genuinely media-relative in this way, then any representational form—political propaganda, fantasy narrative, ritual performance—can insulate itself from moral criticism merely by declaring its own artificial conventions essential to its truth-claims. (5) Williams clearly rejects that permissive conclusion; he distinguishes opera's structured artifice from mere escapism or manipulation. (6) So either (3) is false and there are universal credibility conditions after all, which undermines (2), or (5) depends on an unstated restriction that is incompatible with the media-relativity asserted in (3). Which step should I reject, and what does that rejection cost for Williams's broader aesthetic and moral theory? Please write your answer in American English.
