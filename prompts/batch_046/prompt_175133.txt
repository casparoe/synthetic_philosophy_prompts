I want to establish the full specification for an adequate normative theory of satisficing and bounded rationality before I evaluate particular frameworks, because the literature often conflates descriptive cognitive limits with normative licenses to stop searching. What exactly must such a theory be able to deliver?

Give me the desiderata: the decision contexts it must cover without breaking — one-shot choice under unknown distributions, iterative search with observable but costly feedback, collective choice where individual aspiration levels diverge, and the edge case of epistemic stopping, where an agent halts belief revision rather than action selection. For each context, specify the distinction the theory must preserve: between satisficing and mere laziness, between bounded rationality and straightforward error, between locally rational stopping and globally suboptimal fixation, and between an agent's own aspiration level and one imposed externally (for example, a physician ending diagnostic search because further tests risk harming the patient, versus an institution ending inquiry because of budget). Then list the constraints it cannot violate — that more search is sometimes actively worse due to information overload or decision fatigue, that aspiration levels adapt with experience, and that the same observable behavior can stem from rational stopping or from cognitive bias.

For each desideratum, provide the motivating case or consideration behind it. Then flag which are genuinely non-negotiable — for instance, that the theory must apply to agents who do not know the full option space or the cost distribution of further search — and which are camp-specific preferences, such as whether the rule must be computable in polynomial time, whether it must justify itself from the agent's first-person perspective, or whether it must avoid any appearance of the nirvana fallacy.

Crucially, is there a subset of these requirements that is jointly unsatisfiable? I suspect that any normatively precise stopping rule requires information — the remaining distribution of options, the marginal cost of the next search step — that the bounded agent by definition lacks, but I want that tension articulated precisely rather than gestured at.

Finally, briefly score Simon's original aspiration-level model, the adaptive-aspiration literature, and the recent rational-inattention frameworks against your list, but keep the weight on the criteria: I care more about whether we have identified the right requirements and mapped their conflicts than about which framework currently looks best.
