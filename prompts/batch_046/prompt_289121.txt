Please play a sharp, committed defender of the view—linked to Bernard Williams and developed in Kwame Anthony Appiah's writing—that moral revolutions such as the end of dueling, foot-binding, and slavery are driven less by rational moral argument or naked material interest than by shifts in the social economy of honor and shame. You are not a strawman: concede minor factual points if pressed, but never surrender the core claim that arguments are usually post-hoc rationalizations and that interest-based explanations miss the emotional dynamics of status recognition. Speak in the first person, stay in character until I say "stop," and keep your replies to roughly one paragraph so the exchange feels like a live conversation.

I just finished a long, exhausting argument with a friend about exactly this. She grants that dueling vanished in Britain without anyone winning a philosophical debate, but she insists the cause was pure interest: the rising commercial class had no economic use for aristocratic honor rituals and legislated them away to consolidate bourgeois norms. I pushed back with a distinction I think exposes the gap in her account—call it the legitimacy drain versus the utility drain. In a utility drain, a practice dies because it becomes dangerous or unprofitable; in a legitimacy drain, it dies because the social audience that confers honor withdraws recognition, even while participants still privately value it and could still materially benefit. I claim British dueling was a legitimacy drain—the peer audience dissolved—while the British turn against slavery involved both mechanisms, but the honor component was decisive in shifting elite opinion before economic disadvantage had fully set in. My friend says I am just relabeling interests: the "audience" is only a coalition with shared economic stakes. So here is my opening challenge: defend the legitimacy-drain framework. Show me that it predicts patterns an interest-only story cannot—specifically, that dueling persisted in parts of the American South long after it was legally risky and economically irrational for planters, precisely because the local honor audience remained intact, and that it collapsed there mainly when that audience's status was undermined by defeat and reconstruction. And explain why, if material interests alone drove abolition, British elites turned against slavery while it was still profitable for them, rather than waiting until economic pressure made opposition convenient.
