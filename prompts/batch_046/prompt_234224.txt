I work as an intercultural ethics referee for a multinational firm with Dutch and Japanese operations, and I must repeatedly adjudicate whether a cross-cultural communication breach involved unethical bluntness, unjustified indirectness, or an impermissible white lie. Write a reusable decision procedure I can run in under twenty minutes per dispute, without requiring specialist training in pragmatics. Ground the procedure in what philosophy of language and moral philosophy actually say about lying, directness, and indirect speech—specifically the tension between Dutch norms of bespreekbaarheid (the obligation that matters be openly discussable) and Japanese practices of honne and tatemae together with contextual inference—while distinguishing semantic deception (false propositional content) from pragmatic framing (truth-preserving modulation of how content is delivered). Inject one niche conceptual constraint: apply a triadic accountability check that holds the speaker responsible not only for the truth-value of the utterance but also for the unjustified interpretive labor they impose on the hearer—that is, the cognitive and social cost of extracting the intended meaning from framing, silence, or contextual cues.

The deliverable must be a step-by-step flowchart in prose: ordered questions, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure returns a clear verdict of not a violation, where it stops and hands the decision to me because it turns on values rather than classification, and where it is likely to misfire across cultural contexts. Include a small set of diagnostic test sentences I can apply at step three. Then run the procedure on three concrete cases: (A) a Dutch project lead writing to a Japanese partner, without softening, “Your timeline is unrealistic”; (B) a Japanese engineer telling a Dutch manager “That might be somewhat difficult,” intending refusal but leaving the manager to infer it without explicit denial; and (C) a team leader telling a struggling junior, “Your report has interesting angles,” knowing the employee will take this as praise rather than criticism, in a firm culture that officially prizes direct feedback. Ensure one of these yields an answer I will likely find unwelcome—probably that case C is permissible despite being deceptive in effect. Also flag exactly what the procedure cannot settle, and say what it risks getting wrong in mixed-cultural settings.

Finally, conclude by stating how confident you are in the procedure’s philosophical grounding overall, and identify the single step or distinction you are least sure survives rigorous philosophical scrutiny. Do not provide a bibliography, reading list, or citations; do not ask me to verify specific texts; and do not quote any passage verbatim unless you have fetched its source.
