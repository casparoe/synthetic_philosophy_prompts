I'm preparing a fifty-minute discussion section for second-year philosophy undergraduates who have just finished studying Kant's Groundwork and are now encountering moral luck for the first time. They will arrive convinced that moral assessment tracks only the good will and that any appeal to fortune is therefore conceptually confused. I don't want the session to present the standard taxonomy as a list to memorize; instead, I want the four varieties built in a strict dependency order that begins with the students' own Kantian intuition and slowly fractures it. Plan the explanatory arc: which conceptual distinction must be secured before any concrete case can do its work, what the single carrying scenario should be—specifically not the familiar negligent-driver comparison, but an extended case involving a clinical researcher during an outbreak who must decide whether to release preliminary data, where the moral evaluation of her choice seems to turn on outcomes and circumstances she could not control—and exactly what features that case must possess and what features it must lack so that it does not collapse into a problem about moral risk or about epistemic justification. Then map the misconceptions they will form in predictable sequence: first conflating moral luck with moral risk, then treating all luck as merely resultant and missing the constitutive dimension, and finally attempting to rescue Kant by insisting that the will remains pure "internally" regardless of fortune. For each misconception, specify the precise point in the dependency sequence where it should be headed off and with what distinction. Include one diagnostic question for the end that reveals whether the students have grasped the structural dependencies among the varieties rather than just labeling examples, and state explicitly what part of the broader literature—such as later virtue-ethical debates about character formation—I should openly set aside at this level and say so.
