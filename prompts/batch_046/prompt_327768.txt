As a novelist constructing characters whose decisions must feel both psychologically credible and philosophically defensible, I have been turning over an argument about bounded rationality that seems to prove that no human choice can ever be rational. Here are the steps:

(1) Rational choice requires selecting the option with the highest expected value from the full feasible set of alternatives.
(2) A satisficing agent employs sequential search governed by an endogenous aspiration level that adapts upward when perceived search costs are low and downward when they are high, terminating immediately once an option exceeds the current threshold.
(3) Because termination is cost-sensitive, some non-empty portion of the feasible set always remains unexamined.
(4) Given only the agent's epistemic position at the moment of termination, the global maximum could consistently lie within that unexamined portion.
(5) Therefore, after any act of satisficing, the agent's knowledge is compatible with having missed the best available option.
(6) To be rational is not merely to succeed in selecting the best option, but to occupy an epistemic position that justifies the claim that one has done so; without that justification, the choice lacks rational warrant.
(7) Hence every satisficing agent necessarily fails to satisfy the conditions for rational choice, regardless of how well-calibrated the aspiration mechanism is.
(8) Since all actual human cognition is bounded, rational choice is impossible for real agents.

The conclusion strikes me as wrong—surely bounded rationality is not self-refuting—but I cannot locate the slip. I would most like to preserve (2) and (6), since my characters need both realistic cognitive limits and the capacity to defend their choices. Which premise or step should I reject? Is the error an equivocation between the feasible set and the search set, a modal slide from epistemic possibility to actual failure, or an unjustified addition in (6) that transforms rationality from success into justified success? Explain why the targeted step looked plausible, and price the cost of abandoning it: in particular, does rejecting (6) reduce rationality to mere luck, and does rejecting (4) require implausible assumptions about what agents can know about unexamined options?
