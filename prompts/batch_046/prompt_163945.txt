Exam question: In about 350 words, compare how institutional incentives distorted collective judgment in three Athenian democratic failures—the Mytilene debate of 427 BCE, the decision to launch and then reinforce the Sicilian Expedition of 415-413 BCE, and the trial and execution of Socrates in 399 BCE. Your answer should identify one structural feature—something about how democratic institutions process, preserve, or discard reasons—that is common to all three cases, and explain why that feature makes democratic deliberation vulnerable to a specific kind of reasoning error that is not reducible to emotional excess or demagoguery.

Student response:
The Mytilene debate of 427 BCE illustrates institutional compression. After the first assembly voted to execute all male citizens of Mytilene, a second ship was sent with a reversal because Diodotus persuaded the Athenians that killing everyone would undermine their strategic position. Thucydides records both speeches, and in his Archaeology he notes that democratic bodies lack the institutional memory to preserve reasoning across sessions, which is why the second assembly treated mercy as a fresh decision rather than a revision of the first. The assembly had no archive of its original fear—rebellion—so the strategic calculus disappeared.

In Sicily, the same gap appears. Alcibiades convinced the assembly in 415 to approve the expedition with optimistic projections, then defected. When Nicias urged withdrawal after the first defeats, the assembly back in Athens refused and voted to send reinforcements under Demosthenes. The generals in the field then stayed partly because of the lunar eclipse, but mainly because they had inherited command without inheriting the strategic conditions—limited objectives, local alliances—that had originally justified the campaign. The democratic body in Athens was operating with compressed information.

The trial of Socrates in 399 BCE shows the feature in judicial form. The 501 jurors heard charges of impiety—failing to recognize the city's gods and introducing new deities—and corrupting the youth. Because the court procedure allowed no preservation of prior philosophical disputes, the prosecution's reasoning was compressed into these formulas. The assembly acted as both accuser and jury, allowing political anxiety to become a capital verdict. Socrates refused to propose any counter-penalty, so the execution followed automatically. This demonstrates that without deliberative archiving, procedural compliance becomes dangerous.

The common feature is "deliberative archiving failure"—no mechanism preserves the reasoning chain behind prior decisions. This creates framing independence: each decision-point treats the problem as new. The vulnerability is not emotional excess—Cleon's argument for severity was actually the rational calculation of deterrence, while Diodotus appealed to compassion—but a structural failure that allows reasoning to be replaced rather than revised.
