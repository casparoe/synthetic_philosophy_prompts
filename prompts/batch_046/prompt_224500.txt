Two colleagues in my philosophy of language reading group, Rina and Jonas, became deadlocked in an argument about pragmatic encroachment and epistemic contextualism, and they asked me to relay the exchange back for an impartial adjudication, since neither would concede.

The case they were debating concerns Dr. Elena Voss, a museum conservator who has examined a disputed seventeenth-century letter. Chemical and paleographic analysis are consistent with authenticity but do not reach definitive proof—roughly a seventy-five percent confidence level. The museum must decide whether to include the letter in a high-profile exhibition opening in two weeks. Voss tells the curator: “I do not know with certainty that it is genuine, but I am satisfied enough to display it with a standard attribution note.”

Rina argued that this illustrates pragmatic encroachment. The practical stakes of displaying the letter are moderate—reputational risk, but nothing life-or-death—so Voss’s evidential position is sufficient for her to know the letter is authentic in this practical context, even though the same evidence would not suffice were the museum being sued for fraud or were the letter being introduced as legal evidence. On Rina’s view, practical stakes genuinely raise or lower the epistemic threshold required for knowledge.

Jonas replied that contextualism explains the same phenomenon more cleanly. In the conversation with the curator about exhibition planning, the conversational context sets professional, cautious, but non-forensic standards for “knowing.” Thus Voss genuinely knows, not because stakes altered the conditions for knowledge, but because the context determines the semantic content of “knows” in that speech situation. Were the same curator to ask in a deposition under oath whether she “knows” it is real, the word would carry stricter standards because the conversational context had changed.

Rina then pressed what she called the “asymmetric stakes test.” She imagined two simultaneous conversations in the same room: Voss is speaking with the curator about the exhibition (moderate stakes), while across the room a lawyer asks her, in a preliminary inquiry, whether she knows the letter is authentic for potential litigation (high stakes). The conversational contexts are both serious and professional, yet the stakes differ. Rina claimed that pragmatic encroachment predicts Voss should withhold a knowledge-attribution in the legal exchange but allow it in the curatorial one, even if both use the same word “know.” Contextualism, she argued, struggles here because there is no clear conversational difference to drive the shift; yet the stakes clearly ought to make a difference.

Jonas responded that contextualism can accommodate this by noting that the relevant context is not merely “professional” but includes the purpose of the inquiry—exhibition planning versus legal liability—so the standards shift with the purpose embedded in the context, not with the stakes per se. He then accused pragmatic encroachment of an unacceptable metaphysics: Voss’s brain states and evidence are identical in both conversations; only the external practical environment changes. That makes knowledge depend on factors external to the subject’s cognitive position, turning knowledge into something closer to a license to act than an epistemic achievement.

Rina countered that Jonas’s contextualism also depends on external factors—namely, the conversational environment—so both views have external dependencies. The real difference, she said, is normative: pragmatic encroachment ties knowledge to responsible action-guidance, while contextualism ties it primarily to appropriate assertion. She then introduced a distinction she labeled “epistemic entitlement versus epistemic achievement,” arguing that contextualism conflates the two by treating knowledge mainly as a matter of when we are entitled to say we know, whereas pragmatic encroachment treats knowledge as genuinely affected by what is at risk for the subject.

Jonas said that distinction is artificial. If knowledge is not fundamentally about appropriate assertion conditions, he asked, then it becomes unclear why we care about attributing it in language at all. He added that pragmatic encroachment produces counterintuitive results in low-stakes, high-evidence cases—such as an ordinary person knowing she has hands—because if practical stakes are genuinely negligible (say, in a philosophical thought experiment), the view might seem to lower the epistemic bar so far that skeptical pressure never arises, which in turn undermines the very intuition that high stakes can raise the bar in other cases.

Rina concluded by saying Jonas was conflating “stakes are high” with “stakes are relevant to the belief-formation process.” She proposed that pragmatic encroachment applies only when the stakes affect how carefully the subject actually formed or maintains the belief—not whenever there is merely an external risk. So in a thought experiment with zero practical concern, the belief-formation process is unchanged, and the standards do not drop; they remain at the baseline.

Please adjudicate this disagreement point by point: who is right about the asymmetric stakes test, about the metaphysical objection, about the entitlement-versus-achievement distinction, and about the low-stakes counterexample? Say also who is more right overall, and identify anything important that both parties are missing—particularly regarding how their respective views handle cases where the subject herself is unaware of the practical stakes or where the conversational purpose and the practical stakes pull in opposite directions. Do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; address only the arguments as actually presented.
