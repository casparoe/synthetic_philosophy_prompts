I'm trying to pin down a weird asymmetry I keep seeing in how archeologists reason about object meaning. Say you've got these small ceramic discs — flat, with a hole in the middle — turning up in burial sites alongside obvious grave goods. The standard move is to say they must be ritual tokens, maybe encoding some cosmologial idea about cycles or passage. But then the exact same disc shape turns up in kitchen middens, and instead of saying "maybe they were just spindle whorls or weights all along," people say the disc was "profaned" or "recontextualized" for daily use. Meanwhile, if you only ever found them in kitchens, nobody would argue they secretly had burial meaning that was just hidden. So the ritual context gets veto power over interpretation, but the mundane context doesn't. Is there an established name for this one-way ratchet — where symbolic meaning can be added by context but never subtracted? And how is it different from plain confirmation bias, or is that realy all it is? I don't need citations or a bibliography, just want to know if I'm describing something with its own edges, or if I should call it "asymmetric sacralization" and move on. Also, does it relate at all to how people talk about "functional ambiguity" in material culture, or is that a seperate thing entirely?
