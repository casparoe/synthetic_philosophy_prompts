I've been trying to formalize a tension I keep running into when I think about recursive self-improvement, and I've built myself into a strange loop. Here is the argument.

(1) Any system that achieves a complete representation of itself must include an inviolate level that lies outside the system—this is the lesson of Escher's Drawing Hands, in which each hand draws the other, but only Escher's own hand exists outside the frame and breaks the loop.

(2) Rational self-improvement is a system that applies norms of reasoning and belief-revision to reasoning itself; in other words, it attempts a complete normative representation of its own operations.

(3) Therefore, any fully consistent framework for rational self-improvement must contain an inviolate meta-level of norms that is not itself subject to those norms.

(4) If such a meta-level exists, then there is an epistemic ceiling—a layer of reasoning that can never be rationally evaluated, revised, or justified from within.

(5) But the rationalist project, as I understand it, denies that any belief or norm is permanently shielded: in principle everything is open to revision.

(6) So either rational self-improvement is impossible, or the analogy in (1)–(3) collapses when applied to normative systems.

I want to keep (1) and (5) if I can. The slip feels like it happens between (3) and (4)—maybe I'm conflating representational incompleteness with normative immunity—but I can't pin it down. Which step should be rejected, and what does rejecting it cost? In particular, can one preserve the idea of an inviolate level in descriptive hierarchies without generating an unexaminable epistemic ceiling in normative ones?
