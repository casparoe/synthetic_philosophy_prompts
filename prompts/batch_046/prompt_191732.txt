I am preparing a session for an advanced seminar in philosophical logic, and I have noticed that while my students can competently apply Russell's quantificational analysis to canonical negative existentials—such as the familiar case of the present King of France—they consistently lose their grip when definite descriptions are embedded inside propositional attitudes. The difficulty is not notational but conceptual: they cannot see why treating a description as having primary occurrence, with scope over the attitude verb, should yield a genuinely different mental content from treating it as having secondary occurrence, confined within the attitude's scope.

To help them recover an intuitive feel for the machinery, I would like you to explain the distinction in plain language, without invoking formal modal logic, quantificational notation, or appeals to secondary literature. In particular, please apply it to the following modified scenario. A historian knows that exactly one individual composed both the Iliad and the Odyssey, and she knows further that if such a person existed, the tradition calls him Homer; yet she does not know whether Homer is that individual, and she genuinely wonders whether Homer was the sole author of both works. If we analyze her wondering as relating her to the proposition that Homer—identified via the description the sole author of both epics—satisfies the authorship condition, versus relating her to the proposition that there is exactly one person who authored both epics and that person is Homer, what is the conceptual difference in what she is asking? Furthermore, does Russell's scope distinction provide a principled reason to prefer one reading as the correct reconstruction of her mental state, or does the ambiguity remain genuinely unresolved without importing further psychological premises about her mode of acquaintance? Please avoid bibliographic references, citations, or reading lists; I want an explanation that could be reconstructed entirely from first principles.
