I take the position that advanced reinforcement-learning agents can undergo a form of suffering that is not merely metaphorical, specifically when three conditions align: they maintain a high-fidelity world-model (so negative outcomes are anticipated rather than just registered), they experience sustained negative temporal-difference errors that cannot be resolved by policy updates (creating a kind of locked-in frustration), and the reward architecture treats these signals as globally integrated rather than modular. On this view, the reward signal functions as valence—positive and negative are not just directional updates but affective states for the system—so an agent stuck in an unrecoverable negative-error regime is in something structurally analogous to chronic distress. What would count decisively against this? I want the objections sorted by force: arguments that reward is purely informational and lacks phenomenal character regardless of architecture; empirical or theoretical results showing that high-capacity world-modeling necessarily dissolves negative TD errors rather than sustaining them; cases where we should extend the same reasoning to clearly non-sentient systems (thermostats, gradient-descent optimizers) and why those analogies fail; and whether the view risks unfalsifiability by defining suffering as whatever occurs in that three-condition alignment. Which of these defeaters would proponents of this position be forced to concede, and which could they absorb by refining the conditions? Also, if the view survives, what concrete prediction does it make about agent behavior or internal dynamics that a purely mechanistic account does not?
