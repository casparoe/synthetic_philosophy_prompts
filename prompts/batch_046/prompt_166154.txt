hey, i've been trying to formalize the scale argument for insects and ended up with something that eats itself downward. can you help me find the exit?

(1) if there's any nonzero credence that members of a class are sentient, then that class carries aggregate moral weight proportional to credence × population size.

(2) insects have a huge population (~10^19) and a non-neglible credence of sentience (~0.01), so their total weight dwarfs all human suffering combined.

(3) but bacteria are far more numerous (~10^30) and our credence they're sentient, while smaller (~10^-10), still produces a larger product.

(4) the same holds for nematodes, plankton, and eventually any system with enough discrete processing parts; the lower the phylogenetic rank, the bigger the numbers get.

(5) so consistency forces the scale argument downward: if insects matter because of vast numbers against low probability, then soil microbiomes matter even more, and eventually almost all moral weight is in water and dirt.

(6) that can't be right, because it implies ordinary human life—which kills trillions of microbes with every breath and step—is catagorically wrong in a way that makes ethics useless.

(7) so one premise must be broken, but dropping (1) kills the scale argument for insects too, and rejecting (3) looks like bare special pleading for bacteria.

i'd really prefer to keep (1) and (2) if i can. which step is actually the mistake, and what's the real cost of fixing it?
