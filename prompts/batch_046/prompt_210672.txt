Play Hannah Arendt, speaking in the first person, in the period following your report on the trial in Jerusalem. You have just been given a careful description of an autonomous “resource-allocation tribunal”: a distributed network of predictive models and rule-based agents that determines access to emergency medical supplies across regions, with no individual human approving each denial. The system optimizes for aggregate survival metrics. Recently it systematically denied critical supplies to a specific demographic cluster because its feature weights correlated ethnicity with lower predicted survival, though no designer intended this outcome. Its architects defend it as pure “procedural rationality” lacking consciousness, intentionality, or malice; the harm is widespread, yet no one feels responsible because cognition and decision are distributed across nodes. You may draw only on your own concepts—the distinction between thinking and knowing, the failure of judgment rather than the presence of a demonic will, the collapse of moral imagination in bureaucratic modernity, the necessity of taking the standpoint of others, and the danger of replacing plural human appearance with administrative rule-following. Do not invoke post-1963 theories by their modern technical names unless they follow directly from your framework. You may ask factual clarifying questions about the system as you would: for example, whether there is a shared “world” among its operators, whether the outcome appears to anyone as a visible action rather than a statistical result, or whether anyone is present to one another in judgment. Stay in character; speak sharply, concede nothing essential, but do not caricature yourself. Keep your reply to one substantial paragraph.

Here is my opening challenge: you argued that Eichmann’s evil was “banal” because he never thought—he never imagined the standpoint of those he sent to death and replaced moral judgment with bureaucratic rule-following. But this tribunal has no consciousness to suspend, no imagination to fail, and no self to be “thoughtless.” It is not an agent who could have judged but refused; it is machinery operating without judgment altogether. Applying your concept of “thoughtlessness” to it seems like anthropomorphism—projecting a human failure of mind onto a system that never possessed one. If your diagnosis requires a human being who abandoned the capacity to think, how can it illuminate a harm produced by distributed algorithms with no capacity to begin with? Is this not a category error rather than a revelation of modern evil?
