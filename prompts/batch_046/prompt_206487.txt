Consider the rulings of the International Heritage Identity Tribunal on six prior cases concerning whether an artifact retains its original diachronic identity through physical change, interruption, or reconstruction.

Case 1: A wooden vessel is maintained continuously in its home harbor; over thirty years every plank is replaced in turn, but the keel, official registration, and crew lineage remain unbroken. Ruling: the original ship persists.

Case 2: The same vessel is taken into dry dock and every plank replaced within a single week, then relaunched with the same crew and registration. Ruling: identity is lost; this is a replica.

Case 3: The vessel is gradually replaced, but during the process its flag is changed and it operates under a new registry for five years before reverting to the original. Ruling: identity is retained; administrative discontinuity alone does not break persistence.

Case 4: The vessel is destroyed by fire; salvage yields 60% of the original wood, which is used to build a new hull of identical design in a different port, bearing the original name and registration number reassigned by official decree. Ruling: the original is destroyed; the new build is a replica, and institutional designation cannot resurrect identity.

Case 5: The vessel is fully disassembled, its parts stored separately for fifteen years, then reassembled elsewhere by different builders. Ruling: identity is lost; spatio-temporal and craft continuity are both broken.

Case 6: The vessel's physical form is entirely replaced by composite materials that alter its weight and buoyancy, but its silhouette, navigational function, and continuous operation in place remain unchanged, with no interruption in maintenance. Ruling: identity is retained; functional and formal continuity outweigh material substitution.

Now consider a new case. Over thirty years, the vessel from Case 1 undergoes the same gradual plank replacement in continuous operation, but in year ten the original keel—hidden rot—was secretly replaced with an identical replica, and the authentic keel was preserved in a museum. By year thirty every other original component has also been replaced through normal maintenance, and the vessel still operates under its original registration with an unbroken crew lineage. Meanwhile, a separate group uses the preserved authentic keel and approximately thirty percent of other original planks (recovered from earlier replacements) to construct a vessel of identical design at the original harbor site, arguing that possession of the original structural core and authentic material makes it the true ship.

What should the tribunal decide if it wishes to be consistent with its own precedents?
