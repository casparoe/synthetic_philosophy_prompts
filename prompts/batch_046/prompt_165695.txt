In historical-Jesus scholarship there is a standardized practice of grading gospel pericopes with “criteria of authenticity”—multiple attestation, dissimilarity to early Judaism or the church, embarrassment to the church, coherence with other material, and sometimes linguistic traces—then mapping the results onto a color scale (red, pink, gray, black) that is treated as measuring how probable it is that Jesus said or did the thing reported. The same researchers usually treat these criteria as supplying independent evidential weights, yet they were formulated inside source-critical traditions that already assumed the texts are layered compilations of communal invention over a separable historical core. At the same time, the “criterion of embarrassment” presupposes a uniform church psychology eager to suppress awkward details, while “dissimilarity” presupposes we can cleanly distinguish church invention from preservation—two pictures of collective agency that sit uneasily together. What theory of historical knowledge does this grading practice presuppose? Does it require that the colors represent objective frequencies or propensities, or could the scale survive as a purely subjectivist confidence ordering? Is the practice committed to an ontology in which a single “historical Jesus” is recoverable beneath the texts, or is it compatible with social-memory approaches that deny any kernel separable from communal performance? Reconstruct the implicit epistemology, distinguish what the procedure strictly needs from what merely accompanies it, and say what the grading system would have to become if its hidden probabilism were replaced by a coherence-only narrative model or by a view that treats the gospels as entirely non-referential communal acts.
