Consider the following argument, which seems to demonstrate that causal-role theories of biological function necessarily collapse into etiological ones.

(1) On the causal-role view, the function of a trait is the contribution it currently makes to the organism's survival or reproductive success.

(2) On the etiological view, the function is the effect for which the trait was historically selected, even if it no longer produces that effect.

(3) Imagine a deep-sea anglerfish lineage in which a bioluminescent lure was selected for attracting prey. In a recently diverged population, a single mutation reroutes the pathway so that the organ secretes a defensive toxin rather than emitting light; the toxin now repels predators and contributes far more to fitness than the original luminescence ever did. The causal-role theorist must say that the organ's function is now toxin secretion, though no evolutionary selection for toxicity has yet occurred. The etiologist maintains that its function remains prey attraction, despite the fact that it never performs it.

(4) This result looks intolerable for causal-role: normative function—what the trait is supposed to do—appears to have flipped instantaneously with a mutation, before evolutionary history could ratify the change. A theory that allows functional reassignment prior to any selective feedback seems to confuse accidental effect with genuine purpose.

(5) Now apply the same reasoning to artifacts. A paperclip is manufactured to hold papers. If I use it once to reset a router, the causal-role theorist—if consistent—should say its function is now router-resetting. But we ordinarily resist this; we hold that its design history fixes its function regardless of my occasional use. By parity, evolutionary design history should fix biological function regardless of recent mutations.

(6) The causal-role theorist therefore faces a dilemma. Either she accepts that artifacts have no stable functions (since any novel use instantly revises them), or she concedes that current contribution is insufficient for functional attribution and that some historical condition—selection or design—is required to privilege one contribution over another.

(7) Once that historical condition is admitted as necessary, however, the causal-role view has abandoned its defining claim: that current causal contribution alone determines function.

(8) Consequently, causal-role theories either fail to distinguish function from accidental utility, or they implicitly depend on etiological conditions and are not genuinely distinct accounts.

The reasoning feels like it proves too much: it suggests that anyone who takes present contribution seriously is committed to an unstable, observer-relative ontology of function. I suspect the error lies either in treating evolutionary history as strictly analogous to artifact design, in smuggling a normative premise into the mutation case, or in assuming that "instantaneous" functional change is conceptually incoherent rather than merely surprising. Which step should be rejected, and what does rejecting it cost? Specifically, if you defend the causal-role position, do you deny the artifact analogy, deny that normativity requires historical ratification, or accept that functions can indeed shift faster than selection? And if you defend the etiological view, do you accept the counterintuitive implication that a completely useless vestigial trait still carries full normative weight—i.e., that there is a determinate sense in which it is failing to do what it ought to do?
