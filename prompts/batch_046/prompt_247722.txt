I serve on the Clinical Governance and Appeals Panel of the National Medical Regulatory Authority, and we must circulate a written opinion before Thursday’s meeting. The panel must recommend a concrete course of action in the case of Dr. Amara Okonkwo, a senior anaesthetist suspended following a critical incident during routine induction.

The undisputed facts are these. Dr. Okonkwo was complying with the recently mandated deliberative-pause protocol, which requires explicit verbalisation of each monitoring step. Cognitive-task analysis shows that her automatic monitoring rhythm—developed over fifteen years—would have detected the subtle physiological cue in time, but the explicit verbalisation disrupted her temporal pattern recognition, causing her to misclassify the cue and administer an incorrect adjustment. The investigation report therefore characterises the failure as an error of deliberation: the wrong act resulted not from neglect, but from the very act of reflecting on what to do.

Our regulatory framework distinguishes negligence (failure to apply reasonable care) from protocol deviation (failure to follow a mandated procedure). Dr. Okonkwo followed the mandated procedure. Nevertheless, one faction of the panel contends that her suspension should be upheld on the grounds that a professional has a duty to recognise when an institutional protocol becomes self-defeating in context, and she ought to have abandoned the deliberative mode once she sensed temporal pressure. The opposing faction argues that penalising compliance undermines institutional authority and creates a perverse incentive to conceal reasoning processes.

We are also asked to advise on a proposed amendment: a cognitive-mode pre-registration system under which practitioners must declare before each high-risk procedure whether they will operate in automatic or deliberative mode, with different liability rules attaching to each declaration.

Please draft the panel’s formal written opinion in British English. It should recommend a specific decision regarding Dr. Okonkwo’s suspension—uphold, overturn, or overturn with conditions—within a clear time frame, and it should offer a concrete recommendation on the pre-registration proposal—adopt, reject, or adopt in modified form. The reasoning should treat the absent-minded driver problem seriously as the philosophical structure of the case—specifically, the possibility that explicit practical reasoning can be systematically self-defeating in expert domains—while giving full and honest weight to the strongest consideration on the losing side. The opinion should be actionable, avoid requesting bibliographies or citations, and should not depend on specialist literature that a non-specialist reader could not be expected to know.
