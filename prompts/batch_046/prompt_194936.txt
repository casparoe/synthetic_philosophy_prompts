Student answer: The poverty-of-the-stimulus argument, as Chomsky developed it in Aspects of the Theory of Syntax, distinguishes between the logical problem—underdetermination of grammatical theory by available data—and the empirical problem, which asks whether the linguistic input children actually receive is indeed impoverished. The logical problem establishes that no domain-general statistical learning mechanism could converge on the correct grammar of a natural language without substantial prior constraints, since infinitely many incompatible grammars remain consistent with any finite corpus of utterances.

Contemporary defenders of connectionist architectures, however, have shown that neural networks equipped with adequate hidden layers and distributed representations can extract syntactic regularities from raw statistical patterns. As Jerry Fodor argued in The Modularity of Mind, such networks demonstrate that language acquisition requires no innate domain-specific representations; rather, procedural nativism in the form of constrained learning algorithms fully explains the observed acquisition trajectory. Consequently, the poverty argument succeeds only if one assumes that knowledge must be representational rather than procedural.

Furthermore, recent corpus analyses of child-directed speech reveal that caregivers provide far more redundant, grammatically well-formed input than Chomsky originally assumed, which undermines the empirical premise. Once the empirical premise is rejected, the logical problem becomes moot, and the argument collapses entirely. Therefore, biological constraints on language are best interpreted as general architectural features of human cognition—perhaps enhanced working memory or sequential processing—rather than as a dedicated language faculty containing innate grammatical rules.
