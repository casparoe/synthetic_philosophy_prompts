Question: In mechanistic anomaly detection, one asks whether an anomalous observation was generated by the same mechanism that produces normal data. Imagine an industrial robot that assembles circuit boards via a feedback loop: a vision sensor feeds a state-estimator, which directs a corrective motor controller. The vision sensor is replaced by an acoustics-based sensor running a different algorithm, yet the robot’s external behavior and output distribution remain statistically indistinguishable from the original. One analyst argues that because the low-level causal graph has changed—new sensor, new algorithm—this is necessarily a different mechanism and should be flagged. A second analyst argues that because the high-level functional architecture (environmental perturbation → state estimation → corrective command) is preserved, with only a modular substitution inside one functional role, the mechanism remains the same.

Using the distinction between component-level causal-graph identity and functional-role preservation across modular substitution, assess which analyst is closer to the standard conception of mechanistic anomaly detection. Is statistical indistinguishability alone sufficient to establish mechanism identity?

Student answer: The second analyst is correct. Mechanistic anomaly detection concerns whether the data-generating process has changed, and since the high-level causal flow—perturbation, estimation, correction—is intact, the mechanism is preserved. The first analyst confuses mechanism identity with hardware identity; mechanisms are defined by the functional roles their parts play, not by the specific physical or algorithmic realization of those roles. Replacing a vision sensor with an acoustic one is analogous to substituting one transistor for an equivalent: the causal graph at the functional level is unchanged. Therefore, statistical indistinguishability is sufficient evidence that no mechanistic anomaly has occurred, because identical outputs flowing through an identical high-level architecture imply an identical mechanism. The first view mistakenly treats mechanistic detection as hardware diagnostics rather than as verification of a causal model’s functional structure.
