I'm preparing a research proposal for a philosophy-of-biology working group that can support one postdoc for two years, and I want to allocate that time to open conceptual work on Darwin's writings about morality and religion rather than to empirical history or laboratory science. To that end, I've sketched three candidate lines of inquiry that seem genuinely unresolved and interdependent, but I need help deciding which deserve the scarce years. Line A is archival-interpretive: re-examining Darwin's private theological notebooks (the so-called "M" and "N" notebooks from the late 1830s and 1840s) to determine whether his loss of theistic belief was driven primarily by the problem of suffering in nature or by a prior methodological commitment to uniformitarian explanation, and then asking whether that original motivation undermines his later claim in *The Descent of Man* that the moral sense has no supernatural origin. Line B is conceptual-translation: mapping Darwin's distinction between "social instincts" and the "moral faculties" onto modern evolutionary-psychology frameworks—specifically, deciding whether his account anticipates multi-level selection, remains an individual-level adaptationist story, or contains an unresolved hybrid—and assessing whether that translation resolves or deepens the tension between his gradualist explanation of conscience and his occasional appeals to a distinctly "higher" human moral capacity. Line C is normative-implication: asking whether Darwin's evolutionary account of sympathy and conscience can support a form of naturalistic moral realism, or whether it collapses into an anti-realist position that makes incoherent his own normative judgments in *Descent* about the comparative superiority of civilized moral codes over those of "savages." I want a defended prioritization, not a survey: for each line, say whether it is upstream of the others (so that settling it changes how we should pursue them), how tractable it is for a philosopher without specialized archival training, and what resolving it would actually change in our understanding of Darwin's legacy for ethics. Rank them, explain the trade-offs among importance, tractability, and downstream dependency, and tell me which single revision—say, discovering that Darwin never intended the notebooks to bear on morality, or that evolutionary psychologists have reached consensus on multi-level selection—would most alter your ordering. Please also flag where your reasoning is most vulnerable or where you are relying on interpretive judgment rather than settled fact. End with \answer{...} enclosing the line you rank first together with the single criterion that most justifies its priority (for example, "B: upstream tractability").
