My friend and I have been arguing about whether death is bad for the one who dies, and he summarised the Epicurean position like this: Epicurus claims that death is nothing to us because harm requires a conscious subject to suffer it, and since death annihilates the subject, there is literally no one present to be harmed. He supports this with the symmetry argument — our pre-natal non-existence was not bad for us, so by parity our post-mortem non-existence should not be either — and concludes that fear of death is always irrational. He then says that modern deprivation accounts, which treat death as bad because it robs you of future goods, miss the point entirely: they treat non-existence as a state that can bear properties like deprivation, whereas for Epicurus non-existence is not a state at all but the absence of any subject to which properties might attach. He adds that anyone who thinks death is bad must be smuggling in a hidden belief in post-mortem survival, since only if you persist as some kind of subject could the loss of future goods matter to you.

This seems partly right but also suspicious. Is it a fair summary? Which parts capture Epicurus's actual reasoning, which parts conflate his hedonism with a broader metaphysical claim about subjects and properties, and does his conclusion — that fear of death is irrational — actually follow once the misrepresentations are corrected? In particular, is the symmetry argument central to his case or a supplementary move, and can the deprivation account respond without committing to survival of the self?
