In a philosophy of science discussion, a colleague kept invoking the principle "It is wrong always, everywhere, and for anyone to believe anything on insufficient evidence" — I take it she was channeling Clifford — to argue that scientists who treat the uniformity of nature or the reliability of inductive inference as working assumptions, despite lacking any non-circular proof of them, are committing an ethical failure rather than making a practical choice. She pressed the slogan specifically against William James's claim that we sometimes have a right to believe in live, forced, and momentous options, saying it made James's conditions irrelevant by flat prohibition. I want to adjudicate the slogan itself before deciding between them. Can you lay out the distinct precise theses that sentence could actually be expressing? Separate the moral, epistemic, and prudential readings; clarify what counts as sufficient evidence in each — conclusive demonstration, a preponderance of reasons, absence of defeaters, or something else; specify whether "believe" applies only to full assent, or also to acting-as-if, partial credence, suspension of judgment, and framework commitments that must be in place for evidence-gathering itself; and determine whether the universal quantifier genuinely covers necessary presuppositions, beliefs formed under coercion, or beliefs held by those who lack the cognitive resources to assess evidence. For each reading, say whether it is a platitude, whether it is defensible, whether it is false, and whether it actually supports her use of the slogan to condemn working scientific assumptions and to block James's four conditions. I am especially interested in whether any non-trivial, defensible reading of the principle turns out to be compatible with James's will-to-believe thesis rather than automatically ruling it out, since she seemed to treat the slogan as self-evidently fatal to it without engaging those conditions.
