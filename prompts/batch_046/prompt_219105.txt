Hey, I'm a retired wetland ecologist who got bored after leaving the field station and started reading philosophy of decision theory for fun. Now I chair this small regional council that meets every other month to vote on ecological intervention proposals -- vaccinating wild herds, culling, feeding programs, that kind of thing. We usually have 5-9 council members plus 2-4 invited experts, about an hour per proposal, and I have to sign the final recommendation. There's no staff to do the math for me.

I've been thinking about Condorcet's jury theorem -- the one about majority accuracy if each voter is a bit better than random and they vote independantly. I want to turn that into an actual reusable checklist my council can run, step by step, to decide whether we should trust a majority vote, go with a minority dissent, or just defer.

Here's what I need in the procedure: first, a quick independance check (who talked to whom, shared raw data, went to the same grad school); second, a competence check (how do we justify that each voter is >50% right, not just "they seem smart"?); third, the aggregation rule for yes/no/defer (we have three options, not just binary); fourth, explicit stop points where the math doesn't apply and it's just a values judgment that I have to own. Include the exact questions in order, what counts as "good enough" to proceed, and where I have to make a call that the procedure can't make for me.

Also build in a "dependency penalty" -- if experts share a mentor or co-authored a paper in the last five years, reduce the effective group size in the calculation. I know that's not in the original theorum, but I want it in my version.

Then apply it to three cases:

1. Vaccinating a badger population against a painful disease. I personally want yes, but there are only 6 voters and two came from the same lab, so independance is shaky and the effective group might be smaller.

2. Culling deer to prevent a winter die-off. I'm personally uneasy about killing healthy animals, but the competence scores look high and correlation is low. I want to see the procedure push toward yes even when my gut says no.

3. A proposal to release gene-drive mosquitoes in a local marsh to suppress malaria, where half the panel are ecologists and half are ethicists. They don't agree on what "getting it right" means, and I want to see exactly where the procedure breaks and hands the decision back to me.

Also tell me flat-out what this procedure cannot do -- for instance, if we can't actually measure correlation, or if our competence estimates are just gut feelings dressed up as numbers, or if the three-option version breaks the binary logic in ways we can't fix. Don't give me a bibliography or cite specific papers; I just want the logic translated into something I can use in a meeting with a timer running. And write it casually -- I make typos all the time, so don't make it sound like a journal article.
