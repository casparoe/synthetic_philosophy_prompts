In a policy seminar on professional ethics, a colleague ended discussion of mandatory pro bono service quotas for licensed practitioners by invoking the familiar Randian line that altruism is evil and that self-sacrifice is immoral. I want to understand exactly what machinery that line could be running. Could you separate out the distinct precise claims it might be expressing—definitional claims about what altruism consists in, normative claims about the wrongness of sacrificing one's own values for others, psychological claims about the destructive effects of such sacrifice, and political claims about the coercive nature of enforced altruism—and classify each as either a platitude, a substantive but defensible thesis, a contentious ethical commitment, or an obvious falsehood? For each reading, indicate whether it actually supports the conclusion that mandatory pro bono obligations are illegitimate, and note which readings remain compatible with allowing or even endorsing such obligations despite the rhetoric. I am less interested in a single correct interpretation than in a disambiguated menu I can use to see where the argument actually lives, especially since the slogan was being used to dismiss the proposal without engaging its institutional design.
