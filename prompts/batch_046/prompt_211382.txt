For the past several months I have been part of an interdisciplinary working group studying how researchers interpret ambiguous qualitative data, and a sustained disagreement with a colleague—someone I regard as my epistemic peer in statistical methodology but not in hermeneutic framing—has forced me to articulate my own commitments about peer disagreement. I want a consistency audit of the package below, not a lecture on the literature.

Here is where I have landed:

(1) I am a conciliationist in the standard sense: when I disagree with someone I take to be an epistemic peer on the specific question at hand, I should reduce my confidence and move partway toward their view.

(2) At the same time, I think there is an asymmetry of epistemic labor: if I personally conducted the interviews, built the coding framework, and lived with the raw transcripts, my direct, first-person access to the interpretive context gives me a legitimate reason to weight my own assessment more heavily—without this being mere stubbornness or bias.

(3) I reject the “equal weight” formulation not as universally false, but as a category error: peerhood is granular. Someone can be my peer about predictive modeling and not about phenomenological description, and applying a uniform conciliation rule across these domains mistakes the structure of expertise.

(4) I also hold that disagreement is always at least minimal evidence that I have made an error, even in domains where I feel expert and even when the asymmetry in (2) applies. The evidential force may be weak, but it is never literally zero.

(5) I think intellectual autonomy is an independent epistemic good: one should not revise one’s belief purely to achieve group harmony or to avoid the social costs of standing apart. Maintaining one’s judgment has rational value that is not reducible to expected accuracy.

(6) Finally, I think the correct response should be domain-sensitive in a specific way: in empirically tractable, predictive disputes I should conciliate more aggressively, whereas in normative or interpretive framing disputes—like the one I am currently in—steadfastness is more permissible, though not unlimited.

My question is whether these six commitments hang together or whether I am helping myself to incompatible motivations. Please audit the set: identify which pairs are genuinely in tension (distinguishing real conflict from surface friction), rank the genuine tensions by severity, and for each say what the cheapest revision would be and what it would cost me. Also identify at least one pair that looks tense but dissolves on inspection, explaining precisely why the conflict is only apparent. Do not treat all tensions as equally bad, and do not assume I am prepared to abandon conciliationism altogether—treat my commitments as fixed for the purpose of the audit and tell me what has to give, not what I should want to give.
