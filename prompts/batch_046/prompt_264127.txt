I am a novelist currently holding a cross-disciplinary fellowship that pairs fiction writers with philosophy departments, and next Thursday I present to the university's moral philosophy colloquium my thesis that genuine moral dilemmas are possible—not in the familiar form of conflicting obligations, but in what I term "constitutive role fractures." By this I mean situations in which an agent's practical identity is genuinely constituted by two incommensurable normative frameworks—say, the unconditional demands of parenthood and the impersonal requirements of civic duty during a crisis—such that resolving the conflict through any higher-order principle necessarily betrays one framework that is not merely valued but constitutive of the agent's selfhood. The audience includes a prominent Kantian scholar who denies dilemmas on grounds of universalizability, a consequentialist convinced that weighted utility always yields a unique optimal act, and several philosophers of action who accept Bernard Williams's notion of moral residue yet insist it proves only epistemic limitation rather than genuine dilemmatic obligation. They are rigorous rather than hostile, I have forty minutes including questions, and I must avoid sounding as though I am rehearsing a debate they have heard a thousand times.

Please prepare me for the objections they will actually raise: order them by likelihood and by how damaging they would be to my specific claim, phrase each as the particular interlocutor who would deliver it, and for each indicate whether I should concede partial ground, hold firm with a brief reply, or mark it as the point I must defend rather than hope they miss. I also need you to identify the weakest point in my own argument—the one about narrative closure and unreconciled practical remainder that allows me to distinguish genuine dilemmas from merely emotional or epistemic conflicts—and tell me exactly what reply must land for the proposal to survive. Finally, include the objection I am secretly hoping nobody asks, and frame everything so that these philosophers understand that my claim is about first-person practical identity and the impossibility of integrating certain choices into a coherent life story without leaving an unreconciled remainder, not about ordinary conflicts of duty.
