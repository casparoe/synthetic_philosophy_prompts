I've been reporting on computation and philosophy for a magazine and I'm stuck on why the Church-Turing debate feels like it's been spinining in place since the 90s. One camp treats the thesis as an empirical claim about physical processes—if we ever build a gadget that computes the non-computable, it's falsified. The other camp says that's missing the point: it's definitional, or at best a mathematical boundary, not a law of nature. But I'm starting to think the real fight is hidden: maybe the thesis is functioning as an epistemic closure rule, telling cognitive scientists when they've hit bedrock mechanistic explanation and shouldn't keep digging for non-algorithmic causes. Is the stall because people are arguing over computation when they're actually disagreeing about the limits of scientific explanation? Or is it verbal—"computable" doing different work for each side? Or are they just answering different questions, one formal and one about nature, under one heading? Diagnose it using what each side actually says. What's the real disagreement—substantive, verbal, or a hidden empirical question? And what would actually count as progress: a formal result, an experiment, or just admitting the question is ill-posed? Be straight with me—can this even move, or are we just repeating ourselves?
