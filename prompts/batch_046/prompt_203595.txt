After a long, exhausting argument with a close friend about whether anything besides the elimination of suffering genuinely matters for well-being, I am preparing a seminar presentation for next Thursday defending affective tranquilism: the view that moral and prudential value attaches solely to the absence of craving (taṇhā) and suffering (dukkha), while states usually taken as positively valuable—intellectual insight, aesthetic rapture, or even the phenomenology of deep jhāna—are valuable only instrumentally, as conditions that stabilize or reveal that absence. My audience will be mostly Aristotelian value theorists and perfectionists who think flourishing requires the actualization of capacities, two scholars of Theravāda Abhidhamma who will insist I am misinterpreting the affective structure of meditative absorption, and one cognitive scientist working on reward-prediction error who will press whether my view is empirically compatible with dopaminergic reward signaling. I need a prep sheet, not a polished defense: list the objections I will actually face, in roughly the order they will come, phrased as each camp would phrase them, with a brief reply I can deliver in under two minutes, a frank note on where that reply is weakest, and an indication of what I should concede in advance. Include the objection I am most afraid someone will ask—the one that could collapse the distinction between my view and standard negative utilitarianism if I mishandle it. For context, my specific thesis is that jhāna should not be read as a positively valenced affective state but as a saturation of absence, and that the normative metric is therefore not aggregate suffering-reduction but the structural dissolution of craving itself. Finally, in your response, state explicitly what kind of argument or empirical finding would cause you to reject this defense of tranquilism, and identify the precise point in the reasoning where failure is most likely.
