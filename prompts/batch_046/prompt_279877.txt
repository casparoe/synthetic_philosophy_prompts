I am currently drafting a seminar contribution on legitimacy crises in European integration, and a recent disagreement with a colleague about the 2015 Greek referendum has convinced me that the standard narratives—Brussels overrode Athens, or Syriza betrayed its mandate—are both too quick. I want you to analyze the July 2015 "Oxi" vote, in which Greek citizens rejected the Troika's proposed bailout conditions by 61 percent, through the lens of the EU's democratic deficit and the doctrine of ever-closer union, but I want to move past the usual framing. Specifically, I ask you to treat the episode as a test case for what we might call "demoi-cratic sovereignty": the view that democratic authority in the Union is not pooled upward into a single European demos, but distributed across distinct national demoi, each retaining a veto-like moral claim over decisions that restructure its basic economic order.

Map the institutional dynamics with precision: the Greek electorate exercising procedural sovereignty through referendum; the Eurogroup and ECB enforcing the structural imperatives of monetary union; the Syriza government caught between its popular mandate and the absence of an exit mechanism. Say what the democratic-deficit thesis predicts about this collision—whether the deficit lies in the lack of transnational democratic authorization, or in the presence of mechanisms that render national democratic outputs structurally non-binding. Then identify exactly where this framing misdescribes or breaks down: did Tsipras's acceptance of harsher terms days later show that substantive sovereignty had already been ceded, or that procedural sovereignty remains real but hollow? Take a committed position: is the Greek referendum best interpreted as confirming a democratic deficit rooted in the suppression of national democratic will by ever-closer union, or as revealing that sovereignty in the Eurozone has been transformed into something that national referenda can register but never genuinely exercise? Defend your answer with explicit reasoning, and specify which feature of the crisis—the ECB's liquidity threat, the secret negotiation dynamics, or the institutional absence of exit—carries the decisive weight for your interpretation.
