I retired from experimental physics a couple years back and have been trying to read actual philosphy instead of just skimming abstracts. Right now I'm in the middle of Sartre's Being and Nothingness, and I think I understand his claim about radical freedom - that there's no human nature, and we're always choosing our attitudes, even our emotions. But then he talks about bad faith, where people deny this freedom and treat themselves like fixed objects, like the waiter who believes he's essentially just a waiter.

I'm genuinely confused about whether this is consistent. If freedom is truly radical and unavoidable, then isn't denying it impossible? In my old field, if a law is fundamental you can't violate it by choice - you can't just decide gravity doesn't apply to you. So if we're necessarily free, how can we ever successfully flee from that fact? It feels like Sartre is describing a contradiction: we are unavoidable free, yet we constantly avoid being free. I keep thinking maybe bad faith is itself a kind of free act, but then I'm not sure why he calls it a kind of lying rather than just another choice. Am I missing a distinction here?
