I direct a clinical assessment unit that evaluates first-person reports from patients recovering from traumatic brain injury, especially reports about internal states—pain, awareness of paralysis, emotional valence, or the sense of agency over movement. We need a reusable decision procedure for our intake staff, grounded in heterophenomenology rather than in direct-access theories of consciousness. The procedure should treat every verbal or behavioral report as a text describing a heterophenomenological world: a fictional but systematically structured narrative that may or may not align with the patient’s neurophysiological and behavioral profile. The team applies this repeatedly, often under time pressure; a neurologist signs off, but the initial screening is performed by clinical staff without philosophical training.

Write a step-by-step protocol they can run in about fifteen minutes per case. Include: the ordered questions to ask of the report; what counts as a sufficient answer at each step; explicit stop-points where the protocol returns a verdict of “do not treat the report as veridical of the clinical state”; points where the decision must be handed to a person because it involves values rather than descriptive consistency; and a small set of concrete test sentences or report-types staff can use to calibrate. The translation must preserve the core heterophenomenological distinction—between describing the world of experience and describing the mechanism that produces the description—while dropping distinctions that require philosophical expertise.

Then apply the procedure to three cases: (1) a patient with anosognosia for hemiplegia who insists they can lift the affected arm and offers a detailed narrative about having done so that morning; (2) a patient in a minimally conscious state who, via an assistive communication device, reports a continuous feeling of calm and denies any discomfort, while nursing records document frequent grimacing and elevated heart rate during care; and (3) a patient with recovered motor function but persistent alexithymia who reports “I feel nothing” when presented with emotionally charged images, yet shows normal autonomic responses and later describes the images in accurate detail.

Include one case where the procedure yields an answer the clinical team will find counterintuitive or ethically uncomfortable, and state exactly what the procedure cannot settle—where judgment or institutional policy must take over. Also flag the specific misfires you anticipate: for instance, reports that are internally coherent but behaviorally contradicted, or reports that align behaviorally but describe impossible phenomenological structures.

Keep the deliverable practical: specify who asks each question, what form the answer takes (checklist entry, scaled rating, brief narrative evaluation), and what documentation is required before sign-off. Do not include bibliographies, citation requests, or references to specific editions or page numbers.
