As a novelist invited to deliver the opening remarks at an interdisciplinary workshop titled "Waking and Doubt," I am addressing an audience divided between philosophers of mind well-versed in Zhuangzi's butterfly dream and fiction writers with little exposure to it. I want a twenty-minute talk that treats the dream not as a skeptical problem to be solved but as a structural crisis in first-person narration: the awakening dissolves the boundary between the dreamer and the dreamed, and I want that dissolution to govern what I can and cannot claim about my own authority as speaker. The address must do genuine philosophical work for the specialists—engaging the parable's instability of self-reference—while remaining entirely accessible to the writers, and it should include one direct moment in which I speak to the split room about the fragility of any narrative "I" without resolving the ambiguity. Please write the speech; include no citations, bibliographies, or reading lists, and avoid framing the dream as an epistemological puzzle that admits of a solution.
