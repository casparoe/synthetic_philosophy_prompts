A legal philosopher has constructed an argument that any legal system containing a genuinely self-applicable rule of amendment—call it the Meta-Rule—must either be permanently frozen or conceptually incoherent. The argument runs as follows.

(1) The Meta-Rule M specifies the conditions for the valid amendment or repeal of any rule within the system, and by its own terms these conditions apply to M itself.

(2) Among those conditions is a continuity requirement: any valid amendment to M must preserve M's authority to govern all subsequent amendments.

(3) Suppose an amendment A is enacted that alters M's procedural requirements—for instance, lowering a required supermajority to a simple majority.

(4) For A to be valid, it must satisfy the conditions specified by M at the moment of enactment, which include the original supermajority threshold.

(5) By hypothesis, A is enacted only by a simple majority; therefore, under M's original conditions, A is invalid.

(6) If A is invalid, M survives unchanged, and the simple-majority procedure never acquires valid authority—so far the result is consistent, if conservative.

(7) But now consider amendment B, enacted under the putative simple-majority procedure that A (if valid) would have established, which repeals the continuity requirement in (2) altogether.

(8) If A is valid, then B operates under valid rules; yet B eliminates the very authority that A was required to preserve, violating (2). Hence A's validity undermines itself.

(9) If A is invalid, B is enacted without valid authority, hence B is also invalid.

(10) Thus, on either horn, any attempt to relax or eliminate M's core conditions either fails or generates a self-undermining result.

(11) Generalizing: a genuinely self-applicable Meta-Rule makes its own substantive revision impossible without contradiction.

(12) But actual constitutional orders contain precisely such mechanisms—provisions that specify how the constitutional text, including the amendment provision itself, may in principle be revised.

(13) Therefore, either all constitutional orders rest on an unacknowledged non-legal foundation, or one of the premises above is false.

The philosopher suspects the error lies in treating the continuity requirement in (2) as applying diachronically to amendments that replace M, but she cannot see how to reject that without also rejecting the idea that legal authority is rule-governed at all. She asks: which step should be abandoned? Is the flaw a scope ambiguity in (4) between the token-rule that governs enactment and the type-rule that survives it? Or is it a modal slide in (8), assuming that what A must preserve is identical to what B may destroy? What does each rejection cost—must one embrace a purely sociological account of legal change, or can one retain a normative theory of constitutional continuity by distinguishing the authority of a rule from the authority of the system it constitutes? She would prefer to keep (1) and (12) intact if possible.
