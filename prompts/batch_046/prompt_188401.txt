I'm putting together a pitch for a three-month summer grant on the Death in Damascus fatalism story — you know, guy meets Death in Damascus, Death says "tomorrow in Aleppo," he rides to Aleppo and dies there, Death says "I was coming for you in Damascus tomorrow but you came here." Most treatments ask if this proves determinism or conflicts with free will, but I think both miss the actual puzzle. The story only works if we treat the guy's decision to flee as causally effective — it changes where he dies — while also treating Death's statement as fixed knowledge about the future. So my precise question is: can we model the reasoning error without collapsing it into either a determinist thesis or a logical contradiction? I want to approach it specificaly through indexical belief updates, treating "I will be in Aleppo tomorrow" as self-locating rather than world-locating, and see whether the paradox dissolves into a mistake about treating your own action as evidence instead of cause. The existing answers either resolve it as a logic trick or jump straight to compatibilism about freedom, but neither explains why fleeing feels rational yet fails to outsmart fate. I'd build a simple formal sketch, probably using decision-theoretic notation, and test it on variations like Death giving a false location or the guy deciding to stay. A good result would seperate fatalistic reasoning from ordinary planning in a clear, non-circular way. Failure would be if the model just restates the contradiction with extra symbols. Realistic scope is 12 weeks, mostly conceptual with one outline paper, no empirical work needed. Don't need citations in the proposal, I'll fill those in later.
