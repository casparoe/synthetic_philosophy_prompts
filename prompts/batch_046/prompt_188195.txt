I am writing a feature on the persistence of Whig narratives in intellectual history, and I want to press a point that seems to me underexplored. Many critics treat Whiggism as essentially an evaluative error—historians praising past figures for anticipating present truths and condemning others for failing to do so. If that diagnosis is correct, then adopting a Bloor-style symmetry principle, applying identical sociological and cognitive explanations to both successful and abandoned theories without privileging the present endpoint, should eradicate the problem entirely. But I wonder whether this misses a structural feature: even under perfect explanatory symmetry, the historian must still select which past events belong to the continuous thread that leads to the present, and that retrospective selection itself might impose a teleological shape regardless of normative language. So is Whig history fundamentally a bias in evaluation that symmetry can cure, or is it embedded in the narrative logic of retrospective selection in ways that survive even the most rigorous symmetrical explanation?
