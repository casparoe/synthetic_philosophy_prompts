I am preparing a research essay for an advanced high school ethics course on Hannah Arendt's analysis of Adolf Eichmann, particularly her argument that extreme wrongdoing can arise from bureaucratic thoughtlessness rather than deep ideological conviction—what scholars term the banality of evil. Rather than revisit the trial itself, I want to apply this framework to the Milgram obedience experiments conducted at Yale in the early 1960s, a case widely studied in psychology and ethics. Please analyze the Milgram study as an institutional episode: treat the laboratory authority structure as a miniature bureaucracy, examine whether the subjects who continued administering shocks exhibited Arendtian thoughtlessness—an unreflective substitution of procedural compliance for moral judgment—rather than mere cruelty or blind conformity. Consider also whether Stanley Milgram himself, in maintaining the experimental protocol despite visible participant distress, fits Arendt's portrait of the bureaucrat who prioritizes role performance over ethical judgment. Your analysis should identify what Arendt's concept explains well about the subjects' behavior, where it misdescribes the psychological dynamics Milgram documented—such as the subjects' visible anxiety, resistance, and explicit requests to stop—and what the episode reveals about the limits of extending the thesis of banal evil beyond the context of state-organized genocide.
