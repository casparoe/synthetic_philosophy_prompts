I've been thinking on my own about the claim that a genuinely private language—something like a personal notation you invent just for your own sensations, with no public check—cannot really exist. In the discussions I've encountered, people often lean on a box analogy: imagine that every person possesses a box, and inside each box is something that its owner calls a beetle, yet the rules of the practice forbid anyone from ever opening another person's box. Even so, people continue to talk about beetles in ordinary conversation, apparently referring to something shared, even though the contents of the boxes are entirely hidden and play no role in how the word is actually used. The analogy is meant to show that if a private sensation were like the object inside the box, then giving it a private name would be equally hollow—the name would not do any genuine work, because there is no public criterion for its correct application.

But I want to know precisely how far this comparison holds, especially since I recently imagined a modified case: suppose two people have encrypted neural interfaces that each grant access to a unique sensory channel, so the raw content is genuinely private to each user, yet they can still exchange structured metadata—whether the channel is active, its intensity, and whether it correlates with shared external events. In this version, there is still an inaccessible inner content, but there is also a shared protocol for reporting on it, which seems to change the dynamics that the box analogy highlights. Could you lay out the full correspondence table—what plays the role of the box, the beetle, the public conversation, and the prohibition on opening—then identify where the mapping breaks, particularly with respect to this encrypted-reporting case? I also want to know whether the conclusion that private language is impossible survives once these disanalogies are on the table, or whether the analogy was carrying the argument all along. Finally, if there is a better analogy for thinking about experience that is private in content but disciplined by shared reporting rules, I would like to hear what it is and why it fits more cleanly.
