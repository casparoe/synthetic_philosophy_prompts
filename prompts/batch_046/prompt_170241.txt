I'm trying to determine whether the paradox of phenomenal judgment undermines epiphenomenalism, testing my intuitions case by case. Judging "I am in pain" where only neural processes cause the judgment feels acceptable — it tracks the physical basis. Judging "this redness has a specific qualitative feel" feels different: if the feel causes nothing, my judgment seems to miss its target rather than track it. Attributing consciousness to others from behavior alone seems practically adequate, yet I feel I am not detecting anything real. The reflexive case is the one I keep returning to: if I conclude "epiphenomenalism renders phenomenal judgments unreliable," and that conclusion is itself physically caused without phenomenal involvement, I seem to undermine my own reasoning — yet I resist letting that reflexive move defeat the argument. Meanwhile, a physically identical zombie who makes the same "I am conscious" judgment with the same causal chain produces an asymmetry: my judgment is true, the zombie's false, despite being indistinguishable, which strikes me as deeply awkward. Is there a principle — perhaps distinguishing causal tracking from representational intent, or first-person present-tense access from third-person inference — that accommodates these five reactions? If not, which verdict should I abandon? And what should it say about introspective judgments formed in direct present awareness: do they count as genuinely about phenomenal properties even when those properties are causally inert?
