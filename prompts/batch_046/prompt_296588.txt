I'm preparing an essay on American exceptionalism conceived not as a positive creed but as a "negative ideology" — an identity constituted by the absence of European feudal residues (aristocracy, established church, rigid class hierarchy), which produces a perpetual narrative of self-invention that obscures structural domination. Read John Steinbeck's The Grapes of Wrath through this lens. The Joads migrate toward California as a promised land of equal opportunity, yet they encounter a labor regime that replicates the dependency and coercion they left behind. Does the novel treat exceptionalism as an operative false consciousness that the migrants must shed, or does it salvage exceptionalism by relocating it in collective solidarity rather than individual mobility? Focus on two specific episodes — for instance, the government camp at Weedpatch versus the Hooper Ranch, or the flood ending — and argue for a definite reading: either the text ultimately dismantles the exceptionalist myth by showing that the absence of formal feudalism does not prevent economic feudalism, or it preserves the myth by making the Joads' endurance itself proof of American regenerative capacity. Be faithful to both directions: show where the negative-ideology framework illuminates a scene that is usually read as mere social realism, and where the novel's emotional structure resists reduction to ideology critique. Conclude by stating what this particular literary case adds to the philosophical debate about exceptionalism — as historical thesis, normative claim, and ideology — that abstract argument alone cannot establish. Take a committed position and defend it; do not lay out both options neutrally.
