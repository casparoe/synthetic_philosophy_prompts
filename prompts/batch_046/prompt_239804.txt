As a journalist who covers biotechnology and philosophy of medicine, I have been trying to formalize my own judgments about the therapy-enhancement boundary, because the familiar framing of “restoration to species-typical functioning” collapses as soon as one examines the interventions now entering clinical trials. Below are my verdicts on concrete cases; I want a principle—or an honest admission that none exists—that captures the pattern.

Case 1: A prenatal gene edit that corrects a fatal lysosomal storage disorder is therapy, full stop.

Case 2: The same editing platform used to confer tetrachromacy on a healthy embryo is enhancement.

Case 3: A stimulant prescribed for clinically diagnosed ADHD is therapy; the identical compound taken by a healthy doctoral candidate to extend sustained attention is enhancement.

Case 4: Reconstructive surgery after severe burns is therapy; using identical surgical techniques to alter bone architecture beyond pre-injury symmetry norms is enhancement.

Case 5: A neural prosthesis implanted after traumatic brain injury to restore working memory to pre-trauma levels is therapy; the same device implanted in a healthy adult to expand capacity past any documented human baseline is enhancement.

Case 6: A pharmacological regimen that lifts a patient from clinical depression back to a stable affective baseline is therapy; using that regimen in healthy individuals to maintain a permanently optimized affective state is enhancement.

Now the boundary cases that strain the frame. Case 7: A cochlear implant for congenital deafness that restores standard auditory range is therapy. Yet the signal-processing architecture required for intelligible speech recognition unavoidably grants the recipient limited ultrasonic perception. I still classify this as therapy—the ultrasonic range is an inseparable byproduct of the repair mechanism, not its purpose. But in Case 8, a hearing individual requests the identical implant specifically to gain ultrasonic hearing, accepting only minor auditory trade-offs. I classify this as enhancement and would resist clinical provision. The functional endpoint is nearly identical; only the pathological origin and the intent behind the request seem to distinguish them.

Case 9: A neural interface developed for congenital amusia—the inability to perceive melodic structure—that restores standard pitch processing. Implanted in an amusic patient, it is therapy. However, the interface necessarily operates by translating harmonic ratios into direct spatial-geometric experience, a cognitive mode no human naturally possesses. If implanted in a hearing musician specifically to access this geometric-musical experience, it is enhancement. But if implanted in the amusic patient, it grants both standard pitch processing and this geometric mode. I want to call it therapy, yet the functional gain far exceeds mere restoration.

Case 10: Surgical correction of a cleft palate is therapy. Surgical alteration of statistically atypical facial asymmetry—with no functional deficit—performed because the asymmetry produces clinically significant social anxiety: I am genuinely uncertain. I lean toward therapy if the psychological distress reaches clinical thresholds, but this seems to import mental-health norms into a physical-intervention taxonomy.

Please provide a precise principle, or a small set of competing candidates, stated with enough rigor that each clause can be mapped to the case that motivates it. Apply the principle to an unlisted case: a cognitive prosthesis originally designed to arrest memory degradation in early-stage Alzheimer’s disease, now requested by a healthy archivist who wants perfect verbatim retention of every document she handles, with engineered safeguards against cognitive overload. Finally, state honestly which of my verdicts—particularly the tension between Case 7 and Case 8, and the ambiguity in Case 9—the principle cannot accommodate without ad hoc complexity, and whether that failure means those verdicts should be overturned in favor of a simpler account.
