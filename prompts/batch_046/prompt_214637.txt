I'm a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, and I'm trying to understand whether my own intuitions about cross-border obligations are consistent. Here are my judgments, presented as concretely as I can. First, when a teammate in Tokyo asks me to stay up through the night to resolve a production failure, I feel genuinely obligated to help, despite our never having met and our not being compatriots. Second, when an equally qualified developer in my own city, with no work or personal connection to me, requests the same kind of emergency assistance, I feel no comparable duty. Third, I believe a government is entitled to reserve certain public-sector technology contracts for domestic firms, even if foreign competitors are more qualified and cheaper. Fourth, I think it is wrong for a company to lay off local engineers while retaining remote contractors abroad solely to avoid stronger local labor protections, though I am unsure whether the wrongness comes from a special claim of compatriots or from a general duty not to exploit regulatory gaps. Fifth, I feel uneasy about supporting immigration restrictions on technology workers to protect domestic wages, yet I do not regard such restrictions as clearly unjust. Sixth, when I maintain open-source tools used globally, I do not experience my contribution as specially benefiting my compatriots, but I also do not object when my tax revenue funds local digital infrastructure that primarily serves domestic users. These cases seem to pull in opposite directions: professional ties generate obligations that ignore nationality entirely, while citizenship appears to justify preferential treatment in some policy contexts but feels arbitrary or even unjust in others. Is there a single principle—perhaps one that distinguishes associative obligations from distributive ones, or that weights proximity differently in different domains—that can accommodate most or all of these verdicts? If not, which of my judgments should be overturned? Please also apply your principle to a case I have not resolved: should a remote-first firm be required to provide the same health and disability benefits to contractors in jurisdictions with weak public systems as it provides to employees at its domestic headquarters, or is a lower standard permissible precisely because no special national bond exists? If your account saves every verdict only by becoming highly complex, say so explicitly, and identify the simplest alternative that sacrifices one of my intuitions.
