Compare Zygmunt Bauman's thesis that the Holocaust was not a regression to pre-modern barbarism but rather a distinctively modern product of bureaucratic rationality, instrumental reason, and the moral distancing enabled by administrative division of labor, with the position defended during the West German Historikerstreit that the Holocaust constitutes a unique, incomparable event that must resist normalization into broader historical categories. In particular, examine whether Bauman's functional-comparative analysis necessarily conflicts with the uniqueness claim, or whether the tension can be dissolved by distinguishing token uniqueness—no other event shares every specific property—from type uniqueness—no other event belongs to the same causal, structural, or moral kind. Assess which form of uniqueness the defenders in the Historikerstreit were primarily advancing, and determine whether Bauman's framework undermines type uniqueness, token uniqueness, both, or neither. Conclude with a reasoned judgment on whether separating causal explanation from normative-moral singularity allows the two positions to coexist without contradiction, or whether one must ultimately yield to the other.
