okay so i'm trying to sort out my gut feelings about advance directives and whether the person who wrote them still has authority over the current patient. here are some cases and what i think should happen.

case 1: a 30-year-old writes a living will saying no feeding tube if ever in a permanent vegetative state. at 75 they have severe dementia, seem content, don't understand medicine, but are physically healthy. my verdict: honor the directive and don't put in the tube, even though the current person seems okay with being alive.

case 2: same setup but the dementia came on suddenly after a stroke, and the family says he'd never want this but there's no document. verdict: still don't treat aggressively if the prognosis is clear, but without the document i'm less sure—maybe the absence of writing shouldn't bind?

case 3: someone spends 10 years gradually converting to a religion that values suffering and rejects life-prolonging tech. they update their directive to match. then they get alzheimer's and revert to wanting comfort care. verdict: honor the updated religious directive, not the alzheimer's wish. but wait, what if the conversion itself was caused by a slow-growing frontal-lobe tumor that altered their values? then i think we should ignore the 'converted' directive and go back to the original. so the cause of the change matters, and this feels different from case 1.

case 4: there's this weird new thing—synthetic proxy directives—where an AI trained on your past emails and medical choices writes a directive predicting what you'd want in scenarios you never actually considered. you never signed it. my gut says it has zero authority, but if the predictions are 99% accurate and you left no other instructions, maybe it should count for something? i'm torn, definately torn.

case 5: a patient with locked-in syndrome writes a clear directive saying if i ever can't communicate, pull the plug. then they learn to communicate via eye-tracking and consistently say they want to live, that the old directive was wrong. verdict: the communicating self overrides the document, even though they can't speak.

case 6: someone writes a directive at 25 saying always save me no matter what. at 60 they develop a painful terminal illness and beg to stop treatment, but they also have mild cognitive decline—not full dementia, just a bit foggy. verdict: i think we should definately listen to the 60-year-old and stop, but i'm not fully sure. the 25-year-old's always feels like it shouldn't be a suicide pact across decades.

so what's the principle? maybe it's about which self is more authentic or continuous? but case 3 and case 4 mess that up—synthetic proxy has continuity but no authorship, and the tumor-conversion has authorship but disrupted continuity. is there a coherent account that saves all these, or do i have to drop one? and what should i say about a case i haven't listed—say, a person who writes a directive, then undergoes a therapy that slowly replaces 40% of their neurons with synthetic analogues over five years, and midway through writes a new directive? does the half-replaced brain have authority over the fully replaced one?

please be honest about which of my verdicts your principle can't save.
