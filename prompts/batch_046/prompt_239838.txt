In debates over whether Popper was correct to treat Darwinism as a metaphysical research programme rather than a genuinely falsifiable scientific theory, two analogies are constantly pressed into service. The first is the scoreboard analogy: critics say that “the fittest survive” operates like the rule that the team with the highest score wins — true by definition, recording an outcome rather than predicting it, so that Darwinism risks being unfalsifiable in precisely Popper’s own terms. The second is the engineering-specification analogy: defenders reply that evolutionary claims work more like a load-bearing calculation — “this beam will fail under ten tonnes” — where fitness is specified by independent structural properties and selection pressures predict which configurations will not persist, not merely rename those that do. Audit both analogies in detail: provide an explicit correspondence table for each (what plays the role of the scoreboard rule or the beam’s material properties; the match conditions or the applied load; the post-hoc reading or the prior design; the referee or the testing engineer), then identify the disanalogies that actually undermine the intended argument from those that are merely decorative. Say whether either analogy survives intact enough to support Popper’s original verdict about Darwinism’s scientific status, or his subsequent recantation, once the broken mappings are on the table. Finally, propose a third analogy drawn from diagnostic medicine rather than sport or engineering — one in which a symptom, a pathogen, and a prognosis correspond to elements of the evolutionary account — and assess whether it captures the predictive structure Popper eventually conceded without smuggling in teleology or collapsing back into tautology. Be explicit about which conclusions each analogy can and cannot carry.
