I am preparing a formal colloquium interview with an invented philosopher who defends a view inspired by Douglas Hofstadter: that the self is not confined to one brain but is a pattern capable of being instantiated, with varying fidelity, across multiple brains—what she calls distributed selfhood. I want ten rigorous interview questions that avoid asking her to restate the thesis and that contain no generic biographical material. The sequence must include: at least one question on how she distinguishes distributed selfhood from standard psychological-continuity theories without collapsing into them; one on the status of first-person authority when the same pattern generates contradictory reports from different brains; one on whether narrative integration must remain unified across all instantiations or whether fragmented replicas can each claim full selfhood; and one on how her view accommodates the intuition that death is singular rather than multiplied across every brain hosting a copy.

For each of these four, include a follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give. For example, if she appeals to pattern fidelity, press her on whether fidelity is a scalar property that permits partial selfhood and, if so, whether that undermines the normative weight of personal responsibility; if she invokes narrative coherence, ask whether coherence is evaluated globally across all replicas or locally within each brain, and what happens when two replicas construct incompatible life stories.

In addition, include two questions that inject a niche conceptual tension not usually covered in summaries of this position: one on asymmetric information between replicas, where one brain retains memories that others lack, and whether that asymmetry creates a hierarchical authority within the distributed self; and one on whether distributed selfhood requires a shared phenomenal present or can tolerate temporal lag across brains separated by space or processing delay.

The questions should be specific to the actual content of her position, anticipate standard defensive moves, and be suitable for a philosophical Q&A in which the interviewee is a defender of this specific view rather than Hofstadter himself. Do not ask for reading lists, citations, or bibliographies.
