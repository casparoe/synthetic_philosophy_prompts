For my upper-division metaphysics seminar, we are examining material constitution through the classic statue-and-clay problem, but our instructor has asked us to apply it to an engineered boundary case rather than the standard museum examples. I would like you to design a decision tree that classifies positions on material constitution according to how they resolve three diagnostic questions arising from the following scenario: an artist casts a bronze statue, which is later melted into ingots and stored for fifty years. During storage, gradual corrosion replaces some atoms, and the ingots are eventually replenished. A different artist then recasts the metal into a form with altered proportions and a new title. Throughout, there is continuous physical custody of the metal, but no continuous artistic form, creator, or shape.

Please structure the tree as follows. Each branch node should be one of the three questions below, phrased so that a student can answer yes or no (or choose among specified options) without already knowing which philosophical label the answer produces:

1. Does the original statue survive if and only if the artistic form intended by the first creator is continuously maintained, or can survival depend solely on the spatiotemporal continuity of the material substrate?
2. Given that the substrate itself undergoes atomic replacement and is then reshaped by a different agent with different intentions, does this yield the survival of the original object, the genesis of a numerically distinct object, or an indeterminate intermediate state lacking clear identity conditions?
3. Must a constituted artifact retain at least one essential identifying property (such as shape, artistic function, or creator relation) across all times at which it exists, or is intermittent existence permissible—allowing the object to persist through a temporal gap during which it possesses none of those properties?

The leaves should include at least: a standard constitution view, a hylomorphic view, a four-dimensionalist temporal-parts view, an eliminativist view about artifacts, and at least one hybrid or unnamed position that combines answers in a logically possible but rarely defended way. Each leaf should state, in one sentence, the most counterintuitive commitment that the position takes on in this specific case.

Please construct the tree from the logical space of answers first, then map the named positions onto that space. Note any combination of answers that yields an unoccupied leaf, and briefly explain whether the gap is due to incoherence, conceptual overlap, or merely an unexplored view. Also flag any pair of named leaves that the three questions cannot genuinely separate, explaining why the distinction collapses here.

Do not include a bibliography, reading list, or citations to specific texts. The analysis should rely solely on conceptual reasoning about the case and the positions. Avoid framing the nodes in terms of obscure sub-theories that are not part of a standard metaphysics curriculum.
