I'm drafting this novel about two sisters who haven't spoken in years, and I was at a bar in Brooklyn where two writer friends—both ex-therapists actually—got into this intense arguement about Ian Hacking's looping effects, you know, the idea that classifying people can actually change the people being classified. I was just sitting there with my notebook trying not to eavesdrop too obviously.

Maya said looping basically only works for psychiatric categories that become part of your identity, like being autistic or having bipolar disorder. She said for prolonged grief disorder—which just got added to the DSM's latest revision a few years back—the loop is broken because grief is universal and nobody reshapes thier whole self around a 12-month clinical timeline. She said Hacking was writing about inventing kinds of people, not just adding another line to a manual.

Jordan said that's way too quick. He said the loop is actually stronger with grief precisely because once prolonged grief exists as a clinical entity, people start monitoring thier mourning at month six instead of just feeling sad, and clinicians start pathologizing normal bereavement. He said Hacking's point wasn't just about identity fusion but about the feedback between institutional labeling and self-knowledge in any setting where people recognize themselves in the category.

Maya came back: okay, but Hacking's classic examples—child abuse classifications, multiple personality—were about inventing entirely new kinds of people. Grief isnt a social kind; it's a biological constant. Applying looping here is a category error, like saying thermometers loop with fever.

Jordan replied that even biological constants get looped once they're medicalized. He said look at how blood pressure categories changed diet and exercise behavior across whole populations. Then he brought up something he called reverse looping, where the classified population starts reshaping the diagnostic criteria themselves—like how ADHD expanded once adults started self-identifying. He said Maya was treating Hacking's framework as only about top-down identity politics when it's really about any institutional classification that feeds back into its conditions of application.

Maya said: if looping applies to literally every medical category including blood pressure, then the concept loses all explanatory power. It's just labels matter, which is trivial. She said Jordan was diluting Hacking into generic sociology of knowledge.

Jordan said no, there's a real distinction: looping requires that the classification provides a narrative framework people use to understand themselves. Insomnia disorder doesnt do that, but prolonged grief does—people ask whether thier grief is abnormal at month eleven. So it's not trivial and it's not universal; it depends on whether the category invites self-recognition.

They turned to me and asked me to referee since I was the only one not talking, and honestly I couldnt pick a side. Can you go through thier exchange move by move and rate who's right at each step? Don't just tell me who wins overall—say what each person gets right and wrong, and point out anything they're both missing. Please write your answer in American English.
