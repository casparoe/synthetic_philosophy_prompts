I serve as the lead referee for an epistemic arbitration board that resolves persistent disagreements between expert forecasters in high-stakes domains—presently epidemiological modeling and climate projection—where both parties have fully disclosed their belief updates but remain at odds. Because these disputes recur weekly and each determination must be signed within thirty minutes, I need a reusable audit procedure grounded in the common prior assumption and its canonical conditions, one that I can execute without access to internal code, private datasets, or proprietary notebooks. The deliverable should be an ordered checklist with explicit go/no-go gates, designed for a practitioner who understands the logic of rational belief revision but is not a formal epistemologist.

The procedure should distinguish four failure modes when disagreement persists despite common knowledge of rationality: genuinely divergent priors; hidden information breaking common knowledge; divergent interpretation of the shared signal space—an "epistemic architecture mismatch" in which agents apply different update rules to identically described evidence; and a failure of correct Bayesian updating within a shared architecture. For each gate, specify the precise question to pose to the parties, the minimal public evidence required to pass or fail the gate, and the explicit point at which the audit must halt and record an irreducible judgment call rather than a procedural verdict. You must flag where the procedure will misfire, especially when an agent strategically frames the signal space to preserve disagreement, and you must mark which steps depend on normative commitments—such as whether a merged prior is obligatory—rather than on descriptive logic alone.

Apply the audit to three cases: (a) two epidemiologists observing the same forty-person cohort study, one applying Bayesian hierarchical shrinkage and the other treating it as a direct frequentist point estimate, so that the disagreement hinges on architecture rather than data; (b) two climate modelers with identical parameter priors who diverge because one updates on an ensemble mean and the other on the full ensemble spread; and (c) a scenario in which the checklist forces the conclusion that disagreement is irrational under common knowledge, yet I have independent reason to believe one party holds an undisclosed proprietary dataset—requiring me to document an explicit override of the procedural verdict. State clearly what the audit cannot settle, such as how to construct a shared prior ex nihilo or whether strategic framing should be penalized. Finally, say what specific argument or empirical finding would lead you to abandon the claim that all persistent disagreement under common knowledge is traceable to these four failure modes, and identify the point in your reasoning most likely to fail.
