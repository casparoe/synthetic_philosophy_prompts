I am preparing a short essay for an advanced seminar on moral epistemology and cultural psychology, tentatively titled “Calibrated Intuitions: Folk Moral Variance and the Transparency Fallacy.” Rather than submit a full draft, I have outlined the core argument below. I would welcome precise, critical comments—particularly on whether the central analogy holds, whether the proposed distinction avoids begging the question against relativism, and whether there is a well-known objection I have overlooked.

Argument notes:

1. The WEIRD literature establishes that Western, educated, industrialized, rich, and democratic populations are cognitive outliers, especially in reasoning about cooperation, fairness, and authority. Parallel cross-cultural work on folk morality shows that different societies assign markedly different weights to care, fairness, loyalty, authority, and sanctity.

2. Philosophers typically interpret this variance in one of two ways: as evidence for moral relativism (no culture-independent truths) or as superficial noise obscuring a universal human moral nature.

3. I propose an intermediate framework: the ecological calibration model. On this view, folk moral cognition is not a direct representation of moral reality but an adaptive heuristic system calibrated to local social ecologies—specifically, to variables such as group scale, kin density, and resource predictability. Divergent surface intuitions can therefore arise from a shared underlying normative architecture shaped by different ecological demands.

4. This leads to what I call the transparency fallacy: the assumption that verbally reported moral intuitions are transparent readouts of metaphysical moral commitments. If calibration shapes both the formation and the expression of moral judgment, then cultural differences in reported morality may reflect differences in social ecology rather than access to distinct moral domains.

5. To defend the compatibility of calibration with objectivity, I invoke an analogy to perceptual constancy—specifically, color vision. Just as the visual system adjusts to illumination conditions while tracking objective surface properties, moral cognition might adjust to social conditions while tracking objective normative properties. The presence of an adjustment mechanism does not, by itself, entail subjectivity.

6. The critical vulnerability is specifying the invariant core. If every normative judgment can be explained away by ecological calibration, objectivity is left with nothing to grip. I tentatively propose that the invariant element is not a set of first-order rules but a second-order cooperative reciprocity structure—though I am uncertain whether this is robust enough to satisfy the objectivist without collapsing back into universalism.

Please comment specifically on: (a) the coherence of the ecological calibration model as a philosophical position distinct from both standard relativism and standard universalism; (b) whether the color-constancy analogy genuinely supports moral objectivity or instead collapses into a form of constructivism; (c) whether the transparency fallacy names a real problem in the literature or whether I have invented a straw target; and (d) whether my proposed invariant core is either too thin to do meaningful philosophical work or so thick that it undermines the calibration thesis I have advanced.
