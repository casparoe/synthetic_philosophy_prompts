Over the past eighteen months I have been working in sensory-evaluation consulting, where calibrated first-person taste reports serve as the primary data for product formulation. Recently I returned to Daniel Dennett's essay Quining Qualia, particularly the Chase and Sanborn coffee-taster scenario in which two subjects appear to have inverted qualitative experiences while maintaining identical behavioral and verbal dispositions. Dennett presses that if qualia are conceived as intrinsic, private, immediately accessible properties, then the possibility of such undetectable inversions renders them an incoherent posit—something to be quined away rather than explained. The issue is no longer abstract for me: if Dennett's challenge succeeds, the subjective reports I treat as tracking real properties may instead be tracking nothing more than functional states dressed in theoretical confusion.

I am now trying to structure a limited research program—roughly three years, conceptual and philosophical rather than empirical neuroscience—to determine whether taking Dennett's argument seriously should actually change practice in fields that depend on first-person reporting, including sensory science, clinical phenomenology, and even emerging protocols for evaluating artificial systems. I do not want a bibliography or a survey of positions. I want a defended agenda: which open questions and downstream lines of work matter most, ordered by criteria that include upstream dependence on other questions, tractability for non-empirical philosophy, and genuine practical stakes versus philosophical noise.

Specifically, please address the following in your ordering and reasoning:

First, among the live conceptual responses to Dennett—the defense of phenomenal concepts, illusionist strategies, appeals to direct acquaintance, and the claim that descriptive revision rather than property elimination resolves the Chase/Sanborn divergence—which are genuinely upstream? That is, which resolution, if correct, would most reshape the others, and which are merely defensive adjustments?

Second, rank the practical implications that actually carry weight for practices relying on subjective reports. Does the incoherence of qualia undermine the validity of sensory-evaluation protocols, alter how clinical phenomenology should be conducted, or affect the design of reporting instruments for artificial systems? Which of these stakes are real and which are overrated?

Third, if one interprets the Chase/Sanborn case not as a failure of private language but as evidence that qualitative descriptions are always revisable functional classifications, what new conceptual problems emerge—particularly concerning inter-subjective calibration, the normativity of report correction, and whether a fully functionalist taxonomy can preserve the explanatory role that qualia were meant to play?

Defend your prioritization with explicit criteria: importance relative to a three-year conceptual research program, tractability without laboratory access, dependency relations among the questions, and what kind of argument or conceptual clarification over the next few years would cause you to reorder them. Deliver an agenda, not a reading list.
