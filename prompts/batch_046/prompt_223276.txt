As a retired physicist who has only recently begun working through philosophy in a systematic way, I find that I keep constructing reductios whose premises feel individually sound yet whose conclusions I cannot accept. The argument below is one such case. I want a committed diagnosis, not a menu of options: identify the specific step that fails, explain why that step initially looked valid, and state clearly what I must concede—conceptually, not merely rhetorically—to reject it. I would prefer to preserve the core asymmetry stated in (1) and the independence claim in (7), but I recognize that preference is not proof.

(1) Benatar's asymmetry holds: the absence of suffering is good even when there is no subject present to enjoy that good, whereas the absence of pleasure is not bad when there is no subject present to miss it.

(2) This evaluation applies not only to people who never come into existence, but to any state of affairs correctly describable as "a sentient being could have existed at time t, but does not."

(3) Consider an existing person, Alice, and any future time t after the present. There is a state W1 in which Alice exists at t and experiences something, and a state W2 in which Alice does not exist at t.

(4) In W2, there is an absence of suffering (good, per (1)) and an absence of pleasure (not bad, per (1)), and there is no subject present at t to be deprived.

(5) In W1, Alice is present, so she can benefit from pleasure or suffer harm; but the asymmetry in (1) supplies no comparable unconditional good of absence to weigh against those contingent risks.

(6) Evaluating W1 and W2 through (1) alone, W2 is asymmetrically preferable: it secures the unconditional good of absent pain without incurring the unconditional bad of absent pleasure, and it avoids the contingent bad of actual pain.

(7) Because (3) holds for every future t independently, the preference for W2 over W1 applies separately and equally to each moment of Alice's potential future.

(8) Therefore, for every future moment, it is better that Alice not exist then.

(9) Consequently, the state of affairs in which Alice ceases to exist now—thereby ensuring W2 for all subsequent t—is superior to any state in which she continues.

The conclusion that one ought to bring about one's own immediate non-existence strikes me as plainly absurd, yet I cannot name the slide. Is the error in extending (1) beyond never-existing people to the future stages of an actual subject (step 2 or 4), in treating temporal moments as independently evaluable (step 7), or in aggregating momentary preferences into a global prescription for cessation (step 8–9)? Give me your definite choice, defend it against the other two candidates, and tell me exactly what accepting your diagnosis costs for the scope and limits of Benatar's reasoning.
