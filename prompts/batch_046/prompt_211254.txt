I'm moderating a colloquium Q&A with an unnamed philosopher who has recently advanced what she calls the "non-substitutability thesis" in debates about higher-order evidence and epistemic akrasia. She holds that when a belief is grounded in an extended, executed first-order reasoning process—say, a long inferential chain or formal derivation—higher-order evidence of one's general fallibility in such domains cannot rationally defeat that specific belief unless one can actually locate the error within the reasoning one performed. On her view, this makes a structured form of epistemic akrasia not merely permissible but rationally obligatory: one ought to maintain the belief because the executed reasoning stands, while simultaneously believing that, statistically, beliefs formed in this manner are likely false. She dismisses the standard conciliationist reply—that one should simply calibrate confidence downward to match one's expected accuracy rate—as illegitimately treating executed reasoning like a black-box statistical prediction.

Write me a progressive sequence of interview questions that makes her dismissal of conciliationism progressively harder to sustain. The questions should press whether her view collapses into a familiar form of level-splitting, whether it can accommodate cases in which the potential error is genuinely unlocatable, and how she distinguishes rational non-substitutability from motivated reasoning. At each stage, anticipate her standard reply and include the immediate follow-up that undermines it. Conclude with a question asking what conceptual or empirical result would genuinely compel her to abandon the non-substitutability claim. Please answer in British English.
