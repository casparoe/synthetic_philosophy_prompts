Write the opening remarks for a philosophy department graduation — about five minutes spoken, casual but not sloppy. Half the audience are epistemic logicians who can smell a misformulated Fitch paradox from the parking lot, and half are relatives who think Fitch is an investment firm — there mostly wrong, but not totally. The speaker should use Fitch's paradox (the idea that if every truth is knowable then every truth is already known) as the actual backbone of the talk, not just a decorative reference. It needs to shape what the speaker can and can't say: no promises that the gradutes will eventually know everything, and a real argument that permanent not-knowing is part of being smart rather than a failure. Inject one specific, slightly offbeat unknown truth that actually matters for living well — not tomorrow's weather, maybe whether consciousness is fundamentally physical, or whether any of them will ever truly understand another person's grief. Make it comfort the parents without talking down to the philosophers. At the end, have the speaker say exactly what would change their mind about this reading of the paradox — for instance, if they were convinced the knowability principle applies only to empirical claims and not to self-knowledge, or if the closure step turns out invalid for contingent truths, and flag where the reasoning is shakiest. Keep it around 600 words, one typo is fine.
