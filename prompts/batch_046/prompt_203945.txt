A friend in my aesthetics reading group offered this argument about tragedy, and I cannot find the exit. (1) If experiencing an emotion is rational, then either that emotion is not genuinely harmful, or it produces a compensating benefit that outweighs the harm. (2) Tragedy makes audiences feel genuine grief and fear—emotions we avoid in ordinary life precisely because they are harmful. (3) So for tragic grief to be rational, there must be a compensating benefit. (4) The usual candidates—catharsis, moral insight, formal beauty—are all available without the grief: catharsis can occur through comedy, insight through philosophy, beauty through non-tragic forms. (5) So tragedy offers no unique compensating benefit; the grief is unnecessary harm. (6) Therefore seeking tragedy is irrational. (7) By parallel reasoning, any art that produces unnecessary negative affect—horror, melancholy music, dark comedy—is also irrational. (8) But if rational agents systematically value a widespread class of irrational practices, then rational choice has no authority in aesthetic life. (9) So either (1) is false and genuinely harmful emotions can be rational without compensation—which removes any principled barrier to seeking harmful emotions outside art—or (6) is false, which requires showing that tragedy’s grief is either not genuinely harmful or uniquely compensated, despite (4). I would prefer to keep (1) intact, since it governs ordinary practical reasoning well. Which step should I reject, why did it look plausible, and what exactly does giving it up cost?
