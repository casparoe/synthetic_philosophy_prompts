The seminar has been working through two-dimensional semantics, specifically the framework that distinguishes a sentence’s primary intension, which assigns extensions across worlds considered as actual and is tied to epistemic possibility, from its secondary intension, which fixes extension in the actual world and is tied to metaphysical possibility, along with its standard treatments of necessary a posteriori identities and the Twin Earth intuition. I would like you to restate that core machinery—primary versus secondary intension, the separation of apriority from necessity, and the behavior of rigid designators or indexicals—using only the resources of epistemic game theory, as precisely and honestly as you can.

For each component, tell me what translates cleanly onto partitions, belief hierarchies, or rationalizability; what requires distortion, such as whether a centered world can be reconstructed as a player-specific information set without losing the idea that the same speaker can occupy different centers, or whether character survives as a strategy mapping contexts to contents; and what simply has no counterpart in this vocabulary at all. Then provide the residue report: does the leftover material indicate that two-dimensional semantics relies on metaphysical commitments about content and modality that do no explanatory work beyond what ex-ante versus ex-post evaluation already captures, or does it show that the game-theoretic framework is missing something essential about linguistic content? I am particularly interested in whether my intuition—that the 2D distinction largely collapses into a familiar distinction between reasoning before and after the true state is commonly known—is defensible, or whether it flattens a genuinely distinct conceptual layer that should not be eliminated.
