Write a sixteen-line blank-verse poem arranged in four quatrains and a closing couplet, spoken by a speaker weighing whether to enter a modified experience machine that offers not pleasure but “borrowed achievement”—genuinely realized within the simulation but absent from the speaker’s actual life (published work, mastered skills, reciprocal friendships). Structure the poem so that each quatrain embodies one theory of well-being: the first hedonism (the machine fails because felt joy is not the point), the second desire-fulfillment (it succeeds because every desire for achievement is satisfied), the third an objective-list theory (it succeeds only if the goods are real, which they are inside the simulation), and the fourth a hybrid view requiring both internal satisfaction and actual-world contact. The closing couplet should break the blank-verse flow with an abrupt caesura or enjambment that performs the collapse of the distinction between being well and merely seeming well, and should leave unresolved whether the speaker enters. The poem should be accurate enough that a philosopher of well-being recognizes the four positions, and the formal progression should make the conceptual argument audible rather than decorative.
