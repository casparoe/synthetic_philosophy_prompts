I'm consulting for a local heritage board and we keep running into these pocket finds during construction — not full excavations, just clusters of objects that might tell us something about beliefs or social institutions or might just be garbage with patterns we imagine. I need a reusable decision procedure my team can run in about an hour, grounded in how archaeologists actually reason from material objects to beliefs and institutions, but stripped down so a non-specialist can use it without guessing at theory. It should ask questions in order, say what counts as a sufficient answer at each step, mark where it stops with a hard no, and flag the points where it's not a fact question anymore but a values call that needs a human sign-off. Be honest about where it will misfire — especially with symmetric redundancy in ritual assemblages and negative-space institutional inference, where absence or repetition is supposed to signal hierarchy or shared belief — and don't drop distinctions that actually matter for the reasoning. Then run the procedure on three cases: a circular pit containing five identical broken ceramic vessels arranged evenly; a carved bone with markings that line up with a lunar calendar we think was used in that region; and a high-status grave full of local goods but completely lacking imported beads, which the board will want to read as proof of economic isolation even though it might just be sampling bias. For at least one of these, the procedure should come out "probably overinterpreting" even though everyone wants a neat story.

Please keep what's fairly settled in archaeological reasoning clearly seperate from what's genuinely contested among people who actually work on this — don't present one interpretive stance as the universal view, and don't dress up a judgment call as if it's an objective result. I don't need citations or a reading list; I just need the steps, the tests, and an honest note about where the answer depends on who you ask.
