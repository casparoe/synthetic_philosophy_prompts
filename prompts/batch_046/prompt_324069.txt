I serve on the clinical-ethics review board for a residential long-term care network, and we are increasingly asked to approve or reject behavioral-intervention plans for residents with advanced cognitive decline who exhibit intense, repetitive actions—pacing an identical corridor loop for hours, arranging objects in unvarying patterns, or repeating a single phrase with mechanical regularity. Staff typically want to interrupt these loops to prevent physical harm or social isolation, while families often object that the repetition is the last trace of the resident's autonomous agency and should not be disrupted. To resolve these disputes consistently, I need a reusable decision procedure—grounded in the philosophical distinction between sphexish mechanical rigidity and genuinely flexible, responsive action—that my board can apply in about fifteen minutes without requiring specialist philosophical training.

The procedure should be structured as a sequence of ordered diagnostic questions: for each behavior, the board must determine whether the action varies with relevant environmental changes, whether it can be interrupted or redirected without severe distress, whether it serves an identifiable goal that survives reflection, and whether its persistence depends on a failure to register changed circumstances. For each question, specify what counts as a sufficient answer, where the answer mandates leaving the behavior intact, where it permits gentle interruption, and—crucially—where the question is not empirical but axiological, requiring the board to hand the decision back to the family or an ethics consultant. The procedure must explicitly flag cases it cannot settle, particularly where dignity and welfare pull in opposite directions, and it must acknowledge that no behavioral checklist can fully replace judgment about what counts as meaningful agency in severe cognitive impairment.

Please apply the procedure to three proposals: first, stopping a resident who paces a hallway loop that now terminates at a locked fire door she no longer recognizes, where the path has lost its original destination; second, allowing a resident to continue folding and refolding the same napkin despite staff concerns about withdrawal, where the action produces visible calm but no external goal; and third, interrupting a resident's repetitive vocalization that disturbs other residents but appears to be the only communicative initiative he makes. Include at least one case where the procedure yields an answer that the direct-care staff will find unacceptable, and conclude by stating precisely what the procedure is incapable of deciding—especially regarding whether preserving a rigid but soothing loop respects autonomy or merely prolongs a sphexish absence of freedom.
