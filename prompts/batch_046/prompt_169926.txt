I work as a software engineer and have no formal training in philosophy, but after a long debugging session last week I found myself wondering what pleasure actually is. I know there is an old philosophical puzzle about whether different pleasures share anything in common, and I want to see whether my own judgments line up with some hidden principle or whether they just fall apart.

Here are my verdicts on six cases. P1: the sharp relief when a failing CI pipeline finally turns green after hours of fixing dependency conflicts. P2: the immediate sensory pleasure of biting into a cold, sweet mango. P3: the quiet, diffuse satisfaction of finishing a long run and sitting in the shade. P4: the intellectual click when an obscure caching bug suddenly makes sense and the architecture feels transparent. P5: the guilty, fleeting enjoyment of procrastinating by reading old commit messages instead of working. P6: the calm aesthetic pleasure of seeing a well-structured recursive function printed out in clean indentation.

My judgments are these. I want to call all six pleasures, but P1 and P4 feel cognitively similar—both involve resolution of tension—while P2 feels purely bodily and P3 feels like background absence of pain rather than presence of something. P5 bothers me: it feels good, yet I hesitate to call it a genuine pleasure because it undermines my goals. P6 seems closer to P4 but thinner, more like appreciating form than achieving anything. My tentative principle is that pleasure is any experience in which an internal expectation is exceeded in a way that does not trigger an immediate action demand—what I might call positive non-urgent discrepancy. But this seems wrong for P2, which feels biologically urgent; wrong for P3, which involves no exceeded expectation; and wrong for P5, which is driven by avoidance rather than discrepancy. So I am stuck.

Is there a coherent account that preserves all six verdicts, perhaps by allowing pleasure to be a family-resemblance category with several sub-types? Or must I drop at least one verdict—probably P5 or P3—to maintain unity? If you think there is a unifying principle, state it precisely, identify the case that motivates each clause, and say what it predicts for two further cases: the pleasure of recognizing a familiar melody after years, and the pleasure of confirming that a colleague's error was exactly where you suspected. Also please be explicit about which of my six verdicts your account cannot accommodate, and whether that means overturning my judgment or splitting pleasure into distinct phenomena. I am not looking for bibliographies or citations; I just want the reasoning made explicit.
