I've been thinking on my own about the Bronze Age collapse, the Maya, and Easter Island, and I've settled on a view I can't quite defend: maybe "collapse" isn't something societies actually do, but just a label we apply when they reorganize into forms with lower complexity that later historians don't recognize, mainly because the palaces, writing, and monuments we use for identification disappear. Before I commit to this, I want to know what would genuinely count against it. Not just more disaster stories, but specifically: what kind of finding—about whether survivors experienced sharp discontinuity, about institutional structures persisting or vanishing independently of monuments, or about energy and demographic trends—would actually threaten the idea that failure is always just an unrecognized transformation? Also, is there a conceptual weakness in defining collapse by the loss of recognizable architecture rather than by the lived experience of the people involved? Please rank the potential defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, say which ones a defender of my position could reasonably dismiss, and answer in American English.
