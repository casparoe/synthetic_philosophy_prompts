Having spent a career in experimental physiology before turning recently to philosophy of mind, I find myself puzzled by the apparent stalemate over Daniel Dennett's fourfold taxonomy of minds—Darwinian, Skinnerian, Popperian, and Gregorian creatures. Comparative researchers now document behaviors in corvids and cephalopods that seem to blur the line between Skinnerian trial-and-error learning and Popperian pre-selection among internal representations, yet philosophers continue to treat the distinction as one of functional architecture rather than observable performance. Meanwhile, the question of whether Gregorian culture requires language proper, or merely sustained tool-mediated transmission, remains equally frozen. Is this impasse a hidden empirical dispute about cognitive mechanisms in non-human species, a verbal disagreement about the criteria for representational pre-selection, or are evolutionary biologists and philosophers of mind simply addressing different questions—phylogenetic adaptation versus design-space navigation—under one label? Diagnose the disagreement concretely: identify whether a new formal distinction, an experimental result, or a revised taxonomy—perhaps one that inserts an intermediate class of predictive-exploratory minds operating via structured error minimization rather than explicit hypothesis testing—could actually move the debate, or whether the four categories are best understood as pedagogical idealizations with no expectation of resolution.
