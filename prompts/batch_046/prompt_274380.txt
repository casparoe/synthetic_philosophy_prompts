Consider the view that paradoxes of legal self-reference—such as a constitutional provision that specifies both its own substantive content and the procedure for altering that content—are dissolved rather than solved: they arise only from conflating object-language rules with meta-language rules about them, and once we distinguish the rule that is amended from the rule of recognition that authorizes the amendment, no genuine circularity remains. Even apparently recursive cases, where an amendment procedure contains provisions for altering itself, are handled by nesting logical levels rather than looping them. I want an honest assessment of what it would take for this dissolution to fail. Which formal properties of a legal system—perhaps involving non-well-founded hierarchies or self-referential amendment clauses that resist termination—would have to obtain for the level-distinction to leave a real, unresolved residue? What would a counterexample have to demonstrate: that the meta-rule is itself part of the object-language being amended, that the nesting never terminates in practice, or that legal actors treat the distinction as pragmatic convention rather than logical structure? Distinguish evidence that would be decisive (for example, a formal result showing unavoidable inconsistency in any hierarchical reconstruction of a particular constitutional design) from evidence that would merely be awkward (for example, historical drafting debates where drafters seemed unaware of the distinction). Say also what defenders of the dissolution view themselves concede as counting against it—do they allow that some constitutional architectures are genuinely paradoxical, or do they claim universal applicability? I want to know whether this position is well insulated by qualifications or genuinely exposed, and whether that insulation is a virtue or a vice. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
