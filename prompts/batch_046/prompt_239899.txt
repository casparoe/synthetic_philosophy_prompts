I have adopted the view that the free energy principle, taken in its normative rather than algorithmic register, states a transcendental condition on any system that maintains its own existence: such a system must approximate variational free energy minimization relative to a Markov blanket, not because neurons execute that computation literally, but because any sustained self-organization is formally equivalent to inference about the causes of sensory exchange. On this reading, predictive processing is not an empirical claim about cortical microcircuits competing with feedforward or reinforcement-learning accounts; it is a claim about the conditions under which a boundary between system and environment can persist at all. The apparent flexibility of the framework—its ability to redescribe almost any adaptive dynamics as precision-weighted prediction-error reduction—is therefore not a scientific vice but a feature of its logical status, analogous to the claim that any rational agent must avoid contradiction.

What would genuinely count against this position? I want an honest map of its exposure, not a list of misunderstandings its defenders already dismiss.

Specifically:

- Which empirical findings—say, about neural dynamics, artificial learning architectures, or pathological states—would have to be true to undermine the normative reading? Would it matter if cortical computation turned out to be purely feedforward with no recurrent predictive dynamics? Would it matter if an artificial system maintained stable organization without any separation of sensory and internal states corresponding to a Markov blanket?

- Which conceptual objections strike at the heart rather than the periphery? For instance, if the formal equivalence between free-energy minimization and self-organization turns out to depend on a particular parameterization that is not invariant across descriptions, does that collapse the normative claim into a redescription? Or if precision-weighting can be shown to be derivable from simpler control-theoretic principles without invoking Bayesian inference, does the framework lose its explanatory privilege?

- How should we rank the defeaters? Distinguish between fatal objections (those that show the normative reading either false or empty), serious but survivable ones (those that require substantial revision of auxiliary claims about Markov blankets or the scope of the principle), and merely awkward results (those that show the framework is less parsimonious than rivals in specific domains but do not threaten its core).

- Which of these would a committed defender of the normative free-energy principle concede as genuinely counting against them, and which would they treat as misapplications—say, criticisms that confuse the algorithmic and normative registers, or that assume a false dichotomy between inference and control?

- Finally, assess whether this position has been qualified into unfalsifiability. If every apparent counterexample can be absorbed by redefining the system's boundary conditions or by shifting between algorithmic and normative registers, is that insulation a philosophical strength—like the immunity of logic to empirical counterexample—or a weakness that renders the view incapable of risking anything? Be direct: is the normative free-energy principle well protected in a way that serves it, or has it become a framework that explains everything and therefore predicts nothing?
