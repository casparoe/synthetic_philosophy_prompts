You are the senior standards editor at a British political magazine, and you need a reusable decision procedure based on Hannah Arendt’s distinction between the traditional political lie, which conceals one specific checkable fact, and the modern organised lie, which replaces shared reality with an institutional fiction that the liar also inhabits, to classify government communications quickly without philosophical consultation. Design an ordered checklist with explicit questions, sufficient answers, and clear stopping points—marking where assessment stops with a factual classification, where it stops because the claim is normative rather than factual, and where it must be handed to a human editor because values rather than conditions decide the outcome—so it can be run on any statement. Apply the procedure to two scenarios: first, a minister’s direct denial of a documented meeting for which minutes exist; second, a sustained official campaign attributing domestic unrest to foreign orchestration despite internal intelligence that contradicts it, and identify which classification the procedure yields that an editor committed to transparency will likely find unwelcome. State explicitly what the procedure cannot settle, particularly where normative framing and factual denial become indistinguishable, and present the entire answer as exactly five numbered items, each no more than two sentences, with no introduction and no conclusion.
