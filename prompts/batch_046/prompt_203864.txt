As a doctoral student in philosophy of language working on comparative skeptical paradoxes, I have been struck by an analogy that circulates both in my graduate seminar and in recent commentary: Kripke's rule-following considerations are presented as structurally parallel to Goodman's grue paradox. The comparison typically runs like this. Just as the claim that all emeralds are green and the rival claim that all emeralds are grue are both inductively compatible with our finite observational history, so the claim that I mean addition (and therefore should answer 125 to 68 plus 57) and the rival claim that I mean quaddition (and therefore should answer 5) are both behaviorally compatible with my past arithmetic practice. From this parallel, my interlocutors draw a specific dissolution: they argue that Kripke's skeptical problem is not a distinctive challenge about semantic normativity or about what it is to grasp a rule, but merely a special case of the general underdetermination of theoretical projection by finite evidence. Once we accept that projectibility is governed by pragmatic convention rather than by metaphysical fact, they maintain, the pressure to discover a fact of the matter about whether I mean plus or quus evaporates, just as the pressure to discover a metaphysical distinction between green and grue evaporates.

I am skeptical that the analogy carries as much weight as they suppose, and I want a careful audit of the mapping. Could you specify precisely what corresponds to what—the observed sample of emeralds, the predicates green and grue, the inductive inference, and Goodman's conventionalist resolution—in the rule-following case? Then identify the disanalogies that matter for the intended use: for instance, does the normative dimension of rule-following, in virtue of which I ought to answer 125 rather than 5 if I genuinely mean addition, have any genuine counterpart in the grue scenario? Does the first-person authority of my own intentions, or the social-corrective structure of linguistic practice, map onto anything in Goodman's epistemological framework? Finally, assess whether the conclusion—that there is no special skeptical problem about rules, only a generic problem about predicate projection—survives once these structural differences are on the table, or whether the analogy quietly imports features, such as an exclusively third-person observational stance or the absence of normative constraint, that the target case lacks. If the analogy fails to license the dissolution, what comparison would better capture what is genuinely at stake in the rule-following paradox?
