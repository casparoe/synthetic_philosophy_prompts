I’m trying to work through the normative structure of promises for a philosophy seminar, and I want to check whether I’m missing a hidden premise in my own reasoning. Last week two colleagues of mine, Nadia and Theo, got into an extended disagreement over a hypothetical case they invented together—the “redundant-lecture” scenario—and their back-and-forth quickly moved from the particular example to a deeper dispute about what makes promises bind at all. I’m recounting their exchange as a neutral bystander; I don’t think either is obviously wrong, but I need a point-by-point adjudication rather than a simple verdict.

Here is how the discussion unfolded. Nadia began by arguing that promises are exercises of a normative power: when I promise you I will attend your lecture, I do not merely predict my behavior or generate an expectation, but place myself under your authority with respect to a specific course of action. That authority relation is directed—you are the party who can release me—and it persists until either I perform the act or you explicitly discharge me. Theo objected that this picture treats promises like hidden private legislation and ignores their essentially cooperative character. Their binding force, he said, derives from mutual recognition that breaking them undermines trust and practical planning. So if you cancel the lecture but forget to tell me, and I show up to an empty room, I have not violated any cooperative norm by failing to sit through nothing; the only failures here are your failure to communicate, or perhaps my failure to confirm. Nadia replied that Theo is smuggling in an unnecessary harm-based condition. Even when no harm results, she insisted, I still owed you the specific performance; the obligation is structurally like an unpaid debt that does not vanish merely because the creditor no longer needs repayment, unless the creditor formally writes it off. Theo then introduced a distinction he thinks Nadia overlooks: there is a difference between a promise being fulfilled and being extinguished. He argued that obligations can suffer “normative obsolescence”—when the practical reasons that originally grounded the cooperative commitment disappear entirely, the obligation self-terminates without requiring explicit release from the promisee. Nadia countered that this sounds like a convenient fiction. If obligations could evaporate whenever circumstances change, promises would be unreliable instruments for intertemporal planning; their whole point is to bind across changes in circumstance, which is precisely why only the promisee’s explicit release—not mere obsolescence—can dissolve them.

In particular: is Nadia correct that promises create a directed authority that survives the disappearance of practical purpose? Is Theo correct that cooperative function is essential, and that normative obsolescence can genuinely extinguish an obligation? Where does each of them miss something—perhaps about the role of notification, about whether harm is necessary for wronging, or about whether a middle ground exists between authority-transfer and pure coordination?
