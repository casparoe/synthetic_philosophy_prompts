I am preparing the interview guide for a philosophy seminar podcast, and next week's guest is an unnamed defender of the strong impossibility thesis about self-deception — the view that first-order self-deception, understood as the simultaneous conscious holding of contradictory beliefs directed at a single state of affairs, is conceptually incoherent rather than merely psychologically uncommon. Rather than asking her to restate the position, I need eight to ten questions that probe its structural commitments and anticipate her standard replies. Please include at least one question on the boundary between strategic belief-avoidance, or motivated ignorance, and genuine self-deception, paired with a follow-up keyed to her likely appeal to a partitioned-mind model: ask specifically how that model avoids reintroducing contradictory beliefs at the subpersonal level that her thesis forbids at the personal level. I also want a question on whether her account requires doxastic voluntarism, given that intentional belief-formation seems necessary for the 'self' in self-deception to operate as an agent rather than a mere causal locus; if she denies voluntarism, the follow-up should press her on how she explains the intentional structure of rationalization without smuggling voluntary control over belief back in. A third question should address higher-order monitoring failures — whether an agent can correctly represent her first-order belief-forming dispositions while systematically misrepresenting them at the second order — together with a follow-up asking whether this constitutes self-deception on her view or merely a cognitive limitation that leaves her conceptual claim untouched.

Beyond these, I would like you to weave in a novel scenario I have been developing, which I call the archivist's dilemma: a researcher deliberately misfiles contradictory data within her own organizational system, later retrieves only the supporting files, sincerely reports that she never encountered the counterevidence, yet retains complete procedural knowledge of the hidden archive's location. Ask whether this case counts as self-deception for her, or whether it falls outside the category altogether, and have the follow-up ask what her answer reveals about whether agency in self-deception requires simultaneous conscious access to both beliefs. Finally, close with a question about what empirical finding — for instance, work on confabulation in split-brain patients or on the effects of self-affirmation on selective information search — could genuinely threaten her conceptual claim rather than simply demonstrating its psychological rarity. Keep all questions focused on the mechanics of the view; avoid generic biographical prompts, and ensure that each primary question is paired with a targeted follow-up.
