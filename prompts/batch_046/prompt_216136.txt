I'm working through a problem in constitutional theory that sits at the intersection of rational-choice precommitment and democratic legitimacy. The familiar idea—sometimes traced to Jon Elster's reading of Ulysses—is that a polity might rationally bind itself against future temptations by entrenching rules that are hard to amend, such as balanced-budget provisions or rights protections. Critics reply that this amounts to intergenerational tyranny: present majorities should not be able to lock in preferences that constrain future democratic majorities. I want to push past this well-rehearsed standoff by introducing a distinction that doesn't usually appear in the standard treatments: between first-order precommitment, which binds future policy choices, and second-order meta-precommitment, which binds the procedures by which first-order commitments can themselves be revised or suspended. The question is whether adding this second layer solves the legitimacy problem or merely relocates it.

Write an essay on whether constitutional Ulysses contracts—understood specifically through the lens of first-order and second-order meta-precommitment—are democratically legitimate. Weigh the main arguments: on one side, the claim that rational self-binding protects long-term interests against myopic majoritarian cycles and that meta-rules can be democratically authorized in advance; on the other side, the objection that any entrenchment, even of meta-procedures, violates the principle that no generation should be permanently bound by another, and that second-order locks may actually deepen the tyranny by making democratic correction structurally impossible. Don't end on a non-committal note: reach and defend a specific verdict about whether meta-precommitment salvages the democratic legitimacy of constitutional precommitment, or whether the legitimacy deficit is irreducible. In particular, address whether there is a principled stopping point—some level of entrenchment that is legitimate and some that is not—or whether the very idea of binding future democratic will is conceptually flawed regardless of how many meta-layers are added.
