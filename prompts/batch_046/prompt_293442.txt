I am preparing a seminar presentation on conceptual slippage in microdomains—specifically, cases in which a concept is copied from its native practical context into a technically adjacent but ontologically alien environment, where it becomes fluid in ways that strain any essentialist definition. To ground the discussion in a concrete but non-standard scenario, I have been working with a synthetic case that does not appear in standard literature: generative culinary algorithms that merge traditional recipe architectures with laboratory-synthesized compounds—molecules engineered to perform the functional roles of salt, fat, or acid, yet lacking any chemical taxonomy shared by natural ingredients—to produce consumable outputs I have been calling ghost dishes. These outputs are reproducible and nutritionally defined, yet they fall outside every established language-game of cooking, cuisine, or gastronomy.

If Ludwig Wittgenstein were alive to observe these algorithmic productions, how would he likely assess their conceptual status? Drawing on his well-known rejection of necessary and sufficient conditions, his account of family resemblance, and his view that conceptual meaning is constituted by use embedded within a form of life rather than by private mental representation or statistical co-occurrence, would he argue that we ought to extend our existing culinary vocabulary through copycat analogies—treating, for instance, a synthetic polymer that behaves like table salt in a traditional Italian pasta recipe as genuinely falling under the concept salt within an expanded language-game? Or would he maintain that the synthetic practice is so detached from the human activities and shared practices that originally gave these concepts their point that any such extension is a category mistake, requiring instead an entirely new, non-copycat conceptual framework?

More precisely, I am interested in how he would adjudicate the tension in a specific edge case within this microdomain. Consider an algorithm that generates a recipe structurally identical to a classic pasta preparation, substituting only a laboratory-synthesized compound for salt; the system classifies the compound as salt purely on the basis of functional and contextual co-occurrence in its training data, without any engagement with the chemical or social practices that constitute salinity in human cooking. Would Wittgenstein see this statistical blending as a legitimate instance of conceptual fluidity—an organic expansion of the language-game—or as a revealing failure that demonstrates why copycat conceptual slippage collapses once the underlying form of life is absent? I want a precise prediction rather than a generic summary of his philosophy, with particular attention to whether he would regard the algorithm’s treatment of the compound as conceptually meaningful or as an empty syntactic mimicry.
