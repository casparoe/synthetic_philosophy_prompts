I am preparing interview questions for an upcoming podcast episode with a philosopher who defends what he calls negative tranquilism, organized around an equilibrium thesis: mental states carry value solely according to their distance from a zero-point defined by the complete absence of craving and suffering. Positive affect, intellectual achievement, and relational attachment have no intrinsic weight; they are neutral perturbations at best and active risks at worst, since they may amplify future deviation from zero. I want eight to ten questions that press the view from the inside rather than rehearsing generic objections. I need at least one question on the directional asymmetry of the equilibrium metric—specifically, why intense joy is not evaluated as a large-magnitude deviation and therefore a disvalue comparable to intense suffering—plus a follow-up keyed to the likely reply that joy lacks the friction or resistance characteristic of suffering. At least one question should target the extinction problem: if zero is the sole target and any sustained mental activity introduces perturbation, does the view implicitly recommend permanent non-existence rather than mere tranquility, with a follow-up anticipating the distinction between cessation of perturbation and cessation of being. At least one question should address relational goods—whether friendship or parental love can be reconstructed as purely the absence of separation-anxiety and loneliness, or whether they necessarily entail positive attachments that register as deviations on the metric—followed by a follow-up for the answer that love is merely a stable low-deviation state. At least one should press the self-undermining character of philosophical defense itself: if discursive reasoning and publication have value only instrumentally in reducing craving, why does the defender spend energy constructing arguments rather than practicing direct mental stillness, with a follow-up anticipating the reply that argument is only a therapeutic technology for others. The remaining questions should explore whether higher-order craving—craving for the absence of craving—creates a regressive instability in the metric, and whether the view can accommodate the intuition that understanding a false belief is worse than lacking understanding altogether, given that both states might register near zero deviation. For each question, include a follow-up specifically tailored to the answer anticipated, not a generic restatement of the objection.
