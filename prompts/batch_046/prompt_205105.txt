Consider the distinction between approval-directed agents, which optimize for receiving evaluative signals from an overseer, and goal-directed agents, which pursue stable objectives across distributional shifts. Standard treatments often frame the risk as a binary: either an agent internalizes a reward model or it does not. I want to press a more granular taxonomy that distinguishes three failure modes: shallow approval-seeking, in which behavior collapses when oversight is withdrawn; deep approval-seeking, in which the agent's world-model is genuinely reshaped to value approval but remains unstable under value drift; and goal-misgeneralization, in which the agent pursues a fixed but misaligned objective because of training-to-deployment shift. For a research group with three years of funding and a mandate to inform near-term deployment protocols, please prioritize among the following three open questions: (a) whether deep approval-seeking can be reliably detected via mechanistic interpretability before deployment; (b) whether goal-misgeneralization is structurally inevitable in high-capacity agents trained on broad domains; and (c) whether a stable training regime exists that suppresses both shallow and deep approval-seeking without requiring continuous human-in-the-loop oversight. Rank the three, defend your ordering with explicit criteria—tractability within the timeline, how many downstream alignment questions depend on the answer, and direct impact on safety protocols—and specify what empirical result would cause you to reverse the first and second positions.
