I'm drafting recommendations for a municipal welfare board comparing targeted relief to universal benefits, and I keep encountering the same defense of prioritarian weighting: it is said to work like emergency-room triage. Just as a triage nurse allocates limited bandages and staff to the worst-injured patients—not because they matter more, but because the same unit of care produces the greatest marginal survival gain there—so should social resources flow first to the worst-off, since well-being gains are steepest at the bottom. The analogy is explicitly used to do two pieces of work: it frames priority to the badly off as a rational, non-egalitarian allocation rule (like medicine, not like envy), and it blocks the leveling-down objection by noting that we do not amputate healthy limbs to equalize the ward. I want a precise audit of this comparison. First, lay out the correspondence table—what plays the role of the nurse, the fixed stock of supplies, the patient's pre-injury baseline, the absence of interaction between patients, and the binary outcome of survival versus death—mapped onto prioritarian distribution, resource budgets, well-being baselines, social interdependence, and continuous utility. Second, identify the disanalogies that matter for the intended use: is well-being genuinely like a wound that can be healed with discrete transfers, or does it have adaptive and relational dimensions that make the treatment model misleading? Does triage presuppose a genuinely fixed budget, and if so, does that match a society where benefits to the well-off might expand the total pool through network effects or productivity spillovers? Does the analogy quietly import a threshold-based, non-comparative urgency that applies to life-or-death medicine but not to continuous welfare distributions? Third, say which of these breaks undermine the analogy's support for the two conclusions—especially whether the leveling-down objection revives once we see that social welfare is not a zero-sum ward with independent patients, and whether recognizing adaptive preferences changes the moral arithmetic. Finally, if the triage model collapses, is there a better analogy—perhaps one drawn from risk-pooling or dynamic investment—that can preserve the prioritarian intuition without smuggling in the disanalogies? I am not asking for a bibliography or literature survey; I want the conceptual mapping, its hidden imports, and its failure conditions laid out clearly.
