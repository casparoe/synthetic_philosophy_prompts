For a novel I am drafting about a mathematician who loses faith in proof, I have adopted the position that Carroll's tortoise demonstrates an inescapable circularity in the justification of deduction: that any attempt to rationally vindicate modus ponens or any comparable inference rule must already presuppose that very rule, leaving deductive reasoning rationally groundless and sustained only by brute practical acceptance. I want an honest map of its exposure—what observations, formal results, or philosophical developments would genuinely count against it, not merely the familiar distinction between using a rule and stating it? Specifically, would a successful non-circular account of logical knowledge—whether through direct rational intuition, pragmatic entrenchment that avoids vicious circularity, or an entitlement to basic logical norms—be fatal, or could a defender of the tortoise's challenge absorb each by reclassifying it as practical rather than rational justification? What weight should be given to evidence that human reasoners can revise logical beliefs in response to counterexamples without presupposing the rule in question? And what about the claim that the regress terminates in norms we are entitled to apply without further justification? Rank these defeaters from those that would force abandonment of the view to those that would require only qualification, distinguishing what defenders of the tortoise's position have themselves conceded as damaging from what they dismiss as category errors. I am especially interested in whether the view risks being qualified into unfalsifiability—if every successful account of logical knowledge is dismissed as not being real justification—and whether that insulation is a philosophical virtue or, for my purposes, a narrative dead end.
