As a journalist who writes about philosophy and cognitive science, I recently observed an undergraduate exam on moral responsibility and found one student answer striking—mostly sharp, but with what appear to be subtle conceptual slips that a hurried grader might miss. The exam question was this: Explain Galen Strawson’s Basic Argument against moral responsibility, with particular attention to his claim that being truly responsible for what one does requires being causa sui, and then apply that framework to the following hypothetical case. Imagine an agent who undergoes a complete, transparent neural character-transplant: she knows exactly which traits, values, and dispositions were implanted, and she understands precisely why they were selected, yet she played no part in choosing them. Does this scenario weaken Strawson’s conclusion, strengthen it, or leave it untouched?

Here is the student’s answer, reproduced in full. The student begins correctly, stating that Strawson’s argument rests on the premise that true moral responsibility for an action requires true moral responsibility for the mental states that generate it. The student then traces the familiar regress: if one is responsible for a mental state, one must be responsible for the prior conditions or actions that produced it, and so on backward, until one reaches an origin that is not itself caused by anything external—an agent who is causa sui. The student correctly notes that no human being can meet this standard, since every trait can be traced to heredity, environment, or prior choices for which the agent was not ultimately responsible. Turning to the neural-transplant case, the student argues that because the agent possesses full transparency about her implanted character, she can now endorse those traits at a second-order level, thereby taking ownership of them and blocking the regress. The student then claims that this result shows Strawson’s argument is really a variant of Peter Strawson’s reactive-attitudes framework, since both ultimately depend on whether a moral community can appropriately hold an agent to account, and that the transparent agent qualifies for such address precisely because her knowledge allows direct engagement. Finally, the student concludes that Strawson’s argument succeeds only against compatibilist positions that deny the importance of origination, but that it collapses under indeterminism, because if an agent is genuinely free to endorse her traits without being determined to do so, she can satisfy the causa sui requirement after all.
