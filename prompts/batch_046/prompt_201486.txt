Below are notes for an essay I am developing on the ontology of death under conditions of sustained mechanical life support. The conventional debate frames brain death, understood through the whole-brain criterion, and circulatory death, understood as the permanent cessation of cardiopulmonary function, as competing biological markers of a single event: the death of the human being. I want to challenge the premise that these are rival indicators of the same underlying fact. Once a patient is maintained on mechanical ventilation, extracorporeal membrane oxygenation, or automated perfusion, the organism is no longer a naturally bounded homeostatic system but a technologically coupled distributed network. In such cases, the loss of integrated brain function does not entail the collapse of organismal metabolism; rather, it marks the dissolution of personal identity and centralized neurological integration, while the body persists as what I call the suspended biological subject—an entity that retains tissue-level viability and homeostatic regulation only through artificial integration with external machinery.

On this view, brain death is not the death of the organism but the death of the person, whereas circulatory death represents the failure of the distributed system itself, including its technological extensions. The mistake in current bioethical and legal frameworks is to conflate these two distinct failures into a single juridical moment. If my distinction holds, then declaring a brain-dead ventilated patient dead for burial or organ procurement involves not a biological discovery but a normative decision to treat technologically mediated organismal persistence as lacking the moral status of a living human being.

I would welcome comments on the following points. First, is the distinction between personal death and distributed organismal persistence conceptually coherent, or does it illegitimately reify medical technology as part of the biological organism? Second, does the category of the suspended biological subject avoid collapsing into either vitalism, by attributing life to tissue alone, or mechanism, by reducing life to pump-and-ventilator function? Third, is there an unavoidable tension between my claim that brain-dead bodies are not dead organisms in the biological sense and the practical necessity of treating them as legally dead for purposes of resource allocation and transplantation? Finally, if this framework is defensible, what should it predict about cases of partial brain death or isolated brainstem preservation, where some integrative functions remain but consciousness is permanently absent?
