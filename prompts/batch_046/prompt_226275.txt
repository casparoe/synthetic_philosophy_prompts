Meta-philosophical debate about whether philosophy makes progress has cycled through familiar patterns for well over a century: proponents cite refined logical frameworks, clarified conceptual distinctions, and the abandonment of clearly false theses; opponents reply that philosophy lacks the cumulative agreement characteristic of science, that old problems resurface in new vocabulary, and that the field exhibits structural stasis rather than genuine advancement. My working hypothesis is that this is not a disagreement about the historical record—both sides can agree on what has been published—but about what progress must involve: convergence on settled truths versus irreversible restructuring of a problem-space, where even open questions are mapped with greater precision. Diagnose this impasse concretely. Is it a verbal dispute about the term 'progress,' a substantive disagreement about standards of intellectual achievement, or a case of two groups answering different questions—one about epistemic consensus, one about conceptual hygiene—under a single heading? Give a committed answer: take a definite position, support it by showing what each side is actually committed to when they cite examples such as the persistence of the mind-body problem or the development of modal semantics, and explain what, if anything, would count as progress in philosophy given your diagnosis. Would a formal result, a new distinction, or a reframing of the question actually move the debate, or is the disagreement bedrock and therefore permanent?
