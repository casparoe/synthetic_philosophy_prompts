You are a professional philosopher of social science with extensive experience teaching conceptual material to interdisciplinary audiences, and you think with particular care about dependency structures and misconception maps. Before presenting your plan, briefly brainstorm the logical order in which the ideas must unfold and the specific points where the audience is most likely to derail.

The question has become personally urgent. A colleague in healthcare administration—convinced that organizational culture explains repeated patient-safety failures—recently pressed me to say whether I believe culture can literally cause outcomes, or whether I am only dressing up correlations in authoritative language. I am now preparing to facilitate a single ninety-minute session for a small reading group of clinicians and managers: intelligent, practically minded, skeptical of “soft” explanations, and entirely without background in sociology or philosophy of science. I want them to understand the conceptual problem of explaining with values, using Weber’s Protestant Ethic thesis not as a historical claim to defend but as a precise, worked example of how cultural causation is meant to function and where it risks collapsing into tautology.

Do not explain Weber to me. Plan exactly how I should structure the explanation for them.

Lay out the explanatory arc in strict dependency order: which idea must be firmly in place before the audience can even grasp what is at stake in treating values as causal agents, and why reversing that order would cause the session to fail? Identify the one carrying example or comparative case—drawn from Weber’s contrast between the psychological orientation generated by Calvinist and Lutheran conceptions of worldly vocation—that should anchor the entire discussion. Specify precisely what that example must include (so the causal claim remains tangible and non-psychological) and what it must exclude (so it cannot be reduced to economic determinism or to individual personality). Then map the misconceptions: they will likely arrive believing that cultural explanation is merely post-hoc storytelling, that Weber argued religion created capitalism ex nihilo, and that saying values cause behavior is always tautological because values are observable only through the behavior they are said to explain. Design one check—whether a brief counterfactual prompt, a conceptual sorting task, or a targeted question—that I can deploy near the end to determine whether they have grasped the distinction between value-relevance in explanation and genuine causal explanation, rather than having merely memorized the historical narrative. Explicitly state what should be left out at this level—for instance, the full comparative material on Confucianism and Hinduism, the technical historiography of rationalization, or the theological fine points of double predestination—and justify why omitting each protects the core concept rather than falsifying it. Finally, identify the one misconception you judge will be most resistant in this particular audience, and describe how the sequence should absorb it without breaking the arc.
