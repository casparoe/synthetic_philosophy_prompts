In our philosophy of mathematics seminar we have been working through Benacerraf's epistemological challenge: if mathematical objects are causally inert abstract entities, then any plausible causal theory of knowledge seems to block platonists from explaining how we know anything about them. Reading outside the syllabus, I encountered evolutionary debunking arguments in ethics, which claim that evolutionary explanations of our moral beliefs undermine those beliefs' claim to track objective moral facts, since natural selection accounts for the beliefs without invoking the facts themselves. This led me to think that Benacerraf's dilemma might be structurally the same kind of problem: evolutionary and cognitive-developmental pressures explain why humans reliably form arithmetic and geometric beliefs, yet they do so without any causal interaction with abstract numbers or sets, so platonists face not merely an "access" gap but a full debunking challenge. If that framing is correct, then wouldn't a naturalized epistemology—one that allows knowledge through reliable cognitive processes even in the absence of causal contact with the objects believed in—dissolve Benacerraf's problem for platonists in exactly the way some philosophers think it dissolves evolutionary debunking elsewhere? Or is there a disanalogy I'm missing between worries about causal explanation undermining truth-tracking and Benacerraf's specific concern about the causal conditions on knowledge?
