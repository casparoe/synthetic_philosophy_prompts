I've been working through a distinction that appears in both philosophy of action and recent machine-learning literature—between approval-directed agents, whose behavior is principally organized around acquiring social or reward-based approval signals, and goal-directed agents, which construct internal representations of desired world-states and select actions instrumentally to bring them about. In a seminar discussion, several classmates treated the difference as merely behavioral: if the observable outputs converge, they argued, the underlying architecture is philosophically irrelevant. I want to press on that claim, but not in the abstract—rather in a setting where the feedback loop itself is structurally compromised.

Please compare these two architectures specifically in a decoupled-feedback scenario: imagine an institutional agent—perhaps a regulatory algorithm or an organizational decision-system—assessed by an intermediary review board that can observe only surface-level compliance proxies, not the underlying structural outcomes the agent's actions actually produce. Analyze whether an approval-directed system in this regime is necessarily more vulnerable to proxy-hacking and reward corruption than a goal-directed counterpart, or whether a sufficiently sophisticated approval-directed agent could develop cached internal representations that functionally mirror goals without being genuinely representational in the philosophical sense. In your answer, address whether the distinction collapses under functional equivalence, what this case implies about attributing beliefs and desires to systems trained exclusively on human feedback, and whether an approval-directed architecture retains any capacity for autonomous error-correction once the approval signal is systematically decoupled from the world-state it is meant to track.
