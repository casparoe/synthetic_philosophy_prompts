I was arguing with two people from my rationalist meetup about Aquinas on tyrannicide — we got onto it after someone mentioned De Regno in passing. (1) Maya said Aquinas is basically a proto-liberal about rebellion: he only allows killing a tyrant when there's a public authority involved and literally no other remedy, which sounds like a realy strict last-resort rule. (2) Jake said that's anachronistic; for Aquinas the real split is between a usurper (who was never a true ruler) and a legitimate ruler who abuses power. Killing a usurper isn't rebellion against authority at all, it's just removing an obstacle, so the last-resort framing doesn't even apply in the same way. (3) Maya pushed back that even for usurpers Aquinas still requires the act not cause more harm and that it be done by public authority, so there's still a high bar — not an open license. (4) Jake then introduced this framing he made up on the spot, calling it the delegation gap: if the people never actually delegated power to the tyrant, then resisting him isn't revoking delegated authority but exercising original sovereignty, which makes the moral calculus totally different from modern social-contract rebellion. Maya said that's just Locke with extra steps and Aquinas never talks about delegation that way. (5) Jake admitted he was stretching but said the point is Aquinas runs two separate tracks — usurpation vs. abuse — and modern readers keep smushing them together into one rebellion concept.

Can you go through their exchange move by move and rate each from clearly mistaken to clearly right?
