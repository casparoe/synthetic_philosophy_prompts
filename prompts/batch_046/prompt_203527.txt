I'm in a philosophy of law seminar and want a consistency check on my view of legal self-amendment before I finalize my essay. Here are my commitments: (1) A legal system can contain a rule that authorizes amendment of that very rule—reflexive amendment authority—without generating the classic paradox of constitutional self-reference, provided the rule operates at a different logical level than the provisions it governs. (2) If reflexive authority is legitimate, then democratic legitimacy must be evaluated recursively: the constituency that authorizes an amendment is itself constituted by rules that were previously valid, so authorization never bottoms out in an unconstituted people. (3) I think this recursion terminates in a non-amendable ground norm whose authority derives not from further legal rules but from a substantive normative principle—specifically, that the system must preserve the conditions for its own future self-amendment in order to be genuinely self-governing. (4) At the same time, I reject treating that ground norm as a mere presupposition or fiction, and I want to assign it moral content: it must protect participatory conditions, not just procedural coherence. Do these hang together? In particular, does (1) conflict with (3) if the ground norm is truly non-amendable, since that seems to block the reflexivity I claim is harmless? And does importing moral content into the ground norm in (4) collapse my view into natural law, undermining the legal-positivist framework implied by (1) and (2)? Please take a definite position on which commitment has to be revised or abandoned, with your reasoning, rather than laying out options and leaving me to choose. Also tell me what this package predicts for a case I haven't considered: a constitutional clause that tries to amend the ground norm itself by declaring it a revocable procedural default.
