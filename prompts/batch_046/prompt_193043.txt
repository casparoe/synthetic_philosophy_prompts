As I construct the moral architecture of my novel, I find myself repeatedly judging actions in which Buddhist categories of intention and karma collide with consequentialist pressure, and I want to know whether my judgments form a coherent pattern. Here are my verdicts.

First, when a bodhisattva deliberately takes rebirth in a suffering realm to guide one deluded being toward awakening, knowing she will endure centuries of pain, I regard the choice as ethically supreme: the purity of compassion seems to sanctify even radical self-harm.

Second, when a physician lies to a dying patient about his prognosis so that he dies without terror—thereby sparing months of anguish and allowing his family peace—I think the deception is permissible and perhaps meritorious, provided the physician’s mind is free of aversion, pride, or self-interest.

Third, when a monastery secretly diverts famine-relief donations to build an ornate stupa, reasoning that the monument will inspire thousands toward virtue for generations, I judge the act gravely wrong, even if the long-term reduction in suffering exceeds the immediate harm.

Fourth, when a meditator uses a powerful, non-canonical psychoactive method to dissolve ego-boundaries rapidly, then teaches others, but in doing so neglects her children and destabilizes her community, I think the pursuit of rapid awakening is ethically corrupted, regardless of how many people she eventually helps.

Fifth, when a government mandates daily mindfulness training for prisoners, producing measurable drops in violence and self-harm, but enforces participation through isolation and public shame, I regard the program as unethical despite the statistical good.

Sixth—here is the tension—when a soldier kills an enemy commander who is about to deploy a weapon that would kill ten thousand civilians, but the soldier feels intense hatred and satisfaction in the act, I think the outcome is good yet the act is karmically poisonous for the soldier; but if the same soldier had killed with perfect equanimity and compassion for the commander’s delusion, I would call the act ethically sound. Meanwhile, in the second case the physician’s mental purity seemed sufficient to justify deception, yet in the sixth case mental purity transforms the moral quality of killing rather than merely cleansing the agent’s inner state, and in the first case I accept extreme self-instrumentalization while in the third and fifth I reject the instrumentalization of others or institutions.

Is there one principle—perhaps centered on the purity of intention weighted against foreseeable harm, constrained by a prohibition on treating sentient beings or communal goods as mere means—that delivers all six verdicts? Or do the cases show that I am implicitly invoking incompatible frameworks? If you must overturn one of my judgments to preserve coherence, which should go, and what does the repaired principle predict for a case I have not listed: a teacher who fabricates a false miracle to prevent an imminent war, knowing the lie will eventually be exposed but will save thousands in the interim? Please also state honestly which of my original verdicts the principle cannot accommodate.
