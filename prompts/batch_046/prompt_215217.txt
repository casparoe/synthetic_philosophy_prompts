I am preparing questions for a podcast interview with a philosopher of biology who defends a position she calls constrained adaptationism. She accepts that the Gould-Lewontin spandrels critique correctly identifies many traits as architectural byproducts or exaptations rather than direct adaptations, but she maintains that adaptationist reasoning remains indispensable in evolutionary biology provided it is coupled with explicit null models of drift and developmental constraint. Draft eight to ten interview questions that do not ask her to restate this view. I want at least one question addressing each of the following four issues, with a follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to provide.

First, how she distinguishes a genuine spandrel from an exaptation that has been co-opted for a current function when the historical evolutionary pathway is poorly resolved; if she appeals to comparative phylogenetics, press her on cases where phylogenetic resolution is inadequate.

Second, whether requiring an explicit null model actually solves the just-so-story problem or merely relocates it to the selection of the null itself; if she argues that the requirement forces empirical testing, ask what criteria distinguish an adequate null from an ad hoc one when both drift and constraint are plausible.

Third, what she makes of modern evolutionary developmental biology evidence that developmental bias channels phenotypic variation before selection acts; if she claims this bias can be parameterized within an adaptive framework, ask whether that absorption collapses the distinction between adaptationism and pluralism.

Fourth, whether there are any evolutionary explanations she thinks are genuinely ruled out by spandrel considerations, or whether every adaptive narrative can be preserved by adding auxiliary hypotheses; if she concedes that no narrative is ever fully falsified, ask whether her position risks unfalsifiability.

In addition, include two or three further questions that probe equally specific terrain of your own design—for example, how she handles the problem of identifying the environment of evolutionary adaptedness for cognitive traits, or how her view relates to the charge of Panglossian optimism—each paired with a tailored follow-up. The tone should be formal and suitable for a philosophical audience; avoid generic biographical questions and do not ask for citations, reading lists, or bibliographies.
