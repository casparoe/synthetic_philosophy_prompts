In a recent office-hours discussion, two of my doctoral students—let us call them Mara and Jonas—became locked in a sustained disagreement over whether the situationist critique genuinely undermines virtue ethics, or whether virtue theorists can absorb it by reconceiving character traits as ecologically coupled capacities rather than as cross-situational behavioral regularities. Mara opened by observing that classic situationist experiments typically measure consistency across decontextualized, low-stakes laboratory settings, and therefore fail to engage the very conditions under which a virtue such as courage or temperance is meant to operate. Drawing on an analogy with ecological psychology, she argued that a virtue is best understood as a disposition that activates only when an environment affords both a significant moral demand and a valued end at risk—much as flight is a real capacity of a bird even if it never manifests in a vacuum. She proposed what she termed a threshold-activation model: possessing a virtue does not entail uniform behavior across all contexts, but rather reliable responsiveness once contextual stakes cross a certain threshold. Jonas countered that this reconception saves virtue ethics only by rendering it empirically empty. If the conditions for activation are specified post hoc—defined precisely by whether the virtue happens to manifest—then the theory becomes unfalsifiable, and any apparent counter-example can be dismissed by claiming the environment was not properly affording. He pressed further that if virtues are so environmentally gated, then moral education collapses into a form of ecological engineering—designing institutions and social structures—rather than cultivating stable individual character, which he took to strip virtue ethics of its normative core. Mara replied that this is not a collapse but an enrichment: educating moral agents necessarily includes structuring the environments that afford virtuous response, and that Jonas was implicitly relying on a frequentist model of trait measurement—counting behaviors across contexts—that virtue ethics was never obliged to accept. Jonas then raised a circularity objection: without an independent, non-circular criterion for what counts as the correct ecological conditions, Mara cannot distinguish a genuinely courageous agent who happens to be in a non-affording situation from a coward who is simply never tested. They asked me to referee.

Please adjudicate their exchange round by round. Assess the strength of Mara’s ecological-coupling and threshold-activation moves, the force of Jonas’s unfalsifiability and circularity objections, and Mara’s response regarding moral education and measurement models. Then reach and defend an overall verdict: does the situationist critique retain its damaging force once virtue is reconceived in this ecologically coupled, threshold-sensitive manner, or does the reconception succeed in defanging it? In your judgment, be explicit about who is right about which specific point, and identify what both interlocutors appear to overlook—whether an implicit assumption about the ontology of dispositions, a neglected empirical constraint, or a normative consequence neither has fully confronted. Do not end on a non-committal note; defend a clear position, even if it involves partitioning the dispute—conceding some ground to each side while ruling decisively on the central issue.
