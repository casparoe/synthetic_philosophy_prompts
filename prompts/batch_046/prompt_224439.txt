As a software engineer who works mostly with distributed systems and causal inference pipelines, I have been trying to understand why the debate over Max Weber's claim—that the Protestant ethic contributed causally to the rise of modern capitalism—has remained so intractable for more than a century. The correlation itself is largely uncontested: regions shaped by Reformation theology did develop distinctive economic behaviors, and historians on all sides have examined the same archives. Yet the disagreement persists with almost no convergence, which makes me suspect the stall is not empirical. My working theory is that the two sides are operating with incompatible notions of what a "cultural value" is permitted to do inside an explanation—whether it can occupy an independent node in a causal structure, or whether it must always be reducible to individual utility functions, institutional rules, or underlying material conditions. Critics often seem to treat values as epiphenomenal, like system logs that record state changes without driving execution, while Weberians treat them as configuration parameters that constrain which economic behaviors are even conceivable. Is this the right diagnosis, or is there a hidden empirical disagreement about counterfactual dependence—for instance, whether capitalist institutions would have emerged in comparable form without Reformation theology? I want a diagnosis of the disagreement's structure: is it verbal, centered on competing standards of explanation in social science; substantive but about which cases count as data rather than about facts; or genuinely empirical in disguise? I also want an honest assessment of what would count as progress given that diagnosis—whether a formal model of cultural transmission, a sharper distinction between selection effects and causal mechanisms, or a reconceptualization of the question itself—rather than a survey of interpretations or an adjudication of Weber's original thesis.
