In debates about constitutional self-amendment, critics often invoke the liar paradox: an article that authorizes its own revision by procedure P is likened to the sentence "this statement is false"—if it succeeds in amending itself to require procedure Q, its original authority is simultaneously affirmed and undermined, producing contradiction. The analogy is used to argue that legal self-reference is inherently incoherent and must therefore rest on an extra-legal, non-self-amending foundation.
