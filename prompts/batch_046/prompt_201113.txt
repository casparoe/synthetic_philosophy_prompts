I am preparing a seminar contribution for my ancient philosophy course on Plato’s treatment of intoxication in the Laws, particularly his claim that properly regulated symposia can serve as a civic examination of character by lowering rational guard-rails in a controlled setting. Rather than rehearsing standard commentary, I want to apply this framework to an unfamiliar conceptual problem: whether Plato’s distinction between beneficial and destructive drunkenness can be translated into a secular theory of institutional stress-testing for moral reliability—structured rituals that deliberately relax social inhibition to reveal ethical dispositions, but without involving alcohol at all. Please describe the most important implications of Plato’s view for this translation. Specifically: does his argument that true character is revealed only when inhibition is partially relaxed require a chemical agent, or can equivalent disclosure be achieved through designed social pressure; what does this imply about the epistemic priority of revealed behaviour over articulated belief in assessing moral character; and if such rituals were adopted by a democratic body, would Plato’s suspicion of collective passion undermine their legitimacy, or can democratic regulatory oversight supply the necessary framework he demands? Please keep the analysis grounded in Plato’s conceptual distinctions rather than contemporary empirical studies, and avoid bibliographies, citations, or lists of further reading.
