I have spent the past several months cataloguing the library of my late mentor, whose books are so densely annotated that the margins often form a parallel argument to the printed text. This work led me back to Douglas Hofstadter's dialogue "A Conversation with Einstein's Brain" in The Mind's I. A colleague recently summarized Hofstadter's position for me in the following way: if the complete functional pattern of a brain is instantiated in a book's lookup rules and response structures, then the book is not merely simulating Einstein but actually is Einstein, possessing the very same stream of consciousness; consciousness is nothing more than the causal organization of information, so substrate is entirely irrelevant, and any residual belief that paper cannot think is pure biological chauvinism. My colleague further suggested that this reasoning extends naturally to partial archives—such as my mentor's annotated volumes—implying that fragments of a person's consciousness could persist in sufficiently dense marginalia, and that once such a pattern is archived the original biological brain becomes theoretically dispensable.

I am unsure whether this summary is fair. Does Hofstadter truly commit to an identity claim of this strength, or does he treat the book as a functional simulation that behaves like Einstein without necessarily being him? To what extent does his argument require substrate-independence in an absolute sense, and to what extent does it depend on preserving the right kind of causal interaction with an environment—interactions that a static book might lack? Finally, does the move from a complete brain-pattern to partial annotations—like those in my mentor's library—follow from Hofstadter's reasoning, or does it depend on conflating a full functional architecture with a mere trace of it?
