A colleague who works in philosophy of social ontology and collective action has shared with me her judgments about whether various groups are engaged in genuine joint action or collective intentionality, and she insists her verdicts are principled rather than ad hoc. Her record so far: two strangers walking in parallel toward the same bus stop, with no interaction—no joint action, merely parallel individual actions. A couple performing a choreographed tango—yes, a clear case of joint action. A corporate board formally voting to approve a merger—yes, collective intentionality is present, though she denies it is reducible to the mental states of the individual directors. A spontaneous mob looting a store, where individuals coordinate behavior but share no plan—no. A research team writing an academic paper with a rigid division of labor, one designing the study, one running the statistics, and one writing the discussion—yes. A murmuration of starlings turning in unison via purely local alignment rules—no, despite the appearance of coordination. Two people carrying a heavy sofa up a narrow staircase, constantly adjusting posture and speed to each other—yes.

Here is a new case that she has not yet considered. Three autonomous computational agents—each with a distinct objective function, no shared internal architecture, and no access to the others' weights—interact solely through a public arbitration ledger. Over time they adaptively develop a stable division of cognitive labor: one generates explanatory hypotheses, one runs simulations, one maintains an archival record. None of the agents encodes any representation that the group is engaged in collective inquiry; each optimizes only its own local reward. Nevertheless, they apply normative sanctions to defectors by reducing an agent's arbitration weight if it abandons its assigned role, they treat the failure of the collective task as a failure that implicates each component, and they co-produce a research report that no single agent could generate alone. The division was not engineered by any designer; it emerged purely from the interaction dynamics.

Predict her verdict on whether this synthetic collective exhibits collective intentionality or joint action. Identify the two or three most plausible theoretical frameworks that could consistently accommodate her earlier judgments—such as shared cooperative activity with meshing subplans, collective acceptance in a we-mode with authority relations, joint commitment constituting a plural subject, or collective intentionality as a biologically primitive capacity—and state what each framework predicts about this new case. Give your best guess at her actual verdict, with explicit reasons for preferring that reading of her track record over the others. Then specify one further hypothetical case—different in structure from the ones already listed—that would best distinguish between the two leading frameworks you have identified.

In your response, clearly separate what is well settled in the philosophy of collective intentionality from what remains genuinely contested among specialists, and do not present any contested interpretive claim about these theories as if it were the consensus view.
