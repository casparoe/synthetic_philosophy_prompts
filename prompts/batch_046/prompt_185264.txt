In historiographical debates, presentism is frequently analogized to ethnocentrism in anthropology: just as an ethnographer who condemns local practices for failing to match her home norms imports an unwarranted universalism, a historian who faults past actors for lacking modern moral or political concepts is said to impose her own temporal standpoint. The analogy is used to license the conclusion that good history requires escaping one’s own standards, much as good fieldwork requires escaping one’s own culture. How far does it hold? Give me an explicit correspondence table—what plays the role of the observer, the home framework, the observed society, the normative judgment, and the possibility of “going native”—then identify where the mapping breaks: whether temporal distance involves an asymmetry that spatial or cultural distance does not, whether historians can genuinely adopt past conceptual schemes in the way an ethnographer can learn a language, and whether the analogy quietly treats moral progress as analogous to cultural difference. Does the anti-presentist conclusion—that historians should suspend their present standards—survive once these disanalogies are on the table, or was the normative work being done by the comparison itself? Finally, say what would change your mind: which consideration—about the inevitability of conceptual frameworks, about the moral obligations of judgment, or about the possibility of neutral description—would most weaken your confidence in the analogy’s strength, and where is your reasoning most likely to fail?
