I'm writing a feature on the recent resurgence of minimalism in the philosophy of language, and in my interviews a recurring comparison keeps surfacing that I can't quite evaluate on my own. Several philosophers describe the deflationist's treatment of the predicate "true" as structurally analogous to the role of the conversational particle "indeed": just as saying "Indeed, snow is white" adds no new descriptive content about snow but rather signals agreement with, or emphatic endorsement of, a claim already registered in discourse, asserting "It is true that snow is white" is said to contribute nothing metaphysically substantive beyond the bare claim that snow is white. From this parallel, they draw the stronger conclusion that debates about the "nature" or "essence" of truth are as conceptually misguided as debates about the "nature" of indeed-ness—there is, on this view, only a pragmatic or grammatical function to be catalogued, not a property to be analyzed or explained.

But I am unsure how far the comparison actually carries the weight being placed upon it, and I want an audit rather than a defense. If we lay out the correspondence table explicitly—what in the source domain of conversational affirmation plays the role of the proposition being affirmed, what plays the role of the speaker's mental state or evidential stance, what plays the role of the semantic content allegedly absent, and what corresponds to the deflationist's disquotational schema—where precisely does the mapping begin to fray? Does "indeed" genuinely lack descriptive content in the same way deflationists claim "true" does, or does it smuggle in expressive, evidential, or illocutionary commitments that "true" does not share? More specifically, if "indeed" operates primarily at the level of speaker attitude and discourse dynamics, while "true" operates at the level of propositional generalization and semantic ascent, does that disanalogy undermine the metaphysical conclusion, or is it merely an incidental divergence? I would like you to separate the fractures that genuinely threaten the deflationist's argument from those that can be repaired without cost, to say whether the conclusion that there is no deep nature of truth survives once the disanalogies are on the table, and to indicate whether a better analogy—perhaps drawn from syntax or logic rather than pragmatics—would serve the deflationist's purpose more honestly, or whether the breakdown suggests that deflationism requires an entirely different kind of support altogether.
