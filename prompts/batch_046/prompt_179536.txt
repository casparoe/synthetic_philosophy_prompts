Philosophers defending the Epicurean claim that death is not bad for the one who dies often lean on a symmetry: being dead is like the period before one's birth, since in both cases there is no experiencing subject present to suffer deprivation. A related but less commonly scrutinized analogy compares a finished human life to a completed novel: once the final page is turned, the characters inside the story do not suffer the absence of further chapters, because there is no internal "reader" within the book to experience the blank pages that follow; the narrative's value is fixed entirely in what was actually written. Some extend this to argue that death is therefore not like tearing pages from an unfinished manuscript but like shelving a completed volume—so cutting a life short is not a harm to its subject, but merely a closure.

Audit this analogy precisely. Give the correspondence table—what plays the role of the author, the narrative arc, the characters, the blank pages after the end, and the reader or audience outside the text. Then identify where the mapping breaks: for instance, whether real persons have future-oriented interests that fictional characters lack; whether a life's "completion" can be premature in ways a novel's ending cannot be; and whether the mere absence of a suffering subject is sufficient to defeat the deprivation account of harm. Separate the disanalogies that matter for the Epicurean conclusion from those that are merely decorative. Determine which features of the source domain—intentional design, fixed content, the lack of internal anticipation—are being quietly imported into the target, and whether those imports do argumentative work or just decorate the conclusion. Finally, say whether the analogy survives this audit well enough to support the claim that death is not bad, or whether it collapses into a different thesis about the shape or narrative coherence of a life. If it fails, suggest what a better comparison would be for assessing whether death harms the deceased.
