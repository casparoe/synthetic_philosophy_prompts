I was discussing Davidson's radical interpetation with some friends over coffee and I think I've pushed it into a weirder corner than usual. The standard story is that interpretation requires charity—mostly true beliefs, mostly rational actions—because you can't assign meanings without assuming the speaker makes sense. I want to run with that harder: if charity is a constraint on all interpretation, then there shouldn't be any genuinely literal/metaphorical distinction, since the 'literal' reading is just whatever assignment makes the speaker maximally rational and correct. That seems to dissolve ambiguity puzzles, but it also implies that what looks like deep irrationality is always just us mistranslating—there are no genuinely crazy agents, only badly interpreted ones.

What would count against this? Not just the familliar indeterminacy worries or the complaint that charity is 'too strong.' I want the defeaters that specifically target the collapse of literal/metaphor and the denial of real irrationality. Which ones would Davidson himself admit are damaging? Which are live—say, from anthropology or cognitive science—versus purely logical? Has the view been qualified so that any counterexample gets dismissed as 'bad interpretation,' and if so, is that insulation a problem? Lead with the bottom line in one sentence, then unpack. Keep the whole thing under 350 words, and don't worry about citing anyone—just argue it straight.
