I'm organizing a graduate philosophy seminar on Hannah Arendt's report on the Eichmann trial and want the students to produce a genuine adversarial-collaboration brief rather than a standard debate essay. The two camps are these. Camp One reads Arendt's thesis about the banality of evil as primarily structural and judgment-based: it holds that modern bureaucratic systems can render independent moral evaluation redundant, that Eichmann's distinctive failure was a specific cognitive-judgmental collapse—an inability to perform the kind of imaginative standpoint-taking Arendt later associated with Kantian representative thinking—and that his personal antisemitism was largely incidental to the machinery he operated, serving more as post-hoc rationalization than as primary motivation. Camp Two reads the trial record and the post-war Argentina interview recordings from 1957 as showing that Eichmann was not an unthinking automaton but an ideologically committed, careerist perpetrator who actively embraced his organizational role; it therefore takes Arendt's framing to risk dangerous exculpation by distributing moral responsibility onto an impersonal system rather than onto an agent with sustained, vicious commitments, and it treats bureaucratic language as defensive self-narrative rather than as explanatory cause. I want the document both sides could sign: first, a numbered list of genuinely shared claims—covering the historical record of Eichmann's organizational function within the RSHA, the bureaucratic logistics of the deportation apparatus, the methodological rule that psychological attributions must remain within the bounds of trial testimony and contemporaneous documents, and the shared normative premise that explaining evil is not equivalent to excusing it; second, a disagreements table in which each camp's core position is phrased in wording its own proponents accept and that the opposing side recognizes as the actual dispute rather than a strawman—for instance, Camp One stating that Eichmann's conduct is best explained by the substitution of bureaucratic rule-following for independent moral judgment, with ideological hatred playing a secondary rationalizing role, against Camp Two stating that his conduct is best explained by the integration of career ambition within a sustained ideological commitment to racial hierarchy, with bureaucratic language serving as retrospective cover; third, the cruxes—I suspect at least three: whether Arendt's notion of thoughtlessness denotes a failure of imaginative judgment specific to her Kantian framework or merely a rhetorical misdescription of active ideological conviction; whether the structural features of modern bureaucracy can cause ordinary agents to participate in genocide without deep personal hatred, or whether such participation always requires some form of positive affective investment; and whether the Argentina recordings reveal genuine ideological enthusiasm or only defensive, self-exculpating narrative construction, with particular attention to whether Eichmann displays what sociologists call role-distance (indicating awareness and choice) versus total role-immersion (indicating absorption into function); and fourth, for each crux, one concrete case or piece of evidence both sides agree is relevant, together with each side's explicit predictive commitment—for example, both might agree that a close analysis of Eichmann's pre-war writings on Jewish emigration policy is a relevant test, with Camp One predicting they display bureaucratic problem-solving without genocidal intent and Camp Two predicting they reveal an evolving, self-radicalizing worldview that makes the later thoughtlessness framing empirically inadequate. The brief should avoid requests for bibliographies, citation lists, or references to obscure scholarship that a model without web access could not verify; it should depend only on widely known historical and conceptual material, with the niche injection being the explicit use of the role-distance versus role-immersion distinction to sharpen the psychological crux. Please format the response as a formal adversarial-collaboration document: numbered agreed-claims section, disagreements table, cruxes with predictive commitments recorded in advance, and a brief concluding note identifying which disagreements are substantive and which are partly verbal.
