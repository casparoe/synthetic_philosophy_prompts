Consider the institutional pattern common to many contemporary democracies that combine direct-democratic instruments—binding referenda, citizens’ initiatives, or recall elections—with robust constitutional review and international human-rights obligations. In these systems, a referendum outcome is routinely described as an expression of “the will of the people” and treated as possessing exceptional democratic authority; yet the same outcome can be suspended, diluted, or overturned by constitutional courts or supranational tribunals when it conflicts with fundamental rights or procedural norms. At the same time, these polities generally reject attempts to approximate popular will through large-scale deliberative polling, statistical sampling of citizens, or algorithmically weighted deliberative assemblies, insisting that only the ritual of mass voting can genuinely disclose the people’s will. What philosophical conception of “the will of the people” does this arrangement presuppose? Does it commit its defenders to a metaphysical view of the people as a unitary agent with a single, discoverable intention, or can it be reconstructed as a purely procedural claim about the legitimacy of particular decision-rules? Is the practice coherent, given that it treats the will as sovereign in one institutional register but subordinate to rights in another, and that it privileges a noisy, framing-sensitive aggregation method over more representative or deliberative alternatives? Reconstruct the implicit theory, distinguishing what the practice strictly requires from what it merely accommodates, and say what institutional features—such as the status of constitutional courts, the design of direct-democratic mechanisms, or the role of deliberative mini-publics—would have to change if we replaced the underlying conception with one that treats popular will as an epistemic construct best approximated through structured deliberation rather than through majoritarian ritual.
