I was talking with my friend last week about those secular mindfulness apps that borrow Buddhist anatta ideas — you know, the ones that tell you to watch your anxiety like it's just weather passing through, not yours. She pointed out something that bugged her: the same app tracks your streaks, saves your journal entries under your email, and makes you agree to terms of service with your legal name. So the whole system is built on this weird split. The technique says there's no owner of the experience, but the platform needs a stable user account to monetize and to cover liability, and they even send you your personalized progress report every monday.

I'm trying to figure out what theory of personal identity that practice actually commit to. Is it implicitly running a conventional-vs-ultimate distinction, where the legal user is just a pragmatic fiction and the real metaphysics is all impermanent dharmas? Or does it smuggle in a narrative-self view for the backend database while preaching bundle-theory in the meditation audio? And if the app really thought there was no continuous subject, how could it justify holding you responsible for breaking a subscription contract or for reporting your mental-health data? I want someone to reconstruct the implicit philosophy here — does the institution need a weak, functional self just for administration, or is that functional self actually doing metaphysical work it claims to reject? Also, could a stricter anatta view even run a business, or does the very idea of a user agreement collapse without some minimal persisting agent?
