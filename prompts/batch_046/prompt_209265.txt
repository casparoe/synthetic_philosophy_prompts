Hey, I need help with an argument that seems to show the wrong-kind-of-reasons problem eats itself alive. Here goes with numbering:

(1) The wrong-kind-reasons problem says that for attitudes like belief, admiration, or love, practical benefits are the wrong kind of reason — they can't justify the attitude itself, only actions that cause it.
(2) But now apply this to the attitude of "caring about the wrong-kind-reasons problem." Call this meta-attitude M.
(3) If I care about M for practical reasons — say, because it makes me a sharper philosopher or avoids a contradiction — then by (1) these are wrong-kind reasons for M.
(4) So I can't genuinely care about the distinction unless I care about it for the right kind of reason.
(5) But the right kind of reason for M would be something like "the distinction genuinely matters for practicle rationality."
(6) Recognizing that it genuinely matters just is having M, or something that presupposes it.
(7) So the right-kind reason for M is circular.
(8) Therefore there are no available right-kind reasons for M — only wrong-kind practical ones.
(9) By (1), wrong-kind reasons can't justify M.
(10) So nobody can be genuinely justified in caring about the wrong-kind-reasons distinction.
(11) But philosophers clearly do care about it, and not just instrumentaly.
(12) So (1) must be false in general — wrong-kind reasons can sometimes justify an attitude after all.
(13) But if that's true, then there's nothing wrong with believing something because it makes you happy, or loving someone because they pay your rent. The distinction collapses everywhere.

My friend says the slip is between (6) and (7), but I don't see why that's not just denying circularity is bad. Where does this go wrong? And if I have to pick a premise to save, I'd rather keep (1) and ditch (11) — but then I'm saying philosophers are all deluded about their own motives, which seems like a big pill to swallow.
