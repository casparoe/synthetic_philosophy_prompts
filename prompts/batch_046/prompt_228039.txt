I've noticed a recurring pattern in collective insect behavior that I can't map cleanly onto the standard philosophy-of-biology vocabulary, and I want to know whether it is already carved out or needs a new term. Imagine an ant colony that keeps using a particular foraging path for weeks after every individual ant that originally established the path has died. The persistence is not stored as a mental representation in any surviving ant, nor is it a static physical trace left in the environment like a worn groove; instead, each new cohort reproduces the same sequence of interactions and chemical modifications, so the trail continuously reconstitutes itself from the dynamics of the present members alone. Philosophers talk about distributed minds and swarm cognition, but this seems different: it is not that many small minds are jointly knowing something, but that the group exhibits a kind of sustained behavioral pattern that has no representational content and no definite bearer—something closer to what one might call "process memory" or "interactional inertia." Is there an established concept in the literature on collective cognition or philosophy of biology for exactly this kind of non-representational, dynamically maintained group-level persistence? If there is, how is it distinguished from stigmergy, emergent computation, and collective memory? If there is not, can you propose a precise term, define its boundaries, and specify what it excludes—especially how it differs both from ordinary distributed knowledge and from mere physical trail maintenance? I am not asking for a bare label; I want to know whether the carving already exists, and if not, what the correct distinctions and edge cases would be.
