I'm writing about the philosophical foundations of scientific inference. There's a standard claim that Solomonoff induction formally vindicates Occam's razor: under the universal prior, hypotheses with lower Kolmogorov complexity receive greater weight. Yet Kolmogorov complexity is invariant only up to an additive constant that depends on the choice of universal Turing machine, which means the syntactic simplicity of a theory is partly an artifact of representational formalism. Take Hamiltonian and Lagrangian mechanics: they are empirically equivalent descriptions of dynamics, yet one can be encoded far more concisely than the other in a given notation. If Solomonoff induction is meant to guide actual theory choice, how should we treat cases where syntactic simplicity and empirical equivalence diverge? Does the framework genuinely vindicate Occam's razor, or does it collapse into a conventionalist preference for notation? And if we respond by designating one formalism as canonical, have we smuggled in a metaphysical commitment that pure induction was supposed to avoid? Explain the conceptual tension, not the formal derivation.
