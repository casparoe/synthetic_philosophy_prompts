The long-running disagreement among Machiavelli scholars over whether fortuna operates as an irreducible structural constraint that locks political history into path-dependent trajectories, or whether virtù represents a genuinely transformative agency capable of breaking that lock, shows no sign of resolution despite decades of commentary. Both sides invoke the same conceptual vocabulary—fortune likened to a violent river, to a force that must be subdued—yet they consistently talk past one another: one camp treats virtù as adaptive strategic calibration within fixed institutional rules, the other treats it as the capacity to rewrite the rules themselves. I suspect the stall is not a substantive clash about Machiavelli's intentions but a hidden disagreement about whether the disputants are asking an explanatory question—what determines outcomes in political history—or a normative question—what a ruler ought to do given contingency—disguised by a single term, virtù, that carries both descriptive and prescriptive weight. Is this diagnosis correct? Examine what each side actually asserts about the relationship between fortuna and virtù, rather than recycling biographical claims about Machiavelli's humanism or cynicism. Is the disagreement verbal, rooted in different standards for what counts as historical explanation, or is there a masked empirical claim about the frequency of institutional rupture? If it is substantive, specify what would advance it: a formal model distinguishing path-dependent equilibrium from agent-driven discontinuity, a conceptual distinction between rule-following and rule-changing excellence, or an argument that the framing itself anachronistically imports modern categories of determinism into a pre-modern political ontology. If no progress should be expected, explain precisely why the question is ill-posed rather than merely hard.
