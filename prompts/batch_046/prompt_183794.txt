I just finished a long, inconclusive argument with a friend about whether the EU principle of subsidiarity genuinely protects local self-rule or merely provides legal cover for centralization. Rather than ask you to referee our own circular exchange, I want you to adjudicate a sharper, reconstructed debate I witnessed recently between two colleagues, Marcus and Leah, both of whom work on comparative governance. They have asked me to settle it, and I honestly could not.

Please respond as an expert in comparative federalism and political philosophy. Before rendering any verdict, think carefully about the conceptual distinctions at stake—start by distinguishing procedural, normative, and legal-institutional readings of subsidiarity, then apply them to the exchange below. Your deliverable is a point-by-point adjudication: who is right about which specific claim, where both are partly correct, and what each is missing. Then say who is more right overall.

Here is the back-and-forth as I recorded it.

Leah began by defending a normative-proximity reading: subsidiarity requires that a decision be made at the lowest level capable of solving the problem without generating significant negative spillovers onto other jurisdictions. She applied this to cross-border river-basin management, arguing that if a watershed authority—spanning two member-state borders—can handle flood adaptation locally, then assigning that task directly to the European Commission violates subsidiarity, regardless of whether Brussels employs better hydrologists.

Marcus replied that this collapses subsidiarity into a romantic preference for small government. In EU Treaty law, he said, subsidiarity is not about minimizing government size but about matching authority to the scale of a problem’s externalities and about democratic accountability. If local bodies lack fiscal capacity, scientific coordination, or legal personality to bind cross-border actors, then Union-level action is necessary—and therefore permitted—even if it feels administratively remote.

Leah countered that Marcus was conflating subsidiarity with proportionality. The Treaty, she insisted, requires both tests separately: subsidiarity asks whether the Union should act at all, and proportionality asks whether the Union’s action exceeds what is necessary. Even if the Commission has superior expertise, if it could instead mandate that member states create and fund watershed authorities, then direct Commission management fails the subsidiarity test and likely the proportionality test as well.

Marcus introduced what he called the “nested-competence paradox.” The legal framework that would allow watershed authorities to exist—cross-border treaty powers, shared funding mechanisms, binding dispute-resolution protocols—simply does not exist in the national law of most member states. Creating that framework requires Union-level legislation. Therefore, insisting on “the lowest level” actually requires a prior centralizing step. He claimed this reveals subsidiarity as recursive: sometimes central authority is necessary precisely to construct the institutional conditions for local autonomy.

Leah dismissed this as a bootstrap argument used to justify every centralization. If one accepts it, she said, then any local failure becomes evidence that the center needs more power to “enable” localism. She proposed what she called “reverse subsidiarity”: rather than the Union running a program and delegating downward, the Union should be constitutionally required to delegate downward and forbidden from retaining direct administrative control over functions that consortia of member states could perform.

Marcus asked whether “reverse subsidiarity” is conceptually coherent. If the Union is forced to delegate, that coercion is itself an exercise of Union authority, which seems to presuppose the very central power it seeks to deny. He also noted that some member states—Belgium and Spain, with their highly asymmetric internal regional regimes—already possess substantial decentralized capacity, yet they still pool sovereignty at the EU level for environmental regulation. This suggests that even where local capacity exists, states sometimes prefer centralization for strategic or distributive reasons.

Leah conceded that strategic preference is real but argued that it proves her normative point: subsidiarity should function as a binding constitutional constraint on those preferences, not merely as a descriptive observation about them. Marcus ended by observing that making it binding requires a court or constitutional body to adjudicate “competence,” which reintroduces central authority through the back door.

Please adjudicate point by point: who is right about the Treaty distinction between subsidiarity and proportionality; about the nested-competence paradox; about whether “reverse subsidiarity” is coherent or self-defeating; and about the significance of asymmetric federalism (Belgium/Spain) for the argument. Say who is more right overall, and identify what both parties are missing—perhaps something about the gap between legal attribution and democratic legitimacy, or about how comparative federal practice should inform institutional design.

Also, please say explicitly what would change your mind: which argument or empirical finding about EU environmental governance, Treaty interpretation, or comparative federal practice would lead you to revise your verdict, and flag the part of your reasoning that is most likely to fail—for example, if your reading of the legal framework turns out to be outdated, or if the comparison with asymmetric federalism is inapt.

Finally, include a brief note on any liberties you take with the conceptual framing—particularly with the idea of “reverse subsidiarity” or the “nested-competence paradox”—since this is an adjudication rather than a creative work.
