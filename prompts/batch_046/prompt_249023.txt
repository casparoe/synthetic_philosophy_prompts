I'm working through Rousseau's Letter to d'Alembert on the theater for a seminar on republican aesthetics, and I keep running into what looks like a reductio of his position. Here is the argument I have reconstructed, which seems to prove that Rousseau must reject not only the Parisian stage but also novels, history, philosophy, and eventually his own writing.

(1) Rousseau's core objection is structural, not content-based: the theater institutionalizes a separation between appearance and being. The actor is paid to simulate passions he does not feel, and the spectator, seated in darkness and silence, enjoys the spectacle of virtue or vice without being required to practice either. The corruption lies in this passive consumption of represented moral qualities in place of lived enactment.

(2) If the danger is this structural split—representation substituting for practice—then it should apply to any medium that lets an audience experience moral or intellectual content representationally rather than practically. A novel like Julie, a historical chronicle, a painted allegory, or a philosophical treatise all allow the reader to grasp justice or compassion without exercising them.

(3) By parity of reasoning, Rousseau should therefore condemn all representational arts and even his own authorship, since The Social Contract lets its readers understand popular sovereignty without living it, and Emile lets tutors study education without actually educating.

(4) Yet Rousseau clearly does not do this. He writes novels and treatises, endorses public civic festivals that involve symbolic display and costume, and permits Emile's education to include carefully staged narrative experiences. He distinguishes these from the theater without, on this structural account, having a principled basis for the distinction.

(5) So either Rousseau is profoundly inconsistent, or premise (2) is false and the corruption of theater depends on something more specific than the mere separation of representation from practice.

(6) But if we drop (2) and say the corruption is specific to the professional actor's economic dependence on deception, we seem to miss Rousseau's broader anxiety about the spectator's passivity, which he applies even to audiences at public festivals if they become mere onlookers. If instead we keep (2) and accept the reductio, we must conclude that Rousseau's philosophy undermines its own conditions of expression.

Where exactly does this argument slip? Which premise should I reject—(1), (2), or the implicit inference in (4)—and what does rejecting it cost? I would prefer to keep (1) intact, since it captures what is distinctive about his critique of the actor-spectator relation, but I want to understand whether saving it forces me to deny that Rousseau can consistently write philosophy, or whether there is a hidden distinction—perhaps between active participatory representation and passive consumptive representation—that blocks the slide from theater to all representation without making the distinction ad hoc.
