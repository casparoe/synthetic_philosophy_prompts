As a novelist constructing a narrative in which the protagonist’s moral transformation is initially rendered as purely self-authored through solitary introspection, I wish to interrogate that framing through an Ubuntu-informed relational conception of personhood. Argue that the idea of a self-originating moral agent is not merely empirically uncommon but conceptually incoherent within this ontology, and that representing solitary moral growth in fiction therefore constitutes a specific ethical misrepresentation—one that mistakes relational embedding for a contingent accessory rather than a constitutive condition of agency. In your argument, distinguish carefully between diachronic relational continuity, through which personhood persists across time via sustained communal bonds, and synchronic relational embedding, through which present others immediately constitute the agent’s capacity for choice; then show why the narrative failure is specifically a failure to honor the synchronic dimension, and why this makes solitary-transformation plots not merely unrealistic but structurally dishonest about the nature of human dignity.
