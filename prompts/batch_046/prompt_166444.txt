You are an expert in philosophy of medicine and experiential pedagogy. Before proposing any rules or roles, briefly brainstorm three distinct mechanical structures—each with different incentive patterns—that could make a group feel a conceptual boundary dissolve in real time rather than merely discussing it.

Design a 90-minute seminar activity for 14 graduate students in bioethics. The domain is the therapy/enhancement distinction, but push well past the familiar medical cases (growth hormone, cosmetic surgery, ADHD medication) into a less-charted institutional frame: a professional licensing board that must classify cognitive augmentations as either “restoring baseline competence” or “exceeding it,” where “baseline” is defined not by a biological norm but by the current performance distribution of the already-licensed cohort. The students should come away seeing that the distinction is not merely vague at the edges but structurally unstable because the reference class shifts as adoption spreads—call this “baseline drift.”

The activity must be fully runnable without outside materials except printed role cards and a simple score sheet. Include:

- A division of roles (board members with voting power, applicant-advocates with hidden profiles, and observer-auditors who can challenge reasoning but not vote) with asymmetric information about what augmentation each applicant actually used.
- Precise rules of deliberation and a voting procedure that creates small, real stakes—e.g., reputation points accumulated across rounds that determine a final ranking, plus a collective penalty if the board’s classifications are inconsistent with the updated baseline.
- A timeline broken into three rounds. In Round 1 the cohort baseline is fixed. In Round 2, the baseline updates to include the augmentations approved in Round 1, so an identical applicant profile now receives a different classification. In Round 3, applicants may strategically choose whether to disclose prior augmentation use.
- A debrief (about 20 minutes) that maps each round onto a specific feature of the philosophical debate: restoration versus improvement, statistical versus normative baselines, individual versus collective definition of normal functioning. Predict exactly what will go wrong the first time you run this—likely that students will invent an ad hoc biological criterion to stop the drift—and explain how the facilitator should handle it without delivering a lecture.
- A frank admission of which feature of the philosophical debate the activity cannot reproduce: specifically, the normative policy argument that societies ought to preserve the therapy/enhancement distinction for regulatory purposes even if it is descriptively unstable. State how you will supply that missing piece in the closing 10 minutes without undermining what the students just experienced.
- No bibliographies, reading lists, or citations. Do not ask participants to look up specific philosophers or texts during the session. Frame everything conceptually but concretely enough that a student who has read only one introductory article on the distinction can participate fully. Keep rules precise enough that a facilitator can run them without improvisation.
