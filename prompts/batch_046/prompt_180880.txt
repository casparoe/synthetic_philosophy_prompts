Since retiring from field ecology, I have recently begun reading philosophy of biology, and a recurring pattern in my own reasoning now troubles me. During morning walks I observe crows caching seeds and, separately, sliding repeatedly down snow-covered roofs. For caching I can cite clear survival advantages; for sliding I can just as easily construct adaptive narratives—motor practice, social signaling, and so on—though these feel invented after the fact. I realize I am using the claim that a behavior "serves a biological function" in two incompatible ways: as a statement about what evolutionary history actually selected for, and as a mere description of whatever useful effect the behavior happens to produce now. Should I therefore treat my evolutionary interpretations as working hypotheses that require independent verification, or is it legitimate to honor them as a default heuristic for understanding what I see? More precisely, does allowing myself to generate adaptive explanations for nearly any observed action risk a particular form of irrational commitment—treating post-hoc stories as if they were predictive—without my noticing?
