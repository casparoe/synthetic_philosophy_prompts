Exam question:
Evolutionary psychology is often accused of constructing just-so stories: adaptive explanations for human traits that are compatible with observed data but not genuinely tested against non-adaptive alternatives. Defenders reply in two ways. First, they claim the field makes risky empirical predictions—e.g., about sex-differentiated patterns of jealousy—that could have turned out false. Second, they appeal to reverse-engineering cognitive mechanisms: identifying the design specifications a mental module would need to solve an ancestral problem, then checking whether observed cognition matches those specs.

Evaluate whether these two defenses jointly resolve the just-so-story worry. Specifically: does reverse-engineering supply independent evidential support, or does it merely restate the adaptive hypothesis in engineering language? And does successful prediction about present-day behavior address the specific concern—raised by Gould and Lewontin in their critique of adaptationism—about spandrels and exaptation, or does it leave that concern untouched?

Student answer:
The just-so-story objection says evolutionary psychologists make up adaptive explanations after seeing the data, so their theories cannot be falsified. The predictive defense is strong: if evolutionary psychology predicts sex differences in jealousy and experiments confirm them, then the theory was not made up after the fact. This shows the field does real science.

The reverse-engineering defense is also strong. By figuring out what design specs a cheater-detection module would need, we can check whether the mind actually has those specs. This gives us independent evidence because engineering principles do not depend on knowing evolutionary history; they depend only on functional logic. So together, prediction and reverse-engineering solve the problem.

However, Gould and Lewontin's spandrels argument says some traits are just by-products of other adaptations, not adaptations themselves. The predictive defense does not fully address this because predicting a current behavior does not prove it was directly selected for in the past; it could be a spandrel that was later co-opted. Still, reverse-engineering can tell spandrels from adaptations by looking at design complexity, so the two defenses together do cover the spandrels worry. Overall, evolutionary psychology escapes the criticism.
