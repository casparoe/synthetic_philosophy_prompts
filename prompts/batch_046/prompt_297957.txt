I'm currently enrolled in a philosophy of language seminar where we've been working through Putnam's Twin Earth thought experiment and its aftermath in the semantic-externalism literature. After class, my study group started discussing whether the argument can be extended to collective or distributed cognitive systems—specifically, to pairs of scientists who jointly maintain an instrument-mediated database rather than storing representations solely in individual brains. I tried to formalize the worry and now have an argument that seems to prove far too much: it suggests that if externalism is true, merging cognitive systems should generate contradictory mental contents, which strikes me as absurd, yet I can't tell whether the error is in extending externalism to groups, in my migration scenario, or in assuming that individual and collective contents must align.

Here is the argument. (1) Semantic externalism holds that the content of a mental or representational state is partly fixed by causal-environmental relations outside the individual brain. (2) If (1) is correct, then any functionally integrated cognitive system—whether an individual brain or a collectively maintained scientific database—should have its representational contents determined by the causal environment of the system as a whole. (3) Imagine Earth and Twin Earth each host a research collective that tracks liquid properties through shared digital instruments; on Earth the liquid is H2O, on Twin Earth it is XYZ, but the internal processing algorithms and the individual neurocognitive profiles of every researcher are identical across worlds. (4) By (1) and (2), the Earth collective's database represents water, while the Twin Earth collective's database represents twin-water, despite the internal identity of the constituent minds. (5) Now suppose all Twin Earth researchers physically migrate to Earth and integrate into the Earth collective without altering their individual brain states; the collective system now tracks H2O, yet each migrant's brain still bears the same causal-historical profile it had on Twin Earth. (6) Therefore, each migrant simultaneously participates in a collective system whose representations concern water, while possessing individual brain states that—according to (1) applied at the individual level—should still concern twin-water. (7) So extending externalism to shared artifacts yields a contradiction within a single cognitive economy: the same person is embedded in representations with incompatible contents. (8) Hence either externalism must be restricted to isolated brains, which undermines the extended-cognition motivation for (2), or it permits contradictions that seem intolerable.

I would prefer to preserve (2), since treating shared instruments and databases as genuinely cognitive seems philosophically attractive, but I want to know exactly which step fails—whether it is (3)'s assumption that internal identity is preserved across integration, (5)'s migration premise, or (6)'s inference that individual and collective contents must be jointly assigned. If the argument is actually valid, which premise is least costly to reject?
