I'm a journalist covering science and ethics, and I've been asked to deliver the opening remarks at a one-day workshop on institutional accountability. The audience is split evenly between moral philosophers who work on standing to blame and practitioners from regulatory agencies and advocacy groups who have no patience for jargon. The workshop asks whether large organizations—say, a carbon-intensive corporation criticizing an individual consumer's flight—can meaningfully blame individuals for actions that mirror the organization's own scaled-up conduct. I need a twelve-minute speech in which the distinction between lacking standing to blame and being hypocritical does real structural work: it should determine which examples I can invoke, which reform proposals sound coherent, and which moral demands come across as empty rather than merely unfair. Specifically, I want to argue that there is a stable middle position—an institution can forfeit its standing without being a hypocrite in the colloquial sense, and that this forfeit produces not outrage but a particular kind of moral silence that reformers need to recognize. The philosophical point should shape the arc and the exclusions of the speech, not decorate it; the practitioners should leave understanding why some accusations fail without feeling lectured, and the philosophers should see that I'm not collapsing standing into hypocrisy. No citations, no bibliographies, no reading lists—this is spoken conviction, not scholarship. Keep it polished, focused, and accessible without dumbing down the concept.
