I've been playing with this idea that in any tangled hierarchy — not just formal logic or art, but in how scientific communities model thier own methods — there's exactly one level that's genuinely inviolate. Not just hard to reach, but a source level: the point where the system generates its own boundary conditions. Escher's Drawing Hands captures it if you read it right — the paper surface isn't background, it's the fixed point where the drawing-hand and the drawn-hand are the same operation seen from two directions. The hands don't draw each other; they draw the condition that lets them exist at all. I'm treating this as structural, not metaphorical: any self-modeling system has exactly one such level, and formalizing it from within always produces the same collpase you see in Russell-type paradoxes. But I want to know what actually counts against this. Not generic "hierarchies can be flat" — which specific results, cases, or intuitions show that either there's no necessary source level, or there can be more than one, or it's reachable after all? Which of these would someone committed to this view concede as damaging, and which would they just absorb by saying the formalization was wrong? Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and say whether this position is actually exposed or if it's already insulated itself against everything.
