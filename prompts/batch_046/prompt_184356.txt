After three decades in low-temperature experimental physics, I have recently turned to philosophy of science and rational decision theory, and I find myself both intrigued and skeptical of anthropic reasoning in demographic forecasting. The so-called doomsday argument—where an observer updates her expectation of total future births based on her own temporal position within a reference class—strikes me as formally neat yet administratively catastrophic if taken seriously. I ask you to draft, in approximately 650 words, a deadpan internal memorandum from a fictional International Actuarial Ethics Board, applying Carter-style self-locating Bayesian reasoning to the governance of public pension systems. The document should adopt the precise, euphemistic register of institutional risk management: tables with invented metrics, clauses that translate existential probability into quarterly contribution adjustments, and recommendations that sound technically prudent while leading to absurd conclusions. Your satire should target the argument's genuine structural vulnerabilities—the indeterminacy of the reference class (all humans ever? all current observers? all enrolled pensioners?), the illegitimacy of treating one's own birth rank as unbiased sample data, and the leap from a statistical prior to a binding policy directive—by exaggerating each into bureaucratic protocol. To prevent the piece from being a generic restatement of familiar objections, embed a fictional technical framework—perhaps an anthropic depreciation coefficient linked to an observer's retirement-age quantile, or a reference-class liquidity index that weights future obligations by temporal sampling uncertainty—so that the argument's logic is pursued through invented but internally consistent machinery. The tone must remain entirely deadpan; the reader should recognize the parody only through the mounting absurdity of the recommendations. Do not include bibliographies, citations, or named references to specific philosophers in quotation marks; paraphrase conceptual influences without attribution. The result should be readable to someone unfamiliar with the doomsday literature, yet precise enough that a specialist sees the satire as fair.
