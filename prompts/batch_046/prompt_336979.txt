I'm preparing a single ninety-minute session for philosophy graduate students who have encountered Searle's Chinese Room in passing but have not studied the philosophy of cognitive science in depth. They can reliably distinguish syntactic manipulation from semantic interpretation, and they understand that large language models operate through statistical token prediction; nevertheless, they tend to assume that sufficiently high-dimensional correlation eventually becomes understanding if the architecture is complex enough. I want to teach the symbol grounding problem in a way that avoids letting the session collapse into a familiar rehearsal of the Chinese Room, and I need a concrete teaching plan rather than an explanation of the concept itself.

Please present the plan as a numbered list of exactly five items, each one or two sentences, with no introduction and no conclusion. First, specify the dependency order: what must be established about formal symbol systems and about intrinsic versus derived intentionality so that the grounding gap appears as a structural failure rather than an engineering limitation. Second, evaluate the carrying example I have in mind—contrasting a warehouse robot that sorts packages by correlating tactile vibration patterns with a language model that predicts the token "fragile" from distributional context—and tell me whether this pairing captures the correct asymmetry or whether it smuggles in hidden assumptions about embodiment that distort the problem. Third, map the misconceptions these students will predictably form, especially that distributed statistical tracking across enough parameters just is semantic grounding and that grounding requires consciousness rather than causal coupling, and state precisely where in the sequence each misconception should be intercepted. Fourth, propose a single diagnostic question that reveals whether a student genuinely grasps the distinction between tracking a feature causally and understanding what that feature means for an object. Fifth, identify one technical development—recent debates about whether transformer attention mechanisms might constitute a form of implicit grounding—that I should explicitly bracket as beyond the session's scope, and explain how admitting that omission honestly strengthens rather than weakens the core argument.
