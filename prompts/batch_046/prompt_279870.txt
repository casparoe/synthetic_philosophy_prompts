Working on a novel where an archivist learns humanity gets replaced by something better in two centuries, not killed, and I'm stuck on whether she should grieve. I got into Scheffler's afterlife conjecture — that extinction's bad mainly because it kills the "afterlife" that gives our projects meaning — and then I built this argument I can't exit.

(1) For Scheffler, human extinction isn't mainly about the deaths; it's that our present work and care only have full value if there's a future humanity to carry them forward. Call this project-closure dependancy.

(2) If value depends on closure by future agents, then it depends on those agents being able to recognize and continue the specific projects we're in now.

(3) So if we're replaced by a flourishing post-human intelligence that doesn't understand our literature or science — doesn't "close" our projects — the structural damage to present value is identical to extinction.

(4) Therefore, on Scheffler's view, a peaceful, painless transition should be just as catastrophic as doomsday.

(5) But that's wrong: we'd celebrate a transition to something wiser and happier, not mourn it like extinction.

(6) So maybe I reject (2) and say value only needs some future beings, not ones who understand us. But then extinction wouldn't be uniquely bad either — we'd just need any future life.

(7) Or I reject (4) by saying replacement isn't identical because nobody dies. But Scheffler says the death-part isn't the main issue.

I think there's an equivocation between "future agents who continue human culture" and "future agents period," but I can't pin the slide. Which step do I actually reject, and what's the price of giving it up?
