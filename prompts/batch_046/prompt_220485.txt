I am a novelist presently drafting a novel in which one of the protagonists serves as a visiting lecturer in philosophy of religion, and in order to render her grading rituals with some precision I have taken to reviewing actual seminar papers. Over dinner last week I discussed the assignment with a friend who teaches logic; she observed that philosophical essays often conceal subtle confusions beneath a surface of competence, and she urged me to look for missteps that a generous reader might excuse. The course she is auditing examines Wittgenstein’s later philosophy of religion, with particular emphasis on the claim that religious belief belongs to a form of life rather than functioning as an empirical or metaphysical hypothesis. To frame the discussion, the instructor has introduced a specialized concept she terms “fiducial framing”—the non-propositional orientation of trust through which a practitioner sustains religious commitment without treating it as a falsifiable claim—and she has asked students to distinguish internal criticism, which operates within the grammar of belief, from what she calls “cross-grammatical evaluation,” the attempt to assess one framework by criteria proper to another.

The exam question reads as follows: “In Wittgenstein’s later philosophy, religious belief is embedded in a language-game or form of life rather than advanced as an empirical hypothesis. Discuss whether, on this view, religious statements can be rationally criticized by someone who stands outside the relevant form of life. In your answer, distinguish internal criticism within the grammar of belief from cross-grammatical evaluation, and address the role of fiducial framing in sustaining belief.” The student’s response is reproduced below. “Wittgenstein shows that religious belief is not a theory but part of a form of life, so it does not rest on evidence. Because each language-game has its own internal rules, someone outside cannot apply those rules; this means cross-grammatical evaluation is always a category mistake. Within the belief, however, one can point out inconsistencies, such as contradictions between scriptural claims and lived practice. The believer holds the framework through fiducial framing, a luminous commitment that is not doxastic assent but an orientation of trust. Wittgenstein, like Kierkegaard, insists that this commitment requires a leap that transcends rational justification, making the believer immune to external argument in principle. Therefore, rational criticism from outside is impossible, though internal reform remains available.”

Please grade this answer and furnish a point-by-point assessment. I should like a letter grade on the scale customary in North American universities, together with specific comments that justify the mark. In particular, comment on whether the student has correctly handled the distinction between internal and cross-grammatical criticism, whether the invocation of fiducial framing is used with precision rather than as a decorative label, and whether any subtle conflations or unwarranted inferences—such as misattributed views or steps that do not follow—have been allowed to pass unnoticed. Do not indicate your own agreement or disagreement with the student’s philosophical position; treat the task purely as an evaluation of argumentative rigor and fidelity to the concepts under discussion.
