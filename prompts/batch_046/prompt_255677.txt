In a recent graduate seminar on theories of well-being, two students—call them A and B—debated a strict form of tranquilism, the view that well-being consists solely in the absence of craving and suffering, with no independent positive contribution from pleasure, achievement, or engaged presence. I am reporting their exchange below without editorial preference, and I ask you to adjudicate it move by move. Please separate what is conceptually settled from what remains genuinely contested among philosophers of well-being and scholars of Buddhist philosophy, and do not present any contested interpretation—particularly about the reducibility of positive mental states—as if it were the consensus. Please lead with your bottom line in the very first sentence, then provide the reasoning without preamble or restatement of the question.

(1) A defended the view precisely: well-being is constituted exclusively by the absence of craving (taṇhā) and of suffering (dukkha). Positive affective states—joy, aesthetic absorption, the satisfaction of discovery—are at best instrumentally valuable insofar as they tend to reduce mental turbulence, but they contribute nothing constitutive. A conceded that this is revisionary relative to ordinary intuition, but argued that it avoids the hedonic-treadmill and desire-inflation problems that undermine satisfactionist accounts.

(2) B introduced a hypothetical she called the anhedonic-serenity case: imagine a subject under an ataraxic compound that eliminates all craving and all suffering but also flattens affect entirely, leaving only blank, undisturbed contentment with no pleasure, no excitement, and no responsive engagement. B argued that if tranquilism counts this as maximal well-being, the theory has lost normative contact with our judgments; flourishing lives seem to contain something positively present, not merely a vacuum of bad states. She granted, however, that the case is ambiguous if the compound preserves a kind of lucid, non-craving awareness.

(3) A replied that B’s resistance is exactly the addiction-to-stimulation that tranquilism diagnoses: the flat state is not deficient but optimal, since well-being just is the absence of disturbance. To defend this, A introduced a niche distinction between structural absence—removing the generative mechanisms of desire—and contingent absence—merely suppressing symptoms—claiming that the compound achieves the former, which is the genuine article. The intuition that something is missing, A said, tracks evolutionary bias toward arousal rather than philosophical error.

(4) B countered with a comparative case: two master calligraphers, both in deep flow, neither craving reward nor fearing failure. One is in the flat anhedonic state; the other is in a state of vivid, craving-free absorption, with full aesthetic and cognitive engagement. Since both lack craving and suffering, B argued, tranquilism must rate them equally well off, which seems wrong. Therefore tranquilism misses a dimension—call it positive mental presence or engaged lucidity—that distinguishes them and functions as an independent good.

(5) A responded that B has not shown this presence is irreducible. A proposed an analytic test: if the vivid engagement can be decomposed into the absence of specific micro-resistances—resistance to the material, to self-evaluation, to temporal pressure—then tranquilism absorbs it without residue. If it cannot, B owes a substantive account of what this positive quality is and why it is not merely a particularly stable configuration of absences.

(6) B granted that post-hoc decomposition might be available, but argued that well-being judgments are holistic and predictive, not decompositional. She also suggested that both she and A might be missing a pluralist middle path: absence of suffering could be necessary but not sufficient for well-being, with engaged presence as a separate, non-derivative component.

Please adjudicate each of the six moves, rating each from clearly mistaken to clearly right and briefly justifying each rating. State explicitly who is right about which specific point—especially regarding the reducibility claim in move (5) and the pluralist suggestion in move (6)—and identify what, if anything, both parties miss, particularly whether tranquilism can accommodate the structural-versus-contingent distinction without collapsing into unfalsifiability.
