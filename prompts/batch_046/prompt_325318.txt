I have been reading Tolstoy’s essay What Is Art? without any formal training in philosophy or art history, and I am trying to make sense of one particular section. There he describes art as a kind of infection: the artist experiences a feeling sincerely, then through lines, colors, or sounds transmits that feeling to the audience, so that the audience is infected with the same emotion. He contrasts this with counterfeit art, which merely produces pleasant effects without transmitting any real feeling. He also argues that true art must be universal—if only a select few can understand or feel it, then it is not art but something artificial.

What I cannot grasp is how this one-way infection model handles two situations that seem common to me. First, in live performances the audience’s visible reaction often changes the performer’s emotional state in real time, which then changes the audience again. If the feeling is being generated back and forth, there is no single original source to be sincere about. Does Tolstoy’s framework allow for this kind of circular transmission, or does it require that the artist alone be the origin?

Second, he demands universality, yet I can imagine two friends sharing a private joke that makes them both feel a sudden, sincere joy that outsiders do not share. By his rules, is this counterfeit art because it is not universal, or is it not art at all because it lacks a public work? I want to know whether these two cases—feedback loops and closed circles—are fatal to the infection metaphor, or whether they can be accommodated without abandoning Tolstoy’s distinction between real and counterfeit art.
