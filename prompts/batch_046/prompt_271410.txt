Next week I defend at our logic and epistemology seminar the view that Fitch's paradox of knowability collapses if we replace the monolithic epistemic operator K with a family of resource-indexed operators K_i, so that the derivation's aggregation step—moving from knowledge of each instance to knowledge of a universal claim—fails for contingent empirical propositions because no single agent i commands the distributed access required, while logical truths remain paradox-vulnerable. The audience comprises two modal logicians wedded to S5 framing, a philosopher of science who regards any epistemic idealization with suspicion, and graduate students steeped in verificationist literature. Give me the objections in the order they will likely arise: first, the modal logicians' charge that I have not dissolved the paradox but relocated it into inter-agent accessibility relations; second, the philosopher of science's demand for a principled boundary between empirical and logical domains that prevents my indexing from being ad hoc; and the objection I am hoping avoids attention altogether—namely, that even fragmented knowledge of individual negative existentials can be statistically or inductively aggregated to recover the universal result. For each, specify what I should concede (for instance, that S5 properties must be relaxed rather than jettisoned), what I must hold firm (that resource-boundedness reflects actual scientific cognition rather than an ad hoc patch), and my weakest structural vulnerability—the absence of a formal completeness proof for the multi-agent system—which I should prepare to defend rather than pray they overlook. Please phrase each objection in the idiom its speaker would actually use, not as a generic counterexample.
