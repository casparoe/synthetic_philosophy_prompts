In comparative-cognitive and philosophical work on analogical reasoning, the dispute over whether Hofstadter-style Copycat architectures exhibit genuinely fluid concepts—representational structures whose extensions shift adaptively with context—or only procedural slippage, in which the matching algorithm relaxes its own constraint parameters without altering any stored concept, has stalled despite broad agreement on the protocol outputs. Defenders of fluidity cite letter-string analogies in which an ‘a’ is treated as a ‘z’ in one framing and as an ‘a’ in another, arguing that this requires context-sensitive representational restructuring rather than associative retrieval. Opponents reply that every observed behavior is reproducible by tuning continuous variables governing bond strength and perceptual grouping, with no independent evidence that any symbolic representation has changed its extension; both sides accept the same formal descriptions of the architecture and the same task corpus, yet neither accepts the other’s criterion for distinguishing representational from procedural change. I suspect the impasse is partly verbal—hinging on whether “concept” requires explicit symbolic storage—but also driven by a hidden divergence in evidential standards, since one camp demands neurocognitive correlates of restructuring while the other demands only predictive success across analogy tasks.
