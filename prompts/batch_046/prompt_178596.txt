In a decision-theory reading group I attend, a colleague repeatedly invokes the line “Information can never hurt you,” usually citing Good’s theorem, to close debates about whether it is rational to seek costly, burdensome, or emotionally damaging evidence. Most recently he used it to argue that a physician should always order an additional diagnostic test before treating, regardless of the patient’s anxiety, the risk of false positives, or the possibility that knowing the result would change the physician’s preferences about treatment. I want to bring precision back to the conversation. Separate the distinct precise claims that slogan could be expressing: the purely mathematical claim about the non-negativity of expected value of information under correct Bayesian updating; the rational-agent claim that applies only when the decision problem, preferences, and action set are held fixed; the psychological claim that acquiring knowledge never causes regret, distress, or preference change; and the normative claim that one ought always to gather more data before acting, regardless of acquisition cost. For each reading, say whether it is a trivial bookkeeping result, a defensible but heavily qualified theorem, or false; identify which hidden assumptions—costless acquisition, fixed preferences, correct updating, absence of any “value of ignorance”—each version requires; and specify which reading the speaker was actually using to dismiss objections about cost and emotional burden. Lay the interpretations out as a menu with verdicts rather than selecting one. Separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who know the topic, and do not present a contested view as the consensus.
