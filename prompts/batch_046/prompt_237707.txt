Since inheriting my grandmother's fermentation archive, I have been trying to classify her reconstructed 1890s sourdough starter for heritage-craft certification. The assessor treats it as a copycat because the biological lineage was broken in 1947, yet the genome was reconstructed from archival DNA and the culture is metabolically identical to the original. This has made me anxious about conceptual slippage between restoration, reconstruction, and copycat practice in microbial heritage.

Consider this argument:

1. A copycat product is defined as one that replicates all properties functionally relevant to identifying an original, without sharing its continuous causal history.
2. The reconstructed starter replicates every microbially and metabolically relevant property of the 1890 original, but its continuous biological lineage was interrupted in 1947.
3. Therefore, the reconstructed starter is a copycat of the original, not the original itself.
4. If lacking continuous causal history makes an item a copycat whenever its properties are restored, then a de-extinct mammoth, a painting restored with reconstructed pigments, or a human body maintained through cell replacement are all copycats.
5. We do not treat de-extinction, art restoration, or biological maintenance as copycat practices; we treat them as genuine restorations or continuations.
6. Hence (3) is false: the starter is genuinely restored, not counterfeit.
7. But if reconstructed causal history can count as continuous, then chemical analysis and replication of branded goods also count as restoration, collapsing the legal distinction between counterfeit and authentic.
8. Therefore, either all restoration is fraud, or the copycat/restoration distinction is useless.

Which premise or step ought I to reject? I should like to preserve both (5) — that restoration is not fraud — and the legal protection against chemical counterfeit goods, so I am reluctant to abandon either (1) or the reasoning in (7). Where exactly does the argument slip, and what do the standard positions on conceptual fluidity in microdomains say about the cost of repairing it?
