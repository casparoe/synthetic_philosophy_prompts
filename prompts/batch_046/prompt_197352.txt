As a retired physicist who spent three decades working on statistical mechanics and thermodynamics, I have recently begun a systematic study of philosophy of science, and I find myself caught in a destructive dilemma about the analysis of causation that I have not seen addressed in exactly this form. After working through standard presentations of interventionist invariance and counterfactual possible-world semantics, I constructed the following argument, which seems to show that both leading accounts are viciously circular and that we are forced into a causal primitivism incompatible with the Humean reduction of laws to regularities that underwrote my scientific career.

(1) Any adequate philosophical analysis of causation must be non-circular: its analysans must not presuppose the very causal relations it aims to explain. (2) The interventionist account defines causation in terms of invariant relationships that persist under interventions, where an intervention is defined as an exogenous change to a variable that breaks its connections to other causal parents. (3) To determine which connections are the causal parents that must be broken, however, one already needs a complete causal map of the system; hence the definition of intervention implicitly relies on prior causal knowledge. (4) Therefore the interventionist analysis is circular and fails as a reduction. (5) The counterfactual account avoids circularity by analyzing causation as counterfactual dependence evaluated across worlds ordered by an overall similarity relation. (6) But in deterministic physical systems of the kind I studied—where small perturbations to initial conditions produce globally divergent trajectories—there is no non-causal way to rank worlds by similarity; we treat a locally perturbed world as closer precisely because we hold its causal antecedents fixed and allow only the targeted variable to vary. (7) Thus the similarity metric required by the counterfactual account smuggles in causal structure rather than reducing it. (8) Since both dominant analyses presuppose causation, either causation is eliminable or it is metaphysically primitive. (9) Eliminativism is incompatible with the explanatory practices of the physical sciences, which genuinely distinguish causal mechanisms from accidental correlations. (10) Hence causation must be primitive. (11) But if causation is primitive, then causal laws cannot be reduced to Humean constant conjunctions, and the ontological framework I had assumed—where macroscopic causation emerges from statistical regularities without irreducible causal powers—collapses.

This conclusion feels far more metaphysically committed than I am prepared to be after a lifetime of explaining phenomena through regularity and symmetry. I would strongly prefer to preserve premise (1), since circular analyses seem worthless, and premise (9), since I do not wish to abandon scientific explanation. If I must weaken something, I would rather damage (3) or (6) than surrender the Humean outlook entirely, but I cannot see whether the flaw is an equivocation between epistemic and metaphysical circularity, an illegitimate global application of deterministic divergence, or a hidden assumption that similarity metrics must be causally innocent. Please diagnose precisely which step in the chain fails, explain why that step appeared plausible to someone with my background, and then specify what each major position—the interventionist, the counterfactualist, and the Humean—would be forced to concede if that step is indeed rejected. I am not asking for bibliographies or reading lists, only for the logical anatomy of the argument and the price of each repair.
