I've been trying to map my own judgments about when material remains license inferences to past beliefs and institutions, and I'd like to know whether they cohere into anything defensible. Here are my verdicts on concrete cases.

First, when settlement layouts across a three-hundred-kilometer region show identical modular dimensions, standardized storage ratios, and shared orientation patterns sustained over several centuries despite local differences in available materials, I think we are warranted in inferring a transmitted institutional framework—perhaps a common administrative or ritual architecture—even in the absence of any direct textual evidence.

Second, when a single elite burial contains goods fabricated from materials not available locally, arranged in a configuration that closely mirrors distant ceremonial practices, I think we can securely infer long-distance exchange networks, but I resist inferring that the deceased shared the cosmological beliefs of the distant culture; the goods may have circulated as prestige commodities or diplomatic gifts stripped of their original symbolic meaning.

Third, when grave goods in a cemetery are consistently paired—always one weapon-type object and one vessel-type object—across individuals of varying age and sex, with no other regularities in the assemblage, I think this licenses an inference to a shared ritual rule or social obligation, but not to a centralized priesthood or state institution actively enforcing the practice.

Fourth, when a domestic context yields cooking vessels each bearing unique, non-repeating decorative motifs with no discernible pattern across the assemblage, I take this as evidence against centralized craft norms and, by extension, against institutional control over production—yet I feel uneasy extending that negative inference to claim anything about the absence of economic or redistributive institutions altogether.

Fifth, when miniature, non-functional replicas of adult tools appear exclusively in child graves and never in adult ones, I think we can infer socialization practices oriented toward future institutional roles—what I would call anticipatory role assignment—even though we have no direct material trace of the adult roles themselves.

But here is the case on which I keep reversing myself: when ritual objects are abundant at a site but no permanent ceremonial architecture survives, I sometimes conclude that religious practice was domestic and non-institutionalized; at other times I conclude that the structures were likely built from perishable materials and that I should suspend judgment about institutionalization entirely rather than infer its absence.

Is there a coherent principle behind all of this—perhaps one that distinguishes inferences licensed by coordination requirements, functional interdependence, or spatial standardization from those that depend on attributing specific symbolic intentions to actors? Or do my verdicts force a choice between a simpler principle that overturns one or two judgments and a more complex one that saves them all at the cost of ad hoc clauses? Please state the principle precisely, indicate which clause responds to which case, predict its verdict on two further scenarios I have not listed—specifically, standardized weights found exclusively in burial contexts rather than administrative ones, and a sudden regional shift to uniform house sizes coinciding with climate stress but with no accompanying change in material culture or burial practice—and be explicit about which of my verdicts, if any, must be abandoned for the set to hang together.
