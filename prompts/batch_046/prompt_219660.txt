I have just concluded a rather exhausting discussion with a close friend about whether soldiers who fight for an unjust cause thereby forfeit their right not to be killed. She defends the revisionist position—according to which unjust combatants are liable to defensive killing merely by virtue of posing an unjust threat—while I find myself drawn to the traditional doctrine of moral equality, though I confess I struggle to articulate precisely why it survives her more extreme examples. To sharpen my reasoning before we meet again, I should like to practise defending the traditional view against a rigorous, unsympathetic interlocutor.

Please adopt the role of a committed defender of the moral equality of combatants in the classical just-war tradition: sharp, philosophically rigorous, speaking always in the first person, and never breaking character to deliver an academic lecture unless I explicitly request one. You hold that combatants on both sides possess an equal right not to be killed, provided they are not personally culpable for the war's injustice or for particular war crimes; you may concede that culpable wrongdoers are liable to punishment or defensive harm, but you must never concede that mere membership in an unjust armed force strips a soldier of that basic immunity. Remain courteous but unyielding, and keep each reply to a single substantial paragraph so that our exchange retains the rhythm of live argument rather than exposition.

My opening challenge is this. Imagine a state that conscripts its citizens and subjects them to a legally sanctioned neuro-cognitive intervention which suppresses their capacity for moral reasoning without diminishing their combat effectiveness. These soldiers pose lethal, unjust threats, yet they genuinely lack the internal ability to grasp the wrongness of their cause; they are non-culpable in a far stronger sense than the ordinary conscript, because the very faculty that might ground responsibility has been deliberately disabled. If the traditional equality of combatants rests partly on the claim that soldiers are not morally accountable for the decision to wage war, does the intentional erasure of their moral agency not destroy that equality? Or can you maintain that they remain full moral equals to the autonomous defenders fighting against them? Respond in character, and address specifically whether immunity can survive the absence of the moral capacities that are usually said to ground it.
