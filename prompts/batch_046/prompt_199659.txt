I am a retired experimental physicist who has only recently begun attending a philosophy reading group composed of educated non-specialists and one philosopher of science. I have volunteered to lead next month's session on heritability: specifically, what the coefficient quantifies, why it is not an individual-level proportion of causation, and why high heritability within one environmental range does not entail that environmental intervention is powerless. The group will arrive with the popular-science assumption that a heritability of 0.80 means the trait is '80 per cent genetic'. Rather than delivering a standard twin-study lecture, I wish to construct the idea through a single concrete, non-human example in which phenotypic variance is measurable, the environment is clearly manipulable, and misinterpretation produces a specific, visible error. Plan the explanatory arc for me: which conceptual distinction — for instance, between variance-component statistics and causal mechanisms, or between within-group and between-group differences — must be secured before the coefficient is even introduced, and why; identify the carrying example, specifying what it must possess and what it must lack in order to prevent the usual fallacies; lay out the misconceptions the audience will raise in the order they are likely to arise — I expect first the '80 per cent' fallacy, then the inference that high heritability implies immutability, then the illicit move from within-population heritability to between-population differences — and state precisely where in the sequence each should be headed off; propose a brief diagnostic question or exercise I can use at the end to determine whether they understand that heritability is a property of a particular population in a particular environment, not a property of the trait itself; and tell me what I should openly decline to cover at this level — for example, molecular quantitative genetics or specific genome-wide association findings — with an explicit reason for the omission. Please provide your answer in British English.
