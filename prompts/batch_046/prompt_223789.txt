Two members of a philosophy seminar I attend were debating Erwin Schrödinger’s Vedanta-influenced claim that consciousness is fundamentally one—that the apparent multiplicity of minds is an illusion produced by language and spatial separation rather than a metaphysical fact. I want a point-by-point adjudication, and I want you to take a definite position rather than laying out options and leaving the verdict to me. Here is the exchange as it unfolded.

(1) The first speaker argued that this is solipsism with extra steps: if there is literally only one subject of experience, then every other person is just a figure in my private mental field, and Schrödinger’s claim that plural minds are a fiction of language does not solve the problem but merely renames it.

(2) The second speaker replied that solipsism misdiagnoses the view. She said Schrödinger is not privileging my mind over others, but denying that there is any metaphysically robust boundary between them—comparable to denying that there are separate oceans where there is only one body of water with local waves. She added that quantum mechanics supports this, since the wavefunction describes a single system, and partitioning it into distinct observers is an epistemic convenience rather than an ontological division.

(3) The first speaker responded that if the division is purely epistemic, then ethics should treat all suffering as undifferentiated harm to the same underlying subject. Yet Schrödinger continues to write and act as if individual moral agency and responsibility are real, which means he smuggles pluralism back in whenever it suits him, making his view ethically incoherent.

(4) The second speaker countered that Vedanta distinguishes practical differentiation, called vyavahāra, from ultimate identity, called paramārtha: treating people separately in ordinary life is compatible with knowing they are one consciousness at the deepest level, just as chemistry treats water molecules separately despite knowing they share an underlying structure.

(5) The first speaker then attacked the evidential basis: Schrödinger appeals to the difficulty of localizing consciousness in specific brains as evidence for unity, but this is an argument from ignorance. The absence of a clear neural boundary does not prove there is no boundary; meanwhile, the phenomenological reality of separate, non-overlapping streams of experience remains unexplained by the ocean analogy.

(6) The second speaker conceded that ignorance cuts both ways: pluralism also assumes discrete subjects without providing a mechanism that could generate genuinely separate consciousnesses from continuous physical processes. She insisted that the burden is symmetric, and that the unity view at least explains why empathy and identification are possible across apparent boundaries.

Please adjudicate each of the six moves. Rate each as clearly mistaken, mostly right, partly right but incomplete, or clearly right, with brief justification. Then give a committed verdict: is Schrödinger’s position coherent, or does it collapse into solipsism, ethical incoherence, or an unsupported argument from ignorance? Identify what both speakers miss—for instance, whether there is a third interpretation of the wavefunction-observer relation that undermines both analogies, or whether the practical-ultimate distinction actually resolves the ethical tension or merely postpones it. Do not list options and ask me to choose; take a definite stand and defend it. If your answer relies on contested interpretive choices—such as whether Vedanta’s two-level ontology genuinely licenses ordinary moral discourse—flag that explicitly and say which reading you are adopting and why.
