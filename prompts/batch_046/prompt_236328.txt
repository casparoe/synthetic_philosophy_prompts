I am a retired experimental biologist who, over the past several years, has turned to philosophy of mind with the same procedural discipline I once applied to experimental design: I want a reusable framework rather than an ad hoc opinion. I now sit on an institutional review board that evaluates research proposals—meeting monthly, with forty minutes allotted per case—concerning potential conscious subjects, including mammals, cephalopods, brain organoids, and synthetic neural architectures. The board must decide whether a system possesses phenomenal consciousness, access consciousness, both, or neither, and that classification determines whether invasive procedures are ethically permissible. Most board members lack philosophical training, so the framework must be operable by scientists.

Please turn the global workspace theory distinction between access consciousness—information globally available for reasoning, report, and behavioral control—and phenomenal consciousness—subjective experience—into a practical decision procedure. Incorporate the following synthesized constraint, which I will call the residual recurrence criterion: phenomenal consciousness requires not only global broadcasting but the persistence of local recurrent processing loops that remain functionally coupled to, yet not fully dissolved into, the global workspace. The procedure should take the form of an ordered sequence of checkpoints: first, evidence of global integration and reportability; second, evidence of sustained local recurrence that survives decoupling stress; third, classification of the interaction between these two conditions; and fourth, an explicit handoff to ethical judgment where science runs out. At each checkpoint, specify what observation counts as sufficient, what counts as failure, and what counts as indeterminate. Identify the exact points where the procedure yields a definitive negative (e.g., access without phenomenal residue, or neither), where it yields a definitive positive (both present), and where it must stop and defer to values rather than facts.

Apply the procedure to three cases: (a) a macaque performing a reportable visual discrimination task; (b) a synthetic neural architecture engineered for full global workspace integration but with local recurrent loops architecturally suppressed; and (c) an octopus solving a complex extraction task with distributed arm-level processing but no detectable central broadcast mechanism. In case (c), I am inclined—based on my scientific background—to deny phenomenal consciousness without central broadcasting; include this as the case where the procedure conflicts with my prior intuition, and say whether the conflict reveals a flaw in the procedure or in my intuition. Then state explicitly what the procedure cannot settle, such as whether phenomenal consciousness without access carries the same ethical weight as access without phenomenal consciousness, and flag the conditions—such as ambiguous decoupling results in hybrid systems—under which the board would risk an inconsistent or dangerous conclusion if it treated the procedure as complete.

Please lead with your bottom-line classification rules for the three cases in the first sentence, then provide the reasoning and the procedure itself, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
