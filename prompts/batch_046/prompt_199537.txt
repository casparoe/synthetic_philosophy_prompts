Assume the role of a philosopher-poet with deep familiarity with Rousseau's moral psychology and the mechanics of verse form. Before composing, briefly brainstorm the three specific mechanisms Rousseau identifies in his Letter to d'Alembert on the Theater by which theatrical spectacle corrupts civic virtue: the audience's sympathy with simulated passions, the substitution of theatrical appearance for moral reality, and the dissolution of collective self-possession into passive spectatorship. Then write a 14-line poem in iambic pentameter — structured as a sonnet with a volta at line 9 — spoken in the voice of a Genevan burgher in 1758 who has just read Rousseau's condemnation of the theater and now watches a traveling troupe perform in the square. Lines 1-8 should build the Rousseauian critique through the speaker's observations of the crowd. At the volta (line 9), the speaker should suddenly recall that Rousseau himself composed the opera Le devin du village, performed before the royal court in 1752. The rhyme scheme should be ABAB CDCD EFEF through line 12, but the final couplet (lines 13-14) should deliberately fail to rhyme with each other or with the preceding quatrain, symbolizing the irreconcilable tension between Rousseau's moral theory and his artistic practice. Do not resolve the contradiction; let the formal rupture do the conceptual work. The poem should be accurate enough that a Rousseau scholar recognizes the argument, and the broken rhyme should feel conceptually necessary rather than arbitrary. Think carefully about how the meter and the rhyme scheme mirror the speaker's shifting state of conviction.
