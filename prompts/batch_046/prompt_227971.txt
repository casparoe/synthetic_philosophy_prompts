Write a dialogue between two philosophers—Alina and Marcus—who are both fluent in decision theory but disagree sharply about how to resolve a hybrid thought experiment that stitches together the Platonia dilemma with the correlation structure of a Luring Lottery. The scenario they discuss, which they call the Damascus Wager, works as follows: an agent learns that an infallible predictor will deposit one million dollars in her account if and only if she boards a train to Damascus tomorrow, but the predictor's continued accuracy is funded by a superrational correlation mechanism. Specifically, whenever an agent in her exact epistemic situation chooses to travel, a causally isolated counterpart in a distant branch—who shares her reasoning procedure—chooses to stay, forfeiting an equivalent sum that finances the payout; if both choose identically, the correlation breaks and no one is paid. Alina, arguing from causal decision theory, contends that the agent should board the train because her local choice cannot retroactively affect the predictor's record, nor can it causally influence her distant counterpart, so avoiding Damascus only sacrifices a guaranteed payout without altering any outcome. Marcus, defending an evidentialist approach that accepts superrational coordination, replies that the agent's decision is evidence about what her counterpart will do: by reasoning her way to travel, she makes it more likely that they both travel, destroying the correlation that sustains the reward, which is structurally analogous to one-boxing in Newcomb's problem while ignoring the mirror-image dependency that makes one-boxing rational.

Have them engage in direct back-and-forth rather than talking past each other. Alina should press Marcus on whether his view collapses into fatalism in the original Platonia case, where there is no counterpart mechanism and the predictor's accuracy seems entirely backward-looking; Marcus should reply by distinguishing correlation structures that depend on shared reasoning from those that depend on direct causal influence, and he should concede that his view requires a specific symmetry condition that may not hold in all predictive puzzles. Alina, in turn, should concede that if the correlation mechanism is genuinely part of the causal story—perhaps through a common prior rather than a causal chain—then her causal analysis needs refinement, but she should insist that without such a mechanism, evidential reasoning still overweights spurious correlations. Both characters should sound equally sharp, each should grant the strongest point made by the other, and the dialogue should remain grounded in the mechanics of the Damascus Wager rather than drifting into generic references to decision-theoretic literature.
