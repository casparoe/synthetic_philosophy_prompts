I have been revisiting Good's theorem on the value of information in preparation for a graduate seminar I am leading on rational choice and epistemic norms, and I find that the standard pedagogical treatment—demonstrating that cost-free information cannot reduce expected utility for a coherent Bayesian updater—leaves a persistent conceptual residue that my students keep circling back to. The residue concerns structurally analogous cases in strategic interaction and in medical ethics under self-binding constraints, where an agent not only benefits from remaining ignorant but can rationally engineer her environment to prevent the acquisition of knowledge that Good's framework treats as unconditionally valuable.

I want a conceptually precise, non-technical explanation of exactly how the theorem's premises foreclose these apparent counter-cases, focusing not on the arithmetic of conditional expectation but on the architecture of the decision problem itself. In particular, please articulate the distinction between informational value in Good's formal sense—where both the state-space partition and the preference ordering are held invariant across the informational update—and what might be termed epistemic burden, in which the very integration of new evidence transforms either the agent's preference structure or the strategic landscape in which she is embedded. I am especially interested in whether the conflict is best diagnosed as a hidden failure of the independence conditions that govern state-space partitioning, or rather as a normative misstep in treating dynamic consistency as an unconditional rational virtue rather than as a contingent commitment that can be rationally suspended when an agent anticipates preference change in her future self.

Please render this conceptual anatomy with sufficient philosophical precision that I can use it to show my students why the familiar reply—that the agent retains the option to ignore the information—begins to look question-begging once we allow for endogenous preference transformation or for decision problems involving temporal misalignment between present and future selves. I do not need formal derivations, bibliographic references, or citations of particular authors; what I need is the gut-level intuition, together with an honest assessment of where the standard defense of the theorem is strongest and where it is most vulnerable to the charge that it presupposes the very stability of preferences that is at issue.
