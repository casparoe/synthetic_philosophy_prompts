Here is an argument I built for a metaphysics workshop and now find suspicious, because if it works it dissolves not just modality but every long-term commitment I have. (1) On the counterpart-theoretic analysis of modality, no individual is strictly identical to anything in another possible world; cross-world relations are relations of qualitative similarity among world-bound entities, not relations of identity. (2) By direct analogy, persistence through time should be analyzed the same way: the person who will complete this project tomorrow is not strictly identical to me now, but is only my best temporal counterpart, related by psychological and bodily continuity. (3) Moral obligations are dyadic relations that require strict identity of subject: the bearer of the burden and the recipient of the benefit must be one and the same individual. Call this the identity-preservation condition on diachronic duty. (4) So if counterpart theory is correct about worlds, consistency demands it apply to times; consequently I have no obligations that genuinely bind me to my future temporal counterparts. (5) Promises, contracts, retirement savings, and long-term care all depend on precisely such diachronic obligations. (6) Therefore either counterpart theory is false, or all our practices of long-term commitment are systematically groundless. (7) The second disjunct is intolerable. (8) Hence counterpart theory must be rejected, and both transworld identity and genuine endurance through time must be restored. Where does this go wrong? I can feel the analogy between modal and temporal relations is being pressed too aggressively in step (2), and I worry that (3) smuggles in a controversial metaphysical requirement on morality, but I want a precise diagnosis: which premise or inference fails, and what does rejecting it cost? If I must choose, I would rather keep (1) and abandon (3), but I need to know whether that leaves me with any explanation of why promises still bind, or whether it collapses into a view on which only present-moment agents matter.
