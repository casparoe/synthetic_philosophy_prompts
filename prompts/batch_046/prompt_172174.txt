I am writing a feature for a science-and-ideas publication on whether artificial reasoning systems can ever safely trust themselves. A recurring concern in discussions of autonomous scientific agents—systems that continuously update their beliefs by treating outputs from an internal verification module as authoritative evidence—is the Löbian obstacle: in standard modal logics, a consistent formal system that accepts the reflection principle that provability implies truth can then prove any statement whatsoever. Several researchers worry that any agent engineered to take its own reasoning processes as generally reliable risks logical collapse or arbitrary belief fixation, but others argue the result is confined to deductive proof architectures and dissolves once we move to probabilistic belief revision.

Please give me your own credence—expressed as a precise number between 0 and 1—that the Löbian obstacle is genuinely insurmountable for a probabilistic, non-deductive autonomous agent (specifically, an agent that revises credences rather than accepting theorems, yet is required to treat its internal verification module’s judgments as epistemically authoritative). Decompose the judgment into three parts: first, the probability that Löb’s theorem or its core logical structure extends meaningfully to probabilistic belief-revision architectures rather than remaining confined to formal proof systems; second, the probability that no consistent, restricted reflection principle can be formulated that permits useful self-trust without licensing arbitrary conclusions; and third, the probability that current or near-future architectures in machine reasoning actually instantiate the kind of self-reference that makes the obstacle bite. For each term, provide the number, one line of justification, and indicate whether you combine them multiplicatively, as weighted averages, or otherwise; then derive your overall figure. Identify which sub-judgment is the weakest link and specify what theoretical result or empirical observation would most shift your number.

In framing your response, please clearly separate what is settled in the logical and philosophical literature on self-reference from what remains genuinely contested among specialists in epistemic logic and AI safety, and do not present a contested interpretation—for instance, that probabilistic agents are straightforwardly immune because they lack proof predicates—as though it were the consensus view. Write in American English.
