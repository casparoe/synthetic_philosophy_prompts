For an upper-level metaphysics seminar of twelve students with ninety minutes, design a structured in-class simulation that makes participants feel the force of the Ship of Theseus paradox as it applies specifically to institutional identity and collective persistence—without beginning with a lecture on the classical story. Split the group into three rotating roles—Curators, Constitutional Drafters, and Adjudicators—who must manage a state-chartered maritime museum over a simulated thirty-year timeline in which every physical component and staff member is gradually replaced, while a rival institution simultaneously reconstructs the original from all discarded parts. At three decision points (years ten, twenty, and thirty), Curators and Drafters must vote on whether the chartered institution retains legal standing, funding eligibility, and historical authority, but each group is bound to vote using only one assigned criterion—material continuity, functional role, or institutional recognition—rather than intuition; Adjudicators score the votes and lose collective reputation points if consensus fails by the final round. Provide minute-by-minute rules, materials (role cards, timeline sheets, a simple point ledger), and a payoff structure that creates real tension without money. Then include a debrief that maps each decision point onto a metaphysical position on persistence, explains what will predictably go wrong—especially the tendency of Adjudicators to collapse the three criteria into a single intuitive judgment—and states frankly which feature of the classical problem, namely spatiotemporal continuity in non-physical entities, the simulation cannot reproduce and must be supplied by the facilitator. Reveal the parallel to the ship only after the final vote.
