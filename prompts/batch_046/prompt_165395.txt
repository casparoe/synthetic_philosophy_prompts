I'm taking an upper-division philosophy of mind seminar on consciousness, and for my adversarial-collaboration assignment I need a joint statement between two camps within higher-order theory that have been talking past each other. Draft the document that an Actualist Higher-Order Thought camp and a Dispositional Availability camp could both sign after a good-faith collaboration.

The Actualist camp holds that a mental state is conscious if and only if one actually tokens a simultaneous, non-inferential higher-order representation of being in that state; the Dispositional camp holds that consciousness requires only that the state be disposed to become the object of such representation under conditions of attention and cognitive access, with no actual tokening required.

Please structure the document as follows.

First, a numbered Agreed Claims section: shared commitments about the architecture of first-order and higher-order states; about the explananda—why some states are conscious and others are not, the existence of rich unconscious first-order content, and the need to account for both reportability and phenomenal character; about what standard objections (the infinite-regress worry, the misrepresentation problem) do and do not establish; and about methodological shared ground, such as that a theory should not collapse consciousness into mere self-reference or into pure first-order richness.

Second, a Disagreements table with precise formulations of the residual dispute, each phrased so its own side accepts the wording. The core disagreement should concern whether actual tokening of a higher-order representation is necessary, or whether standing dispositional availability is sufficient. Within this, include the specific crux about whether "attentional binding" requires an actual synchronous integration event or can be satisfied by a rapid diachronic sequence of updating representations.

Third, the Cruxes: identify the sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one side. I take the central ones to be (a) whether cases of "unconscious commentary"—where subjects generate accurate verbal reports about mental states they do not phenomenally experience—demonstrate that actual higher-order representation can occur without consciousness, thereby supporting the Actualist claim that an additional factor is needed, or whether they show that actual representation is never sufficient and therefore dispositional conditions must do all the work; and (b) whether rapid diachronic self-monitoring can preserve the phenomenology of unified consciousness without a single synchronous higher-order thought.

Fourth, for each crux, specify a concrete conceptual case or argument both sides agree bears on it, with each side's explicit prediction recorded in advance. For example, both might agree that split-attention scenarios with rapid sequential presentation are relevant; state whether the Actualist predicts a fragmentation or loss of phenomenal unity when synchronous integration is disrupted, and whether the Dispositionalist predicts preserved phenomenology as long as the sequential updating disposition remains intact. Another agreed case might be the conceptual possibility of a system that possesses perfect dispositional access structures but never actually exercises them; record each side's judgment about whether such a system would be conscious.

Please do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations. Do not invoke named theories or texts I would need to verify; frame everything conceptually. If any part of the dispute turns out to be verbal—particularly about whether "representation" in this context requires an occurrent mental act or can be satisfied by a functional disposition—identify that part explicitly and explain why the wording difference does not resolve the substantive disagreement. Write in a formal, polished register suitable for submission in a philosophy course.
