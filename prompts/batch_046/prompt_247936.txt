Consider the following argument, which seems to show that any consequentialist who takes the Solomonoff prior as her epistemic framework is rationally required to paralyze herself.

(1) A consequentialist should choose actions that maximize expected welfare, where expectation is taken over all worlds that are actually realized, weighted by their objective probabilities.

(2) If one is embedded in a computable reality and takes the Solomonoff prior seriously, then the prior probability of a computable world is proportional to 2 raised to the negative Kolmogorov complexity of its minimal prefix-free description.

(3) Any decision procedure I might adopt can be specified by a program of some finite minimal length K.

(4) For each positive integer n, construct a computable world that runs exactly that decision procedure, but in which there are n disjoint regions containing suffering agents; specifying n and applying a fixed suffering-template requires only O(log n) additional bits beyond the base program.

(5) The prior weight of each such scaled world is therefore on the order of 2^{-K} multiplied by 2^{-O(log n)}, and if one sums these weights over all n the result behaves like 2^{-K} times the harmonic series, hence diverges.

(6) By contrast, the weight of the local world in which I act and produce some bounded welfare W is a single term of order 2^{-C}, where C is the complexity of this world.

(7) Consequently, for any decision procedure with K comparable to C, the total expected suffering contributed by its scaled instantiations is infinite, while its local benefit is finite.

(8) It follows that I should never execute any decision procedure that could be embedded in larger worlds; the only rational course is to minimize my own descriptive complexity, perhaps by ceasing computation altogether, to avoid being instantiated in suffering-extensions.

(9) But this conclusion is intolerable: it makes rational agency impossible and prescribes self-annihilation as the unique optimal policy.

(10) Therefore, at least one of the premises must be rejected.

I would prefer not to abandon (1) or (2). Which step is actually wrong, why did it seem defensible, and what does rejecting it cost—particularly regarding how an embedded agent should aggregate welfare across computable worlds?
