In a philosophy of medicine seminar, a fellow student has begun using the declaration that 'mental illness is a myth' as a conversation-ending move. Most recently, she invoked it to argue that when clinicians describe a psychiatric patient's refusal of antipsychotic medication as stemming from 'lack of insight,' they are not identifying a cognitive deficit but merely pathologizing a value disagreement—hence, she concluded, that we should never override treatment refusal on psychiatric-competence grounds. Rather than offering a flat contradiction, I want to bring a structured disambiguation to the next session. Could you lay out, as an explicit menu, the distinct precise theses that slogan could actually be expressing? Please separate the purely definitional or taxonomic readings (for instance, that mental conditions fail to satisfy some specific biological criterion for disease) from the metaphysical readings (that there are no mental dysfunctions independent of social norms) and from the normative-political readings (that psychiatric authority is inherently coercive and should be abolished). For each interpretation, indicate whether it is trivially true, substantively defensible, clearly false, or conceptually muddled, and—most importantly—specify which version or versions, if any, actually support her particular conclusion that clinical assessments of decision-making capacity in psychiatric contexts are necessarily illegitimate.
