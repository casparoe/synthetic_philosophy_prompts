As a journalist covering philosophy and the human sciences, I am drafting a feature on the conceptual architecture of Dostoevsky's Grand Inquisitor—not the familiar liberty-versus-security framing, but a granular taxonomy of how one might respond to the Inquisitor's claim that humanity will trade moral responsibility for comfort, miracle, and authoritative order. I need a decision tree (or branching taxonomy) that sorts readers by concrete commitments rather than by which philosophical label they prefer.

The root node should ask whether people genuinely possess a stable, pre-political preference for happiness over freedom, or whether that preference is largely an artifact of oppressive conditions—what might be called an adaptive preference formed under deprivation. From there, the tree should split on three further axes that genuinely divide the field. First, whether freedom is valuable primarily as negative liberty—the absence of external coercion—or as positive liberty requiring specific capacities for self-legislation and moral agency. Second, whether the psychological burden of freedom—existential anxiety, irreversible choice, the absence of definitive moral answers—is an irreducible feature of human existence or a contingent cognitive load that thoughtful social design could reduce without destroying autonomy. Third, whether the Inquisitor's triad—organized around miracle, mystery, and authoritative guidance—represents a coherent, if inferior, form of human flourishing, or whether it is structurally incapable of satisfying the conditions for genuine well-being.

Please phrase every node as a concrete claim that a reader could endorse without already knowing the taxonomy. Each leaf should carry the name of a position (or be marked unnamed if that combination is philosophically unoccupied) and one sentence identifying its most awkward commitment—for example, that it must treat widespread reports of satisfaction under authoritarian comfort as false consciousness, or that it must defend existential anxiety as a kind of public good. Note any pairs of leaves that the tree cannot genuinely separate, and briefly diagnose every unoccupied logical combination: is it incoherent, hidden behind an unattractive intermediate commitment, or merely unexplored?

The instrument should suit an educated general audience of a serious ideas magazine. Do not include a bibliography, reading list, or citation requests, and do not invoke obscure secondary theories or niche historical debates that a well-read non-specialist could not be expected to recognize. Keep the tree to no more than eight leaves, named or unnamed, and ensure that no branch depends on knowing the final label in advance.
