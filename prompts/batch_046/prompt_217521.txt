I've been re-reading The Lord of the Rings and kept thinking about Glaucon's ring of Gyges in Plato's Republic—how invisibility lets you do whatever you want without socal cost. The One Ring obviously echoes this, but there's a weird inversion: it makes you vanish from normal sight, yet you become blazing visible to Sauron's Eye. So I'm wondering if Tolkien is actually complicating Glaucon's thought experiment rather than just illustrating it. Glaucon frames justice as a social contract we keep because we're watched; remove the watchers and you'd steal with impunity. But with the Ring, the bearer isn't unobserved—he's observed exclusively by a single tyrannical gaze. Does that change the moral psychology? I mean, Bilbo and Frodo resist corruption partly because they still carry the Shire's community in their heads, while Gollum has lost all of that. Is Tolkien suggesting justice needs visibility within equals, and that monopolized visibility is actually worse than total invisibility? Or am I forcing the analogy?

I'd like a reading of the Ring's dynamics—especially Bilbo sparing Gollum, Frodo offering the Ring to Galadriel, and his final failure at Mount Doom—through this modified Gyges frame. Where does it illuminate their choices, and where does the text resist being reduced to moral-contract theory? I'm particularly interested in whether corruption works through loss of social recognition, like Gyges, or through something else—maybe the bearer becomes a kind of ghost in the phyiscal world, fragmenting the self across time. Don't treat the books as just philosophy; I'm prepping a book-club guide and want to know if this lens actually adds something Tolkien's Catholic-inflected view of mercy and free will doesn't already cover, or if it distorts the story.
