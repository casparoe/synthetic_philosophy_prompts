After being appointed to a municipal participatory council tasked with deciding whether long-term non-citizen residents should hold formal voting rights in local governance, I have found myself returning to Spinoza's argument that democracy is the absolute and most natural form of government, even as I confront his explicit exclusion of women, servants, and foreigners from democratic citizenship in his political treatises. Write an essay examining whether Spinoza's democratic theory, particularly his distinction between the power of the multitude and sovereign authority, can genuinely sustain an inclusive democratic politics or whether it necessarily collapses into a rationalist restriction of citizenship to those capable of adequate ideas. Weigh carefully the interpretation that his exclusions are historically contingent accommodations against the interpretation that they derive necessarily from his metaphysics of reason, affect, and the fear-driven imagination that governs the uninformed multitude. Reach and defend a specific conclusion: either that Spinoza's framework can be coherently reconstructed to justify modern expansive democracy, or that the tension between his universalist metaphysics and his particularist political prescriptions is structurally irreconcilable.
