I'm writing an essay for my philosophy elective on whether moral status comes in continuous degrees rather than in all-or-nothing categories. I'd like to read Kafka's The Metamorphosis through a gradient framework that assigns moral consideration based on specific capacities—nociception, emotional attachment, self-awareness, future-directed concern—so that an insect might sit low but not at zero, and a simple feedback-loop thermostat might sit near the bottom with minimal status. Gregor Samsa slides along this spectrum: he retains human memories and affection for his sister, yet loses language, upright posture, and human form. Does the story dramatize a coherent gradation—do his family members withdraw care as he loses particular capacities, or do they react to appearance regardless of retained sentience? I'd like you to examine specific scenes—the apple injury, his hidden care for the violinist sister, his retreat under the sofa—to see whether Kafka is testing a continuous moral-status view or exposing its failure, and whether Gregor's partial retention of human mental life means he retains partial moral standing even as he is treated as vermin. Where does the text resist this reading? Also, does the narrative ultimately show that humans impose a false binary (human versus insect) that ignores gradation, or does it suggest that form and capacity are too tightly coupled to separate? I'm not asking for citations—just a reading that takes both the novella and the gradient idea seriously, and tells me honestly where the philosophical lens breaks down.
