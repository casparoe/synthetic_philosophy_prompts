Write a short story, in American English, about a cellist named Mara who is undergoing a legally regulated, neuron-by-neuron replacement of her motor and auditory cortices with synthetic analogs. The analogs preserve her current technical skill, emotional range, and artistic preferences, but they convert her procedural memories of learning the instrument—her teacher’s corrections, the ache of calluses, the particular fear before her first recital—into an accurate but non-experiential data archive she can review without feeling. By the final stage of the eighteen-month procedure, Mara performs with flawless continuity, yet she reports that the music seems to play her rather than the reverse; the gestures arise fully formed, without the sense that she is authoring them. Her mentor argues that because her behavior, relationships, and aesthetic judgments remain unchanged, she is obviously the same person, and that first-person “ownership” was always an illusion. The story should dramatize, through one private rehearsal and one public concert, whether functional and affective continuity are sufficient for personal identity when the formative history that shaped one’s abilities has become an external record rather than a lived past. Do not include bibliographies, citations, or references to specific philosophical texts.
