A colleague circulated a summary from a recent interdisciplinary seminar on grand historical narratives, and I am unsure how much of it to trust. The summary runs as follows. Both Harari's Sapiens and Diamond's Guns, Germs, and Steel are said to advance a form of geographic determinism: continental axis, climate, and the availability of domesticable flora and fauna mechanically determined which societies developed dense populations, centralized states, and technological accumulation by the early modern period, with little room for contingent political choice. Graeber and Wengrow's The Dawn of Everything is then presented as a direct empirical refutation that undermines this determinism by documenting early agricultural communities that maintained egalitarian structures and seasonal mobility for millennia, thereby proving that hierarchy was never an ecological necessity but always a contingent political achievement. From this, the summary draws a stronger conclusion: if ecological and demographic factors do not deterministically drive social form, then the very project of grand narrative explanation collapses, leaving us with only local chronicle and no legitimate macro-historical causation. The summary also claims that Graeber and Wengrow reject any distinction between social scale and organizational complexity, treating small-scale foraging bands and large ceremonial centers as structurally identical except for headcount.

I suspect this characterization is partly fair but contains important distortions, and I want to use it in a graduate seminar on philosophy of history. Could you audit it for accuracy? Specifically: which parts correctly capture Harari's cognitive-fictional framework versus Diamond's biogeographic framework; whether Graeber and Wengrow's argument is primarily empirical, conceptual, or both; whether they indeed deny all macro-causal patterns or only deterministic linear ones; and whether the inference that the failure of determinism eliminates macro-historical explanation is valid. Also, please address whether the summary commits what I would call a scalar fallacy—treating organizational form as invariant across differences in population scale and settlement duration—and whether that error affects its reading of Graeber and Wengrow's examples. I am particularly interested in whether a defender of grand narrative could accept Graeber and Wengrow's empirical findings without abandoning explanatory macro-history altogether.
