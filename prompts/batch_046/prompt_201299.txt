I've been trying to make sense of Integrated Information Theory after encountering it in a podcast and some online forums. The basic idea, as I understand it, is that consciousness isn't just about processing information but about how that information is integrated within a system—measured by something called phi—so that a highly connected brain can be conscious even with simple content, while a vast but loosely coupled computer might not be conscious at all despite complex behavior. To explain this, people often reach for the analogy of a symphony orchestra: each musician is like a neuron, the sheet music is like the input, and the music itself—something no single player produces—is like conscious experience. The analogy is usually used to argue that mere complexity or intelligence isn't enough; you need that tight, mutual interplay, just as an orchestra needs players actually listening and responding to one another, not just playing the same notes in isolation.

But I want to audit this comparison carefully, because it feels like it's doing hidden work. Could you lay out the explicit correspondence—what plays the role of the individual musician, the conductor, the acoustic space, the audience, and the integrated sound? How does this variant map onto IIT's distinction between causal integration and mere functional correlation? Where exactly does the orchestra analogy break down—particularly around the claim that integration must be intrinsic to the system rather than observed from the outside? And finally, does the conclusion that a large but disconnected AI lacks consciousness actually survive once we strip away what the symphony image quietly imports about intentionality, shared awareness, and the value of harmony?
