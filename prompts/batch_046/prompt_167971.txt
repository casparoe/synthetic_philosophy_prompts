Argue that the conventional framework for democratic backsliding—centered on executive aggrandizement, electoral manipulation, and the capture of courts and media—systematically overlooks a parallel erosion mechanism that operates without any single actor's authoritarian intent. Introduce and defend the concept of "jurisdictional drift": the gradual, often legally unremarkable migration of politically consequential authority into technically independent administrative bodies, regulatory agencies, and private arbitration regimes that remain formally democratic in origin but become structurally insulated from electoral accountability and public deliberation. Show how this mechanism differs from classic backsliding in Hungary, Poland, and the United States—not by denying that those cases involve intentional capture, but by identifying in each a dimension of democratic decay that the backsliding literature misattributes or misses entirely because its diagnostic categories presuppose a visible antagonist. Conclude that unless democratic theory can track jurisdictional drift independently of partisan labels, the concept of backsliding risks becoming a rhetorical weapon applied selectively to right-wing governments rather than a neutral analytic tool.
