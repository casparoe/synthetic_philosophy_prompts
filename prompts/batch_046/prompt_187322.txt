I sit on our university's Behavioral Governance Board, which oversees institutional design affecting staff autonomy. Next Thursday we must vote on the administration's proposal—call it Project Lockstep—to restructure the faculty retirement-contribution system. At present, employees may reduce or suspend contributions with immediate effect. The proposal would impose a mandatory 90-day cooling-off period for any downward change, and it would offer an optional five-year binding contract that locks contribution rates above a floor; breaking the contract early triggers a graduated penalty that returns a portion of the foregone employer match. The administration argues that hyperbolic discounting produces predictable preference reversals: staff sincerely intend to save adequately but systematically undervalue distant retirement in favor of present liquidity, then suffer weakness of will—diachronic akrasia—when they reduce contributions impulsively. The binding mechanism, they claim, protects employees' own long-range judgments without removing choice, since enrollment is voluntary and the cooling-off period merely slows execution.

The opposition—led by the faculty senate's autonomy working group—contends that institutionalizing binding contracts mistakes a descriptive model of discounting for a normative license to override present preferences. They argue that weakness of will is not a clinical defect to be engineered away but a feature of practical reasoning under conditions of value pluralism: an employee who reduces contributions to cover an unexpected family expense is not failing rationality but revising priorities in light of new reasons. They further warn that hyperbolic discounting curves are population-level statistical patterns, not individuated diagnostics, so applying them as design defaults treats every staff member as a probable failure of self-control.

A further complication, raised in our preliminary session, is what the working group terms second-order volitional instability: even the desire to be bound may itself be hyperbolically discounted or context-dependent. A staff member might sign the lock-in contract during a period of high motivation, say after a financial-literacy workshop, and later come to view that earlier self as unrepresentative, yet the contract treats the earlier preference as authoritative. This raises a design problem that the administration's memo does not address—namely, whether any institutional mechanism can legitimately privilege one temporal slice of a person's preference ordering over another without smuggling in an external theory of the true self.

Please draft the board's written opinion for circulation before Thursday's vote. It should recommend a concrete course of action—approve Project Lockstep as proposed, approve with specific modifications, or reject—with a clear time frame for implementation or for further review if needed. The reasoning should treat the philosophical issues seriously: engage the distinction between diachronic akrasia and legitimate preference revision, address the second-order instability problem, and explain whether hyperbolic discounting provides normative grounds for institutional binding or merely predictive ones. Most importantly, include an honest, substantial treatment of the strongest argument on the losing side—whether that is the autonomy objection or the behavioral-economics defense—and explain what the board's recommendation costs that position.
