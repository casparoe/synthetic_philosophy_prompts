I'm working through supertask puzzles for my metaphysics seminar, and I want to see whether my reactions form a coherent view or whether I am just being inconsistent.

Consider four setups, all completed within two minutes via actions performed at times 1, 1/2, 1/4, ... minutes.

First, Thomson's lamp: toggled on and off at each step. At the two-minute mark, I think there is simply no fact of the matter about whether it is on; asking seems malformed, like asking for the parity of infinity.

Second, a continuous analog: a dial is rotated 180° at each stage, but continuously, so the total rotation converges to 360° and returns to its original orientation. Here I think there is a definite state at the limit: it is back where it started.

Third, a "reward lamp" with the same discrete toggling, but each "on" state deposits 1/2^n into a box. At two minutes, I think the box definitely contains exactly $2, even though I still deny there is a fact about whether the lamp itself is on.

Fourth, a "report lamp": an infinite chain of observers, each reporting the lamp's state to the next, with no observer situated at the limit point. I think the lamp may well have a state, but our inability to report it is purely epistemic; the gap is in our access, not in the world.

My pattern, then, is that discrete toggling undermines determinacy for the lamp-state itself, continuous motion preserves it, accumulated quantities can converge despite the absence of a final toggle, and missing observers generate ignorance rather than ontological gaps.

Is there one principle—about limits, supertasks, or the conditions for state-attribution—that delivers all four verdicts? If not, which of my judgments should I overturn, and why? And what should the resulting view say about a case I have not listed: a lamp whose toggle-rule at each stage references the running total in the reward box, so that at the limit the rule governing the lamp's state appeals to the very sum it produces?
