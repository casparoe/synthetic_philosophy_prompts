I come from a rationalist background where I tend to think about motivation in terms of reward signals and policy updates rather than phenomenological descriptions. Take the philosophical literature on pain asymbolia — subjects who report experiencing pain yet display no protective behavior, no negative affect, and sometimes even approach the stimulus — and the debate over whether this dissociates a sensory-discriminative component from an affective-motivational component that supplies the intrinsic badness. I want you to translate the three main philosophical positions on this — that pain is necessarily bad by its nature, that badness is an independent affective layer that can be missing, and that asymbolia reveals a breakdown in the link between representation and motivation — into the vocabulary of reinforcement learning: reward prediction errors, state-value estimates, action-selection weights, and the distinction between a sensory feature extractor and a reward model. For each translation, indicate what carries over without distortion, what must be forced or redefined to fit (for example, whether a negative reward signal that produces no policy change can still count as badness), and what disappears entirely, especially anything that depends on intrinsic phenomenal quality or on first-person authority about suffering. Then say whether the untranslatable residue indicates that the RL framework is missing something essential about affective experience, or whether the philosophical debate was relying on distinctions that look substantive only because they are articulated in a vocabulary that pretends to describe mechanisms but actually describes phenomenology. Present this as continuous prose only — no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables — as though writing a single letter.
