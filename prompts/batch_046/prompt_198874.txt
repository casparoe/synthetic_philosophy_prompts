I have been trying to work out a unified interpretation of Einstein’s metaphysical commitments, particularly the relationship between his well-known Spinozistic “cosmic religion” and his persistent rejection of quantum-mechanical indeterminacy. The view I am drawn to is that these are not merely parallel attitudes but expressions of a single, rigorous determinism: not just that physical processes are law-governed, but that mental and moral phenomena are equally embedded in a universal causal order, leaving no ontological space for libertarian free will. On this reading, Einstein’s occasional ethical admiration for figures who appear to exercise radical voluntary choice—say, in contexts of nonviolent resistance—must be interpreted either as purely phenomenological description or as an implicit compatibilism he never explicitly theorized. The position therefore risks being exposed if there is clear evidence that Einstein genuinely affirmed a non-physical or contra-causal capacity for agency, or if his determinism turns out to be restricted to physics alone rather than extending, in something like a methodological monism, to the structure of reason and moral action itself.

What would count against this unified deterministic interpretation? I want an honest assessment of its exposure, not a catalog of standard objections. Specifically: which remarks in his essays and correspondence—beyond the familiar references to Spinoza—would defenders of the view have to concede as genuinely problematic? Are there distinctions in his writing between causal determinism and mere statistical regularity that allow him to escape the hard determinist label? Could his rejection of quantum randomness be read as an epistemic preference for hidden variables rather than as a metaphysical commitment to universal law? And if his ethical language genuinely presupposes a form of agency incompatible with strict determinism, is that a fatal blow or merely an awkward tension that can be resolved by reinterpreting “freedom” as rational self-determination within a fixed causal chain? I am also interested in whether the view has been qualified into unfalsifiability—if any apparently libertarian remark can be dismissed as metaphor—and whether that insulation is a virtue or a vice for a metaphysical interpretation of this kind. Please rank the potential defeaters from decisive to merely inconvenient, and indicate which ones a committed defender of the unified reading would themselves acknowledge as counting against them.
