I've been trying to work out whether the standard critique of Whig history—writing the past as an inevitable march toward the present—really distinguishes itself from ordinary causal explanation, or whether it collapses the two together in a way that makes all historical reasoning guilty. Here is the argument that has me stuck, which I constructed for a seminar and now find suspicious because its conclusion seems to dissolve the boundary between narrative teleology and causal analysis altogether.

(1) Whig history is defective precisely because it treats earlier events as meaningful only as steps in a progressive trajectory that culminates in the present. (2) Any causal explanation of a later event E cites prior conditions C that made E occur or made it more probable. (3) To cite C as explaining E is therefore to represent C as oriented toward E—to treat C as belonging to a directional chain that terminates in E. (4) Consequently, every causal explanation in historiography implicitly structures the past as a trajectory leading to some present outcome, replicating the directional logic of Whig narrative under the guise of neutral analysis. (5) If that directional logic is what makes Whig history illegitimate, then causal explanation is equally illegitimate. (6) Yet we plainly can and should offer causal explanations—say, linking the fiscal crisis of the 1780s to the convening of the Estates-General—without endorsing any progressive story. (7) Therefore, the initial objection to Whig history must be mistaken, and writing the past as progress toward the present is unavoidable once we admit causal reasoning.

I want to preserve both (1) and (6): I think Whig history is genuinely wrong, but I also think causal explanation survives. So where does the slip happen—between (2) and (3), or elsewhere? In particular, is there a defensible distinction between causal "orientation" (a retrospective explanatory relation) and narrative "progression" (a normative, teleological arc), or does accepting that distinction commit me to some implausible metaphysics of causation—say, that causes have no intrinsic directionality except the one we impose retrospectively? Give me a committed answer: pick the exact premise or inference you think fails, explain why it looked plausible, and tell me what I pay if I reject it while holding onto (1) and (6). Don't just map the options neutrally; take a stand on whether the "directionality problem" I've sketched is real or a pseudo-problem generated by an equivocation, and be explicit about whether saving causal history forces me into a radically non-directional view of the past.
