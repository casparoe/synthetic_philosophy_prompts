Since retiring from a career in experimental neurobiology—where I spent decades mapping reward-circuit dynamics in mammalian models—I have turned my attention to philosophy of action and the structure of intertemporal decision-making. I am now attempting to master classical Greek in order to read ancient medical texts in their original form, a project I genuinely value and have structured with strict discipline: each evening I resolve that, immediately after my morning coffee, I will study for ninety minutes before engaging with anything else.

Yet the resolution dissolves reliably. At the moment of execution, the immediate cognitive strain of parsing unfamiliar syntax feels overwhelmingly aversive relative to the distant, abstract satisfaction of future fluency. I defer to the afternoon, then to the next day. What complicates the picture is that my evaluative stance itself seems to shift with my temporal position: in the planning state the study session appears clearly preferable, whereas in the execution state relaxation appears clearly preferable, and neither stance strikes me as erroneous at the moment it is held. From my scientific training, I am accustomed to modeling such conflicts not as a single agent betraying itself, but as competing subsystems—prefrontal planning mechanisms versus limbic reward signals—without presupposing a unitary "self" that must adjudicate between them.

Consequently, I am uncertain whether my postponement should be understood as a failure to carry out a plan that I genuinely endorsed as best, or rather as rational behavior governed by a temporarily dominant preference structure that is as authentically "mine" as the planning structure. If the pattern I observe is the familiar one in which near-term costs and rewards receive disproportionate weight relative to distant ones, does the very idea of having acted "against my better judgment" depend on a hidden normative commitment to treating present and future goods with equal weight—a commitment I have never explicitly endorsed? I have attempted mechanical commitment devices—locking my texts in a timed container—but I notice that the anticipated effort of retrieval is subject to precisely the same disproportionate weighting, undermining the device from within. I am not seeking practical advice; I want to know whether the philosophical framework that separates genuine self-betrayal from ordinary temporal preference change can accommodate an agent conceived as a temporally extended but non-unitary system, or whether it necessarily requires a single, continuous normative self against which one can genuinely fall.
