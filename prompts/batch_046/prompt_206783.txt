I'm a phd student in philosophy of medicine and I've been going in circles with my advisor about whether "health" is just a statistical thing or if it necessarily involves values. You know, the old naturalism vs normativism thing that's been around since like the 70s — the biostatistical camp says a disease is basically a deviation from species-typical functioning, while the normativists insist you can't call something pathological unless it's actually harmful or unwanted by the person. Every time I dig into the literature it feels like the same moves get replayed: naturalists point to heart failure being an obvious malfunction regardless of culture, normativists point to how homosexuality or deafness got de-pathologized even though they aren't statistically typical. Nobody actually changes their mind, and I'm wondering if we're even having one argument.

Here's my working guess: part of the stall comes from mixing up three seperate questions — what evolution designed the body to do, what doctors should treat, and what people experience as suffering — and calling them all "health." But there's also this newer wrinkle that's been bugging me: with all these genomic screening tools now, we can flag people as having "pre-disease" states (like sitting in the 99th percentile for diabetes risk) long before they feel anything wrong. The naturalists want to call that a dysfunction because it's statistically deviant; the normativists want to say it's not a disease unless it actually harms the person's life plan. But both sides seem to talk past each other about whether "silent dysfunction" is even coherent, and I think that might be where the real action is hiding.

So I want a diagnosis, not a verdict: is this disagreement substantive, verbal, or are the two camps actually answering different questions about biology, medicine, and lived experience? Is there a hidden empirical question about how many of these silent dysfunctions eventually turn into real harm, or is the impasse just a sign that "health" is too loose a concept to do the work we're asking of it? And if you think it's stalled for good reason, what would actually count as progress — a sharper distinction between risk and disease, a clinical study tracking outcomes, or just admitting we need seperate concepts for epidemiology and therapy? Please write your answer in American English, and don't worry about citing specific papers — I'm just trying to think clearly.
