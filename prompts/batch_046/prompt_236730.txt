I am a retired physicist who, since leaving the laboratory, have taken up philosophy of mind as a serious avocation, and I now attend a small reading group devoted to consciousness studies. At our last meeting, one of the members—who has read Dennett's Consciousness Explained but little of the surrounding literature on temporal phenomenology—summarized for me the Orwellian versus Stalinesque account of the color phi phenomenon. He explained that when two differently colored dots are flashed in rapid succession at separate spatial positions, observers typically report seeing a single dot that travels through an intermediate location and changes color midway. He said that Dennett presents two competing revisionary explanations for this: an Orwellian revision, in which the brain genuinely generates an intermediate phenomenal experience—say, of a greenish dot in the middle—and then retroactively alters the memory trace of the first dot so that it never seemed red; and a Stalinesque revision, in which no intermediate experience ever occurs, but the brain fabricates a false historical record after the fact, analogous to airbrushing a photograph, so that the subject later reports a smooth trajectory that was never actually present to consciousness. My interlocutor then added that Dennett treats the Stalinesque mechanism as essentially a species of unconscious Bayesian inference, comparable to Helmholtz's theory of perception, whereby the visual system computes the most probable motion path and inserts it into the stream of consciousness without any corresponding phenomenal content at the intermediate stage. He also invoked what he called a 2018 high-temporal-resolution EEG study, which he claimed showed that the neural signature associated with motion perception in area V5 arises only after the neural preparation of the verbal report, and he took this temporal ordering to demonstrate conclusively that the intermediate motion is never consciously experienced but is constructed post-hoc. From these premises he drew a broad conclusion: that Dennett has thereby shown there is no specious present in human consciousness, that all introspective reports of smooth temporal flow are Stalinesque fabrications, and that any philosophy of mind that relies on direct access to the temporal structure of experience is therefore undermined.

I am inclined to think that this summary mixes accurate exposition with some subtle but important distortions, though my own background is in physics rather than philosophy, and I cannot easily separate the two. Could you examine the characterization point by point? Specifically, I would like to know which parts a careful reader of Dennett would accept as accurate, which involve conflations—perhaps between Dennett's anti-psychologism and Helmholtzian inference, or between neural timing and phenomenal timing—that a defender of the view would reject, and whether the EEG claim genuinely supports the Stalinesque interpretation or whether it rests on a confusion. I also want to understand whether Dennett's argument about the color phi case is meant to license the sweeping denial of any specious present, or whether my friend has overextended a localized claim about one perceptual illusion into a general thesis about consciousness. Please separate accurate representation from misrepresentation, indicate what objections Dennett or his defenders would raise, and tell me whether the final verdict—that all temporal flow is fabricated—survives once the errors and overstatements are corrected.
