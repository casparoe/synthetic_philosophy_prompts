In my philosophy of ethics course we have been working through Kant's universalizability procedure in the Groundwork, specifically the distinction between maxims that generate a contradiction in conception and those that generate a contradiction in will. I am writing an essay that compares these two tests, but I want to push the analysis beyond the standard four examples by considering maxims that include an implicit threshold condition: that is, maxims whose practical viability depends on the assumption that adoption remains partial, and whose efficacy collapses not through strict logical contradiction but through quantitative overextension once adoption crosses a boundary. I would like to compare the contradiction-in-conception test and the contradiction-in-will test with respect to how each handles such threshold-sensitive maxims. Specifically, does the contradiction-in-will framework—which asks whether a rational being could consistently will the universalized maxim—have the resources to diagnose a problem when the contradiction only emerges under conditions of near-universal rather than fully universal compliance? Or should these cases be treated within the contradiction-in-conception framework, even though the universalized maxim does not seem logically self-defeating in the way that, say, a universalized false-promising maxim is? Please compare the logical structure and normative force of the two tests as applied to this kind of boundary case, and indicate whether you think Kant's distinction can accommodate partial-compliance failures or whether it leaves an unaddressed gap between logical and rational-volitional contradiction.
