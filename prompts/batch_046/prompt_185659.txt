You are to deliver the opening remarks at a retirement symposium honoring Dr. Marisol Varga, who directed her university's Office of Circumstance and Choice for thirty years. She designed the office to administer need-blind grants, emergency aid, and work-study exemptions through a framework drawn from luck egalitarianism. The audience is divided: roughly half are moral and political philosophers familiar with distributive justice, and half are family, retired staff, and alumni donors with no philosophical background. The symposium theme is "Institutional Boundaries." Compose a ten-minute address that honors Varga's career without reducing it to charity or bureaucratic management. Structure the speech around the luck-egalitarian distinction between brute luck and option luck, applied institutionally: her office treated certain hardships as unchosen circumstances warranting unconditional support, and others as products of choice warranting conditional or withheld support. Acknowledge—without lecturing either audience—that this distinction is philosophically unstable, since choices are themselves shaped by prior circumstances, and that any institution applying it draws lines it cannot fully justify. Illustrate this with one concrete, invented scenario: for example, a student who loses scholarship eligibility after a parent's sudden illness versus a student who declines work-study for an unpaid internship in a field they freely chose. Do not resolve the scenario; let it stand as an example of the boundary she was forced to draw. Speak simultaneously to both audiences: allow the philosophers to hear you concede the standard objections—the recursive problem of identifying choice, the difficulty of isolating circumstance—without requesting citations, texts, or bibliographies; allow the non-philosophers to understand why her administrative labor was conceptually serious. Defend her practice of drawing the line anyway, framing it not as hypocrisy but as institutional humility: her office publicly acknowledged which hardships it classified as unchosen and which as chosen, rather than disguising the classification behind neutral procedure. Conclude by suggesting that her legacy is not that she solved the choice-circumstance problem, but that she maintained an institution that refused to pretend it had solved it.
