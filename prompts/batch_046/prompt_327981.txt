I've been trying to understand why promises bind by analogizing them to temporary moral liens: when I promise, I encumber my future autonomy in favor of the promisee, and breaking the promise is like reclaiming property that was already assigned. This explains the intuition that breach is a wrong to the promisee specifically, not just a general failure of benevolence. But the model creates an odd asymmetry. If the binding force comes from having "locked away" my future options, then promises made far in the future should bind less—there's less present autonomy being encumbered—yet we often think long-term commitments carry special weight. Should we solve this by saying the lien "matures" with proximity to performance, or does the asymmetry show that promises don't actually work like property encumbrances at all? Give a committed answer: pick one horn, explain what it implies for whether promises can bind across generations or to strangers, and say explicitly where your answer rests on a contested assumption about what autonomy consists in rather than on an uncontroversial feature of promises.
