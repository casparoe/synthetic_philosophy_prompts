I've been thinking about how courts treat parents who refuse life-saving medical care for their children because of religious belief. The law usually grants an exemption only when the belief belongs to an established religious tradition with shared doctrines and community recognition. If a parent instead claims a private, direct command—similar to the biblical story in which Abraham is told to sacrifice his son—there is no legal protection, and the parent can be charged with neglect or worse. This creates a puzzling pattern: universal ethical duty, such as saving a child, can apparently be suspended, but only when that suspension is institutional and collective, never when it is solitary and personal.

What theory of moral obligation and religious authority does this practice presuppose? Does it assume that ethics can be overridden for a higher purpose only if that purpose is socially validated, which would exclude the idea of an individual standing alone outside universal ethics in direct relation to the divine? Or is the distinction purely administrative—meant only to prevent fraud—without any deeper philosophical commitment?

Furthermore, is the practice consistent with the rest of the legal system? We generally hold that personal conscience does not excuse breaking criminal law, yet here we carve out a precise exception for conscience, provided it wears an institutional form. Reconstruct the implicit view of value, responsibility, or the nature of the ethical that makes this split coherent, distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what merely fits it well, and tell me what a legal framework would have to change if it genuinely took the solitary, Abraham-like case as seriously as the institutional one.
