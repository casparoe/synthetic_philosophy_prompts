I'm taking a course on philosophy of language and we're stuck in the weeds on deflationary theories of truth. The basic pitch makes sense — truth isn't some heavy metaphysical property, it's mostly just a tool for disquoting and making generalizations like "everything Sarah said is true." But then the professor brought up this problem about explanatory asymmetry, and now half the study group is arguing about whether deflationists can actually account for it.

Here's the setup. Normally we explain why someone holds a true belief by pointing to the world — snow is white, so the belief that snow is white is true. But nobody ever explains why snow is white by pointing to the belief being true. There's this clear directional flow, world to belief, and not the other way around. My friend thinks deflationists can brush this off easily. She says it's just like smoke detectors and fires — detectors don't cause fires, so of course we don't explain fires by citing detectors. The asymmetry is causal or pragmatic, not metaphysical. But another friend in the group thinks that's cheating. He argues that if truth is literally just disquotation — if saying "the belief is true" is just a roundabout way of saying "snow is white" — then the two should be interchangable in explanations. So "snow is white explains the belief's truth" becomes "snow is white explains snow is white," which is circular. And if you try to run it backwards, you get the belief explaining the world, which is exactly the wrong direction. So he thinks deflationists are stuck: either they admit there's some hidden substantive feature of truth that blocks the swap, or they accept that explanations can run symmetrically, which sounds nuts.

Can you walk me through this? I want to understand whether there's a real conceptual problem for deflationism here, or whether my first friend is right that we're just confusing explanatory practices with the nature of truth. Specifically, if a deflationist appeals to generalization — using "is true" to endorse a bunch of claims at once — does that actually help explain the directional flow, or does it just change the subject? And do the different flavors of deflationism, like the redundancy view versus the one that treats "is true" like a pronoun for sentences, handle this asymmetry differently, or do they all fall into the same trap?
