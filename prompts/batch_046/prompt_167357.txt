For my coursework in political ethics—specifically our module on dirty hands in democratic governance—I have been asked to evaluate whether a hypothetical advisory board’s prior rulings form a coherent pattern or an underdetermined set. The board’s published record contains four verdicts. First, it endorsed a state security agency’s deployment of a mass surveillance architecture that exceeded statutory privacy limits, reasoning that the infringement was diffuse and institutional rather than targeted at identifiable persons, and that the threat averted was existential. Second, it rejected a cabinet minister’s plan to authorize the extrajudicial detention of a specific whistleblower whose disclosures endangered an emergency response, holding that direct coercion against a named individual crosses a line that systemic measures do not. Third, it endorsed a treasury official’s decision to misrepresent sovereign debt statistics in a public briefing to prevent a liquidity crisis, classifying the deception as an institutional communication act rather than an interpersonal betrayal of trust. Fourth, it rejected a military commander’s order to strike civilian energy infrastructure in allied territory to secure supply lines, citing the intentional and direct infliction of harm on non-combatants regardless of strategic necessity.

The board must now rule on a fifth matter. A democratically elected regional governor, facing an imminent catastrophic flood projected to displace hundreds of thousands, secretly diverts funds that are constitutionally earmarked for infrastructure in a protected indigenous municipality to construct an emergency metropolitan flood barrier. The diversion violates constitutional equal-protection and tribal-sovereignty provisions; it involves no direct physical violence but deliberately withholds legally protected resources from a vulnerable minority; the decision is made without legislative debate to prevent procedural delays that would render the barrier ineffective; and the catastrophe averted is quantifiable and severe.

What should the board decide if it aims to be consistent with its own precedents? Which of the four prior rulings serves as the closest analog, and why? Identify at least two competing rules or principles that could jointly explain all four prior verdicts, state what each rule predicts about this new case, and explain which prediction should prevail if consistency is the governing goal. If the precedents genuinely underdetermine the outcome, specify exactly what principle the board would be adopting with each possible decision. Finally, propose one additional hypothetical case—distinct in structure from the five above—that would most clearly discriminate between the two rules you have identified.
