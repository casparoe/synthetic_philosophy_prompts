I serve on my university's interspecies and synthetic-subject ethics board. A joint neuroscience and robotics lab has submitted a protocol that would replace our binary "human/non-human subject" classification with a four-tier graded moral-status framework for all research entities: Tier 1 (full protection, e.g., competent humans); Tier 2 (high partial status, e.g., cephalopods, great apes, synthetic architectures with sustained affective integration and goal-directed flexibility); Tier 3 (moderate partial status, e.g., insects with demonstrated nociceptive learning, adaptive robots with integrated sensory loops); and Tier 4 (minimal or no status, e.g., thermostats, basic algorithms). Each tier would determine permitted invasive procedures, consent-substitute requirements, and post-study obligations. The proponents argue that binary classification forces us to treat a hive-insect and a thermostat identically despite meaningful differences in integrated sensory processing, and that comparative cross-domain studies require finer distinctions. The opponents argue that introducing gradation into institutional ethics erodes the categorical safeguard for human subjects, creates unenforceable review burdens, and risks "status inflation" for entities lacking the unified perspective required for any moral claim. Half the board favors approval with a one-year pilot restricted to Tier 3 biological subjects; half favors outright rejection and retention of the binary rule. Draft the board's decision memo—approve, reject, or approve with conditions—recommending a concrete, votable action. The memo must treat the strongest argument for the opposing position fairly, address whether a scalar framework can avoid sliding human protections downward, specify exactly which entities and procedures the recommendation covers and excludes, and avoid any bibliography or citation list. Keep it under 600 words.
