Having retired from evolutionary biology and only recently begun systematic study in moral philosophy, I want to check whether the following commitments form a coherent package before presenting them to a discussion group.

(1) I treat suffering risks—especially those involving artificial minds, digital ecosystems, or large-scale biological engineering—as among the most urgent moral problems, with potential scale that dwarfs historical catastrophes.

(2) I accept an axiological asymmetry: suffering is not merely the absence of well-being but carries a distinct, heavier weight, so reducing extreme suffering takes near-lexical priority over producing equivalent happiness.

(3) Phenomenal consciousness is the sole ground for moral status, yet I maintain that we currently lack any reliable method to detect or attribute it to artificial architectures or radically redesigned organisms; our epistemic position is profoundly weak.

(4) I therefore advocate aggressive near-term intervention into wild-animal suffering—including ecosystem redesign and targeted genetic modification—because present natural suffering is vast and largely unaddressed.

(5) By contrast, I support a stringent precautionary moratorium on the deliberate creation of digital minds or synthetic conscious systems until we possess a validated, empirically grounded theory of phenomenal consciousness.

(6) I hold that human extinction, should it occur without inflicting additional suffering, would likely be net-positive over cosmic timescales; nevertheless, I actively support human survival and flourishing out of obligations to present and near-future moral patients.

Please audit this set. Which pairs are genuinely in tension, which only appear to conflict, and how severe is each tension? Rank the real conflicts by severity, specify for each the least costly revision that would restore consistency, and identify which single change would clean up the most problems. Do not treat every friction as equally damaging.
