I take the view that the moral saint—defined not merely as someone with exceptional moral character but as one who maximizes moral goodness across every sphere of life—is an undesirable and conceptually unstable ideal. The core problem is not just that such a life crowds out non-moral goods, but that it depends on a hidden zero-sum model of psychological and social resources: it treats aesthetic commitment, partial love, and play as residual categories that must be minimized for moral maximization, and it assumes that moral labor must be personally accumulated rather than institutionally distributed. What would genuinely count against this position? I want a map of its exposure, sorted from fatal to merely awkward: which observed or conceivable lives, which results in moral psychology or value theory, and which structural arguments about resource allocation or value integration would force a revision? Would defenders concede that cases of sustained near-maximal moral achievement combined with robust non-moral flourishing are damaging, or would they absorb them by redefining "maximal"? Is there a risk the view has been insulated by auxiliary qualifications—say, by treating any counterexample as proof that the subject was not truly a saint—and if so, is that insulation a problem for an ideal that claims normative authority? Please include an honest assessment of whether the zero-sum premise is load-bearing or expendable.
