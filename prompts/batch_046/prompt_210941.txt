Write a branching taxonomy of positions on the special composition question, designed as a diagnostic instrument rather than a label quiz. Each internal node should present one concrete case or claim—e.g., whether a statue whose original parts have all been replaced over centuries is still one object; whether a swarm of drones maintaining formation via a distributed algorithm with no central controller counts as a single thing; whether a dispersed cloud of hydrogen atoms spread across light-years but gravitationally destined to collapse into one star is now one object—so that a respondent can answer yes or no without knowing the philosophical names. Label each branch by the principle that splits it: first, do any composite objects exist at all? If yes, is spatiotemporal continuity necessary? If not, is causal-functional integration sufficient? Then branch on whether living or artifactual organization is required, and finally on whether vagueness about boundaries undermines any precise answer. The leaves must include universalism, mereological nihilism, moderate restriction (organisms and/or artifacts only), and contact- or fastenation-style moderation; note any logically possible combination of answers that remains unoccupied and briefly diagnose why (incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored?). Flag explicitly which pairs of named positions your tree genuinely cannot separate. Keep the tree to about six to eight nodes, and do not ask which label the respondent prefers—the sorting should come entirely from verdicts on cases. In your explanation, separate what is largely settled in contemporary metaphysics from what remains genuinely contested among specialists, and do not present any contested view as the consensus.
