In a discussion of statutory interpretation I heard a speaker defend the view that legal codes can contain genuinely contradictory directives—one provision requiring an action that another forbids—by invoking the line, "Contradictions can be true without everything being true." The speaker seemed to be using it to block the inference that such contradictions render the law useless or license arbitrary judicial discretion. I want to know what precise theses are packed into that one sentence. Disentangle the logical thesis (that explosion, ex contradictione quodlibet, can fail in a formal system), the metaphysical thesis (that some contradictions actually obtain), the epistemic thesis (that agents can rationally hold contradictory beliefs without revising them all), and the normative thesis (that practical reasoning remains possible in the presence of contradiction). For each, say whether it is a tautology, a substantive and defensible claim, or false. Then tell me which of these readings actually supports the speaker's legal argument, which ones are irrelevant, and which one they were most likely invoking without noticing the ambiguity.
