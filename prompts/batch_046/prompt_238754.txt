In modern Stoic revival circles and the philosophical commentary around them, a debate has stalled over the political implications of Epictetus's distinction between what lies within our power and what does not. One camp reads the dichotomy as applying strictly to an agent's own judgments, intentions, and moral character — what Stoics call prohairesis — and therefore holds that organizing against institutional injustice is fully compatible with Stoic ethics, since action itself falls within our power even when outcomes do not. The opposing camp insists that treating large-scale social structures, economic conditions, and systemic harm as externals necessarily produces moral quietism, because Stoic virtue requires accepting whatever happens outside the sphere of choice, including collective suffering that one might otherwise resist. Both sides invoke the same texts — the opening of the Enchiridion and parallel passages in the Discourses — both agree that injustice is genuinely bad, and both accuse the other of misreading Stoicism; yet after years of exchange neither has revised its interpretation of the core distinction or conceded that Stoicism can, or cannot, ground political activism. My hypothesis is that the stalemate is not philological but conceptual: the two sides are importing different contemporary accounts of moral responsibility — one requiring causal efficacy over outcomes, the other requiring only right intention — and projecting them onto Epictetus's framework, which was designed for individual ethical formation rather than collective action. Is that diagnosis correct, or is there a substantive interpretive disagreement about the scope of prohairesis that closer attention to the Greek or to Stoic physics could resolve? I want a diagnosis of the disagreement's structure: is it verbal, with both sides using control to mean different things; is it a clash of bedrock political intuitions that neither can argue for; are the sides answering different questions — one about individual virtue, one about collective ethics — under one heading; or is a hidden empirical claim about psychological resilience or about the efficacy of activism doing the work? And given that diagnosis, what — a conceptual distinction, a reframing of the question, or an admission that the framework is silent here — could actually move the debate, or is permanent stalemate the right expectation?
