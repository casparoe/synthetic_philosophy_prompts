I am a doctoral student in comparative philosophy of religion, and I have been trying to articulate my own reflective judgments about personal persistence through the lens of Buddhist anattā without merely repeating the standard aggregate or chariot analogies I encountered in survey courses. Rather than asking for an exegesis, I want to know whether there is a coherent reconstructive principle behind the following five gut-level verdicts, all described in ordinary language.

First, when an advanced meditator reports that sustained practice has dissolved any sense of an internal observer—there is seeing, but no seer—she takes this as direct evidence that there never was a substantial self, only impersonal processes. I agree with her conclusion, yet I feel an immediate tension: if there is genuinely no subject present, then it is unclear what exactly underwent the transformative practice, or in what sense the pre-meditative and post-meditative phases belong to one life.

Second, imagine a gradual procedure in which every functional component of a monastic's cognitive architecture is replaced over decades with synthetic analogues that preserve all relational and mnemonic patterns. At the end, the figure remembers ordination, recognizes disciples, and performs rituals flawlessly. My intuition is that this is still the same continuing practitioner, but this seems hard to square with the denial of any underlying substrate: if identity is nothing but a pattern of aggregates, then sudden death followed by causal rebirth should be no different from this gradual replacement, yet I treat the two cases differently.

Third, when a dying layperson asks whether she will be reborn as her granddaughter's child, a teacher responds that there is no traveler, only causal continuity analogous to one flame igniting another. I find this refusal correct. Nevertheless, if causal continuity is all that exists, I struggle to see why present ethical effort matters: the chain will propagate regardless of my current intentions, so the normative urgency of practice seems to evaporate unless something more is at stake.

Fourth, consider a person with profound dementia who has lost language, religious commitments, familial recognition, and autobiographical memory, yet still breathes and responds to warmth. The family says she is already gone. I resist this: there still seems to be a living being present, capable of suffering or comfort, even though the mental aggregates usually said to constitute identity are largely absent.

Fifth, I find compelling the view that nirvāṇa should be understood as the permanent cessation of the aggregates that generate suffering, not as a state experienced by a persisting subject. At the same time, I recall descriptions of the Tathāgata after death as deep, boundless, and immeasurable—language that sounds like the presence of something limitless rather than the simple absence of a person. I want to say that the liberated one is neither identical to the former practitioner nor a wholly different entity, but I am unsure whether that middle position is conceptually stable.

These judgments pull in opposing directions: Case Two seems to require some form of functional continuity that Case Three denies as a basis for identity; Case Four resists reducing the self to mental aggregates alone, while Case One denies any subject altogether. Is there a single principle—perhaps reconstructed from Buddhist metaphysics of selfhood, or from a related comparative framework—that can accommodate all five reactions without ad hoc adjustments? If coherence demands that I abandon one or two verdicts, which ones should go, and why? Finally, what would your proposed principle predict for a case I have not listed: a long-term practitioner who, after intensive meditation, deliberately cultivates an entirely new personality with different moral commitments and reconstructed memories, insisting that this is not the transformation of an underlying self but the replacement of one aggregate-pattern with another?
