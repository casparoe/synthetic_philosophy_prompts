I sit on my university's field-research ethics commitee and we're in a bit of a stalemate over a proposal from a behavioural ecology group. They want three years of funding to monitor red deer and ground-nesting birds on a remote moor using drone telemetry and passive acoustic sensors — all non-invasive, which sounds fine — but they've built their ethical protocol around something they call 'trophic baseline ethics'. The idea, as I understand it, is that wild-animal suffering only becomes a researcher's problem when human activity has pushed the ecosystem's aggregate suffering load above a reconstructed pre-anthropogenic baseline; they claim the moor's current suffering density is actually below that baseline thanks to historical over-culling, so non-intervention is not just permitted but ethically required. Half the panel thinks this is clever philosophy; the other half thinks it's a fancy excuse to avoid treating injured fawns or feeding starving birds, and that you can't hang a duty of care on a counterfactual model with that many unknowns.

Could you draft the memo I'll circulate before Tuesday's vote? I need a concrete recommendation — approve, reject, or approve with a mandatory intervention trigger — plus a clear time-frame, say a six-month review point. The reasoning should take the trophic-baseline argument seriously rather than mocking it, but it also needs to deal honestly with the strongest objection on the other side, which is that constructing a pre-anthropogenic suffering baseline involves so much speculative modelling about predator densities, climate variability, and population dynamics that it can't actually carry the ethical weight being placed on it. I'd like the memo written in a tone the biologists will read — formal but not legalistic — and please don't just list philosophers I should read; I need something the commitee can act on.
