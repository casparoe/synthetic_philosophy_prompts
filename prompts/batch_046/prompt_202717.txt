I am a doctoral candidate in philosophy of action preparing a ninety-minute session for a graduate seminar of twelve students. I need a runnable structured game—not a lecture or discussion—around Kavka's toxin puzzle, but with a specific conceptual twist that destabilizes the standard resolution in which one rationally forms an intention yet avoids execution. The twist is this: introduce an institutional “credibility monitor” that can verify with approximately 80% accuracy whether a participant genuinely formed the requisite intention at the designated moment, releasing an immediate reward upon verification, while actual ingestion of the toxin is only detected after a 24-hour delay and is physically irreversible. Assign roles—subjects, monitors, and a collective pool whose payout depends on aggregate verified intentions—so that participants face real, small monetary stakes: each verified intention earns the group one dollar in pooled cash divided at the end, but any actual ingestion costs the ingester five dollars and voids the entire pool. The sequence should be staged: private deliberation, then public commitment, then monitored verification, then a private execution window. Design rules and payoffs so that participants discover for themselves the strategic pressure to form hollow intentions, the risk that forming an intention causally facilitates execution, and the corruption introduced by noisy verification—before any facilitator names the paradox. Include a debrief that maps each stage onto evidential versus causal decision-theoretic reasoning; predicts what will predictably go wrong in practice (e.g., role-confusion, strategic deception of monitors, group coercion) and how the facilitator should handle it; and is explicit about which feature of the original single-agent puzzle—pure introspective transparency of mental states—is necessarily absent here and must be supplied by the facilitator in discussion. The participants should leave seeing precisely why the gap between rational commitment and rational action becomes unstable once intention is externally monitored and strategically embedded.
