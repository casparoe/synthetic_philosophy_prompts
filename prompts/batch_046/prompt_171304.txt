I cover science and philosophy for a general audience, and I am preparing a feature on whether emerging reproductive technologies compel us to revise standard intuitions about personal origin. Rather than a survey of names, I need a rigorous conceptual framework I can use in interviews and in the piece itself.

Please write the desiderata that any adequate theory of origin essentialism for human persons must satisfy. For each requirement, give the motivating case or conceptual puzzle that generates it, and mark whether you regard it as non-negotiable or as the preference of a particular camp. Your list must address the canonical problem—whether the same individual could have originated from a different sperm and egg pair—but it must also accommodate a constructed scenario central to my reporting: imagine a future procedure in which reproductive cells are generated in vitro from induced pluripotent stem cells taken from an adult person's own tissue, then fused with a synthetic epigenetic scaffold that introduces developmental modifications absent from both biological parents, yielding an embryo that is genetically identical to the source individual but mediated by artificial structures never involved in natural conception.

Identify any pair of desiderata that appear jointly unsatisfiable once this synthetic case is taken seriously. Then, briefly and without bibliography or citation, indicate how the principal philosophical stances—strict genetic origin essentialism, psychological-continuity accounts, and the view that origin is metaphysically irrelevant—fare against your list, noting specifically where the synthetic scaffold case disrupts the usual assessment. Keep the weight on the criteria and their motivating conflicts rather than on the scorecard. Where the evaluation necessarily turns on a judgment of value or metaphysical taste rather than on a determinable fact, flag that point explicitly so I know where philosophical reasoning ends and editorial judgment begins.
