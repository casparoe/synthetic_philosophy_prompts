I am writing a research presentation for my high-school philosophy elective on what my instructor calls the aggregation problem for rational belief. Our seminar has spent several weeks on the lottery paradox—where it seems rational to believe that any particular ticket will lose, yet also rational to believe that at least one ticket in a large draw will win, with closure apparently forcing an inconsistency—and on the preface paradox, in which an author can rationally believe each specific claim in her monograph while also rationally believing that at least one claim is false. I want to move beyond the standard encyclopedia divisions—threshold views, contextualism, denial of agglomeration, graded belief, skeptical bullet-biting—because my classmates keep conflating the labels with the actual commitments. Instead, I need a diagnostic decision tree or taxonomy, built first from the logical space of answers and only then mapped onto named positions, that sorts people by what they actually believe about concrete cases.

The instrument should contain roughly eight to ten branching questions, each phrased as a scenario with two or three answer options, so that someone can navigate it without knowing any philosophical terminology. Please include a scoring key that assigns answer patterns to positions: strict Lockean uniform-threshold views; Lockean views with context-sensitive thresholds; all-or-nothing belief coupled with denial of closure; graded or Bayesian treatments; skeptical resolutions that reject one of the apparently rational beliefs; and any genuinely hybrid or unexplored combinations. For each empty branch—logical combinations that no named view occupies—include a brief diagnosis of whether the gap is due to incoherence, unattractiveness, or mere neglect. To keep this from being a generic summary, inject an unconventional framing: treat the preface not merely as a book-author case but as an epistemic portfolio, in which an agent distributes confidence across a finite set of predictions. Construct at least two diagnostic nodes around a hybrid nested lottery-preface scenario—specifically, a meteorologist who assigns high confidence to each of ten individual daily forecasts, knows that the underlying model entails at least one error is probable, and must decide whether to issue a collective disclaimer. Note explicitly which positions the tree fails to separate—certain forms of contextualism and certain threshold-shifting views, for example—and flag them rather than papering over the overlap. The audience is my seminar of advanced high-school students who understand basic probability but have not read the technical literature, so the cases should be answerable without prior knowledge of the debates, yet the taxonomy itself should be philosophically precise. Do not include a bibliography, reading list, or citations; I need the logical architecture and the case-based questions, not sources.
