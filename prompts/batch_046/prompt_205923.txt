i've been sitting with this idea that the Protestant work ethic never actually died, it just got secularized into a "calling without salvation" — so karoshi isn't some weird Japanese cultural glitch but the logical endpoint of Weber's ascetic self-denial once you strip away the theological payoff. meanwhile the 35-hour week push (France, the EU debates, etc.) isn't really an economic demand, it's an attempt to reclaim leisure as a right rather than a reward for productivity. so debates about work hours are mostly about whether your time is yours by default or belongs to your vocation. i think this explains why people get weirdly moralistic about "laziness" even in totally secular contexts.

but i'm not sure this isn't just a neat story. what would actually count against it? i want the defeaters sorted by how fatal they'd be: maybe historical evidence that Weber was wrong about the causal link to capitalism, or that karoshi is driven more by corporate hierarchy than internalized ethics? or psychological data showing people don't actually feel guilt about leisure? what would defenders of this view concede is a problem, versus what they'd dismiss? and has this been qualified into unfalsifiability — like, if every overworked person is just "secularized calling" and every relaxed person is "resisting the ethic," does the view risk predicting everything? i'd like an honest map of where it's actually exposed, including whether this framing occured to anyone before and whether that matters.
