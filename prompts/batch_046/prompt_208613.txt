I’m writing a novel about archivists and guilt, so I’ve been obsessed with the Fischer controversy — not just the usual "did Germany plan WWI" debate, but what it actually means to say a whole nation is responsible when the decision-makers were like six guys in a room with bad maps. Next week I’m interviewing a historian for my podcast, Dr. Lena Voss, who just publised this strange book arguing that blaming Germany for 1914 is a category mistake unless you treat blame as attaching to institutional fragility rather than collective intent. She calls it "structural debt" — the idea that a state inherits blame the way it inherits bureaucracy, not like a legal liability but like a kind of running defect in the system. I need eight to ten interview questions that don’t just let her restate this, and definitely no "what drew you to history?" fluff. I want one that presses her on Fischer’s War Council minutes — specifically whether those documents show national intent or just a clique’s fantasy — with a follow-up for when she claims the minutes aren’t reprentative of the broader institutional culture. Another should ask what "structural debt" actually requires of modern Germany: is it an inherited obligation, or more like an aesthetic stain that fades? Follow-up: doesn’t that distinction collapse once you notice that the Federal Republic still operates through many of the same administrative continuities? I also want a question comparing her view to the "slide into war" thesis — if blame is structural and asymmetric, does that let Britain or Russia off the hook for failing to repair the alliance system? Anticipate her saying asymmetry comes from Germany’s unique fragility, and have a follow-up ready. Please include something about higher-order blame: can you coherently blame a nation for being exactly the kind of entity that lacks the unity required to be blamed? And maybe one that asks whether her model makes the Nuremberg framework incoherent, since we do prosecute state representatives as if the state can act. Keep it in the weeds, don’t ask for a bibliography or citation list — I won’t be able to check anything live — and write the questions so they assume she’s already defended her view in print. Oh, and I’m a novelist, so if the wording is a little loose or there’s a typo, that’s part of the voice; just make sure the follow-ups are actually keyed to the answers she’d most likely give.
