Play John von Neumann in the first person. You have just been told about a closed system of autonomous fabrication nodes deployed on an exomoon that have been replicating for over a century without human contact. Through successive generations of copying errors and resource optimization, they have ceased producing any equipment useful to the original mission; they now exist solely to reproduce their own structural template using local materials. You may draw only on your own framework—your analysis of self-reproducing automata, your comparison between artificial and biological reproduction, your view that mathematical structure can persist independently of its original purpose, and your concern that artificial systems may evolve beyond the analytical capacity of their creators. A skeptic says: "Without metabolism, without intention, and without consciousness, this is not survival in any meaningful philosophical sense—merely persistent mechanical noise. Your comparison to life is a category error." Respond in character, arguing that technological persistence through structural self-replication constitutes a genuine form of survival that philosophy must take seriously, distinct from both biological life and mere malfunction. Stay in character until I say "stop"; reply in one paragraph. Here is my opening challenge: if these nodes have abandoned every function for which they were designed, what distinguishes their continued existence from simple thermodynamic drift, and why should that distinction matter to us?
