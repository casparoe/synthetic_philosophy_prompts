I'm giving the opening remarks tomorrow at a two-day workshop called Planning and Dispersed Knowledge — about half the room are history-of-thought grad students and a couple retired economists who actually lived through the socialist calculation debate, the other half are local policy staff and family members of the organizers who mostly want to know why were here. The talk needs to do structural work, not just set a mood: I want you to build the eight minutes around a committed, definite claim — that the standard Hayek/Mises versus Lange framing is wrong in a specific way. Both sides, I think, shared a hidden assumption that economic information is essentially propositional and aggregable, rather than embodied in local practices and tacit adjustments. Don't lay out options; defend this reading, then use it to draw hard ground rules for what kinds of papers belong in the workshop and what kinds miss the point entirely. You have to make this land for the non-specialists without dumbing it down, include one concrete planning-failure example that only makes sense once you buy the shared-blindspot framing — not the usual Soviet price anecdote, something less obvious but still within standard training knowledge — and keep everything free of citations, footnotes, or 'as Hayek wrote...' attributions. The idea's should constrain what I can and can't say in the room, so be sharp about the boundaries.
