In a graduate metaethics seminar, a colleague keeps compressing evolutionary debunking arguments into the line “moral belief is just an evolutionary spandrel that mistakes its own utility for truth,” most recently using it to reject the idea that we have any non-circular reason to trust our first-order judgments about obligations to distant strangers. I want to bring a disambiguation to the next session rather than a counter-argument. What are the distinct precise theses that slogan could actually be expressing—ranging from the trivial claim that natural selection shaped our cognitive architecture, through various epistemic underminers that target reliability without touching truth, to the strong claim that evolutionary etiology is incompatible with moral realism? For each reading, specify whether it genuinely supports dismissing trust in judgments about distant strangers, flag which versions depend on controversial auxiliary premises (such as a direct correlation between evolutionary origin and present unreliability, or the absence of a plausible third-factor explanation linking fitness and moral truth), and note which interpretations collapse into banalities that do no argumentative work at all. Lay the readings out as a menu I can distribute, with brief verdicts on defensibility and fit to the seminar context, rather than selecting one correct interpretation and defending it.
