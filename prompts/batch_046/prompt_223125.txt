I am a doctoral candidate in philosophy of mind and normative ethics, and next month I am defending a dissertation chapter at my department’s colloquium. My thesis is that the classic heterogeneity problem for hedonism—what, if anything, do the pleasure of tasting wine, the pleasure of solving a proof, and the pleasure of revenge have in common—cannot be solved by appealing to a shared qualitative “hedonic tone” (which collapses under phenomenological scrutiny) nor by a generic pro-attitude (which collapses hedonism into desire-satisfaction theories). Instead, I propose a “structural-congruence” account: pleasures are mental states that exhibit unconflicted practical uptake, meaning that in the moment of experience they function in practical reasoning as self-justifying reasons for their own continuation, without requiring an independent antecedent desire. This captures bodily, intellectual, and even malicious pleasures under one formal feature. To handle “alienated pleasures”—cases where a subject takes pleasure in something she globally judges wrong—I borrow analogously from predictive-processing models of interoception: I treat alienation not as the absence of pleasure but as a conflict between sub-personal congruence (local error-minimization with positive valence) and global evaluative integration. The cost is that my account risks circularity in identifying pleasure through practical function and then using pleasure to explain that function, and it makes hedonism vulnerable to engineered or addictive cases where practical uptake is present but normative value seems absent.

The audience is precise: a philosophy department with strong moral-philosophy and philosophy-of-mind representation, several faculty who work on naturalism and cognitive science, two senior scholars of nineteenth-century British moral philosophy who know Mill and Sidgwick intimately, and no dedicated value theorists. They are generally skeptical of revisionary metaphysics of value, prefer empirically tractable distinctions, and tend to treat predictive-processing borrowings in normative philosophy with suspicion. They will not ask for a bibliography; they will attack the argument’s coherence, its relation to historical hedonism, and its normative implications.
