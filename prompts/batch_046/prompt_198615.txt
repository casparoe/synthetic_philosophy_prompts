As a journalist covering philosophy of science and metaethics, I am preparing a feature on whether normative reasons can exist without anyone to hold them—what some call free-floating rationales. Before you answer, please take a moment to brainstorm three distinctions: between a reason's metaphysical existence, its conditions of applicability, and its motivational accessibility to an agent. Then consider the argument below, which a philosopher I interviewed laid out. It seems to show that free-floating rationales collapse into incoherence, but I cannot pinpoint the misstep.

You are an expert in metaethics and philosophy of action. Think step by step. The argument runs as follows.

(1) For something to be a normative reason, it must possess normative force: the capacity to make an action correct or an attitude rational.
(2) Normative force is essentially relational—it must relate a consideration to an agent.
(3) Free-floating rationales exist independently of any agent's mental states or cognitive capacities.
(4) Therefore, free-floating rationales possess normative force without being related to any particular agent.
(5) But if normative force is relational and there is no relatum—no agent—then there is no normative force.
(6) So free-floating rationales either lack normative force, or they are not genuinely independent of agents.
(7) Defenders claim these reasons still determine what specific agents ought to do, regardless of whether those agents know about them.
(8) This generates obligations for agents who have no epistemic or causal access to the supporting considerations.
(9) An agent can be obligated only to perform actions within their capacity; this is the principle that ought implies can.
(10) If an agent lacks all access to the considerations behind an obligation, then fulfilling it is not within their capacity.
(11) Therefore, free-floating rationales entail obligations that violate ought-implies-can.
(12) A normative framework that systematically violates ought-implies-can is incoherent.
(13) Hence, the doctrine of free-floating rationales is self-defeating.

The source insists the reasoning is valid and that the only question is which premise to abandon. I am inclined to preserve (1) and (9), but I want to understand the cost of saving others. Where does this chain slip? Is there an equivocation between being related to an agent and being accessed by one? Is there a scope error in applying ought-implies-can to epistemic rather than practical limits? Identify the weakest step, explain why it initially looks plausible, and price out what rejecting each of the major premises—especially (2), (5), and (10)—would cost for the metaphysics of normativity. Do not provide a bibliography or reading list; work from the conceptual distinctions alone.
