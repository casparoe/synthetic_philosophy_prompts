I have no formal training in philosophy, but I have been turning over the old Ship of Theseus problem on my own, and I keep complicating it beyond the version everyone repeats. The standard case asks whether a ship whose every plank has been replaced over years remains the same vessel, especially if the old planks are gathered and reassembled into a second ship. I want to push further: imagine the replacements happen twice over, so the present ship at sea contains none of the planks from either the first or the second generation; meanwhile, the discarded planks are not kept in one warehouse but divided among several smaller boats, each holding only a scattered share of the originals, with no single reconstructed vessel to claim the title.

Write me a pantoum—quatrains in which the second and fourth lines of each stanza return as the first and third lines of the next—of about twenty-four lines. Let the form’s repetition-with-substitution enact the philosophical work: the poem should carry forward a continuous “ship” through successive stanzas even as the constituent lines (like planks) are replaced, and it should present three figures without adjudicating among them—the twice-replaced ship now sailing, the dispersed original planks distributed among smaller craft, and the absence of any unified repository of “the real” vessel. Do not resolve which is the true Ship of Theseus; let the structural repetition insist on continuity while the content insists on dispersal, leaving the tension open exactly where my own thinking stalls. Make it precise enough that someone familiar with the puzzle recognizes the stakes, but do not cite philosophers, texts, or historical sources; work only with the idea as I have laid it out here.
