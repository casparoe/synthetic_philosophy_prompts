Consider the institutional practice in representative democracies by which citizens routinely disclaim personal responsibility for specific government actions they opposed at the ballot—"I did not vote for this"—while the same legal and political system holds the entire citizenry collectively liable for national debts, treaty obligations, and reparations agreed upon by representatives they rejected. What view of moral responsibility, and specifically what interpretation of the principle of alternative possibilities, does this asymmetry presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory: does democratic participation function as a mechanism that supplies or withholds alternative possibilities for collective blame, or does the practice quietly rely on a Frankfurt-style separation of responsibility from the ability to do otherwise? Assess whether the practice is philosophically coherent, whether its treatment of individual and collective agency presupposes consistent commitments, and describe what democratic accountability mechanisms would have to change if we replaced the underlying view with one that made collective responsibility strictly proportional to individual causal influence.
