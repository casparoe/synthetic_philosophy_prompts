Consider a decision procedure now standard in large-scale institutional risk review—used in pharmaceutical rollout committees, algorithmic-impact boards, and environmental permitting bodies—though it is rarely named or defended explicitly. The procedure operates in two registers. In the first register, reviewers aggregate small, probabilistic harms and benefits across an anonymous population: they treat ten thousand people each facing a 0.1 percent chance of serious injury as ethically equivalent to ten people facing near-certain injury, and they authorize the action if the aggregate expected benefit exceeds the aggregate expected harm. In the second register, however, the same committee applies an absolute prohibition: if the risk assessment can identify even one specific individual—by name, fixed role, or determinable position—who will suffer a severe, non-compensable harm with high probability, the proposal is vetoed regardless of the aggregate calculus.

What philosophical commitments about moral reasons, interpersonal justification, and the moral status of statistical versus identified harm does this two-register practice presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory. Does it presuppose a Scanlon-style contractualist constraint—namely, that a principle permitting harm must be justifiable to each affected person individually, and that aggregation fails precisely when an identifiable complainant can reject the principle? Or does it instead presuppose a deontological side-constraint that applies only to direct harm, with statistical harm treated as a categorically different moral kind?

Distinguish rigorously: what the practice strictly requires philosophically, what it merely accommodates, and where it is philosophically underdetermined. Is there a coherent intermediate position—perhaps one that distinguishes fungible from non-fungible harms, or that treats identifiability as a moral-epistemic trigger rather than a metaphysical boundary?
