Having spent my career in experimental physics and only recently begun systematic study of moral and political philosophy, I find that arguments in this field often rely on compressed maxims whose logical structure is left unstated. In a recent seminar on political obligation and civil disobedience, a fellow participant ended a discussion about refusing an allegedly immoral statute by invoking the maxim that "an unjust law is no law at all." He treated this as sufficient to show that obedience to the statute was not merely unwise but conceptually impossible—there was, he claimed, literally no law to obey, hence no breach of political obligation in disregarding it.

I suspect this reasoning conflates several distinct theses, but I want to articulate the ambiguity precisely. Could you lay out the menu of precise claims this slogan might be advancing? I ask that you distinguish at least the following dimensions: (a) the domain of "law" (positive legal enactment, institutional rule, moral law in the natural-law tradition, or a normative system analogous to a theoretical framework); (b) the sense of "is not" (ontological non-existence, legal nullity from inception, moral non-bindingness, or epistemic disqualification); (c) the criterion of "unjust" (procedural illegitimacy, substantive violation of rights, or inconsistency with a higher-order normative principle); and (d) whether the claim is universal, agent-relative, or conditional on the agent's access to moral knowledge. For each distinct combination, indicate whether it is trivial, substantive and defensible, or false, and—most importantly—whether it actually supports his inference that disobedience involves no failure of obligation whatsoever.

As someone accustomed to the distinction in science between a falsified model and the absence of any model at all, I am particularly interested in whether there is a coherent intermediate reading: a statute might be defective or disqualified as law in some normative respect without being literally "no law," just as a disproved physical equation remains an equation that failed rather than a non-equation. Does any such intermediate reading preserve the rhetorical force of the maxim while blocking his conclusion?
