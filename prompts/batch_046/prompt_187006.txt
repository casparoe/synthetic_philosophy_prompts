I have been thinking on my own about whether a work of art can be genuinely good if the person who made it acted badly, or if the badness is always part of the work in some hidden way. I have four situations where I feel fairly sure of my judgment, but they do not sit together comfortably.

First situation: a museum holds a marble statue of remarkable balance and calm. The sculptor was cruel to her apprentices, yet that cruelty left no mark on the stone and had nothing to do with the statue's form. I believe the statue is still excellent art, and that condemning the sculptor is a separate matter.

Second situation: a documentary film about ecological harmony is beautiful and accurate. To obtain one essential scene, the director secretly destroyed a small habitat; the destruction is invisible to viewers. I believe the film is not fully good art, because the very feature we admire was bought by betraying the value the film promotes.

Third situation: a novel is widely praised as a masterpiece of empathy. It was later discovered that its emotional core was taken, without acknowledgment, from the unpublished diary of a person who died in poverty; readers cannot detect the theft from the text alone. I believe the novel is artistically weaker, as if its empathy were counterfeit, even though every sentence remains unchanged.

Fourth situation: an album contains technically innovative compositions. The session musicians were compelled to work in dangerous conditions, and the urgency audible in the recordings comes directly from that stress. I believe the album remains artistically excellent; the harm belongs to the labor practice, not to the music itself.

The pattern I tried to extract is this: a moral fault damages the art only when it is a necessary cause of the specific aesthetic quality we value. The cruelty in the first case does not cause the balance; the destruction in the second causes the admired scene; the plagiarism in the third causes the emotional effect; the forced labor in the fourth causes the urgency. But this rule fails, because in the fourth case I still want to say the art is good despite the causal link. So my principle is broken.

Can you propose one precise principle, or a small family of competing ones, that delivers all four verdicts? For each clause, please name the case that motivates it. Then tell me what your principle predicts for a case I have not listed: a painter produces images of childhood innocence that viewers find deeply moving, but the images are generated by an algorithm trained only on family photographs taken without consent; the painter never sees the source photographs and only adjusts the output. Would this be good art? Finally, please be honest about which of my four verdicts your best principle cannot accommodate, and whether I should overturn that verdict or complicate the principle to save it.
