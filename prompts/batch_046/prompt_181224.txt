Write a short story set in a near-future administrative body called the Bureau of Commensurability, whose technicians calibrate moral allocation algorithms by mapping subjective reports of pleasure and suffering onto a single quantitative axis. Center the narrative on a senior auditor who must evaluate an exceptional case: a collective of subjects has developed an intense, sustained form of aesthetic experience—produced through a precisely engineered synesthetic environment—that the Bureau’s instruments register as structurally incomparable with physical pain, not merely greater or lesser in magnitude but belonging to a qualitatively separate register. Under the Bureau’s governing framework, which applies a lexical threshold to suffering such that any severe pain is categorically not to be exchanged for any quantity of pleasure, the auditor faces a crisis: the algorithm demands a numerical comparison to justify continued funding of the environment, yet the device refuses to render one, suggesting that pleasure and pain may not share a common metric after all. Do not allow the character to deliver an explicit philosophical lecture; instead, render the tension through the tactile details of the calibration procedure, the readouts that fail to align, and a concrete decision the auditor makes at the end—perhaps altering a record, halting the program, or enforcing the allocation despite the broken comparison. The story should quietly ask whether forcing pleasure and suffering onto one scale respects the reality of either, and what is lost when a moral system treats them as fully interchangeable.
