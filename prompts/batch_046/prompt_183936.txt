I'm working through a problem set for a seminar on Quine's "Two Dogmas of Empiricism" and the fate of formalized theories, and I keep running into an argument I built that seems to collapse the normative structure of logic itself. Here it is, laid out carefully. (1) Quine's central attack on analyticity shows that every proposed criterion—whether synonymy, explicit definability, or Carnap-style meaning postulates—either presupposes the very notion it aims to explain or collapses into empirical claims about how speakers actually use terms. (2) If the analytic-synthetic boundary cannot be drawn in natural language, then logical truths have no special epistemic status: they sit on the same continuum as well-confirmed empirical generalizations, revisable in principle in light of recalcitrant experience. (3) Now apply this not to ordinary speech but to an explicitly axiomatized formal system F, with fixed inference rules and a precisely defined proof relation. One might think that here, at least, the analytic survives: the claim that sentence S is derivable in F is true purely in virtue of F's rules, with no empirical content. (4) But Quine's argument, if it holds, undermines even this refuge. Without a non-circular account of what the rules "really" entail, the statement "S follows from the axioms of F" is indistinguishable from the empirical observation "procedures implementing F consistently output S." The rules do not determine their own application; they are just patterns in symbol-manipulation behavior. (5) Consequently, logical consequence in F is not an analytic relation but an empirical regularity about how human or mechanical processors behave when they apply F. (6) If that is right, then there is no normative gap between a correct derivation of S and a buggy implementation that fails to derive S: both are merely different empirical patterns of processing, comparable to a thermometer reading 100°C versus 98°C. (7) Yet we unmistakably treat logical errors as mistakes—failures of reasoning—not as statistical outliers or alternative physical regularities. (8) So the argument concludes that either Quine's elimination of analyticity is false (at least for formal domains), or we must abandon the idea that logic carries normative force and treat it as purely descriptive psychology.

This strikes me as proving far too much, but I can't locate the weak point. Is there an equivocation between epistemic access to rules and the metaphysical status of logical relations? Does Quine's critique apply with equal force to formal languages, or does explicit axiomatization provide a non-circular fix for analyticity that natural language lacks? And if I reject premise (4) or (5) to save normativity, what exactly am I committing myself to—some form of logical conventionalism, or a distinction between the rule and its implementation that Quine would still reject? I'd prefer to keep (2) and (7) if I can, but I want to know what preserving them costs me, and whether there's a more subtle flaw—perhaps a scope shift between "no sharp boundary in natural language" and "no normative structure in formal systems"—that I've missed. Where does the argument actually break down?
