I'm working through a problem in confirmation theory with a study group, and we've constructed an argument that seems to show Bayesian epistemology makes scientific reasoning with legacy data impossible. The argument runs as follows. (1) By the Bayesian rule of conditionalization, a body of evidence E confirms a hypothesis H only if P(H|E) exceeds P(H). (2) In any case of old evidence, E was already observed before H was formulated, and therefore E is already part of the background knowledge K against which H is evaluated; hence P(H|E,K) equals P(H|K). (3) So old evidence cannot raise the posterior probability of H relative to the prior that rationally includes E. (4) Yet working scientists routinely treat decades-old datasets—say, 1992 satellite altimetry of Antarctic ice shelves—as genuinely confirming a 2024 stochastic-resonance model of marine ice-sheet instability, not merely as consistent with it. (5) To accommodate this practice within Bayesianism, one might allow the scientist to reset her prior, excluding E from K for the purpose of evaluating H, thereby restoring P(H|E) > P(H|K'). (6) But if prior-resetting is permitted whenever it suits confirmation, then an agent can always engineer confirmation of her favorite hypothesis by strategically forgetting inconvenient old evidence; rational belief revision becomes arbitrary. (7) Therefore Bayesian epistemology either denies that old evidence can ever confirm new theories—contradicting actual scientific practice—or licenses arbitrary belief manipulation. (8) Hence the framework proves too much: it undermines the very rationality of theory choice it was meant to formalize.

We feel the tension is real, but we also feel something must have slipped between (2) and (5), or between (5) and (6). Which premise or inference should we reject? We'd prefer to keep the intuition that old data can genuinely support new models (4) and that confirmation shouldn't be arbitrary (6), but we want to understand what giving up either (2) or (5) costs for the Bayesian. In particular, is there a principled distinction between legitimate prior restructuring and illegitimate forgetting that saves the framework without collapsing into ad hocery, and does that distinction survive once we consider that any restructuring can be redescribed as selective amnesia?
