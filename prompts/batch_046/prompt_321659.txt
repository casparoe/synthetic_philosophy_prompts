I'm in this philosophy of law seminar and we just finished the Hart-Fuller readings, but I'm still kinda stuck on how the debate actually shows up in real institutions. So like, in several US states there are these algorithmic bail-risk scores—COMPAS-type tools—and the appellate courts have this weird habit: they'll reverse a trial judge for ignoring the score or failing to document why they deviated, but they basically never strike the score down as legally invalid just because it weights prior arrests way more than current charge severity or embeds racial disparities. That means you can challenge the procedure, but not the moral architechture of the rule itself. What does that practice presuppose about legal validity? Is it just Hart's rule of recognition—whatever the legislature enacted plus judicial deference counts as law, full stop? Or does it secretly depend on Fuller's idea of an internal morality, since the courts check consistency and generality of application but never ask whether the rule is substantively just? And if Fuller's right, wouldn't there be some point where a morally rotten algorithm isn't just bad law but fails to be law at all?

I'm trying to figure out if this institutional pattern is philosophically underdetermined—maybe both positivism and natural law can explain why judges act this way for differnt reasons—or if there's a hidden commitment I'm missing. Could a natural-law theorist accept this setup as long as the procedural checks are there, or does it force them to reject the algorithm's authority entirely? Also, is there any real gap between saying the rule is "legally valid but unjust" versus "not law because it violates the internal morality of lawmaking"? I feel like the courts are doing something that looks Hartian on the surface but might smuggle in Fuller's demands for coherence and reciprocity without admitting it.
