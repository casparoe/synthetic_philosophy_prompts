After several weeks of unusually vivid lucid dreams that persisted into my waking judgments—so that I genuinely paused upon opening my eyes to ask whether the people in my kitchen were continuous with figures I had just interacted with in sleep—I have found Zhuangzi’s butterfly dream impossible to treat as a quaint ancient parable, and I need to understand whether the skepticism it expresses can be made to bite in practice rather than dismissed as a verbal trick. Specifically, I am struck by what I will call the problem of intersubjective awakening: if I conclude that I have woken up, I still seem unable to verify that anyone else around me has also done so, since their testimony that they are awake could always belong to my dream narrative rather than to an independent shared reality. I teach a philosophy seminar of about twelve advanced students in a single ninety-minute session, and I want an in-class game or structured role-play that makes them run into this failure themselves before it is named. Design clear rules, assigned roles, and a payoff scheme using only small non-monetary stakes—points, speaking privileges, or symbolic tokens—arranged so that participants first invent and attempt a verification protocol meant to establish common knowledge of shared wakefulness, and then discover that every proposed check either collapses into circularity or remains locked inside private experience that cannot be shared across subjects. The participants should come away seeing that mutual knowledge of awakening is structurally unattainable without an external anchor that neither party can access from within the dream, and that this transforms Zhuangzi’s puzzle from a private doubt into a shared coordination problem. Include a debrief that maps the precise moments of breakdown in the activity onto Zhuangzi’s original concern and onto the modern question of whether a private language about one’s own mental state could ever serve as a bridge to others; be frank about which feature of dream skepticism the game genuinely reproduces and which feature I as facilitator must supply because no classroom simulation can recreate it. Please also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this, especially if students treat the roles as theatrical rather than epistemic, and how to recover without breaking the experience. Present everything in continuous prose only, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, as one might write in a detailed letter.
