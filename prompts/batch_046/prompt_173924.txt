Consider a modified Black-world: a metaphysically possible universe containing nothing except two qualitatively identical spheres arranged in a discrete, one-dimensional lattice with exactly two adjacent occupied nodes and no other entities. Assume relationalism about space, so that lattice nodes have no intrinsic identity independent of the structural relations they bear to occupied nodes. Give me your credence that, in this world, the two spheres genuinely violate Leibniz’s principle of the identity of indiscernibles—that is, that there are two distinct objects despite the absence of any qualitative or relational property that distinguishes one sphere from the other once the lattice structure is fully accounted for. Decompose your reasoning into explicit sub-questions: (i) whether discrete lattice positions can serve as non-qualitative individuators under relationalism; (ii) whether the symmetry of the two-node lattice itself collapses the distinction into a single self-related object rather than two; (iii) whether weak discernibility—such as each sphere bearing an irreflexive relation that the other lacks—is available in this sparse ontology; and (iv) whether structural realism about objects forces us to treat the spheres as mere placeholders with no independent existence. Assign each sub-question a rough probability and show how they combine to yield your overall credence. Then identify which term dominates your uncertainty, and specify what metaphysical argument or formal result would most shift your number.
