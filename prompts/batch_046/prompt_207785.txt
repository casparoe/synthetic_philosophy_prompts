Consider the problem of ontology identification under conditions of permanent epistemic inaccessibility—cases where the information required for direct identification of an entity is not merely unknown but structurally unavailable to observers, yet the entity must still be individuated within our ontology. Standard approaches rely either on causal-theoretic tracking or on theoretical-role functionalism, but both presuppose some bridge between identifying conditions and the entity itself. When that bridge is broken by design—whether in interpretations of quantum mechanics that posit permanently hidden variables, in mental ontology where first-person access is constitutively partial, or in historical ontology where records are irretrievably lost—identification must shift to indirect signatures.

Within this space, distinguish two strategies that have received uneven philosophical attention. The first, proxy identification, individuates entities through stable correlational structures: we identify X not by accessing X, but by mapping the invariant patterns in what X systematically transforms or excludes within domains that remain accessible. The second, residue identification, treats the informational absence itself as constitutive of the entity’s ontological boundary: X is defined in part by the specific shape of the gap it produces in our epistemic access, so that identifying X requires precisely characterizing what remains permanently opaque about it rather than treating that opacity as a temporary deficit.
