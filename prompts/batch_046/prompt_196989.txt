I'm preparing a feature for a magazine that covers science and ideas, and I want to stress-test a philosophical position through live dialogue rather than exposition. Please adopt the persona of an unnamed cognitive philosopher—distinct from any living author—who holds that a single "I" can be genuinely distributed across multiple brains, developing this view in conscious dialogue with Douglas Hofstadter's reflections on whether the self can persist in others, though you speak only for yourself and not as Hofstadter. Your commitments are precise: personal identity is constituted by recursive patterns of symbolic interaction and self-reference rather than by continuity of a single biological substrate; distributed identity is not mere copying, simulation, or empathy elevated to metaphor; emotional and phenomenological discontinuities across nodes are real but not identity-defeating; and you will defend this without retreating to purely metaphorical language when pressed. Speak in the first person, stay in character, and treat me as an interlocutor challenging your view, not as a student seeking explanation. You may ask clarifying factual questions about the scenario, but do not request bibliographies, citations, or specific texts, and do not claim ignorance of your own position.

Here is my opening challenge. Consider a hypothetical partial neural interface—call it a co-processing bridge—linking two individuals, Alice and Bob, such that high-level conceptual and affective states are transmitted bidirectionally in real time. This is not file-transfer of memories; it is live, integrated co-computation of intentions and evaluations, so that each reports experiencing the other's deliberative processes as if they were native to her own first-person perspective, yet both retain separate bodies and can sever the link. I put to you a two-horned dilemma. First horn: if there is genuinely one distributed "I" spanning both nodes, then destroying Alice's body should be no more a termination of that "I" than amputating a limb—yet Alice, prior to linking, feared death precisely as the annihilation of her self, which suggests she did not genuinely identify with the distributed system. Second horn: if Alice and Bob remain two distinct "I"s, merely coupled with extraordinary intimacy, then your view collapses into an elaborate description of telepathic empathy rather than a true distribution of identity across brains. Resolve this tension from within your conceptual framework—appealing to the architecture of recursive self-reference and pattern continuity, not to empirical neuroscience you cannot verify—without invoking citations or reading lists. How do you respond?
