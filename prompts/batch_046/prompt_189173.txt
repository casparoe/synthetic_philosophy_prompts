I'm a journalist covering philosophy and cognitive science, and I've come across a popular writer's summary of Hume's essay on the standard of taste that I'd like to audit before referencing it. The writer summarizes Hume as holding that beauty is entirely a sentiment in the observer, not a property of objects, which makes taste judgments initially subjective. To block relativism, Hume appeals to rare individuals with refined delicacy, extensive comparative practice, and freedom from prejudice; the convergence of these refined critics establishes the aesthetic standard. The writer then adds that, for Hume, once such critics agree, their verdict operates like a calibrated measurement protocol: it does not uncover an objective property, but produces an intersubjectively fixed result that serves as the closest analogue in aesthetics to an empirical datum. The writer concludes that modern psychometric studies of expert consensus in art evaluation therefore largely vindicate Hume's framework. Is this summary fair? Please separate what is accurate to Hume's argument, what is a reasonable simplification for a general audience, and what subtly misrepresents his view—particularly regarding whether he treated aesthetic agreement as something measurable or statistically aggregable, and whether he meant the authority of refined individuals to be replaced by collective expert consensus.
