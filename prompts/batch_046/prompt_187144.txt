I have recently taken on a governance role in a cooperative where members face genuinely non-repeated, anonymous allocation decisions—situations that appear structurally identical to a one-shot prisoner's dilemma. Having studied Axelrod's tournaments and the evolutionary dynamics of the iterated prisoner's dilemma, I have come to treat cooperation as evolutionarily stable only under conditions of sufficient repetition and a non-negligible shadow of the future, with strategies like tit-for-tat depending essentially on anticipated future interaction. Consequently, I have concluded that in our one-shot allocations any cooperative behavior must be either irrational or explicable only through hidden repetition, misidentified partners, or an implicit reputation mechanism that effectively transforms the interaction into an iterated one. Yet I am troubled by evidence of stable cooperation in anonymous one-shot laboratory settings with no feedback or repetition, which seems to resist this explanation. Does the evolutionary account of cooperation actually entail that anonymous one-shot cooperation is necessarily irrational or artifactually driven by concealed iteration, or am I misapplying the conditions for evolutionary stability? Specifically, if mechanisms such as indirect reciprocity with image scoring or partner-choice dynamics can sustain cooperation without direct repetition, do they falsify the claim that the shadow of the future is a necessary condition for rational cooperation, or do they instead indicate that the prisoner's dilemma framing itself collapses in those contexts?
