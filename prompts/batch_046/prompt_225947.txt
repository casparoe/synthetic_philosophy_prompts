Since my mother was recently evaluated for entry into an experimental treatment programme, I have been sitting in on consultations with the clinical ethics team. During one session, a sharp disagreement broke out between two senior members — I'll call them Sarah and David — over whether the consent she had already signed was genuinely valid. Their exchange went as follows, and I should be grateful if you could adjudicate it point by point.

(1) Sarah began by insisting that the consent was valid because every explicit statement made to my mother was factually correct. The researcher had described all known physical risks, explained the procedure clearly, and confirmed that participation was voluntary. Deception, she argued, requires the utterance of a falsehood; merely omitting that the principal investigator holds shares in the company manufacturing the compound is not a lie, and since that financial interest does not alter the biological risk profile, it is not material information for the purpose of consent.

(2) David replied that this view is unduly narrow. The point of informed consent is not merely to avoid false sentences but to place the patient in a position to make an autonomous decision based on an accurate overall picture. By withholding the conflict of interest, the researcher shaped my mother's trust in the risk assessment without technically lying. The false overall impression created is precisely what deception aims at, and here it was achieved through strategic omission. Consequently, the epistemic conditions necessary for valid consent were undermined.

(3) Sarah countered that, even if omission can constitute deception, one must still demonstrate that the omitted fact was material. My mother's decision turned on whether she wished to undergo the treatment, not on the researcher's private motives. If she would have consented regardless of knowing about the shareholding, then the omission made no difference to her autonomy. Without evidence that the omission actually altered her decision, calling the fact material is mere speculation.

(4) David answered that materiality should not depend on a counterfactual about whether she would have changed her mind — otherwise we would need to read minds in order to validate consent. A fact is material if a reasonable person in the patient's position would regard it as relevant to evaluating the recommendation being made. A direct equity stake clearly satisfies that standard. Furthermore, he noted that my mother had been experiencing mild cognitive fluctuation on the morning of signing: she could accurately repeat the procedural steps, yet struggled to hold in mind the complex probabilistic reasoning about long-term risk trade-offs. Thus, her competence was also questionable, quite apart from the deception issue.

(5) Sarah responded that competence is task-specific, not a demand for philosophical sophistication. My mother demonstrated adequate understanding of the procedure, its purpose, and its principal risks, and she expressed a consistent, stable preference. By David's standard, anyone who is anxious or mildly confused could not consent, which would bring clinical practice to a standstill. Both the conflict of interest and the cognitive state, she concluded, were being inflated in order to invalidate a consent that was, in practice, sufficiently informed and voluntary.

(6) David concluded that he was not proposing that anxiety invalidates consent, but that there exists a threshold below which understanding is too thin to ground autonomy. Merely parroting steps is not the same as grasping implications. And regarding the conflict: Sarah was treating consent as an isolated transaction between atomised individuals, whereas it is embedded in institutional relationships of trust. Concealing a direct financial stake corrupts that relational context, rendering the consent invalid in a structural sense even if the patient would not have withdrawn.

Please go through their exchange move by move: rate each of the six moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right, briefly justify each rating, and say who is more right overall and what — if anything — both are missing.
