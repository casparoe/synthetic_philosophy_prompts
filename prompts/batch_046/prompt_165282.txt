I was trying to formilize Keynes's view of the good life from that old 1930s essay where he predicts we'll all work about 15 hours a week once technology solves scarcity, and now I have an argument that sounds like it turns him into a leisure-mandate authoritarian. Here goes:

(1) Keynes distinguishes absolute needs (food, shelter) from relative needs (status, being richer than your neighbor), and he says relative needs are insatiable and morally worthless once absolute needs are met.

(2) He also predicts that in about a hundred years technology will solve the economic problem, leaving people with maybe 15 hours of necessary labor per week and the rest for the good life — art, friendship, contemplation.

(3) Now, if someone's absolute needs are already satisfied, then any extra labor they perform must be serving relative needs, since theres no absolute need left to drive them.

(4) But serving relative needs is, by Keynes's own lights, a kind of moral failure — he literally calls the love of money a disgusting morbid craving.

(5) So if you're in a post-scarcity society and you choose to work 60 hours running a company, Keynes's framework classifies your choice as a moral failure driven by insatiable status-seeking.

(6) However, if a choice is a moral failure driven by an insatiable craving, society is not just permitted but arguably obligated to structure incentives so people can't easily indulge it — just like we tax cigarettes or limit gambling.

(7) Therefore Keynes's view of the good life implies that an abundant society should impose a hard cap on paid work, maybe 15 hours, to protect people from their own morbid relative cravings.

(8) But Keynes clearly wasn't arguing for state-mandated leisure quotas; he thought people would naturally prefer the good life. So the conclusion is absurd, but I can't see which premise to kill without damaging the rest.

I'd rather keep (1) and (4) since they sound like real Keynes, but I'm willing to pay for whichever step is the leak. Which one do I reject, and what does that cost me?
