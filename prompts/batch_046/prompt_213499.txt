Having spent thirty years in experimental physics maintaining calibration protocols and measurement standards, I have recently turned to philosophy of language and found myself struck by the longevity of the debate surrounding Kripkenstein’s skeptical paradox about rule-following. Since the early 1980s, the literature has crystallized into two familiar camps: those pursuing a “straight” solution by identifying some natural fact—dispositional, causal, or communal—that fixes correct application of a rule like addition, and those accepting the skeptical conclusion that no such fact exists but defending a “skeptical” solution in terms of communal assertibility conditions or primitive normative practices. Despite decades of refinement—objections to dispositionalism regarding error, finitude, and normativity; replies that the skeptical solution changes the subject from meaning to use—the disagreement strikes me as entirely static. Every move is well catalogued; neither side seems surprised by the other.

My working hypothesis, shaped perhaps too heavily by my scientific background, is that the debate has stalled because both camps implicitly model rule-following on either an internal mental representation or a collective social convention, while neglecting a third mode familiar to laboratory practice: rule-following as an embodied, error-correcting activity mediated by material protocols that are neither purely psychological nor purely sociological. In measurement science, a calibration rule is not “in the head” of the technician, nor is it a democratic vote of the laboratory; it is sustained through iterative comparison with reference standards, documented drift corrections, and institutional feedback loops that carry normative force precisely because they are tied to predictive success. If philosophical discussions of “plus” and “quus” systematically ignore this instrumental, practice-embedded structure—where normativity and descriptive correction are inseparable—might the disagreement be partly verbal, with each side attacking a false dichotomy? Or is it substantive: a genuine dispute about whether normative facts can be reduced to descriptive ones, masked by shared but ambiguous language?

I want an honest diagnosis rather than confirmation of my own analogy. Is this impasse a case of two groups answering different questions—one semantic, one metaphysical—under the single heading “rule-following”? Do the sides differ about which phenomena count as data, with one treating ordinary talk about “meaning addition” as the explanandum and the other treating it as a surface feature to be explained away? Is there a hidden empirical question about human cognition or social coordination that neither side has made explicit? Or is the stall a sign that the original question is ill-posed, presupposing a model of mental content that scientific practice itself should lead us to abandon?

Given whatever diagnosis you offer, I also want to know what would genuinely count as progress. Would it require a formal result demonstrating the irreducibility of normativity, a conceptual distinction that dissolves the paradox without solving it, an empirical finding about how humans actually correct errors in rule-governed behavior, or a change of subject away from “what fact makes me mean addition?” And if none of these could resolve it, is that because the disagreement ultimately rests on bedrock intuitions about explanation that neither side can argue for?
