Next week I present at an interdisciplinary workshop on death and the self—half the room are specialists in pre-Qin texts who read Zhuangzi against the grain of later Daoist institutionalization, and half are analytic philosophers of death and personal identity who treat “Eastern consolation narratives” with suspicion. I am defending a deliberately narrow thesis: that in the inner-chapter discussions surrounding the well-known episode of Zhuangzi’s response to his wife’s death—where he is found singing and drumming on a tub rather than mourning—the text is not offering a metaphysical doctrine of peaceful transformation (the “butterfly” or “seasons” model in which the self merely changes form), but is instead performing what I call an indexical un-anchoring. By this I mean that the emotional logic of bereavement depends not on diachronic continuity of the other—whether she survives as soil, wind, or qi—but on the mourner’s present first-person standpoint being stably indexed to her absence; Zhuangzi’s practice is meant to dissolve that indexical relation itself, not to deny that something has changed. This makes his position closer to a practical phenomenological suspension than to either annihilationism or survivalism, but it also means his view is not “comforting” in any ordinary sense.

The audience divides into three camps I need to prepare for. The textual scholars will ask whether I am imposing an anachronistic phenomenological vocabulary onto narratives that are better read as rhetorical paradox or biographical legend; they care about whether the tub-drumming episode belongs to the philosophical core or to a later hagiographic layer, and they will press me on whether “indexical anchoring” is recoverable from the grammar of the passage without importing modern philosophy of mind. The analytic philosophers will want to know whether my “indexical un-anchoring” is distinguishable from the familiar “no-subject” argument against the badness of death—if there is no stable subject to be harmed by absence, then grief is irrational for structural reasons, and Zhuangzi is just giving this a poetic costume; they will also ask whether my reading makes Zhuangzi’s view indistinguishable from a form of dissociative denial rather than a reasoned stance. A smaller group of comparative philosophers will ask whether I am smuggling in Madhyamaka-style emptiness talk under a new name, since “dissolving the boundary between mourner and mourned” sounds like a denial of svabhāva applied to personal relations.

Please prepare me as if for the Q&A: list the objections in the order they are most likely to land and in the voice of the camp that raises them, indicate for each whether I should concede ground, hold firm, or partially qualify, and flag the reply that is weakest in my own position—the point I should prepare to defend actively rather than hope they overlook. Specifically, I need to know what the textual scholars will say about anachronism and narrative genre, what the analytic philosophers will say about the “no-subject” reduction and about clinical dissociation, and whether there is an objection from the comparative side that collapses my view into Nāgārjuna’s tetralemma in a damaging way. Include the one question I am hoping nobody asks—probably about whether this reading makes grief itself always a kind of metaphysical error, with troubling implications for how we evaluate mourning practices cross-culturally—and tell me exactly how to answer it without sounding either callous or evasive, given that I have about ninety seconds in the session. I do not need bibliographies or citations; I need the objections phrased the way these people actually talk, with the conceptual pressure points exposed.
