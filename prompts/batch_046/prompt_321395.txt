Here's an argument I sketched for a grad seminar on transformative choice and now it feels like it eats itself. (1) If an experience is genuinely transformative in Paul's sense, then teh preferences you'll have afterwards are epistemically closed to you now—no first-person simulation is possible. (2) A rational agent should only endorse actions that maximize expected value relative to her current preference ordering, since otherwise she's just guessing at what 'she' wants. (3) So undergoing a transformative experience for its intrinsic value can never be rationally justified; you can only do it for instrumental reasons you already understand. (4) Now apply the same logic diachronically: your current preferences are themselves the output of past transformations—education, trauma, religious conversion, whatever—that your earlier self didn't rationally authorize. (5) If (2) is right, those past transformations robbed your earlier self of rational authority, which means your present preferences inherit that defect; they lack rational pedigree. (6) But then (2) also undermines itself, because it tells you to defer to preferences that are themselves rationally tainted. (7) The only consistent escape is to deny that transformative experiences ever happen—treat all value-change as incremental updating within a stable core—but that's empiricly false and kind of insulting to anyone who's actually changed. (8) So we seem stuck: either rational choice is impossible across transformation, or transformation never really occurs. I want to keep (1) and I really don't want to bite the bullet on (7). Which step slips? Is (2) just too narrow, or is there a hidden equivocation between epistemic access and normative authority somewhere in teh move from (4) to (6)? What does fixing it cost?
