I am preparing the question sheet for a recorded colloquium interview with a political theorist who has recently advanced what she terms the "intermittent community" thesis. She contends that both Hobsbawm's framework of invented tradition and Anderson's account of imagined communities overstate the necessity of continuous bureaucratic or print-mediated narrative for sustaining collective identity. In her view, certain stateless and pre-modern groups maintained national consciousness primarily through periodic ritual reconvening—seasonal assemblies, pilgrimage cycles, and oral contract renewals—rather than through the sustained, simultaneous awareness that standard theories assume. I want eight to ten interview questions that treat this thesis as a genuine conceptual alternative rather than an historical curiosity. Please ensure the sequence includes: one that asks how intermittent community differs in kind from weak or episodic nationalism; one that presses her on the Scottish and Catalan cases, where modern movements clearly deploy reconstructed rituals and revived symbolic dress, and appear to depend on precisely the continuous institutional memory she denies; and one that asks what kind of evidence—archival, ethnographic, or negative—could falsify the claim that a given group was formed through intermittent rather than continuous imagination. For each of these three, provide a follow-up keyed to the reply she is most likely to offer, especially the claim that ritual periodicity was itself suppressed or transformed by state-building projects. The remaining questions should explore normative implications: whether intermittent community generates weaker or different claims to self-determination, and whether secessionist demands can be coherently articulated without the bureaucratic apparatus usually associated with modern nationhood. Write in polished British English, frame the questions so that an informed but non-specialist audience can follow the conceptual stakes, and avoid generic biographical or definitional openings. Do not include requests for bibliographies, reading lists, or citation lists.
