I was arguing with my friend over coffee about Ulysses contracts and constitutional precommitment, and he walked me into an argument that seems to prove far too much but I can't find the exit. (1) A Ulysses contract is rational only if the agent who binds herself is numerically the same agent who'd otherwise break the promise later on. (2) In a democracy, "the people" at time t and "the people" fifty years later are not the same collective agent, since membership has turned over almost entirely through births, deaths, migration, and generational replacement. (3) So any constitutional entrenchment — say, requiring a super-majority to amend basic rights — isn't genuinely self-binding; it's one cohort of citizens coerting a different cohort. (4) If democratic legitimacy requires that every group affected by a rule actually consent to it, then all long-term constitutional constraints are illegitimate. (5) But if we drop that consent requirement, then there's no principled barrier to a present majority binding future minorities however they like, which collapses the distinction between constitutionalism and raw majoritarian domination. (6) Therefore constitutional precommitment is either conceptually incoherent or normatively self-defeating.

I feel like there's a hidden slide between treating "the people" as a corporate body with diachronic identity and treating them as a mere aggregate of individuals, but I can't name the exact step. Which premise should I reject, why did it look plausible, and what do the standard positions — diachronic peoplehood, trustee representation, or the binding-successors view — each have to pay to save it?
