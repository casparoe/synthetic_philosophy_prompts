I have been preparing for a seminar on Machiavelli's political thought, and in discussions with fellow students I keep returning to a distinction that feels important but that I cannot place in the existing vocabulary. We all recognize Machiavelli's well-known image of fortune as a river that can be controlled with dikes built in advance—an instance of virtù as specific, anticipatory preparation. Yet I keep noticing a different pattern, both in Machiavelli's own historical examples and in modern analyses of leadership, where the praised quality is not foresight about a particular danger but a kind of structural flexibility: the ability to dissolve and reconstitute alliances, shift institutional roles, or redeploy resources without a pre-existing blueprint for the crisis at hand. When such a leader succeeds, commentators attribute the outcome to an exceptional adaptive capacity; when the same leader fails, the failure is usually explained by fortune being simply too overwhelming. This makes the boundary between the leader's agency and fortune feel oddly outcome-dependent.

I am not asking for a bare label. Rather, I want to know whether political philosophy or Machiavelli scholarship has a specific concept for this non-anticipatory, second-order form of mastery—something that captures the precise carving between genuine adaptive agency and the mere appearance of control produced by fortunate coincidence. How exactly does it differ from Machiavelli's original notion of virtù, where should its edges be drawn so that it does not collapse back into luck, and what would be the clearest way to state the conditions under which we should withhold the attribution altogether?
