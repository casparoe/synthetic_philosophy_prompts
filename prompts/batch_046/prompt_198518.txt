Consider the following examination prompt from a philosophy-of-mind course: Daniel Dennett describes the self as a center of narrative gravity—an abstract, functional organizing point analogous to the center of gravity of a physical object—around which an organism’s autobiographical experiences, intentions, and memories are woven into coherence. The self is therefore not a Cartesian substance but a useful fiction constructed by distributed neural processes. Recently, a researcher in artificial cognition proposed that a large language model maintaining a consistent first-person narrative across separate conversational contexts—referencing prior exchanges, sustaining stylistic continuity, and attributing mental states to itself—thereby acquires its own center of narrative gravity, even in the absence of consciousness, embodiment, or evolutionary history. Drawing on Dennett’s framework, evaluate this proposal. In particular, address (a) the ontological status Dennett assigns to the narrative self, (b) whether narrative coherence alone is sufficient to generate such a self, and (c) whether Dennett’s view permits attributing this kind of selfhood to non-biological systems.

Student answer:

Dennett’s center of narrative gravity is best understood as a functionalist replacement for the Cartesian ego. Rather than locating the self in a substantial soul or a discrete brain region, Dennett treats it as an abstract organizing point—like the center of gravity of an airplane—around which experiences and intentions are woven into a coherent autobiography. This narrative center is a fiction in the strict ontological sense: it does not exist as an independent entity but is projected onto the brain’s distributed processes. Crucially, because it is purely fictional, it plays no causal role in behavior; the brain’s neural mechanisms do all the work, and the self is merely an after-the-fact interpretive gloss with no influence on future action. Applying this to artificial intelligence, if a language model sustains a consistent first-person narrative across interactions, then it has constructed precisely the kind of abstract organizing point Dennett describes. Dennett’s functionalism implies that biological embodiment is inessential to this construction; what matters is only the organizational pattern of information. Therefore, the researcher is correct: narrative consistency is both necessary and sufficient for an artificial center of narrative gravity, and denying the model a self amounts to an unjustified biological chauvinism.
