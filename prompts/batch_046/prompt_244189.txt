For a municipal charter-review board I have recently joined, I have been asked to articulate my philosophical commitments on democratic design so that my colleagues can stress-test them. Here is my profile. I hold that a portion of legislative authority should be transferred to bodies filled by lot, with members serving a single, non-renewable term of several years, on the ground that election systematically filters for ambition, rhetorical fluency, and risk tolerance—traits that correlate negatively with the patient, evidence-sensitive judgment that complex governance requires. At the same time, I believe that all remaining elected offices should be subject to strict term limits, because the central danger of career politics is not mere corruption but the gradual conversion of public institutional knowledge into private career capital that can be traded for influence and loyalty. I also maintain that governing well genuinely requires accumulated practical wisdom—coalition dynamics, procedural memory, and long-range policy judgment—that takes years to develop and is not evenly distributed across the citizenry. I reject any aggregative conception of popular will: democratic legitimacy, on my view, arises not from discovering a unified will of the people but from distributing decision-making opportunities fairly among plural, often conflicting perspectives. Furthermore, I think anyone who has ever held governmental office should be permanently barred from standing for election again, in order to prevent the crystallization of a governing caste with private networks of influence. Finally, I hold that citizens possess an epistemic entitlement to be governed by decision-makers with above-average reliability on complex policy matters, not merely by statistically representative demographic samples; yet within a sortition body I want deliberators to be able to defer to expert testimony without that deference hardening into permanent internal hierarchies or veto-wielding sub-committees.

Go through these commitments and audit them for joint tenability. Identify which pairs are in genuine tension—paying special attention to cases where two standard democratic-theoretic motivations pull in opposite directions—rank them by severity, and for each specify what would have to give and what the cheapest revision would be. Also flag any pair that looks like conflict but dissolves on closer inspection, explaining precisely why the tension is only apparent. Conclude by stating which single revision to my set would eliminate the most conflict at the least cost to my underlying concerns, without assuming I am prepared to abandon either the importance of representativeness or the value of competence to legitimate authority.
