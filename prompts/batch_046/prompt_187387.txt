I recently heard a speaker summarize Benjamin Franklin’s project of moral perfection in his autobiography. The speaker claimed that Franklin treated virtue as a kind of engineering problem: he believed human character is essentially malleable material that can be shaped into perfection through disciplined self-surveillance, so he constructed his list of thirteen virtues as an exhaustive moral taxonomy and tracked his daily compliance in a ledger. The speaker said Franklin abandoned the project only after realizing that turning morality into a quantified scorecard creates a split self—an observer and an observed—which makes virtue into mere performance rather than genuine character. From this, the speaker concluded that Franklin’s failure demonstrates a general philosophical point: any systematic attempt to rationalize ethics, whether through Enlightenment self-improvement or modern behavioral tracking, necessarily collapses into hypocrisy, and that Franklin’s true legacy is the proof that virtue must remain spontaneous and unmeasured.

How much of this characterization is accurate? Which parts reflect Franklin’s actual reasoning, which are modern projections onto his text, and does the speaker’s broader conclusion about systematized ethics follow from what Franklin actually wrote? I am particularly unsure whether Franklin really saw his list as exhaustive, whether his abandonment was driven by this psychological split rather than other reasons, and whether he intended his failure as a general philosophical argument or as something more personal and ironic.
