After a long argument with a friend about whether world history can genuinely be told from everywhere at once without secretly reinstalling a Eurocentric chronology, I need a workshop design for 14 graduate students in a single 90-minute session. Create a structured, runnable game or simulation—not a lecture—in which participants must collaboratively construct one shared timeline from 1200 to 1900 using event-cards that describe the same occurrences (the Mongol expansions, the Haitian Revolution, the Opium Wars, the Berlin Conference) through three incompatible registers: linear-progressive, cyclical-genealogical, and event-archival, each with its own ontology of causation and significance. Add at least two “ghost events” that one register recognizes but the others lack the conceptual framework even to place. The rules should require that adjacent cards share an agreed causal connector; when agreement fails, the card is moved to an exclusion zone with a visible cost (lost narrative continuity or reduced collective score). Participants should feel the pull of a unified chronology and the concrete price of leaving material out before anyone names the problem. Include a debrief that maps specific game moments onto the conceptual issue—especially whether “adding voices” resolves the tension or whether the grammar of narration itself is the obstacle—predict the failure points (e.g., one register dominating by default, ghost events being silently translated rather than excluded), say how to handle them, and state frankly which feature of the idea the mechanics cannot reproduce and the facilitator must supply verbally. Write the answer in American English. Do not ask for bibliographies, citations, or reading lists.
