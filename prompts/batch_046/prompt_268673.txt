I'm designing the final unit for my metaphysics seminar, and I want to move beyond the standard textbook cases—vases, poison, and antidotes—by asking students to treat Oscar Wilde's The Picture of Dorian Gray as an extended, literary-scale model of finkish and masked dispositions. In the novel, Dorian's body appears to retain every natural disposition to age, scar, and corrupt physically, yet these manifestations are systematically intercepted and redirected into the portrait. Read the novel through this precise metaphysical lens: does the painting operate as a fink, breaking the causal chain that would ordinarily link Dorian's underlying state to its manifestation, or does it operate as a mask, allowing the manifestation to occur but concealing it in an unexpected substrate? Focus particularly on the moment after Basil's death when Dorian first observes the portrait's alteration, and on the climactic destruction of the canvas—when the substrate is destroyed, the accumulated manifestations suddenly appear in Dorian himself. Does this ending support the view that the dispositions were genuinely present in Dorian all along, merely masked, or does it suggest that outsourcing manifestation to an external object eventually dissolves the original attribution?

To prevent the reading from becoming a mere allegory, I want the analysis to take the metaphysical distinction seriously in both directions. Consider whether Wilde implicitly distinguishes physical dispositions—those redirected into the portrait—from moral dispositions, which seem to manifest normally in Dorian's social cruelty and vanity despite his unchanging appearance. Is there a coherent metaphysics on which one class of dispositions can be finked while another remains fully manifest in the same subject? And does the novel's resolution—where destroying the portrait restores the physical manifestations—vindicate a dispositional essentialist claim that these properties were never truly absent from Dorian, or does it instead show that treating dispositions as portable across substrates undermines the very idea of a stable bearer? I'm writing this for advanced undergraduates who have encountered the basic literature on dispositions but need practice applying it to narrative structure without reducing the work to a philosophical illustration; I want the reading to say what the novel adds to the debate that a formal argument about finks and masks alone cannot capture, and to identify at least one scene where the text resists the metaphysical reading and an honest critic must concede the mismatch.
