I'm preparing a long-form feature on how grand narratives of human history survive their empirical critics, and I've been discussing the problem with colleagues who cover archaeology and anthropology over the past several weeks. The familiar arc — foragers yielding to farmers yielding to states, as narrated in Yuval Noah Harari's Sapiens and, with a different causal emphasis, in Jared Diamond's Guns, Germs, and Steel — has been sharply challenged by David Graeber and David Wengrow in The Dawn of Everything, who argue that what is usually called the Agricultural Revolution was not a singular, irreversible threshold but rather a diffuse, millennia-long field of experiments in which people moved seasonally between cultivated and wild resources, and between temporary hierarchy and egalitarian organization, without locking themselves into permanent inequality or private property. I want to close my research notes with a poem that takes this specific revision seriously: not a generic observation that history is more complex, but the precise idea that domestication was often reversible, practiced as a seasonal option rather than a one-way door into civilization.

Please write a villanelle — nineteen lines, five tercets followed by a quatrain, with two refrains alternating according to the A1bA2 scheme — in which one refrain captures the macrohistorical claim that large-scale cooperation requires shared fictions and an irreversible scaling-up of social order, while the other refrain captures the counter-claim that societies oscillated between organizational modes without ever committing to a permanent structural shift. The form should do the conceptual work: the two refrains should keep returning in altered contexts across the tercets, and in the final couplet they should sit side by side without merging or resolving, so that the reader feels the two historiographical positions passing each other rather than reaching synthesis. The language should be accurate enough that someone familiar with the archaeological debate can identify which line belongs to which tradition — for instance, distinguishing between the language of cognitive revolution and shared myth on one side, and of seasonal dualism and reversible cultivation on the other — but it should remain poetic rather than expository. Do not resolve the tension in favor of either view; leave it open, as our discussion did. Present the poem in continuous lines without headings, bullet points, bold annotations, or tables; if you include any framing commentary, write it as continuous prose in the manner of an email or letter.
