At a study group, Marcus and Elena argued about whether consequentialism’s demandingness can be solved by treating moral obligations as structurally distributed. Marcus said the theory does not ask each person to solve global poverty alone; if N agents share a collective burden of X, each owes only X/N, so the demand is modest—like paying taxes, not heroic sacrifice. Elena replied that this assumes perfect coordination, which never exists: in reality each agent faces the full undivided burden with no guarantee others contribute, so “distributed demandingness” is a theoretical fiction that ignores the actual bill each person receives. Marcus answered that even individually, consequentialism only requires giving until further donation causes comparable harm to oneself, which rules out life-ruining sacrifice; the “life-ruining” scenarios are edge cases. Elena countered that consequentialism provides no principled threshold for “comparable harm,” leaving agents in perpetual moral audit—every unspent dollar is a debt, every leisure hour a failure—and that this open-ended accounting, with no bankruptcy clause, is itself a ruinous burden that undermines the stable life projects the theory should protect. Marcus concluded that all moral theories require accounting, and consequentialism is merely honest about costs; people reject it not because it is too demanding but because they prefer theories that excuse them. Elena closed by saying that if adopting a theory systematically destroys the goods that make life worth living, rational agents should reject it—not out of weakness, but because a theory that undermines its own prerequisites is incoherent.

Please adjudicate their exchange move by move: assess Marcus’s opening claim about structural distribution, Elena’s coordination objection, Marcus’s comparable-harm threshold, Elena’s audit-and-bankruptcy reply, Marcus’s comparison to other moral theories, and Elena’s incoherence charge. For each, say whether it is clearly right, partly right, or clearly mistaken, with brief justification. Then identify what both are missing—particularly whether there is a principled way to set the threshold Marcus needs, or whether Elena’s incoherence claim overreaches. Lead with your bottom-line verdict in the first sentence, then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
