I am a high school senior enrolled in an independent-study philosophy seminar that meets twice weekly after school, and following a lengthy discussion with my study partner about whether empirical psychology has genuinely undermined the idea of moral character, I have decided to design a formal exam question for my instructor's portfolio review. The question should address virtue ethics and the situationist critique, but I do not want it to be answerable by merely reciting the standard summary of global-trait objections and virtue-ethical responses; instead, I want it to work at a more granular conceptual level by introducing a framing device we have been developing in our sessions, which I call "dispositional density." By this I mean the claim that a virtue such as compassion or honesty is not a single cross-situational trait but a tightly integrated cluster of context-sensitive response patterns that only manifest when several situational parameters—such as social proximity, time pressure, and perceived stakes—align simultaneously. The exam should ask students to evaluate whether this density model genuinely answers the situationist challenge that behavioral consistency across situations is too low to support global trait attribution, or whether it merely relocates the problem to the circular task of defining which responses belong inside the cluster without already presupposing the virtue in question. Please provide the exam question itself, followed by a model answer of approximately 500 to 600 words and a marking scheme totaling 20 marks that assigns specific point values to particular argumentative moves—such as explaining the circularity risk in cluster-definition, identifying the empirical burden the density model inherits regarding multi-cue alignment, assessing whether predictive accuracy actually improves, and addressing whether the model collapses back into a form of situationism. In both the model answer and the marking scheme, please take a committed, definite position: defend an explicit verdict on whether dispositional density succeeds in rescuing virtue ethics from the situationist critique, support that verdict with clear reasoning rather than surveying options neutrally, and in the scheme penalize answers that retreat into indecision or that misrepresent the defended view.
