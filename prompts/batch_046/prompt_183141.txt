Consider the practice in several common-law jurisdictions where forensic psychiatrists evaluate whether a defendant who has suffered severe dissociative trauma or prolonged psychotic episodes remains legally identical to the person who allegedly committed the offense. These assessments often hinge on whether the defendant can construct a coherent autobiographical narrative linking prior intentions to present mental states, rather than relying on bodily continuity or bare psychological similarity. At the same time, the same legal systems determine corporate continuity through charter and asset succession, and withhold personhood from non-human animals regardless of any narrative capacity they might display. What philosophical commitments about personal identity does this cluster of practices presuppose? Does it treat the self as a center of narrative gravity—an abstract organizational point around which actions and experiences are arranged—or does it quietly depend on a substance-based ontology? Reconstruct the implicit theory, distinguish what the narrative criterion strictly requires from what it merely accommodates, assess whether the practice is consistent with its own treatment of corporate and animal identity, and specify what would have to change in criminal-responsibility doctrine if the narrative model were replaced by a purely biological or purely psychological-continuity account.
