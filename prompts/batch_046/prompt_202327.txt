I am preparing a long-form feature for a science-and-ideas magazine on whether performative utterances can survive the displacement of the human speaker—specifically, whether institutional formulas still do things with words when they are generated by distributed systems rather than by identifiable agents. To ground the philosophical argument, I have been tracking the informal rulings of a working group—philosophers of language and institutional ethicists—who have been evaluating boundary cases without publishing formal criteria. Their verdicts so far form a coherent pattern, but I am not certain whether they are driven by conventional authorization conditions in an Austinian sense, by conditions of mutual intentional uptake in a Searlean sense, or by a purely functionalist criterion that cares only about whether institutional reality is reliably altered within a recognized framework.

Their judgments have been consistent across eight cases. A ship captain with proper authority speaking the naming formula during the ceremonial breaking of the bottle counts as performative; an actor speaking the identical line during a theatrical rehearsal does not. An authorized municipal AI, operating within a pre-approved legal framework and with correct procedural form, declaring a bridge open once safety sensors are satisfied, counts as performative even though no human was present at the moment of utterance; the same system issuing the identical phrase in a public demonstration mode lacking the legal backend does not. A consecrated religious official performing a recognized ritual formula over a live video link to a believer who knowingly accepts the remote form, where the governing institution explicitly recognizes such transmissions, counts as performative; the same formula spoken over video by an unconsecrated individual, even if the institution later retroactively validates the outcome, does not. A corporate AI holding specific contractual authority automatically terminating an account using the prescribed legal formula, in an interaction where the customer clearly understood they were dealing with an automated agent, counts as performative; and the same chatbot using an identical phrase in a standard customer-service interaction that lacks the specific authorization framework does not.

Next week the group will review what I am calling a fragmented performative: a distributed language-model network, with no single identifiable speaker and no human reviewing the final utterance, generates the formula We hereby dissolve this cooperative, where each node contributed only a fragment of the sentence, but the cooperative's governing charter explicitly designates such algorithmic consensus outputs as having binding authority, and every member had previously consented to be bound by them. Predict the group's verdict.
