I am designing a single fifty-minute graduate seminar in philosophy of mind for students who have read Keith Frankish's defense of illusionism but have not worked through Daniel Dennett's heterophenomenology in detail. Rather than walking through the standard zombie or Mary arguments, I want to carry the session with a constructed case: imagine a hypothetical sensory modality—call it structural echolocation—in which organisms navigate complex environments via acoustic reflection and report an accompanying subjective resonance that feels immediately present yet carries no informational content beyond what the representational tracking already delivers. My goal is to use this case to teach the distinction between weak illusionism (introspection misrepresents phenomenal properties as non-representational) and strong illusionism (there simply are no phenomenal properties, only representational states with a particular functional profile), and to show why this position does not collapse into eliminativism about subjective experience or into behaviorism.

Please plan the teaching sequence, not deliver the explanation itself. Specifically:

1. The dependency order: which conceptual distinction must be secured first—access consciousness versus phenomenal consciousness, representational content versus phenomenal character, or the transparency of experience—and why the others depend on it?

2. The carrying case: what precise features must the structural echolocation resonance possess (and what must it lack) to function as the central example? In particular, explain why this constructed modality is preferable here to both the Mary-in-the-black-room scenario and the blindsight comparison, given the specific misconception each of those tends to trigger.

3. The misconception map: identify the three misconceptions this audience is most likely to form (for instance, that illusionism denies the existence of subjective reports, that the resonance is reducible to an emotional tag, or that representationalism necessarily eliminates first-person authority). For each, specify exactly where in the sequence it should be anticipated and defused.

4. The check for understanding: provide one targeted question I can ask at the end that would reveal whether students genuinely grasp why illusionism about phenomenal consciousness is compatible with the reality of conscious access and introspective belief, rather than revealing only that they can repeat the slogan.

5. The exclusions: state explicitly what should be left out of this session—whether particular neuroscientific data, metaphysical debates about physicalism, or historical references—and justify each omission in terms of the session's specific pedagogical target.
