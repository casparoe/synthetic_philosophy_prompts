I have noticed a recurring configuration in metaphysical debates about dispositions—particularly in the literature on finks and antidotes—that I cannot match to any established term, and I want to know whether it has been carved out as its own kind of case. Consider an object that genuinely possesses a disposition D (for instance, solubility in water, or fragility under impact), but that also possesses a second-order disposition D* that is triggered by the very stimulus conditions that would manifest D. When the stimulus arrives, D is not removed or lost; rather, its manifestation is concurrently blocked by the manifestation of D*, where D* is part of the same object’s own dispositional profile. A concrete instance: a compound that would dissolve in water except that contact with water immediately activates its tendency to form a protective polymer shell; the polymerization is not an externally applied antidote, nor is it a permanent structural feature present independently of the water, but a conditional response that belongs to the compound itself and is elicited precisely by the conditions for dissolution. Another instance might be a material that is brittle, but whose being struck hard triggers an internal crystalline rearrangement that absorbs the impact—again, the protective change is not an outside fink but an intrinsic concurrent manifestation. This seems different from a standard mask, where an independent factor removes or suppresses the disposition, and different from a Lewis-style fink, where the disposition itself is lost upon the arrival of the stimulus. Here the disposition remains intact throughout, yet its exercise is systematically preempted by a co-triggered rival disposition of the same subject. Is there an established label for this—perhaps “intrinsic mask,” “dispositional interference,” or “self-antidoting disposition”? How should it be distinguished precisely from Martin-style antidote cases, from finked dispositions, and from accounts that treat all such conflicts as revealing that the original disposition was never genuinely present? If the literature offers no dedicated term, what name and boundary conditions would best capture the phenomenon, and which canonical examples—chemical, biological, or structural—should serve as the fixed points for defining it? I am not looking for a bare label; I want to know exactly where the edges lie, whether the case is genuinely distinct, and what theoretical commitments follow from treating it as its own category.
