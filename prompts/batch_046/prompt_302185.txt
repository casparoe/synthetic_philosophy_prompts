I have been trying to formalize an evolutionary-debunking argument about cultural belief, but it ends up swallowing rationality whole, and I cannot locate the leak.

(1) If the Darwinian theory of culture is correct, then beliefs spread primarily according to replicative fitness—fidelity, fecundity, and longevity—rather than according to epistemic merit.

(2) Human cognitive architecture is itself a product of gene-culture coevolution, meaning the very mechanisms we use to evaluate beliefs (attention, emotional tagging, social credibility heuristics) have been selected for their susceptibility to high-fitness memes, not for truth-tracking.

(3) Therefore, when an individual engages in what she takes to be rational reflection, she is running software that was optimized to propagate memes.

(4) Now consider the specific meme-complex we call scientific rationality—norms of evidence, consistency, predictive testing. These norms spread widely not because they are true, but because they form a cooperative memplex that outcompetes rival complexes by producing visible technological success, which functions as a transmission booster.

(5) But if (3) is true, then the belief that scientific rationality is truth-tracking is itself the output of memetically optimized software evaluating a high-fitness meme-complex.

(6) So we have no independent, non-circular access to whether our rational procedures are genuinely truth-tracking; any attempt to check them uses the same infected machinery.

(7) The only consistent response is global skepticism about belief: every belief, including skepticism itself, is probably just a well-adapted replicator.

(8) Yet global skepticism is self-defeating—if believed, it undermines the grounds for believing it—and it is also pragmatically impossible to live.

(9) So the argument proves too much: it starts from a plausible evolutionary account of culture and ends in an unlivable epistemic collapse.

Which step should I reject? I want to keep (1) and (2)—the idea that cultural transmission is partly memetic and that cognition is shaped by it—because they seem well-supported. I also want to preserve the weaker claim that rational reflection is not pure, that it is influenced by evolutionary and cultural history. But I do not want to end in global skepticism. Is the error in (4), treating scientific norms as purely parasitic? Or in (5), the slide from causal origin to epistemic defeat? Or is there a hidden scope shift between (3) and (6) that illegitimately moves from “our machinery is influenced” to “it is entirely unreliable”? If I reject (5) or (6), what do I have to pay—do I need to posit some kind of epistemic escape clause that looks ad hoc?
