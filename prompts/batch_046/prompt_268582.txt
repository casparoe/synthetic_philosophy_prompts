I have been trying to understand, without any formal training, the old British argument about whether criminal law should enforce private morality even when no direct harm is shown. The concrete case that keeps returning to me is the government report in the 1950s that advised decriminalizing private homosexual acts between consenting adults, followed by the well-known exchange between Lord Devlin—who argued that society may use law to defend its shared moral fabric even absent injury to others—and H. L. A. Hart, who insisted that punishment requires harm in roughly the sense Mill described. I would like you to treat this episode—the committee's recommendation, Devlin's public lecture, Hart's reply, and the legislative reform that eventually came in 1967—not merely as legal history but as a live test of legal moralism itself. Specifically: in this particular chain of events, who occupies the position of the "victim" that legal moralism seems to require? Is the committee's appeal to privacy best read as a neutral procedural boundary on state power, or does it smuggle in a substantive moral judgment about which intimate lives count as worthy of protection? Does Devlin's position depend on an empirical prediction that loosening sexual norms will eventually unravel social cohesion, or is it a purely normative claim that collective disgust is by itself sufficient reason for coercion? And what does the eventual change in the law reveal about the theory—does it show that legal moralism was never consistently applied in Britain, or does it suggest that Devlin was describing a moral consensus that had already dissolved by the time Parliament acted? I am also struck by what seems to me a hidden assumption in the debate: that the law either expresses a moral view or suppresses it, with no middle ground. Could there be a distinction between symbolic condemnation and actual punishment that the exchange misses? Please analyze the episode through that lens, mapping precisely who faced which options and what counted as evidence, and say clearly where the theory illuminates the history and where the history breaks or exceeds the theory.
