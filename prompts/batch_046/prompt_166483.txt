I have been thinking about moral blame and free will ever since a casual conversation with friends turned to an essay I encountered—P.F. Strawson’s discussion of reactive attitudes, the emotional responses like resentment and gratitude that we direct at people for what they do. I do not have formal training in philosophy, but the basic idea made sense to me: we hold each other responsible not because we possess some mysterious metaphysical freedom, but because of the ways we relate to one another in ordinary social life. Lately, though, I have been trying to work out the details on my own, and two people in my study group—Maya and Leo—ended up in a long disagreement about exactly when these reactive attitudes are appropriate. They asked me to listen, but they never resolved it, and I want an outside adjudication.

Here is how their argument went, as I understand it. Maya defended what she called a capacity-centered view: a reactive attitude like resentment is warranted only when the person currently has the psychological capacities for moral understanding, emotional responsiveness, and self-control. If a brain injury, severe mental illness, or extreme coercion removes those capacities, then blame is out of place; we should instead take what Strawson called an objective attitude, treating the person as something to be managed rather than as a fellow participant in moral life. Leo largely agreed that capacities matter, but he insisted they are not enough. He introduced what he named the interlocutor continuity condition: for resentment to be genuinely fitting, the harmful action must also belong to an ongoing narrative in which we recognize the agent as the same moral interlocutor over time. If the behavior is radically discontinuous—caused, say, by a sudden unprecedented brain lesion in someone with no prior history—we should withhold blame not merely because capacities are missing, but because the act no longer expresses the kind of sustained selfhood that makes reactive attitudes appropriate. Conversely, Leo argued, if someone has a long-established pattern of harmful conduct and then suffers a temporary impairment, continuity might allow reactive attitudes to persist, since the action still fits the narrative of that particular interlocutor.

Maya pushed back hard. She said Leo was smuggling a metaphysical self back into Strawson’s framework, which was designed precisely to avoid such hidden essences. On her view, continuity is either redundant—if capacities are present, some continuity usually follows—or actively harmful, because it lets us blame people for actions that are genuinely outside their present control just because those actions match an old pattern. She offered two cases. First, a stranger with no history of violence suffers a brief psychotic break and harms someone; here there is little continuity, yet Maya said the right reason to withhold blame is the temporary loss of capacity, not the narrative break. Second, a person with intermittent psychosis is fully lucid at the moment of action; by Leo’s reasoning, prior continuity might license blame even though present capacities are intact, which Maya found clearly wrong. Leo replied that he was not invoking a soul or hidden essence, but describing the actual social practice of blame: reactive attitudes are relational and historical, not inspections of a momentary mental mechanism. He claimed that Maya’s pure capacity view cannot explain why we often treat the same harmful act differently depending on whether it comes from a close partner with a shared history or from an unknown stranger—a difference that, for Leo, reflects continuity rather than any change in present capacities. Please adjudicate their disagreement point by point: is Leo’s continuity condition a legitimate extension of the reactive-attitudes framework, or does it illegitimately reintroduce metaphysics? Is Maya right that capacities alone do all the work? And what, if anything, are both missing—perhaps about the role of community norms, or about how objective attitudes might still include care rather than mere management? Reach a definite conclusion and justify it; do not end on a non-committal note.
