Having spent my career in experimental chemistry and only recently turned to philosophy with any rigor, I find myself dissatisfied with the textbook presentations of the Epicurean argument. I want your own credence—stated as a specific number between 0 and 1—that death is not bad for the one who dies, but I want the reasoning decomposed rather than collapsed into a bare assertion. Please break your judgment into four components: (i) the probability that the 'no subject' objection succeeds given plausible theories of personal identity; (ii) the probability that, should that objection fail, deprivation of future goods is the correct account of death's badness; (iii) the probability that the asymmetry between prenatal and posthumous non-existence genuinely undermines the deprivation account, rather than being dissolved by independent asymmetries in value or concern; and (iv) the probability that a hybrid 'narrative-completion threshold' view—on which death is harmful primarily when it interrupts a life that has achieved minimal integrative autobiographical coherence—better captures our intuitions than either pure Epicureanism or unqualified deprivationism. For each term, provide a rough number and a single line of justification, then combine them into your overall figure.

Please also tell me which of these four terms is the dominant source of uncertainty in your final number, and specify what kind of conceptual argument or empirical finding—say, about personal identity, about the structure of well-being over time, or about the psychology of narrative selfhood—would most substantially shift that term and thus your overall credence. Throughout, distinguish what is relatively settled in the literature—such as the formal structure of the Epicurean challenge—from what remains genuinely contested, and do not present any contested claim, including the decisive force of the asymmetry argument or the philosophical viability of narrative-completion conditions, as though it were the consensus view among specialists.
