I'm working through a comparative neuroscience reading group, and we recently debated whether consciousness is graded continuously across the animal kingdom or appears in discrete evolutionary leaps. I want to defend the discrete-leap view in a specific form: that consciousness requires a global workspace architecture capable of broadcasting integrated sensory-motor information across modality-specific systems, and that this architecture first emerges not gradually but abruptly with the evolution of pallial structures in early vertebrates. On this account, cephalopods and insects, despite sophisticated behavioral repertoires and large neural populations, lack the requisite integrative loop and are therefore not conscious in any meaningful sense; among vertebrates, however, consciousness is present as a binary property that differs only in complexity, not in kind, across fish, birds, and mammals.

What would count against this position? I want the observations, formal results, or conceptual arguments that should genuinely move me—sorted by how decisive they would be—including findings that comparative neuroscientists have arguably already reported. I also want an honest assessment of whether this view can be qualified into unfalsifiability (for instance, by retreating to a claim about "degrees of broadcasting" that applies to every nervous system) and whether such insulation is a defect for a thesis of this kind. Please distinguish what defenders of the discrete-leap account would themselves concede as damaging from what only critics allege, and indicate whether the position currently risks anything empirically concrete or has already been rendered safe by ad hoc adjustments.
