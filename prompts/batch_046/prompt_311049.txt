I'm preparing a long-form feature on whether philosophy retains any practical purchase on working science, and in drafting my own working profile I've assembled a cluster of commitments that feel coherent but may not survive close inspection. Here is where I stand. First, I take Weinberg's ornithology comparison seriously: philosophy of science stands to practicing scientists roughly as ornithology stands to birds—an external taxonomy that describes behavior without improving it. Second, I think Hawking's declaration that philosophy is dead was rhetorical overreach, but I accept the core claim behind it: philosophers do not generate the conceptual frameworks that drive physics, and attempts to legislate scientific method from outside usually misrepresent how discovery actually proceeds. Third, I agree with Feynman's sharper charge that philosophers of science often lack the quantitative intuition required to see why a scientific problem is genuinely hard, which explains why their interventions frequently feel like semantic word-games rather than clarifications. Fourth, I do think there is a narrow service role for philosophy—what I call conceptual hygiene—in emerging interdisciplinary zones, such as clarifying whether information in quantum computing denotes a physical quantity or a statistical measure, or whether selection in evolutionary biology carries residual intentional connotations; yet I want to insist that this work is essentially applied logic and conceptual history, not philosophy proper. Fifth, I hold that if philosophy is to justify any institutional presence near science, it must be evaluated by scientific standards of progress—cumulative convergence, predictive yield, resolvable disagreement—and by that metric it largely fails. Sixth, I also hold that scientific realism, the thesis that mature scientific theories approximate the truth about unobservable entities, is a philosophical commitment that working scientists implicitly rely on, and that anti-realist philosophical arguments, if taken seriously within science, would undermine the motivation for ambitious theoretical work.

These six claims feel like a consistent package: philosophy is useless for generating science except in a non-philosophical service role, should be judged by scientific metrics, and its only genuinely indispensable contribution is a background metaphysical assumption that scientists do not need philosophers to defend. But I suspect there are hidden fractures. For instance, if conceptual hygiene is genuinely useful and requires philosophical training, does that not undermine the ornithology analogy? And if scientific realism is indispensable yet philosophy fails by the scientific metrics I endorse, why does that particular philosophical thesis survive evaluation while others do not? Or is there a contradiction between claiming that philosophers lack quantitative intuition and allowing them to clean up quantitative concepts in physics and biology? I want an audit, not a lecture: go through these commitments pair by pair, flag which pairs are genuinely in tension and which only appear to be, rank the tensions by severity rather than treating them as equally damaging, and for each genuine tension say what the cheapest revision would be and what it costs in terms of the overall profile. Then identify which single revision—giving up or modifying one commitment—would resolve the greatest number of inconsistencies at once. I am not signaling which view I am most attached to; treat the set as a working philosophical profile that needs consistency, not as a sermon that needs defending.
