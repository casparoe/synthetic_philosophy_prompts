I've been turning over a question that sits somewhere between popular history and philosophy, though I have no formal training in either. History books tend to treat plagues, volcanic winters, and long climate shifts as mere backdrop to human drama—wars, treaties, revolutions—while giving all the agency to people. Yet the ash cloud from Tambora in 1815, the spread of plague along trade routes, or a drought that collapses harvests can redirect societies as decisively as any decree. I wonder whether it is legitimate to call these forces "actors" in history, with a real causal power of their own, or whether that language quietly smuggles in an environmental determinism that makes human choice seem illusory.

Please write a sonnet—fourteen lines, roughly in iambic pentametre, with a clear volta around line nine. In the octave, present germs, climate patterns, and volcanic eruptions not as passive scenery but as entities whose effects genuinely reshape human affairs; use concrete imagery, perhaps the 1815 Tambora eruption, the march of plague, or a failed harvest, without giving these forces human intentions or purposes. At the volta, shift to the anxiety that speaking this way risks determinism: if nature writes the conditions, do human decisions become mere epiphenomena? The sestet should hold that tension open rather than resolve it. The closing couplet ought to leave both insights intact—the undeniable power of non-human forces and the persistence of human responsibility—without pretending they fit together neatly.

After the poem, include a brief reflective note: identify exactly where the poem takes liberties with the philosophical concept of agency (for instance, by attributing directional historical effect to entities that lack intention) and where it relies on a contested assumption about determinism and causation. Then state clearly what kind of historical case or philosophical argument would change your mind about whether it is appropriate to speak of germs, climate, and volcanoes as historical actors.
