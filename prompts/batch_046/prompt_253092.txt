Compare Alan Turing’s behavioural criterion for attributing thought—that a machine ought to be deemed intelligent if its verbal outputs are indistinguishable from a human’s—with the stricter “integrated behavioural” criterion, which holds that indistinguishable outputs are insufficient unless they arise from a system causally coupled to its environment in a manner that supports genuine error-correction and adaptive feedback. Focus especially on whether Turing’s original formulation is uniquely vulnerable to the blockhead-style objection (a lookup-table system producing correct answers without understanding), and whether adding the integration requirement rescues behaviourism or merely reintroduces internal-state conditions through the back door. Which criterion offers the more coherent boundary for machine minds, and can either genuinely address the problem of other minds for artificial agents?
