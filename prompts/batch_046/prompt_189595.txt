Below are notes for a short essay. The essay takes up Weber’s thesis in *The Protestant Ethic and the Spirit of Capitalism*, but frames it through a distinction I am tentatively calling “modal scaffolding” versus “efficient causation.” The core claim is that Calvinist predestination and its associated psychological tension over salvation did not function as a direct causal mechanism producing capitalist rationalization; instead, they constituted a normative-modal environment that made capitalist bookkeeping, deferred gratification, and reinvestment the only psychologically sustainable equilibrium for a specific social stratum. I then ask whether this framing saves Weber from the charge of cultural determinism only by collapsing his “elective affinity” (*Wahlverwandtschaft*) into a functionalist selection mechanism he elsewhere rejects. Specifically, I want to examine whether treating values as “switching conditions” that alter the payoff structure of rational choice—without being reducible either to material interests or to brute psychological drives—allows culture to be an irreducible cause, or whether it merely relocates the explanatory burden to an unexamined theory of motivation. Please comment on whether this distinction is conceptually coherent, whether it misreads Weber’s explicit methodological individualism, and whether the “switching conditions” metaphor adds anything beyond standard rational-choice sociology. I am especially interested in whether the argument avoids the problem of explaining with values—that is, whether attributing causal efficacy to a value-complex requires us to treat that complex as itself caused, thereby launching an infinite regress of cultural explanation.
