I'm drafting an essay on Émilie du Châtelet's theory of happiness, especially as developed in her Discourse on Happiness, and I would appreciate critical comments on the interpretive line I'm pursuing. Rather than treating her account as straightforward hedonism or Stoic moderation, I want to argue that her idea of the "great machines of happiness"—the structured passions, studies, and social commitments she describes—operates analogously to her defense of Leibnizian vis viva against Cartesian mechanics. On this reading, she conceives happiness not as a static state of pleasure but as a sustained dynamic equilibrium maintained by active, self-correcting processes: just as living force must continuously express itself to avoid decaying into dead force, the happy agent must continuously deploy regulated illusion and cultivated passion to prevent motivation from collapsing into passive satisfaction. I further suggest that her emphasis on illusion is not merely deceptive self-indulgence but a functional feedback mechanism—similar in structure, though not in scientific content, to a self-regulating system—without which the "machine" stalls and the agent falls into the kind of inertia she associates with misfortune.

My concern is that this framing may impose an anachronistic physicalist vocabulary onto an essentially ethical and psychological treatise. I also worry that by foregrounding the Leibnizian dynamics of her metaphysics, I risk obscuring the explicit debts she owes to Lucretius, Seneca, and Pascal that clearly structure the Discourse. At the same time, I think the analogy might illuminate why she insists that happiness requires robust external scaffolding rather than pure introspective discipline, and why she treats the passions as instruments to be engineered rather than suppressed. Could you comment on whether this physical-ethical analogy is textually defensible, whether it distorts her specific treatment of illusion, and whether the "feedback" framing imports conceptual baggage that her framework cannot accommodate? I would also welcome corrections to the philosophical background I have sketched, particularly regarding the relationship between her natural philosophy and her moral psychology.
