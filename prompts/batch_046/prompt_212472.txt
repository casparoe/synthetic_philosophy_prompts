I am constructing a take-home examination for a graduate seminar in philosophy of mind and aesthetics. The students have encountered Douglas Hofstadter’s Letter Spirit project—specifically its treatment of grid-based letterforms, stylistic variation, and the problem of what makes every instance of an “a” an “a”—but they are not expected to have memorized implementation details or source code. I need an essay prompt (approximately 1,500 words; 48-hour window; worth 40 percent of the final grade) that cannot be answered by summarizing the project and that forces students to take a position on whether letter-identity is anchored in geometric invariants, in relational differences within a stylistic system, or in a normative “spirit” that survives structural inversion.

To keep the question in the weeds rather than a recitation exercise, inject the following hypothetical apparatus: the “Inversion Grid,” a module that generates forms from a base alphabet by systematically reversing every stylistic parameter—swapping open and closed counter-spaces, inverting stroke-weight gradients, reversing the directional dynamics of serifs, and mirroring stress axes—while preserving the topological connectivity of the original letter (e.g., an inverted “a” still has one closed counter and one open stem, but their spatial and weight relations are flipped). Ask students to consider whether outputs from the Inversion Grid are still members of the original letter categories, whether they constitute an “anti-style,” and what this reveals about the ontology of letterforms.

The exam question should require students to distinguish and adjudicate among three positions: (1) the statistical-style view, which identifies a letter with a cluster of weighted feature distributions; (2) the topological-invariant view, which identifies a letter by connectivity and hole-count alone; and (3) the relational-structuralist view, which holds that letter-identity depends on internal relational dynamics (e.g., tension between stem and bowl) that can survive inversion only if the relational logic is preserved rather than negated.

Please provide:
1. The exam question itself, worded for a graduate audience, with clear instructions regarding length, scope, and a prohibition on external research.
2. A model answer of approximately 800 words that demonstrates the specific philosophical moves a top response must make: defining the three positions precisely; applying the Inversion Grid to each; identifying the crux where statistical and structuralist accounts diverge on whether inversion produces a new letter or a distorted same-letter; and defending one view with an argument about normativity or category boundaries.
3. A marking scheme (scored out of 100) that separates A-range answers (85–100) from B-range answers (70–84). For each band, list the specific argumentative moves that must be present (e.g., recognizing that topological identity is insufficient because an inverted “b” and an inverted “d” share connectivity patterns; explaining why anti-style is not merely noise but a systematic transformation) and the specific misconceptions that should cost marks (e.g., conflating style inversion with random distortion, treating Hofstadter’s grid as merely a computer-science implementation rather than a philosophical model of categorization, or assuming that human recognizability is sufficient for letter-identity).

The deliverable should be answerable by a well-prepared student without web access and should not request bibliographies, citation lists, or references to texts beyond the course material.
