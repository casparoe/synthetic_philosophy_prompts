I am attempting to systematize my intuitions about political liberty by testing them against concrete institutional designs. For each case below, my verdict is given; I want to know whether a coherent principle—drawing on distinctions among negative, positive, and republican liberty, or on some other framework—can accommodate all of them.

Case 1. A municipality bans private automobiles from its center, allowing only emergency and transit vehicles. Verdict: permissible, because it eliminates a structural domination of pedestrians rather than imposing arbitrary will.

Case 2. The state automatically enrolls every worker in a retirement fund, with opt-out available through a simple administrative form. Verdict: this enhances positive liberty without violating negative liberty, since the architecture preserves genuine choice.

Case 3. An employer uses an algorithm to assign shifts and ties access to employer-subsidized housing and healthcare to continued employment; quitting is legally permitted, but doing so means immediate loss of shelter and medical coverage. Verdict: this is a form of domination or unfreedom, not a genuinely voluntary contract.

Case 4. A constitutional provision, enforced by an independent tribunal, binds future democratic majorities against privatizing the water supply. Verdict: this expands republican liberty for citizens, despite restricting the negative liberty of future voters.

Case 5. Every adolescent must serve one year in mandatory deliberative assemblies that can veto local zoning decisions; refusal results in exclusion from public university admission. Verdict: unjustified interference, even though it builds participatory capacity.

Case 6. A universal basic income is funded by carbon taxes and granted without any work requirement. Verdict: it clearly expands freedom for the poor, but I feel uneasy about whether it conflicts with the idea that economic reward should track contribution or effort.

What troubles me is the tension between Case 2 and Case 3: both shape choice through structural conditions rather than direct legal prohibition, yet I judge one liberating and the other oppressive. Can a single principle capture all six verdicts? If not, which verdict should be abandoned, and why? Please also state what the resulting principle predicts for a case I have not listed: a society where citizens must periodically justify their consumption patterns to an AI auditor that possesses no legal enforcement authority, but where the published scores determine eligibility for cooperative housing.
