At a philosophy department reading group, two graduate students—Jordan and Sam—fell into a sustained argument over Parfit’s hitchhiker scenario, in which a driver rescues a stranded traveler only on the promise of later payment, yet once safely delivered the traveler has no instrumental reason to pay. Jordan argued that calling the refusal irrational confuses diachronic preference with momentary rationality: at the moment of decision, the rescue is a sunk benefit and the money is a present cost, so paying is straightforwardly suboptimal. Sam replied that this treats the agent as a series of disconnected time-slices, ignoring that rational planning requires treating one’s future actions as predictable; if the traveler anticipates her own defection, the driver never stops, so the ex ante rational choice is to cultivate a disposition to pay. Jordan then introduced an analogy to Kavka’s toxin puzzle—where an agent receives a large reward for sincerely intending to drink a mildly harmful toxin the next day, after which she need not actually drink—to claim that Sam was smuggling in a false parallel: in the toxin case the reward is contingent on the formation of an intention, not on its execution, whereas in the hitchhiker case the benefit (the ride) is already secured regardless of any present mental state. Sam conceded the structural difference but insisted it actually strengthens the point: both cases show that rational agency requires the ability to form intentions that are not optimally executed at the time of action. She then introduced the idea of counterfactual stability—the constraint that a rational plan must be one that the agent’s past and future selves can treat as fixed across counterfactual scenarios—to argue that paying is rational not out of long-run self-interest but out of diachronic coherence. Jordan countered that counterfactual stability is merely a rebranding of concern for one’s reputation with oneself, and that without a causal or evidential link between present refusal and past planning, invoking it collapses into sentimentality rather than rationality. Sam’s final move was that such a link need not be causal but logical: if an agent systematically violates her own prior intentions whenever execution becomes costly, she undermines the very framework that makes planning possible, so honoring the promise is a precondition of agency rather than a sentimental add-on. They turned to me, an observer with no stake in the dispute, and asked for a point-by-point verdict.

Please adjudicate this exchange move by move: evaluate Jordan’s initial time-slice argument, Sam’s ex ante response, Jordan’s Kavka-toxin objection, Sam’s counterfactual-stability reply, Jordan’s charge of rebranding, and Sam’s logical-link defense. For each move, rate it from clearly mistaken to clearly right and briefly justify the rating. Then say precisely who is right about which specific claim—whether the hitchhiker demonstrates rational irrationality, whether the Kavka analogy holds, and whether counterfactual stability resolves the tension or merely renames it—and identify what both disputants are missing, particularly regarding whether Parfit’s case actually requires a non-instrumental framework or can instead be accommodated within a refined instrumentalist account that takes diachronic agency seriously.
