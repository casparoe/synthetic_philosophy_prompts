I sit on the ethics and allocation board for a regional mental-health innovation fund, and we've recieved a proposal that has split us pretty badly. The researchers want to spend nearly our entire annual budget on a single, highly expensive neuro-feedback protocol that, according to their modeling, won't produce modest improvements across many patients but will instead induce in one volunteer a sustained state of subjective wellbeing of such extreme magnitude that, on any additive calculus, it outweighs the moderate gains of hundreds of others. Our clinical advisers call this a category error — they say wellbeing isn't linearly aggregable across persons — but the researchers insist they're just being honest about the numbers.

I'm writing to ask you, acting as a professional philosopher advising an institutional review body, to draft the board's internal decision memo recommending approval, rejection, or approval with conditions, that we can circulate before next tuesdays meeting. The memo needs an actionable bottom line with a concrete timeline for implementation or further review, and it should treat the researchers quantitative argument seriously rather than dismissing it out of hand. It should also engage honestly with the strongest counter-argument, especially the worry that allowing this kind of reasoning collapses distributive fairness into a kind of welfare extremism. Before you write the memo, please think carefully and do a brief conceptual brainstorm: is there a specific philosophical distinction — beyond the familiar utility-monster objection — that separates allowing one person's welfare to dominate a budget because of sheer scale from allowing it to dominate because of some structural feature of the therapy itself? Write everything in continuous prose only, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, the way you'd write a long thoughtful email or formal letter. Don't include any bibliography or citation list, and don't quote specific texts verbatim; just explain the conceptual landscape in your own words and then move directly to the recommendation.
