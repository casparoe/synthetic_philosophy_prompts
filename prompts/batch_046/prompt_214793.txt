I am giving a seminar paper next week in our philosophy-of-economics reading group defending a revisionist framing of the socialist calculation debate. My central claim is that Oskar Lange’s market-socialist reply to Mises and Hayek solves the formal allocation problem—how to compute equilibrium prices through trial and error—while leaving intact a deeper epistemic asymmetry I call the “distributed discovery constraint”: the institutional conditions that generate genuinely new, localized knowledge about scarcity and preference are not replicable by simulated price mechanisms without entrepreneurial risk-bearing and residual claimancy. I argue that this constraint undermines not only classical central planning but also hybrid “market-socialist” designs that treat prices as purely informational signals divorced from their generative institutional context. I want the audience to take the constraint seriously as a conceptual point, not merely as an ideological defense of capitalism.

The audience is precisely calibrated to make this hard: two Austrian economists who have the Mises-Hayek tacit-knowledge argument at their fingertips and will press me on whether my constraint is just a redescription of it; one historian of economic thought who specializes in the Polish socialist school and will check my reading of Lange’s actual procedural rules against his texts; and three analytical Marxists who accept the computational critique of central planning but reject any framework that makes market mechanisms epistemically indispensable, since they view that indispensability as a contingent feature of capitalist social relations rather than a structural constraint. I need a Q&A prep sheet—not a defense of the thesis, but an ordered map of the objections I will actually get, phrased the way each camp would phrase them, with a concise reply for each and an explicit note on where the reply is weakest or where I should concede ground up front. The objections should be grouped by whether they target my historical interpretation of Lange, the conceptual coherence of the distributed discovery constraint, or its normative implications for socialist institutional design.

Please include the objection I am hoping nobody asks—likely from the Marxist contingent, asking whether the distributed discovery constraint is merely ideological window-dressing that naturalizes capitalist property relations by relabeling them as epistemic necessities. For every reply, state clearly what consideration or counter-example would change my mind: for instance, a historical case where simulated price mechanisms without risk-bearing entrepreneurs generated genuinely novel localized knowledge, or a conceptual argument showing that tacit knowledge can be fully externalized into algorithmic feedback loops without loss. Finally, flag the weakest point in my own position that I should prepare to defend rather than hope they overlook—probably the move from an epistemic observation about knowledge generation to a normative conclusion about institutional design—and be explicit about whether I am making that move or deliberately stopping short of it.
