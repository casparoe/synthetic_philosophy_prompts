I'm putting together a little workshop on reasons and belief-formation for my MA students, and I realised every example of the wrong-kind-of-reasons problem they see is either the demon-and-belief-in-p case or some tired moral realism debate. I want something fresher but still grounded in the actual conceptual machinery, not some made-up jargon.

So: write me a dialogue between two equally sharp philosophers—call them Mara and Lin—who are arguing over whether 'aesthetic coherence' counts as a reason of the right kind for preferring one scientific model over another. Specifically, they've both been looking at this hypothetical climate-modelling framework that sacrifices about 3% predictive accuracy but intergrates oceanic, atmospheric and biospheric data into one mathematically symetric architecture. Mara thinks coherence here is purely the wrong kind of reason—like being bribed to believe something—and Lin thinks there's a distinct category of structural reasons that aren't evidential in the narrow sense but aren't pragmatically corrupt either. They should actually respond to each other: Mara needs to address Lin's point about predictive tractability being itself an aesthetic virtue, and Lin needs to deal with Mara's objection that symmetry is just a heuristic that hides missing variables. Keep it casual, maybe they're chatting over coffe, and please don't have either of them just cite standard textbooks—let them reason it out in plain language. No strawmen, real back-and-forth only.
