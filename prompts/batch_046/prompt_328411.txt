Here is an argument I have been turning over and now find suspicious, because it seems to dissolve every long-term commitment into irrationality. (1) Rational choice requires that only future costs and benefits determine present action; irrecoverable past expenditures are causally inert and must carry no deliberative weight. (2) To honour a sunk cost is to allow precisely such an expenditure—whether of money, time, or effort—to influence present choice by continuing a project rather than selecting the option with the highest expected utility. (3) Therefore honouring sunk costs is always irrational. (4) Now apply this consistently. A scholar who has spent twenty years acquiring an embodied expertise, or a polity that has sustained a constitutional tradition across generations, faces periodic opportunities to abandon that path for a more efficient alternative. To continue in either case is to treat the accumulated past investment as a reason for present loyalty, which is structurally identical to the sunk-cost error. (5) By (3), such commitments are always irrational, and practical reason demands perpetual reinvention without attachment to one's past self or collective history. (6) Yet this dissolves not only loyalty but also the strategic value of credible commitment—without which intertemporal cooperation, institutional trust, and the very coherence of long-term planning collapse. (7) That conclusion is intolerable. (8) So (3) must be false: honouring sunk costs is not always irrational.

Where exactly does the slip occur? I am reluctant to abandon (1), since it expresses the core of economic rationality. Is the error an equivocation in (2) between a financial loss and the path-dependent transformation of present preferences or capacities? Or must I qualify (1) by distinguishing static choice from dynamic consistency, and if so, what does that qualification cost?
