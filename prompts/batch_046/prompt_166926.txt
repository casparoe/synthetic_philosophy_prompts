I'm drafting a feature on rational paralysis for a science-and-ideas publication, and I want to test consistent reasoning about symmetric choice without relying on external literature. Here are three rulings from a decision-theoretic framework we'll call System O: (1) when two goods are identical in value, distance, and accessibility, rationality requires choice via an arbitrary internal tie-breaker rather than indefinite suspension; (2) when the goods are identical except that one was selected in a prior indistinguishable scenario, historical repetition does not break symmetry—the agent must still apply an arbitrary tie-breaker, not default to precedent; (3) when the goods differ only in a purely representational feature—such as being described with a longer but semantically equivalent phrase—representational framing without outcome-relevant change cannot justify preference. Now apply System O consistently to this new case: a clinical algorithm must assign one of two risk-equivalent, clinically identical treatment protocols. The protocols differ solely in their database keys—Protocol 7-Alpha versus Protocol 7-Alpha-Prime—where the "Prime" suffix derives from an automated legacy-migration artifact carrying zero clinical significance. The system can either apply an arbitrary tie-breaker and assign one, or suspend assignment pending human review. What ruling should System O issue? Identify the closest analog among (1)–(3), articulate the unifying principle that covers all four judgments, and specify the competing principle—one that treats representational or artifactual distinctions as symmetry-breaking—that would predict suspension or preferential assignment. Finally, name the minimal further case that would distinguish these two principles.
