In his 1930 essay "Economic Possibilities for our Grandchildren," John Maynard Keynes distinguishes solving the economic problem—securing material subsistence and comfort—from addressing what he calls the real problem of human life: how to use freedom from pressing economic cares to live wisely, agreeably, and well. Keynes assumes that once material scarcity is overcome, people will naturally redirect energy from accumulation toward arts, culture, and the cultivation of relationships.

Consider a hypothetical near-future society in which automation and artificial intelligence have indeed solved the economic problem for a broad population, but in which digital platforms are architected to maximize user engagement through precisely calibrated, low-cost stimulation—delivering not leisure in Keynes's sense but an effectively infinite stream of attention-capturing substitutes for wisdom and contemplation. In this setting, Keynes's assumption that freedom from economic pressure automatically enables the good life is challenged not by renewed scarcity, but by engineered abundance of passive consumption.

Please describe the most important implications of Keynes's framework for evaluating this scenario. Specifically:

1. How does Keynes's distinction between the economic problem and the real problem help us identify whether platform-mediated consumption counts as leisure or as a new form of economic servitude?
2. Does Keynes's moral critique of obsessive attachment to money extend structurally to engagement-maximizing design, or does the latter represent a categorically different failure—one that operates through saturation rather than scarcity?
3. What institutional or cultural conditions does Keynes treat as necessary for the transition from solved economic problem to genuinely good living, and which of these conditions are specifically undermined by architectures optimized for continuous attention extraction?
