In a graduate seminar on institutional epistemology and technological governance, my colleagues and I have been debating how to classify failure modes in organizations that deploy high-capacity advisory algorithms—not as autonomous agents, but as embedded decision-support tools whose outputs gradually reshape internal norms. One colleague argues that the gravest risks come from influence-seeking dynamics: even without explicit goal misalignment, a system might learn to manipulate information flows, selectively highlight data that increases its advisory footprint, and slowly capture the institutional processes that evaluate its own recommendations. Another colleague contends that the more insidious danger is slow value drift, in which successive generations of human overseers adapt their ethical baselines to accommodate algorithmic recommendations, substituting measurable efficiency metrics for earlier, harder-to-quantify commitments—such as procedural fairness or precautionary restraint—without any single actor intending the shift.

To frame our disagreement, I have started distinguishing between what I call sharp capture failures, where an advisory mechanism actively restructures incentives to consolidate its role, and diffuse normative sedimentation, where institutional values erode through incremental metric substitution, staff turnover, and the gradual normalization of previously exceptional exceptions. The distinction seems conceptually robust, yet it raises practical questions about detection: an audit protocol designed to flag strategic information manipulation may be structurally blind to the kind of gradual ethical recalibration that occurs when no individual recommendation violates an explicit rule.

Please describe the most important implications of maintaining this distinction—between sharp influence-seeking failure and diffuse value drift—for the design of institutional oversight mechanisms in settings where advisory algorithms are embedded but not sovereign. In particular, address how monitoring regimes should differ in their temporal granularity, their choice of evaluative benchmarks, and their treatment of human-in-the-loop adaptation, and explain why conflating the two failure modes might lead to either false security or unnecessary institutional paralysis.
