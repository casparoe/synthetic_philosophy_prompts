I am drafting a long-form feature for a science-and-ideas publication on a structural blind spot in the philosophy of collective intentionality. The standard literature—whether framing joint action through shared cooperative planning or through the constitution of plural subjects—tends to analyze withdrawal as either a unilateral revision of subplans or as a normative breach requiring mutual dissolution. I propose that both frameworks overlook an intermediate ontological state that I call anticipatory residue: the continued activation, in a withdrawing agent's cognitive architecture, of mutually modeled future pathways that were established during coordination but are no longer supported by present behavior or explicit commitment. I sketch an argument that this residue is not reducible to ordinary disappointed expectation, because it carries a directional, forward-looking structure tied specifically to the joint task rather than to the partner's general reliability. At the same time, I argue it falls short of broken promise because no standing obligation is violated—merely a shared anticipatory map that remains locally active. My essay asks whether recognizing this state can explain why aborted collaborations frequently generate a distinctive form of normative unease: stronger than mere frustration, yet lacking the clear moral grammar of betrayal. I would welcome your critical comments specifically on three points: first, whether anticipatory residue is conceptually distinct from existing accounts of frustrated joint intention or psychological contract breach; second, whether it mischaracterizes the dissolution conditions in planning-based versus plural-subject accounts of joint action; and third, whether this framing has any empirical traction—say, in studies of distributed cognition or organizational exit—without collapsing into purely psychological description. I am not seeking a literature survey, but an assessment of whether this injection actually advances conceptual clarity or merely renames familiar phenomena.
