As a journalist who writes about science and institutional design, I am preparing a guided discussion for policy analysts with no formal philosophy background. I want them to understand David Lewis's account of convention as a solution to coordination problems sustained by mutual expectation rather than by explicit agreement or enforcement. Rather than using the standard traffic-direction example, I intend to build the session around the historical emergence of priority and citation norms in early modern scientific correspondence. Plan the explanatory arc: what conceptual prerequisite about coordination failure must be in place before that example can carry weight; what the correspondence case needs to display and what it must omit so it is not mistaken for moral obligation or mere habit; the misconceptions this audience will likely form — for instance, that conventions require a legislator, or that they are purely descriptive regularities — and exactly where in the sequence each should be defused; one check question that reveals whether participants have grasped the mutual-expectation structure rather than only the behavioral pattern; and which formal apparatus, such as evolutionary game-theoretic modeling, should be explicitly set aside and why.
