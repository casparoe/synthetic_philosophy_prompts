A grave facing east becomes belief in solar resurrection; a repeated geometric border becomes cosmological symbolism. I want to challenge the default assumption that every stable material pattern must map onto a recoverable mental representation.

Instead, I propose distinguishing institutional choreography from doctrinal commitment. Institutional choreography refers to the way objects stabilize collective action—coordinating labor, marking temporal rhythms, or segmenting social space—without requiring that any participant hold an explicit belief about why the pattern is maintained. Doctrinal commitment, by contrast, requires that individuals possess propositional attitudes that justify or explain the practice.

The critical claim is not that belief is absent, but that it is not the necessary substrate of every material regularity. A pottery workshop might reproduce a spiral motif for generations because the form regulates hand-movement and kiln-loading, not because artisans subscribe to a cosmology. The motif leaves a robust archaeological trace, yet there may be no corresponding belief to excavate—only a habituated body-object assemblage.

This creates a residue category: practices that generate durable material signatures but correspond to no recoverable propositional attitude. The residue is not merely an evidentiary gap; it is an ontological category—social life sustained by distributed objects and embodied routines rather than by shared mental content.
