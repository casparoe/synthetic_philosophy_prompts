The Cartesian theater is the view that mental representations are brought together in a single functional location—the theater—where they are displayed to a central observer or self. Dennett's Multiple Drafts Model rejects this by proposing that consciousness arises from parallel, distributed editing processes across the brain, producing multiple simultaneous drafts of experience. A draft achieves consciousness not by being shown to anyone, but by reaching fame in the brain—widespread influence over other processes. Dennett introduces the center of narrative gravity only as a useful fiction for describing this distributed system, and he treats it as causally inert and therefore dispensable. From this it follows that there is no real-time phenomenal experience, only retrospective narratives constructed after the fact; consequently, the MDM is best understood as a form of eliminativism about phenomenal consciousness. The model is supported chiefly by split-brain studies, which show that the hemispheres can maintain independent streams of narrative, proving there is no unified neural workspace. More recently, some researchers have noted that hierarchical predictive processing offers a similar multi-layered architecture, but they argue that predictive coding actually reinstates a Cartesian theater at the top of the hierarchy, where prediction errors are resolved for a single global model.
