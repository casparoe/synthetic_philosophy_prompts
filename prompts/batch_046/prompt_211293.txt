I'm currently writing a paper for a seminar on early modern political philosophy, and we've been working closely with Machiavelli's distinction between fortuna and virtù—especially his argument in The Prince that fortune governs roughly half of human affairs while the remainder depends on our own capacity to act with foresight and adaptability. In conversation with a colleague who studies algorithmic governance, I began wondering how Machiavelli might have evaluated a problem that did not exist in the sixteenth century: modern democratic states that delegate consequential decisions—such as real-time fiscal adjustments, emergency resource allocation, or predictive border-management protocols—to autonomous systems whose decision-rules are opaque even to the elected officials who authorize them.

Would he have seen this delegation as a dangerous surrender of virtù, leaving the commonwealth naked before an unpredictable fortuna now mediated by machines? Or could he have interpreted a well-designed algorithmic apparatus as a kind of artificial dike—an extension of princely foresight that actually enlarges the sphere of human agency against chance? I am trying to reconstruct this counterfactual view with some care, using Machiavelli's own vocabulary of fortune, virtù, and the necessity for rulers to adapt their nature to the times, rather than importing anachronistic frameworks about democratic accountability or technological determinism. Could you help me think through what his position would likely have been, particularly regarding whether algorithmic opacity undermines the personal responsibility he demands of an effective prince?
