In a study-group discussion about Parfit's hitchhiker, two participants have reached an impasse over whether rational agents can credibly commit to actions they will later have no reason to perform. The scenario is familiar: a driver will deliver you only if she predicts you will pay upon arrival, but once delivered, payment serves no further purpose for you. The exchange proceeded as follows. (1) One participant argued that the rational agent should cultivate a stable disposition to pay after delivery, because the expected benefit of being picked up outweighs the cost of payment, and because people can indeed train themselves to honor such commitments. (2) The other objected that this merely relocates the problem: at the moment of decision, a rational agent still faces no incentive to pay, and if she anticipates her own future defection, the driver will not find her credible in the first place. (3) The first replied that this objection presupposes a time-slice conception of rationality; she proposed instead that rationality should be evaluated over an agent's entire deliberative history, so that adopting and maintaining a commitment-rule can itself be rational even when particular acts of compliance seem locally suboptimal. (4) The second responded that this conflates rational planning with the specific puzzle Parfit poses, which concerns whether one can rationally cause oneself to perform an action that, at the time of performance, offers no benefit. He added that if global-plan reasoning were sufficient, then any locally harmful habit—such as smoking—could be rationalized as part of a global lifestyle, undermining the normative force of the objection. (5) The first countered that the analogy is disanalogous: in the hitchhiker case, the commitment is instrumental to securing a distinct good (the ride), whereas smoking is not instrumentally tied to an independent benefit, so global-plan reasoning applies selectively rather than universally.

Please adjudicate the exchange move by move: assess steps (1) through (5) from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with brief justification for each rating. Then indicate who is closer to being correct overall, and specify what both interlocutors are overlooking—especially any unstated distinctions among credibility conditions, dispositional stability mechanisms, and the scope of time-slice versus diachronic rationality.
