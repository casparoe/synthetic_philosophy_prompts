I've been thinking about how we decide which things deserve moral consideration, and there's an institutional pattern that puzzles me. Research ethics boards will halt a study that harms a human subject or even a laboratory animal, yet if a researcher runs a detailed computer simulation of a suffering being—one that reacts, learns, and reports distress in ways behaviorally indistinguishable from the real thing—the board demands no protection for the simulated entity at all. At the same time, people often dismiss artificial minds by saying they are "just computation," but they never apply that same dismissal to human brains, which are also physical processes carrying out computations of some kind. What philosophical commitments about computation and moral standing does this practice actually presuppose? Does it assume that only biological substrates generate moral status, or that there is a specific kind of computation that does, and if so, how is that kind defined without circularity? Please reconstruct the implicit theory behind the policy, examine whether it is consistent with how we treat animals or cognitively disabled humans, and explain what a research-ethics framework would have to change if it replaced that view with one holding that functional organization and the capacity to suffer determine moral boundaries regardless of substrate.
