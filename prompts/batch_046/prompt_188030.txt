I am preparing a seminar paper on the philosophy of cognitive science, and I have become preoccupied by a paired institutional distinction that seems philosophically loaded but is rarely defended explicitly. In university examination policy, an open-notes rule typically permits a student to consult handwritten notebooks that she herself compiled, yet the same policy usually forbids consulting cloud-synced documents or conversational logs with an AI assistant, even when those digital artifacts contain precisely the same information and were authored by the same student. In parallel, clinical neuropsychological assessment protocols commonly treat successful navigation with a self-drawn paper map as evidence of intact spatial memory, whereas successful navigation aided by a personally configured GPS device—with saved waypoints, custom labels, and years of integrated use—is classified not as an expression of retained cognitive capacity but as a compensatory external dependency.

What theory of the mind-world boundary do these two practices jointly presuppose? Do they implicitly commit to a functionalist parity condition, according to which an external resource counts as part of a cognitive system whenever it plays a role functionally equivalent to an internal process? Or do they instead rely on a causal-historical or ownership-based criterion, one that distinguishes mental from non-mental resources according to their origin in biological memory rather than according to their present functional integration? Reconstruct the implicit philosophy of mind that makes both policies coherent, distinguish what they strictly require from what they merely accommodate, and say whether the pair is genuinely consistent with one another or whether exam integrity and clinical assessment are quietly presupposing rival views. Finally, if one were to take the extended mind thesis seriously—treating well-integrated, reliably accessible external structures as literally constitutive of cognition—what concrete revisions would be necessary in examination design and in the evaluation of cognitive impairment?
