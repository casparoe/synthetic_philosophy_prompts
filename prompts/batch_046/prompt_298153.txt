I've been stuck in this EU studies arguement for months and it's driving me nuts. One camp says the democratic deficit is basically proof that ever-closer union hits a hard wall: member-state parliaments still hold the budget levers and European Parliament turnout is consistently low, so sovereignty never really pooled, it just got masked behind Commission regulations. The other camp says that's exactly backwards—the deficit isn't a failure of integration but evidence that sovereignty was always shared, and the real mismatch is that citizens expect input legitimacy (elections, representation) in a system built for output legitimacy (regulatory efficiency, technocratic problem-solving). Both sides cite the same post-Lisbon map—co-decision, the European Council's agenda control, national parliamentary subsidiarity checks, the spitzenkandidat episode—and yet nobody moves an inch. Is this actually an empirical disagreement about institutional power, or a verbal fight over whether sovereignty is a stock you pool or a flow you exercise? Or are they answering seperate questions: one asking if the EU is democratic by national standards, the other asking if it's democratic by its own functionalist logic? I want a diagnosis, not a survey. What kind of stall is this—hidden normative baseline, ill-posed question, two programs talking past each other? And given your call, what would actually shift it: a new treaty mechanism, a sharp conceptual distinction, or just admitting progress shouldn't be expected here?
