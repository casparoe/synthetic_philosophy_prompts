i work as the documentation lead for a small regional human rights collective, and we recieve about two reports a week describing mass violence or systematic destruction against a specific group. we have to decide whether to use the word genocide in our public statements, because once we do, it triggers legal referrals and donor expectations we can't easily walk back. our team has legal training but we're not scholars, and the director has to sign off within 48 hours. we've been trying to adapt lemkin's original framework - the eight fields where a group can be destroyed, from physical and biological through cultural, economic, political, social, religious and moral - but our legal adviser added something she calls the distributed intent threshold, or the grey command problem: situations where no single official issues an order to destroy, yet the institutional architecture - budget flows, permit regimes, logistics chains - makes group destruction the only stable outcome. it's not in the standard textbooks we have, but it's the difference between calling something genocide and calling it a policy disaster with deadly side effects.

can you turn this into a reusable decision procedure my team could actually run? i need the steps in order, what counts as a sufficient answer at each stage, where the procedure stops with a clear no, and where it stops because the question is about values rather than facts and needs the director's call. please include the grey command problem as one of the layers, not as an afterthought, and try to seperate out the factual questions from the value ones clearly. write it all in continuous prose, no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables - just like a long email. use british english. then apply it to three reports: one where village burnings come with explicit written orders to eliminate the group; one where a ministry runs a forced assimilation programme destroying language and religious practice across generations, with high-level memos praising the erasure but no physical violence; and one where a government blocks food and medicine to a region, internal communications show they know people are dying, but there's no explicit kill order and the logistics are run by private contractors. include at least one case where the procedure gives an answer i won't like - probably the blockade one - and say clearly what the procedure can't settle. please don't ask for citations or reading lists; we don't have library access in the field. and keep it practical - this is for people who have to sign papers, not for a seminar. also analyse where the procedure is likely to misfire because of the grey command layer.
