I'm designing a fifty-minute seminar for second-year philosophy majors on Quine's critique of the analytic-synthetic distinction. The students arrive treating mathematics as the paradigm of analytic truth and empirical science as purely synthetic, and they have heard that Quine rejects all a priori knowledge outright. I want to avoid the overused bachelor/unmarried-man case and instead build the explanation around a carrying example that has both a definitional face and an empirical flank—perhaps the concept of mass across Newtonian and relativistic physics, or gold being atomic number 79 versus its observable properties—so they can see why the boundary collapses rather than learning only that definitions are conventional. Plan the explanatory arc: which dependency must be in place before they can understand Quine's holistic web of belief, what comes next, and exactly where to place the carrying example. Identify the misconceptions they will form—likely that Quine is merely attacking dictionaries, that he denies conceptual analysis entirely, or that he thinks every belief is equally revisable—and specify the point in the sequence where each should be intercepted. Provide one check-for-understanding question at the end that reveals whether they grasp belief interdependence rather than just repeating the idea that no statement is immune to revision. Also specify what should be left out at this level—perhaps the full machinery of Quine's naturalized epistemology or the semantic holism developed in Word and Object—and say so explicitly.
