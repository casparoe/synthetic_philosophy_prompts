For the past several months I have volunteered with a small community archive that records oral histories about urban development in our district. Our verification protocol currently accepts any testimony that can be independently corroborated, regardless of whether the speaker understands why the claim is true; we have recently begun debating whether this is adequate. Our director insists that we should privilege accounts in which the speaker can articulate a non-accidental basis for their claim—being correct in a way that is not merely lucky—while I have argued that a true belief without justification should carry the same archival weight, since the historical record cares only about accuracy. This disagreement has led me to study the philosophical question of why knowledge is superior to mere true belief, and I now want to apply that analysis directly to our practice.

Please describe the most important implications of the Meno problem—that is, the problem of explaining why knowledge is more valuable than true belief, including the roles that justification and the avoidance of epistemic luck play in the standard resolution—for how a volunteer-run archive should design its verification and weighting procedures. I am particularly interested in whether the superiority of knowledge is primarily a matter of practical reliability, social accountability, or intrinsic value, and how that distinction should affect cases where justification and truth come apart. To make this concrete, apply your analysis to two scenarios we are currently facing: first, an elderly resident who correctly identifies the original owner of a demolished factory but admits she may be conflating this site with one in a neighboring town; second, a retired engineer who provides a richly detailed but ultimately false account of a bridge's structural design, grounded in a sincere but mistaken belief that he personally worked on its renovation. Finally, indicate clearly where the philosophical analysis reaches its limit and an archive must make a normative judgment that the theory itself cannot settle.
