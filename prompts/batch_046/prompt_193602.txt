I am preparing a handout for my philosophy club meeting on whether pain is intrinsically bad, using clinical cases of pain asymbolia—patients who report feeling pain but display no emotional distress or avoidance behavior. Please write a decision tree that sorts philosophical positions on the badness of pain. The root question should ask whether pain’s badness depends essentially on its felt phenomenal quality or on its functional role in motivating avoidance. From there, branch by how one interprets asymbolia: does it show that phenomenal quality can exist without suffering, that suffering can exist without motivation, or that the two are always coupled but differently weighted? Each leaf should name a position—such as strong phenomenism, motivational essentialism, coupled dualism, or unnamed—and state in one sentence its most awkward commitment regarding a hypothetical patient with complete pain asymbolia but otherwise intact emotional and cognitive life. I also want you to include a specific hybrid branch for what I will call dissociation dualism: the view that pain carries two independent bad-making properties, one phenomenal and one motivational, and that asymbolia selectively disables only one. Please flag any pair of leaves your tree cannot cleanly separate, limit the instrument to about six nodes so it fits on one page, and do not include citations, bibliographies, or reading lists; I need only the logical structure and the commitments.
