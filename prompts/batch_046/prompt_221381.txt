In a recent seminar, a colleague argued that algorithmic complexity is philosophically inert because it is both uncomputable and representation-dependent, while another maintained that universal induction dissolves Occam’s razor entirely; I want to step back from that exchange and establish the conceptual ground rules before assessing any particular synthesis.

Write the full list of desiderata that an adequate philosophical account of Occam’s razor must satisfy if it genuinely integrates Solomonoff induction and Kolmogorov complexity. For each requirement, specify the phenomenon it must explain or the distinction it must preserve—such as the difference between syntactic and ontological parsimony, the treatment of ad hoc adjustments, the relation between predictive success and compression length, and the status of language-invariance claims up to a constant. Indicate which criteria are non-negotiable for any account in this family and which are camp-specific preferences (for example, whether practical computability for bounded agents counts as a hard constraint or merely an applied-science preference, or whether a universal prior can be modified by domain-specific background knowledge). Crucially, identify any pairs or subsets of desiderata that appear jointly unsatisfiable—especially the tension between demanding objective, representation-independent simplicity judgments and demanding that induction be executable by finite agents, and any conflict between universal priors and the need for structured empirical priors. Keep the weight on the conceptual architecture and its motivating cases rather than on scoring existing theories, though brief allusion to standard information-theoretic approaches is welcome. I do not need bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; I need the specification itself.
