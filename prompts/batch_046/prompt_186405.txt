I am preparing an interview for a philosophy-of-mind podcast, and the guest is an unnamed contemporary defender of eliminative materialism about beliefs and desires—though not in the familiar Churchland-style “folk psychology is a stagnant empirical theory” form. Instead, she argues that the elimination should reach into computational cognitive science itself: she holds that even the “propositional attitude” framework used in predictive-processing and Bayesian models is an ontological mistake, and she proposes replacing belief-desire pairs with what she terms “dynamic state-space ascription,” in which mental states are treated as continuous trajectories through a high-dimensional neural activation space rather than as discrete, content-bearing entities. She anticipates the standard objection that this is merely a redescription of the same functional roles, and her counter is that continuous neural dynamics lack stable, individuable “content-bearing” boundaries, so the redescription collapses into something genuinely different.

Please draft eight to ten interview questions that engage the actual substance of her position, not generic biography or “how did you come to this view” prompts. I want at least one question pressing how she accounts for the predictive and explanatory success of folk-psychological reasoning in everyday social interaction, paired with a follow-up keyed to her likely reply that predictive accuracy does not entail ontological commitment. Include at least one question about the “dynamic state-space ascription” framework itself—specifically, how it distinguishes two behaviorally identical subjects who differ in their underlying neural trajectories—with a follow-up anticipating her probable appeal to future neuroscientific resolution. Add at least one question on whether her eliminativism applies asymmetrically: if beliefs and desires are eliminated, why preserve intentional realism for perceptions or pains, and what principled criterion justifies the line—followed by a follow-up that presses the risk of arbitrary boundary-drawing. Finally, include at least one question on the implications for moral responsibility and the concept of “acting for reasons,” with a follow-up addressing her expected move that reasons-talk can be translated into norms governing state-transitions rather than into propositional-attitude ascriptions. For each question, briefly indicate in parentheses the standard reply you expect and how the follow-up should target its weakest point. Keep the tone rigorous and conversational, suitable for an audience already familiar with the philosophy of mind literature but not requiring specialist bibliographic knowledge.
