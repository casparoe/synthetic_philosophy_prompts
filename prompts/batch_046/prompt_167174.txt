I'm in the middle of a novel about a city that abolished career politicians through strict sortition and one-term rotation — you serve once, drawn by lot, and then you're permenantly ineligable to run again. I'm trying to understand what that actually feels like on the ground, not just the democratic-theory summaries I keep skimming. So I'm running a workshop next Tuesday — about 15 people, 80 minutes, mix of writers and a few philosophy grad studnets — and I want a structured in-class game or role-play with actual rules and token payoffs that makes them feel the specifc accountability vacuum: citizens can't reward or punish long track records because the face always changes, but the bureacratic staff stay and accumulate hidden power. Design it so the group runs into the problem themselves — maybe rotating council members negotiating with permenant administrators over a budget — then include a debrief that maps the moments onto arguments about rotational legitimacy versus institutional memory. Be honest about what will predictably go wrong the first run, and tell me which emotional piece — probably the comfort of recognizing a familiar name on a ballot — the activity just can't reproduce. I don't need citations or a reading list; I'm not writing an academic paper, I'm trying to feel the contradiction in my bones.
