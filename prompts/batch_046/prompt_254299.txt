As a journalist covering neuroscience and philosophy of mind, I have been trying to articulate my own stance on whether split-brain patients host one consciousness or two, and I find myself holding judgments that may not fully cohere.

Case one: the classic post-callosotomy patient in the Sperry tradition. The left hemisphere can name an object presented to the right visual field, while the right hemisphere—without speech—can guide the left hand to select the matching item, yet neither hemisphere reports awareness of the other’s action. My verdict: this is not a single subject with a blind spot, but two co-existing streams of experience, each with its own perceptual field and volitional capacity, though they share an affective baseline via intact subcortical pathways.

Case two: congenital absence of the corpus callosum—agenesis—where compensatory mechanisms allow near-normal behavior, and the subject reports a seamless first-person perspective despite clear hemispheric specialization on testing. My verdict: one consciousness, but its unity is achieved functionally rather than anatomically; the absence of direct interhemispheric fibers does not entail duality if information is eventually integrated elsewhere.

Case three: a hypothetical complete callosotomy preserving only the anterior commissure and habenular commissures, leaving emotional and olfactory integration intact but severing all neocortical cross-talk. The left hemisphere can speak and reports feeling calm; the right hemisphere, tested via left-hand responses, selects images matching the emotional tone of stimuli in the left visual field, yet cannot access linguistic categories. My verdict: this is the hardest case. I am inclined to say there is one emotional subject and two cognitive agents, which suggests consciousness might be layered rather than unitary.

Case four: an intact-brain patient with alien-hand syndrome following a focal parietal lesion—no callosal section—where one hand performs goal-directed actions that the patient disavows. My verdict: still one consciousness; the disowned action is a malfunctioning subsystem within a single subject, analogous to a compulsive urge rather than a second self.

Case five: a speculative gradual replacement in which every corpus callosum fiber is substituted by a high-bandwidth wireless brain-computer interface preserving real-time information exchange but eliminating direct axonal continuity. At completion, the hemispheres are anatomically disconnected but functionally coupled with negligible latency. My verdict: one consciousness persists, because what matters for unity is dynamic integration, not physical contiguity.

These verdicts pull in different directions. Cases one and five both involve anatomical disconnection, yet I judge duality in the first and unity in the second, apparently depending on whether functional coupling is preserved. Case two suggests functional integration can overcome structural absence, while case three suggests partial integration yields a hybrid. Case four warns against treating every internal conflict as evidence of multiplicity.

What principle—functional integration, narrative coherence, anatomical continuity, or access unity—best captures this pattern? Which of my five verdicts is the outlier that must be revised for coherence? And what further case—perhaps involving a split-brain subject with preserved interhemispheric transfer via subcortical loops but no conscious access to the other hemisphere’s contents—would best discriminate between the remaining candidates?
