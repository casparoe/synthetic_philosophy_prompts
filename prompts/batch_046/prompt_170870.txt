I'm drafting a 75-minute public lecture for subscribers to my science-writing newsletter—an audience of educated generalists who trust me to correct popular myths about scientific history. The subject is Johannes Kepler's actual stance on astrology. I expect them to arrive holding one of two caricatures: that Kepler was a rational astronomer who privately dismissed astrology as nonsense, or that he was essentially a court mystic who cast deterministic horoscopes for income. I want to replace both with a more precise, historically grounded view: that Kepler practiced a form of 'natural astrology,' treating celestial configurations as environmental physical influences—analogous to tides or magnetic fields—rather than as deterministic signatures of individual fate, and that he explicitly rejected 'judicial' prediction of specific events in human lives. Plan the explanatory arc for the lecture: which conceptual distinction must be in place before the audience can understand why he calculated horoscopes at all; the single carrying example or historical moment that makes the distinction tangible—showing both what his model predicts and what it explicitly forbids; the predictable misconceptions they will raise in sequence (for instance, that computing planetary positions necessarily implies belief in destiny; that 'natural influence' is merely astrology rebranded; and that this historical curiosity has no bearing on modern science communication), together with the point in the arc where each should be headed off and how the sequence absorbs it; the check for understanding that would show they have grasped the conceptual structure rather than simply traded one caricature for a more sophisticated one; and what should be deliberately left out at this level—such as the full mathematical apparatus of his harmonic cosmology—with an explicit acknowledgment of the omission. The tone should match science journalism: skeptical of myth, precise about evidence, yet willing to sit with historical strangeness rather than dismiss it.
