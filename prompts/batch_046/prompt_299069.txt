I've been reading some popular history on my own and discussing with friends whether certain "why this place and not that one" questions are actually well formed, or whether they quietly assume parallels that do not exist. One friend has been quite consistent in her judgments. She thinks asking why representative institutions developed in the Roman Republic rather than in the Persian Empire is fair, since both were large multi-ethnic states facing comparable administrative challenges in roughly the same era. She says asking why sustained philosophical inquiry took root in ancient Greece rather than in the Indus Valley civilization is poorly formed, because the societies were too different in structure, geography, and era to treat them as parallel tracks. She approves of comparing Japan's Meiji modernization with Qing China's Self-Strengthening Movement, viewing both as responses to Western imperial pressure in the same century with similar institutional starting points. She rejects comparing why the Inca lacked writing and the Maya possessed it, arguing that presence and absence of a single technology are not symmetric outcomes when the societies differed in scale, environment, and administrative needs. And she finds it sensible to ask why nuclear power spread widely in France but not in Germany, since both were advanced industrial democracies confronting the same 1970s energy crisis with comparable technical capacity.

Given that pattern, I am curious how she would treat the classic comparison behind what is sometimes called the Great Divergence or the Needham question: why did sustained industrialization emerge in Britain and not in Qing China? Would she regard that contrast as well posed? I am trying to determine whether she is implicitly running a rule about parallel institutional tracks—holding that a comparison is valid only when both societies share comparable prerequisites before the divergence—or whether she is running a rule about factor isolation, allowing the comparison whenever we can point to specific conditions present in one case and absent in the other regardless of background differences. Depending on which rule she holds, I think her verdict might differ, since Britain and China had diverged in property rights, state structure, and resource geography long before the eighteenth century, even though one can still list Britain's coal, colonies, and patent arrangements against China's different conditions. Could you predict her verdict, lay out the competing rules that fit her earlier answers, indicate which one best explains her overall pattern, and suggest a specific further comparison—perhaps between two societies with very similar backgrounds but a diffuse, hard-to-isolate cultural difference—that would best distinguish the two rules from one another?
