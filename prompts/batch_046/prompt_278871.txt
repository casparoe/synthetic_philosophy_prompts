As a journalist writing on the institutional architecture of military ethics, I have been drafting notes for an essay on what I take to be an under-examined tension in contemporary just war practice, and I would welcome your critical comments on the framing, its philosophical coherence, and whether the argument actually holds together. The practice in question is well documented: modern military institutions and international legal frameworks preserve the moral equality of combatants—treating soldiers on an unjust side as legitimate targets with the same rights and liabilities as those fighting for a just cause—while simultaneously building elaborate clinical, pastoral, and legal infrastructures around moral injury, rules of engagement, and post-deployment ethical review. These infrastructures treat soldiers as profoundly responsible moral agents capable of being genuinely harmed by, and genuinely culpable for, tactical violations of jus in bello, such as harming non-combatants or violating proportionality. Yet the same institutions treat the strategic decision to fight for an unjust cause—an unjust war in the jus ad bellum sense—as legally invisible and morally irrelevant to a soldier’s standing, liability, or psychological accountability.

My working thesis is that this is not merely an application of the classic separability thesis between jus ad bellum and jus in bello, but rather a functional moral partition: agency is constructed as robust and blame-sensitive at the tactical level, where institutions need tractable rules and accountable actors, and dissolved at the strategic level, where political authority and collective decision-making would make individual moral assignment institutionally unmanageable. The implicit theory, I suggest, is not that soldiers lack moral agency altogether, but that agency is administratively localized—made thick where institutional oversight is feasible and thin where it is not. This raises several questions I am trying to sharpen. First, if the defense of moral equality relies on an epistemic humility argument—that soldiers rarely have adequate knowledge to judge the justice of their cause—why does that same epistemic limitation not apply to tactical judgments made under extreme uncertainty, where soldiers are still held fully responsible? Second, does the practice actually presuppose a consistent theory of responsibility, or does it quietly combine a control-based view at the tactical level with an authority-based or role-based exemption at the strategic level, without resolving the tension between them? Third, is the “governance technology” framing defensible, or does it collapse the normative claim—that combatants have equal moral rights regardless of cause—into a mere descriptive claim about how militaries organize accountability? I am particularly interested in whether this bifurcation can survive without implicitly committing to the view that strategic causes are not proper objects of soldierly moral judgment at all, and if so, what that implies for the concept of patriotic duty or conscientious refusal. Please comment on the coherence of this line of argument, point out where it overstates institutional consistency or misses a well-established distinction in just war literature, and suggest whether there is a more parsimonious philosophical reconstruction—one that does not require inventing a new category of administratively localized agency—capable of explaining why we treat tactical and strategic moral domains so differently.
