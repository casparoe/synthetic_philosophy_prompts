Consider the long-running stall in philosophy of history over whether writing scientific development as movement toward present theories is invariably Whiggish. One camp holds that ordering past theories by their proximity to current predictive success covertly imposes present norms and distorts the past's own conceptual framework; the other holds that one can construct a thin, structural narrative that merely tracks which representations survived successive empirical filtering, without normative evaluation of past aims. Both sides accept the same case studies—the abandonment of caloric theory for thermodynamics, the shift from geometric intuition to algebraic formalism in topology—and both reject vulgar presentism, yet neither has moved in decades. Is the disagreement verbal, turning on whether progress must carry normative weight? Is there a hidden formal question about whether predictive success can be indexed across conceptually incommensurable schemes? Or are the sides answering different questions—one about the ethics of representation, the other about the causal structure of theory change—under one label? Given your diagnosis, what would count as progress: a formal model separating structural succession from normative appraisal, an empirical study of actual historiographic practice, or an argument that the demand for a single correct narrative is itself ill-posed?
