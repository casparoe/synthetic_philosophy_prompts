Adam Smith maintained that territorial colonies burden the mother country primarily through mercantilist monopoly privileges rather than through the expense of defense alone, and he treated chartered companies such as the East India Company as paradigmatic cases of private extraction operating under state protection. Consider a modern analog he could not have anticipated, which we might call platform imperialism: digital infrastructure firms headquartered in wealthy nations extract data and surplus value from users across the Global South through terms-of-service monopolies rather than through territorial sovereignty, while weak host states bear the costs of regulation, security, and social disruption without receiving corresponding tax revenues or political representation. Would Smith have classified this as a form of the colonial monopoly he condemned—thereby supporting antitrust dissolution or data-sovereignty independence—or would he regard the absence of territorial occupation and direct military expenditure as removing the mercantilist distortion, leaving only voluntary exchange distorted by asymmetry of power rather than by chartered privilege? In constructing your answer, apply his distinction between the expense of defense and the expense of monopoly to determine whether the externalized regulatory and security costs borne by host states constitute the kind of imperial burden he criticized, and explain whether his framework can distinguish such platform extraction from ordinary international trade without importing assumptions he never held.
