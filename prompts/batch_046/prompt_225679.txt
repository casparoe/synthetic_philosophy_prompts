I am preparing discussion notes for an advanced seminar on the architecture of political freedom, and I keep returning to a tension my students treat as circular rather than deep. The tension concerns Philip Pettit’s republican claim that freedom as non-domination—immunity to arbitrary interference—captures something Isaiah Berlin’s negative liberty, understood as freedom from actual interference, systematically misses, especially in cases like the benevolent master or the structurally subordinate citizen whose formal rights are never violated. My working hypothesis is that much of the stalemate is verbal: republican theorists restrict “interference” to arbitrary or uncontrolled impediments, which lets them classify domination-without-meddling as unfreedom, while negative-liberty defenders treat any reduction in the option set as interference, which risks making republican freedom either redundant or already included. Yet I am unsure whether this is merely a dispute over the lexical entry for “liberty,” or whether there remains a substantive disagreement about whether the bare capacity for arbitrary control—held in reserve, never exercised—constitutes a distinct harm that institutional safeguards should eliminate.

I want a diagnosis that engages what each side actually says about institutional design: specifically, whether removing a master’s power to interfere through constitutional checks restores freedom in the republican sense even when the subject never experienced concrete obstruction, and whether negative-liberty theorists can accommodate that intuition without expanding their concept beyond recognition. Is the impasse driven by competing intuitions about social power that neither side can argue for, by a hidden empirical question about how people experience vulnerability, or by the fact that the two sides answer different questions—one normative, about the value of not being subject to another’s will; one descriptive, about the absence of concrete obstacles? Please also address what would genuinely count as progress: a conceptual distinction that dissolves the overlap, an empirical result about perceived autonomy, or a formal argument that one framework subsumes the other. If none of these would resolve the dispute, explain whether that intractability signals that the underlying question is ill-posed, or simply that our foundational commitments about freedom and domination are incommensurable.
