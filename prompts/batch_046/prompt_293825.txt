For decades, bioethicists and philosophers of personal identity have argued over whether an advance directive—say, a competent patient’s refusal of life-sustaining treatment—retains binding authority once severe dementia transforms that patient into someone with radically different preferences, values, and psychological traits. The familiar camps are these: a “precedent autonomy” view holds that the directive expresses an enduring normative choice that survives psychological discontinuity; a “present interests” view holds that authority collapses when the subject of the choice no longer exists in any meaningful sense, leaving only welfare-based considerations for the current patient; and a “narrative unity” view attempts to preserve partial authority by appealing to the biographical continuity of the life rather than to strict psychological identity. My working hypothesis is that the debate has stalled not for want of empirical data about dementia or formal models of identity, but because these three camps are actually answering different questions under the single label “authority of the earlier self.” One treats the directive as an exercise of normative power that creates obligations in caregivers regardless of the patient’s current mental state; another treats it as a predictive welfare judgment that becomes obsolete when its intended subject is replaced; and the third treats it as a constraint on the narrative coherence of a single life, binding only if we presuppose that lives ought to be governed by their earlier chapters. I want a diagnosis, not a verdict: is the disagreement verbal, substantive, or hidden-empirical? Do the sides differ about facts, about which clinical cases count as relevant data, about standards of evidence for psychological continuity, or about starting intuitions—perhaps about whether autonomy is essentially diachronic or essentially present-tense—that neither side can argue for without begging the question? Is there a hidden formal issue, say in the logic of de re versus de dicto commitments or in the structure of intertemporal authority, doing the real work? And if the diagnosis is largely conceptual, what kind of distinction, formal clarification, or reframing could actually move things forward—perhaps by separating the question of who bears the obligation to honor the directive from the question of whether anyone with present standing can revoke it? Or is the impasse a sign that the question itself is ill-posed, and if so, why?
