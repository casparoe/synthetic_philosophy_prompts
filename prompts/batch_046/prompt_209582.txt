Write the desiderata that any philosophically adequate theory of deterministic chaos must satisfy if its aim is to account for long-range predictive failure without abandoning determinism or introducing hidden stochasticity. I want the full specification, not generic bullet points. For each requirement, state the phenomenon or case that motivates it, the distinction it must preserve, and whether it is non-negotiable or specific to one camp. In particular, include: (1) how the theory must distinguish practical unpredictability arising from finite measurement precision from structural unpredictability arising from dynamical properties; (2) whether it must preserve a sharp boundary between epistemic limitation and ontological openness, and what breaks if that boundary collapses; (3) how it should treat coexisting periodic windows and computable invariant measures within apparently chaotic regimes; and (4) one constraint imported from an institutional practice—specifically, the design logic of deterministic weather-model ensembles that produce probabilistic forecasts—explaining why that practice imposes a philosophical requirement rather than a merely engineering one. Then identify any pair of desiderata you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied—e.g., strict Laplacian determinism together with the claim that chaos generates genuinely novel information not present in initial conditions—and briefly assess how standard approaches (Lyapunov-divergence accounts, algorithmic-complexity frameworks, and coarse-graining interpretations) fare against the list. Keep the analytical weight on the criteria themselves; the scorecard is secondary.
