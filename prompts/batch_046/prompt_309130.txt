I was recently appointed to the ethics advisory board of a mid-sized regional museum that holds a collection of approximately thirty carved ritual figures acquired during a colonial expedition in the 1920s. The provenance records are ambiguous: there is no direct documentation of theft, but the acquisition occurred through an intermediary operating under the legal and economic authority of a colonial administration, and oral histories from the source community describe the removal as coercive. Last quarter, representatives from the descendant community proposed not a straightforward repatriation of every object, but a shared-stewardship framework in which physical custody might rotate over time, interpretive authority would rest primarily with the community, and the museum would retain only a climate-controlled storage and conservation role. Several trustees have objected, arguing that the museum's charter obligates it to preserve these artifacts for universal scholarly access, and that the source community's current facilities cannot guarantee long-term preservation. I am genuinely uncertain how to weigh these considerations. The situation feels like it involves more than property law; it touches on whether repairing a historical wrong requires returning physical possession, or whether restructuring the relationship around the objects can ever be sufficient.

Please approach this as a professional philosopher specializing in ethics and political theory. Before offering any recommendation, take a moment to think carefully and outline the distinct moral dimensions that are in tension here—such as the correction of past injustice, the present-day recognition of cultural authority, and the obligations of custodianship—without collapsing them into a single principle. Then advise me specifically: can a co-stewardship arrangement genuinely satisfy the demands of justice in this context, or does accepting it risk allowing the museum to retain moral and institutional comfort while deferring the harder claim of full restitution? I am not asking for a legal analysis, a bibliography, or citations of specific restitution cases; I want to understand whether the philosophical structure of the dilemma makes shared authority a legitimate resolution or an evasion.
