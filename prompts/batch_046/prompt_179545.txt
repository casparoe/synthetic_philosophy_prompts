I am taking a graduate seminar on jurisprudence, and after a long discussion about textualism and purposivism, my classmates and I started wondering how deceased judges might have reacted to interpretive technologies that emerged only after their deaths. In particular, since Justice Scalia passed away, legal technologists have proposed using large language models not merely to retrieve legislative history, but to generate what proponents call counterfactual legislative intent—synthetic reconstructions of how a hypothetical, fully informed legislature might have addressed a technological or social scenario that did not exist when a statute was enacted. This practice moves well beyond conventional purposivism, which examines actual historical purposes, and instead derives normative guidance from corpus-wide statistical patterns in legislative drafting.

Given Scalia’s well-known resistance to legislative history as an interpretive source and his insistence that judges must apply the ordinary public meaning of enacted text rather than reconstructed legislative desires, how do you think he would have evaluated this specific form of algorithmic purposivism? Would he have regarded it as an especially extreme version of the intent-scavenging he criticized, or might he have accepted it as a form of objective linguistic analysis if the model were restricted to mapping statutory language patterns rather than inventing hypothetical legislative responses? Please predict his view in detail, and specifically address whether he would draw a sharp distinction between using such models for semantic disambiguation of statutory terms versus employing them to produce synthetic legislative answers to unforeseen problems.
