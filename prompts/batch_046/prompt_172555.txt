In philosophy-of-pain discussions about asymbolia — the clinical phenomenon in which patients report pain yet show neither avoidance behavior nor emotional distress — I have heard the philosophical puzzle dismissed with the claim that pain is bad in virtue of being felt, and that is the end of the matter. I would like you to disambiguate that slogan. What are the precise theses it could be expressing: that the sensory quality alone bears intrinsic negative value; that an affective or motivational component is essential to harm; that phenomenal consciousness is necessary for damage to be bad; or something else? For each reading, indicate whether it is a platitude, whether it is defensible, and whether it actually addresses the asymbolia cases or merely assumes what is in question. Specifically, if the slogan is deployed to reject functionalist or purely motivational accounts of why pain is bad, does that rejection succeed, or does it beg the question? Please present the options as a menu rather than selecting one.

If my framing wrongly presupposes that asymbolia cleanly separates the feeling of pain from its harmfulness — or if the disambiguation task cannot be completed without first resolving deeper disputes about phenomenal consciousness — say so plainly instead of complying within the frame. I am not asking for references or a reading list; I just want the conceptual map and an honest verdict on where it fails.
