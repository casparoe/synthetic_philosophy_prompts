My colleague, who works on practical rationality and intertemporal choice, has given clear verdicts on six cases that bear on weakness of will and hyperbolic discounting. First, a smoker with an all-things-considered resolution to quit lights a cigarette after a stressful meeting and, upon calm reflection, judges that she ought not to have done so; my colleague calls this akrasia. Second, a person consistently prefers £10 today to £15 in one month and, when calmly asked, confirms this trade-off is correct for her; she rules this out as weakness of will, treating it as a stable rational preference. Third, a dieter abandons a meal plan after seeing an advertisement, feels the decision was correct at the moment, and still endorses it upon reflection, having never formed a firm long-term plan; my colleague classifies this as preference revision, not akrasia. Fourth, a saver repeatedly lowers his monthly contribution target every time he reviews his budget, never endorsing the reduction in a calm state despite an initial plan formed reflectively; she judges this to be akrasia driven by hyperbolic discounting. Fifth, an individual takes a prescribed medication that genuinely alters her time preferences, revises her financial plan accordingly, and endorses the revision, then spends in ways that would have seemed impulsive by her old standards; my colleague rules this not akrasia but a transformed preference. Sixth, a gambler sets a strict loss limit in advance, exceeds it during play, and afterwards insists that even while violating it she knew she was breaking her own rule; she calls this akrasia.

Now consider this further case. An agent places her savings in a time-locked vault with a heavy early-withdrawal penalty, having designed the lock in a calm, reflective state. She discovers that whenever she contemplates opening it early, she experiences intense anticipatory anxiety about the future delay; yet she also knows that if she actually pays the penalty and opens it, she will feel immediate relief and will retrospectively endorse the decision. She does open it, feels the relief, endorses the choice, but admits that had she resisted, she would have endorsed keeping the vault sealed. She has no prior history of revising such commitment devices.

If my colleague wishes to remain consistent with her six earlier verdicts, what should she say about this case?
