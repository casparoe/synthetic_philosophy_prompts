Consider the U.S. withdrawal from the Paris Climate Agreement announced in 2017, examined through a hybrid theory of promissory obligation: promises bind primarily through the reliance they generate in promisees and the moral cost of frustrating that reliance, yet this binding force is conditional on continuity in the practical identity of the promisor—so that a collective agent such as a democratic state may weaken its obligations if it undergoes a genuine change in governing identity rather than a mere shift in preference. Identify the relevant promisees (other signatories, domestic constituencies, affected populations), specify the forms of reliance that had been generated by the 2015 commitments (financial pledges, regulatory planning, investment patterns), and assess whether the 2016 transition represented a discontinuity in practical identity or only a change in priorities. Then determine what the episode reveals about the hybrid account: does the expectation-based strand dominate regardless of identity change; does the practical-identity condition capture the intuitive difference between legitimate revision and bad-faith breach; or does the case expose a silence in the theory about promises made by democratic states whose governments change? Be disciplined about the mapping—who counts as the promisor, what counts as the reliance, and where the theory offers no verdict.
