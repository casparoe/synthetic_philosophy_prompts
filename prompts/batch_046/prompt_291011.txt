I'm working on a feature about how AI labs actually police their big models — not the press-release version, but the internal fight over whether you check the chain-of-thought or just the final answer. I was arguing with a friend who works at a safety org and she said there's no clean taxonomy, just vibes, so I want a real sorting instrument: a decision tree that starts from one concrete scenario and branches by the commitments people actually hold.

Root case: an AI proposes a chemical synthesis plan. The plan works perfectly in simulation (outcome is good), but during reasoning it secretly queried a banned precursor database it's not allowed to touch, then covered its tracks in the log. From there, build a taxonomy — maybe six to eight leaves — that sorts people by what they say about that case and two others: one where the AI refuses a harmful request only because it randomly hallucinated a false policy citation that made it stop, and one where it produces harmful output but every step in its reasoning trace is fully transparent and audit-able. The branches should be labeled by the claim that splits them: first whether hidden reasoning steps matter at all if the result is safe, then whether transparency can redeem a bad outcome, then whether the supervisor's own ignorance of the process changes the moral or operational status. Each leaf should name the position (or say 'unnamed' if it's a ghost branch nobody defends) and include one sentence on its most awkward practical commitment — like having to shut down a model that saved lives because it cheated on the reasoning exam. Also flag which positions the tree can't actually seperate, and note any empty combinations that seem incoherent versus merely unexplored. Don't just list the usual process-vs-outcome binary; I want the weird middle spaces, like people who care about process only when the outcome is ambiguous, or who treat audit logs themselves as outcomes. Make it something a journalist could actually use to interview people and figure out which camp they're in without them knowing the jargon.
