I have been observing the long-running dispute over Frankfurt-style counterexamples to the principle of alternative possibilities, and I find the stall remarkable: for decades, compatibilists have refined the interventionist scenario in which an agent acts without being able to do otherwise, while incompatibilists have pressed the flicker-of-freedom objection and various modal-relevance conditions, yet neither camp has shifted its core commitments. I want a diagnosis of the disagreement's structure rather than another adjudication of the cases.

My own hypothesis is that the impasse is not primarily about causal determinism or about free will in general, but about a hidden distinction I shall call 'deliberative openness' — the requirement that an agent's first-person practical reasoning contain an intelligible, unactualised branch that remains live as a real option in the agent's own perspective, quite apart from whether that branch was metaphysically available or causally blocked by an external controller. On this framing, the compatibilist treats the absence of causal alternatives as sufficient for responsibility, whereas the incompatibilist treats the absence of deliberative openness as fatal, regardless of counterfactual blockers.
