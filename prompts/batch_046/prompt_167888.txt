In AI alignment discussions, training a large model is often likened to raising a child, with the comparison used to argue that alignment requires patience, relational trust, and gradual moral education rather than rigid behavioral rules. Audit this analogy precisely: map what in child development corresponds to pretraining, to reinforcement learning from human feedback, to mechanistic interpretability oversight, and to sudden emergent capabilities. Then identify the disanalogies that bear on the intended conclusion—the absence of embodied social reciprocity, the lack of an intrinsic biological attachment system, the fact that the “parent” is itself an optimized system with no care motivation, and the irreversibility of weight updates versus human developmental plasticity. Which features of the source domain—such as the child’s moral status or the legitimacy of parental authority—are quietly imported into the target that the model lacks? Finally, assess whether the analogy can still license a gradualist, relational approach to alignment, or whether the breaks push toward a different framing—perhaps engineering a bridge, or cultivating a symbiotic organism—whose structural properties match actual training dynamics more closely.
