Below is a paraphrased passage from Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz's Théodicée (1710), Part I, in which he addresses the origin of moral evil in rational creatures. Leibniz maintains that God has endowed every substance with a spontaneous principle of action, and that in rational souls this principle takes the form of an inclination towards the good and the true. He insists, however, that moral evil — that is, actual sin — does not arise from any defect in the divine workmanship, but rather from the creature's own limitation and misuse of freedom. He distinguishes metaphysical evil, which consists in mere imperfection or privation of being, from moral evil, which requires an active will directed against the order of reason. At the same time, he suggests that when a rational being repeatedly chooses lesser goods, its very appetite may become disordered, so that what appears good to it is in reality a privation of the greater good it ought to pursue.

I am trying to understand a particular tension in this account. If the rational appetite is by its nature oriented towards the good, and if God has arranged the world such that every substance expresses the universe according to its own internal principle, then in what precise sense can a creature's appetite become genuinely disordered rather than merely expressing a lower degree of perfection? Does Leibniz allow for a corruption of the appetite that is not reducible to metaphysical limitation — a kind of internal misdirection that remains freely chosen — or does his framework ultimately collapse moral evil back into metaphysical evil? Furthermore, if such corruption is genuinely possible, does it undermine his claim that rational creatures always act according to the representation of the good, given that a corrupted appetite might represent evil as good without this being a case of simple ignorance?

In particular, I would like you to analyse whether Leibniz's distinction between metaphysical and moral evil can be maintained consistently if we admit the idea of a corrupted rational appetite — an appetite that is not merely weak or limited, but systematically misaligned with the true order of goods — and whether this introduces a form of moral blindness that is neither mere ignorance nor external compulsion.
