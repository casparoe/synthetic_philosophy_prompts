I am a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, and I have volunteered to lead a ninety-minute discussion for an interdisciplinary group—mostly fellow engineers and a few clinicians—on the conceptual distinction between naturalist and normativist accounts of health and disease. I want the session to turn on whether “health” can be specified as pure biological fact—roughly, statistical conformity to species-typical functional design—or whether labeling a condition a “disease” necessarily requires an embedded judgment that the condition is harmful or undesirable, not merely statistically uncommon. Before I build the session, I need a teaching plan rather than the explanation itself.

What conceptual layer must be in place first—perhaps the distinction between describing a biological state and evaluating its clinical significance—before I can introduce statistical deviation, evolutionary design, and the harm criterion, and in what precise order should these three components be introduced so that each depends on the prior one? What specific properties must this ML-genetic-variant example possess, and what must it deliberately lack, so that it actually carries the philosophical distinction rather than collapsing into a generic technology-ethics debate about algorithmic bias? I anticipate specific misconceptions: that naturalism is just “objective data science” while normativism is “subjective opinion”; that statistical rarity automatically implies pathology; and that if clinicians disagree, there is no fact of the matter about health—so please map each misconception to the exact point in the sequence where it should be anticipated and defused. I also need one targeted check for understanding I can pose at the end to test whether participants have grasped the dependency structure—specifically, whether they can identify which premise in the naturalist account would have to be abandoned if they insisted on treating this variant as diseased despite its functional neutrality. Finally, tell me what I should explicitly leave out at this level—whether particular philosophers’ canonical texts, technical evolutionary biology, or formal analyses of normative concepts—and state that omission clearly.
