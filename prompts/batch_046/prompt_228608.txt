I have been working through Benjamin Franklin's project of moral perfection in a seminar on early American practical philosophy, concentrating on the ordered scheme of thirteen virtues—Temperance, Silence, Order, Resolution, Frugality, Industry, Sincerity, Justice, Moderation, Cleanliness, Tranquility, Chastity, and finally Humility—that he intended to be cultivated one after another. Franklin frankly admits that he never achieved true mastery of Humility, observing that the very effort to suppress pride can itself become a hidden source of pride. Rather than reading this as a simple biographical failure of will, I want you to defend what I will call the "capstone paradox" of his system: that the successful acquisition of the first twelve virtues—above all Industry, Resolution, and the coherent narrative of self-mastery they generate—necessarily constructs psychological conditions that make the self-effacing posture of Humility structurally unattainable within the framework itself.

Please take a definite, committed position: argue that Franklin's inability to reach the final virtue was conceptually inevitable, not merely psychologically contingent, and that any self-improvement project treating humility as a sequential endpoint is therefore conceptually self-defeating rather than just difficult. Be explicit about whether reordering the virtues or abandoning sequential acquisition altogether could dissolve the tension, and do not present a neutral survey of interpretations—state clearly which resolution, if any, succeeds and give your definite reasons for that judgment.
