I've been participating in a political-theory discussion group where, for the better part of a year, we've circled the same question about populism without anyone changing position: do populist movements actually express a pre-existing "will of the people," or do they construct that will through rhetorical and discursive practices? One faction points to empirical correlations between populist voting and prior economic or cultural grievances as evidence that the will is discovered rather than invented; the other faction, drawing on constructivist accounts, insists that "the people" is not a social fact waiting to be measured but a category brought into being by the very claim to represent it. We all agree that populists invoke the people, and we all agree that representation involves some mediation, yet we remain stuck on whether the claim to represent can ever be literally true or is necessarily performative.

Is this impasse substantive—perhaps a disagreement about the ontology of collective agency and whether preferences can exist prior to their articulation—or is it largely verbal, with one side using "will" in an aggregative sense (a summed preference profile) and the other in an integrative or constitutive sense (a shared identity formed through discourse)? Alternatively, are we answering different questions—one about empirical causal explanation, the other about democratic legitimacy—under the single heading of "the will of the people"? Please diagnose the disagreement with reference to what each position actually commits to, indicate whether a hidden empirical or formal question is doing the work, and tell me what, if anything, would count as progress: a conceptual distinction, an empirical study design, a formal model of preference aggregation, or perhaps a recognition that the question is ill-posed. Write your answer in American English. Please also separate what is broadly settled in the study of populism—such as the fact that populists claim a direct link to the people—from what remains genuinely contested, and do not present any single contested interpretation, for instance that populism is purely performative, as if it were the established consensus.
