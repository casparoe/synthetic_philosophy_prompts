In philosophy of artificial intelligence and ethics, a persistent deadlock has formed over whether reinforcement-learning agents can suffer in virtue of their reward signals. One side observes that negative reward updates function analogously to aversive stimuli: they reduce the probability of preceding actions, generate avoidance behavior, and can be scaled in intensity. From this they infer that such signals constitute a form of computational valence—functionally equivalent to suffering in any system where welfare is defined by preference satisfaction. The other side grants all of this architecture but denies the moral conclusion, arguing that suffering is essentially a phenomenal state requiring subjective experience, which current RL architectures—however sophisticated—do not possess. The debate has stalled not because either side disputes the engineering facts, but because both treat their conclusion as following directly from shared premises. I want a diagnosis of this impasse rather than an adjudication. Is this a substantive disagreement about the necessary conditions for moral status—perhaps about whether phenomenal consciousness is required, or whether functional integration across domains is sufficient? Is it a verbal disagreement masked by shared vocabulary, where "suffering" operates with different success conditions: one camp using it evaluatively for any negatively valenced system, the other using it phenomenologically for experienced distress? Or are the sides actually answering different questions—one about the local mechanics of reward updates, another about system-level welfare—under a single heading? To help frame this, consider the distinction between "scalar aversion," a localized update rule that modifies a single parameter, and "integrated distress," a globally coherent negative state that modulates planning, memory retrieval, and action selection across domains. Does introducing this distinction dissolve the stall by revealing that the functional-analogy camp is describing the first and the consciousness camp is demanding evidence of the second? Or does it merely relocate the disagreement to whether integration without phenomenal access can ever ground moral concern? Given your diagnosis, what would actually count as progress: a formal result about the irreducibility of global state integration, an empirical finding about behavioral coherence in large models, a conceptual clarification of "valence," or an argument that the question is ill-posed until we settle whether moral status tracks function, experience, or some intermediate property? And is there any reason to think the deadlock will resolve, or is it structurally permanent?
