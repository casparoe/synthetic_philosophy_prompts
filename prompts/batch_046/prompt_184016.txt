Design a thought experiment in a realistic materials-science context that isolates why metric-based measures of verisimilitude fail when theories differ in both predictive precision and ontological commitment. Specify exactly three competing theories—T1, T2, and T3—about the thermal response of an engineered ceramic-metal composite between 400 K and 600 K. T1 offers five precise quantitative predictions; four match observation within error, but the fifth predicts catastrophic brittle fracture at 515 K, whereas the material only shows reversible grain-boundary relaxation. T2 offers three predictions, all observationally correct, but each is deliberately vague—e.g., predicting only that mechanical properties shift in the mid-range. T3 replicates T1's correct quantitative predictions exactly, but explains them via a postulated latent-heat substrate that has no independent testable consequences beyond reproducing those same predictions. First, lay out the scenario in a single paragraph with concrete enough predictions that the divergence is tangible. Second, show why counting correct predictions ranks T1 closer to the truth than T2, while a parsimony-weighted measure might rank T2 closer; identify the structural feature responsible for the split. Third, show that introducing T3 forces the disagreement onto ontological cost rather than predictive accuracy—state precisely which feature of T3 creates this shift. Fourth, explain whether stripping T3 of its ontology (leaving its predictions intact) collapses the case back into the T1-vs-T2 disagreement, and if not, what residual disagreement remains. Fifth, say which theory each measure ultimately selects, and sketch the strongest objection a defender of the losing theory could raise—without citing named philosophers or invoking formal logical notation. Keep it readable for someone comfortable with basic epistemology but unfamiliar with the technical verisimilitude literature; avoid bibliographies, citation requests, and science-fictional elements.
