Two scholars—Dr. Aris Thorne and Dr. Mira Castellanos—argued over whether republican liberty (freedom as non-domination) offers a genuinely distinct evaluative lens from Berlin's negative and positive liberty, using as their test case a proposed municipal "predictive resource allocation" system. The system imposes no legal restrictions and expands service access, but its algorithmic weights are proprietary, its appeals mechanism is nominal, and citizens have no effective pathway to reshape or reject it. Here is the exchange, reconstructed from my notes.

(1) Thorne opened by saying negative liberty is clearly untouched: no citizen is physically or legally prevented from acting, and resources are being redirected, not withheld. He argued that if we call this a freedom deficit, negative liberty loses its sharp boundary and becomes a sponge for every grievance about governance.

(2) Castellanos replied that republican liberty is exactly what negative liberty misses. Citizens live under the arbitrary will of the system's operators, who can alter weights or deny appeals without accountability. The absence of actual interference today does not erase domination, because the capacity for arbitrary interference remains intact and unchallengeable.

(3) Thorne countered that this risks collapsing republican liberty into negative liberty plus procedural safeguards. If rules are transparent, contestable, and subject to democratic revision, domination is removed; republican liberty then becomes well-structured negative liberty rather than an independent normative category.

(4) Castellanos said that misses the status dimension. Even with perfect rules, if citizens permanently lack the effective capacity to contest, exit, or reshape the system—if they remain subordinate to an unaccountable power—domination persists. She added that positive liberty is also in play: being shaped by an opaque collective mechanism rather than directing one's shared conditions undermines self-mastery, and treating that as mere preference satisfaction confuses freedom with welfare.
