In discussions of combinatorial game theory—particularly around Hex, Chomp, and the broader family of symmetric, finite, perfect-information games—the strategy-stealing argument is usually presented as a quick proof that the first player cannot lose. Yet in the philosophy-of-mathematics literature there is a persistent, oddly static disagreement about what the argument actually establishes. One camp treats it as a paradigmatic non-constructive existence proof: it demonstrates that a winning strategy exists without exhibiting it, relying only on symmetry and the monotonicity of moves. Another camp insists that the argument smuggles in a substantive modal claim—call it the portability thesis—that a strategy executable in one game-tree can be executed in a counterfactual tree where an extra piece already occupies a square, and that this portability fails precisely in games with hidden information, simultaneous moves, or non-local win conditions. A third group suggests the disagreement is purely verbal: both sides accept the formal symmetry conditions and the Hex result, but one uses “strategy” to mean a decision procedure responsive to current board-state, while the other uses it to mean a complete game-tree mapping, so they talk past each other about whether anything has genuinely been “stolen.”

The debate has stalled for years: everyone accepts the formal conditions, everyone agrees Hex is a first-player win, and yet the philosophical disagreement about whether the argument is rigorous, modal, or misdescribed remains exactly where it was. Is this impasse substantive—does it turn on a hidden formal question about strategy portability in generalized or imperfect-information game spaces—or is it a disagreement about what counts as an explanation in mathematics? Or are the two sides actually answering different questions, one about epistemic access to strategies and one about ontological existence of winning procedures? Given your diagnosis, what kind of development—perhaps a formal result about portability in simultaneous-move extensions, or a conceptual distinction between decision-procedures and tree-mappings—would actually move the needle, or should we expect no resolution because the underlying question is ill-posed?
