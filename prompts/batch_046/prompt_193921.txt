so i'm a compliance officer at a mid-sized medical-device firm and every quarter i get maybe eight to twelve proposals to tweak something -- software patch, coating change, new supplier for a part -- and i have to sign off on whether we run one more round of bench testing or simulation before filing with the regulator. right now it's vibes-based and my boss wants something reusable.

i want a decision procedure grounded in Good's idea about the value of information, but built for someone with squishy priors and no stats phd. the core logic should be: information is only worth the delay if it could actually flip our decision, and the cost of waiting is real. but i need it turned into something i can run in about 20 minutes per proposal, with ordered questions, clear stop points, and places where i have to admit it's a judgment call not a calculation.

please include:
- the steps in order (like: does this change our regulatory tier? what's the worst credible belief shift? etc.)
- what counts as a sufficient answer at each step when i don't have exact probabilites
- a hard "epistemic risk budget" -- a cap on delay-cost that kills further testing even if more data is technically valuable
- explicit flags for "stop here, don't test" vs "this is values, hand it to a human"
- worked applications for three proposals:
  1. a glucose-monitor software patch that might cut false alarms by 5-15%
  2. switching to a cheaper polymer in a catheter with a well-known failure mode
  3. a case where the procedure says "don't run the study" but that feels wrong -- maybe a pediatric implant with tiny sample sizes

for each, show the steps, where it stops, and what i still decide by gut.

also say what the procedure can never do, and what will predictably break the first few times i try it.

constraints: 20 minutes max, i can pull an engineer for numbers but not for philosophy, sign-off is mine but i need to document the stop reason. no reading lists, just the procedure. and please don't ask me to compute EVPI exactly -- i can't.
