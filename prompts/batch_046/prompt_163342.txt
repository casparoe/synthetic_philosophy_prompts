I'm working through a problem set in the philosophy of autonomous systems and moral agency, and my study group has walked me into an argument about responsibility gaps that I cannot exit. The argument proceeds in ten steps. First, an agent is morally responsible for an outcome only if there exists a control node in the outcome's causal history—identical to the agent's decision-making apparatus—that could have produced a different result without destroying the functional identity of the system being controlled. Second, in continuously learning autonomous systems deployed in open environments—consider a reinforcement-learning agent—the specific pathway to any harmful output is shaped by post-deployment weight updates driven by interactions that are neither recoverable nor foreseeable by the designer. Third, the designer therefore fails the control-node condition: to intervene on that specific pathway, they would need to alter the learning architecture or disable the system, which destroys precisely the autonomy that defines it. Fourth, the user or operator lacks causal influence over those internal updates and provides only inputs. Fifth, the system itself is not a moral agent, since it possesses no capacity for normative reasoning or for recognizing reasons as reasons. Sixth, it follows that specific harmful outputs of such systems generate a responsibility gap. Seventh, apply the same structure to ordinary human moral agency: a person's decision at time t is produced by neural pathways shaped by prior learning experiences that are not recoverable or alterable by the person's conscious self without breaking psychological continuity. Eighth, that conscious self can influence future learning but cannot retroactively intervene on the pathway that already produced the decision. Ninth, therefore either human agents fall into the same universal responsibility gap, or the control-node condition stated in step one is false. Tenth, because universal moral skepticism is unacceptable, step one must be rejected—yet that rejection seems to dissolve the gap for autonomous systems as well.

Something slipped between steps six and nine, though I cannot name whether it is an equivocation on "functional identity," a modal slide from retrospective intervention to counterfactual ability, or a hidden premise about the relation between consciousness and control. Which step do I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? I would particularly like to preserve two commitments: that designers are not fully responsible for specific post-deployment harmful outputs, and that autonomous systems are not themselves moral agents. Please price the alternatives with those commitments in view, and explain why the rejected step looked plausible despite failing.
