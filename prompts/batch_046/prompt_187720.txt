I am preparing the final assessment for an advanced undergraduate seminar on philosophy of religion, and I want an essay question that pushes students past standard summaries of Wittgenstein’s view that religious belief is not empirically grounded. To do that, I want the question built around a specific interpretive tension I have been pressing in class: Wittgenstein insists that religious belief is embedded in a form of life and expresses itself through practices rather than functioning as a hypothesis, yet he also describes religious pictures—such as the image of the Last Judgment—as exercising a kind of regulative authority over a person’s entire life. The exam should ask students to determine whether this picture-language reintroduces a representational or cognitive structure that the form-of-life framing was intended to dissolve, and to defend their answer by analyzing a precise grammatical contrast: the difference between uttering I believe in the Last Judgment because I have seen evidence for it and uttering The picture of the Last Judgment governs how I live, even though both employ the same surface grammatical form I believe that p. I want the question designed for a 90-minute written response of about 800 to 1000 words, aimed at students who have encountered Wittgenstein’s later remarks on religious belief but should not be able to answer by reciting secondary literature.

Please provide: (1) the exam question itself, clearly worded so that it forces engagement with both the form-of-life thesis and the picture metaphor and requires students to take a stand on whether the two are compatible; (2) a model answer of approximately 650 to 750 words that navigates the tension without collapsing Wittgenstein into either a pure fideist or a pure cognitivist, specifically addressing why the picture is neither an empirical hypothesis nor a mere emotional attitude, explaining how religious disagreement can be understood within this framework without reducing it to a clash of lifestyles, and using the grammatical contrast above as a central illustrative device; and (3) a marking scheme out of 25 marks that distinguishes an A-range answer (22–25) from a B-range answer (18–21) by naming the exact conceptual moves required—such as correctly identifying the distinction between belief-state and belief-content, explaining why the authority of the picture is practical rather than evidential, and handling the disagreement problem—while also listing the specific errors that should cost marks, for example treating Wittgenstein as a straightforward non-cognitivist about all religious language, ignoring the picture vocabulary entirely, or assuming that form-of-life embedding eliminates the possibility of genuine religious conflict.
