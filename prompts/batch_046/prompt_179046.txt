I'm currently drafting a feature for a science and ideas magazine on the methodological foundations of consciousness studies, and a conversation I had last week with a cognitive neuroscientist and a philosopher of mind has left me uncertain about how to frame one particular tension. We were discussing Daniel Dennett's heterophenomenology, which treats first-person reports as texts describing a heterophenomenological world rather than as direct windows onto consciousness, and contrasting it with neurophenomenology, the approach that seeks a reciprocal circulation between disciplined first-person experience and third-person neural dynamics. Both are familiar to anyone in the field, yet I keep returning to a problem that neither seems to address head-on: what I am calling phenomenal inertia—the tendency for first-person descriptive categories to persist structurally unchanged even when confronted with contradictory predictive or neural feedback, as though the report has its own grammatical momentum independent of either verification or revision.

Could you compare heterophenomenology and neurophenomenology specifically through the lens of phenomenal inertia? I am interested in which framework offers the more coherent account of why a subject's descriptive categories might resist correction by either behavioural prediction or neurophysiological data, and whether one approach risks reducing the subject's report to a useful fiction while the other risks over-privileging neural dynamics as an authoritative corrective. In particular, does heterophenomenology's insistence on textual neutrality leave it unable to explain why some reports feel stubbornly resistant to reinterpretation, and does neurophenomenology's mutual circulation provide the resources to explain that resistance without collapsing first-person authority into mere neural correlation? I would like a comparison that takes both the philosophical commitments and the practical research programmes seriously, and that does not merely illustrate one with the other.

Please be explicit about what argument or empirical consideration would lead you to favour the other framework—what evidence about the stability of phenomenological categories, or about the success of intersubjective calibration, would change your verdict? Also indicate where your reasoning is most likely to fail: for instance, if phenomenal inertia turns out to be an artefact of poor experimental design rather than a genuine methodological challenge, or if heterophenomenology's textual neutrality actually explains the resistance better than I have assumed. I am writing for an informed general audience, so please avoid bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; a precise conceptual analysis is exactly what I need for the piece.
