I am preparing teaching materials for a graduate seminar on textual interpretation, and I want a dialogue that moves past the familiar author-versus-reader framing that students have already encountered in introductory courses.

Please write a dialogue between two equally sophisticated interlocutors — call them Rowan and Sage — who disagree about whether authorial intention can be meaningfully recovered in texts that have no singular author, such as anonymously compiled liturgical books or collaboratively drafted legal codes. Rowan should defend a refined intentionalist view: that collective authorship still involves distributed mental states that can be reconstructed through historical and textual analysis, and that abandoning this recovery leads to interpretive arbitrariness. Sage should defend a practice-based autonomy view: that in these cases 'intention' is not an aggregate mental state but a regulative fiction maintained by interpretive communities, and that attempting to recover distributed intentions commits a category error by treating institutional practices as hidden psychology. Ground their exchange in a concrete case: the interpretation of a particular passage in an anonymously compiled medieval ritual manual where the wording is ambiguous between prescribing action and describing belief.

The exchange should contain genuine back-and-forth: each speaker should respond to the specific examples and objections raised by the other — for instance, Sage should address Rowan's worry about arbitrariness, and Rowan should reply to the charge of category mistake — rather than restating general positions. Please begin the response with the dialogue itself, with no preface, framing, or commentary. In presenting the arguments, separate what is broadly settled among specialists in philosophical hermeneutics from what remains genuinely contested, and do not present a disputed claim as if it were consensus.
