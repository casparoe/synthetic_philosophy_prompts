I am writing a philosophical novel whose central theologian advances what she calls the “economy of silent witnesses” — a theodicy that abandons both the free-will defence and the soul-making account. She contends that evil is permitted not because it produces compensatory goods visible to us, but because genuinely unobserved suffering is structurally necessary for any redemption arc that is earned rather than bestowed; if every instance of pain were accounted for within human epistemic reach, redemption would collapse into mere transactional compensation and lose its narrative coherence.

Please set out the most important implications of this framework for the classical problem of evil, with particular attention to the evidential argument from apparently gratuitous suffering. You must take a committed stance: either argue that the framework resolves the evidential problem by reconceiving “gratuitousness” as an artefact of limited observational scope, or maintain that it merely relocates the difficulty into an unverifiable metaphysics of hidden narrative coherence and therefore fails. Give your reasons decisively rather than laying out options for me to choose between, address the strongest objection to your chosen view, and explain why it does not defeat you. Write in British English, and do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations.
