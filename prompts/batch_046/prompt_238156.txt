I'm working on a piece about estate taxes and equal opportunity, and I've kind of settled on a view that feels a bit too neat, so I want to poke holes in it. The view is that inheritence taxes mostly miss the actual machinery of dynastic wealth. By the time someone dies, the real transmission has already happened: not just cash, but what I'd call "risk infrastructure"—the family capacity to absorb failure, access private deal-flow, or turn elite education into a credential that functions like capital. Meanwhile, the bequest itself is partly a form of intergenerational reciprocity, not pure greed; parents accumulate to insure kids against state failure and market volatility, and taxing it hard just erodes the democratic middle class while ultra-wealthy families shift value into trusts, family offices, and consumption that looks like spending but works like investment. So high estate taxes hit the wrong target and weaken a stabilizing social contract. Play it straight: what would actually count against this? I want the defeaters sorted by how fatal they'd be—empirical patterns that contradict the claim, moral arguments that dissolve the reciprocity idea, institutional examples where estate taxes clearly worked. Which of these would defenders of the view concede are damaging, and which would they brush off? Does the position predict anything its rivals don't—say, that countries with high inheritence taxes should show no drop in intergenerational elasticity once you control for invisible transfers? And has it been qualified into unfalsifiability: if every failure of the tax just proves the wealth was hidden better? Is that insulation a problem? Don't give me a reading list—just honest reasoning, and tell me if I'm just defending a comfortable intuition.
