Question: Distinguish the etiological account of biological function from the causal-role account, naming the principal philosophers associated with each. Then examine whether the etiological account can genuinely ascribe malfunction to a heart in a fetus that dies in utero and never pumps blood, without circularity. Your analysis should explicitly address how each view handles the token-type distinction.

Student answer:
The etiological account, developed by Larry Wright and Ruth Millikan, holds that a biological trait's function is fixed by its evolutionary selection history: a heart pumps blood because ancestral hearts were naturally selected for that effect. The causal-role account, defended by Robert Cummins, defines function through present systemic contribution, so a heart's function is whatever capacity it currently exercises that serves the survival and reproduction of the organism in which it resides.

Considering the fetal heart that never pumps and dies before birth: the etiological view cannot genuinely assign malfunction without circularity. The token heart possesses no selection history of its own, so to claim it should have pumped, we must borrow the history of other heart tokens. This is a greedy reduction of the token's actual properties into the type's historical properties, collapsing explanatory levels and assuming precisely what needs to be shown. By contrast, the causal-role account avoids this problem entirely. Even in utero, the heart occupies a defined structural position within the circulatory system, and we can identify malfunction by measuring its actual contribution—zero—against the system's present organizational needs, with no appeal to distant evolutionary narrative. Thus, while etiological theories illuminate population-level norms, they depend on reducing token-level biological reality to type-level history, a move that undermines the autonomy of lower-level explanation. Cummins's framework, remaining at the level of current functional organization, provides the more rigorous analysis of individual malfunction.
