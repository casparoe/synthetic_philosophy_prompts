I'm organizing a philosophy-of-mind discussion group around Hofstadter's Ant Fugue, and we've reached an impasse over whether the colony Aunt Hillary could literally possess a mind. Rather than asking participants which label they prefer, I want a taxonomy—structured as a branching decision tree—that sorts positions by the concrete judgments they make about the colony's mental status. The root should split on whether the colony has any mental properties that are not fully reducible to the mental properties of individual ants; from there, each branch should be labeled by a specific diagnostic claim or case judgment that a respondent can answer without knowing the philosophical vocabulary in advance. I need the tree to cover at least the following positions as leaves: reductive eliminativism (only ants think), biological-substrate exclusivism (only centralized nervous systems qualify), functionalist collective emergentism (organization is sufficient regardless of substrate), part-whole panpsychist aggregation (the colony's experience is composed of ant-level micro-experiences), and what I will call distributed-subjectivity emergentism—the view that the colony can have intentional states and even phenomenal content, but lacks a singular first-person point of view because inter-ant signaling latency and decentralized architecture prevent the kind of unified temporal frame required for one subject.

For every unoccupied combination of answers—say, a view that grants phenomenal consciousness to the colony while insisting that every ant is entirely non-conscious—please note whether the gap looks incoherent, merely unattractive, or genuinely unexplored. Also flag any pair of leaves that your tree cannot genuinely separate on behavioral or structural criteria alone; for instance, if functionalist emergentism and a weak organizational-panpsychist view converge on every practical test case, say so explicitly. Keep the nodes phrased as questions about specific scenarios: for example, whether finding that the colony learns a maze faster than any individual ant licenses attributing a collective belief; whether gradual replacement of every ant over months destroys the colony's mental continuity in the same way gradual neuron replacement does for a human brain; and whether a latency gap of several seconds between pheromonal signals is enough to break the phenomenal-binding conditions that a unified mind requires. The audience is advanced undergraduates who have not read the specialist literature on collective consciousness, so do not ask for citations, reading lists, or named theorists—just build the instrument from the logical space and locate the named positions within it. Aim for roughly six to eight leaves and five or six branching nodes, and make sure at least one branch turns on the niche issue of whether collective phenomenal binding requires a single temporal frame of reference across all constituent members, since that is the distinction I think separates distributed-subjectivity emergentism from the other emergentist views.
