Produce the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between inside-view epistemic autonomists and outside-view reference-class prioritists, concerning the proper role of epistemic modesty in forecasting and decision-making under structural novelty and sparse feedback.

The collaboration should address domains characterized by long time horizons, mechanistically ambiguous historical analogues, and low-resolution outcome tracking—such as predictions about transformative technological change, macroeconomic regime shifts, or long-run climate adaptation pathways. The deliverable must be a single formal document suitable for co-signature, structured exactly as follows.

First, a numbered section of agreed claims. These must be substantive and constraining, not generic platitudes. They should cover: the empirical reality of systematic forecasting biases (overconfidence in causal narratives, planning fallacy, and selection effects in expert judgment); the formal structure of reference-class forecasting and its necessary dependence on criteria for class membership; the normative thesis that epistemic modesty is a virtue in high-uncertainty domains; and the shared observation that available historical data underdetermines predictions in structurally novel cases. Include an explicit agreed claim about the "nested forecasting" problem—that selecting a reference class requires judgments about structural similarity which themselves depend on reasoning about the target domain.

Second, a disagreements table with two columns, "Inside-View Position" and "Outside-View Position," each row stating the precise disagreement in wording its own side accepts. Address at least: (i) whether the nested-forecasting problem undermines the autonomy of the outside view by reintroducing inside-view judgments at the level of class selection; (ii) whether epistemic modesty is best operationalized as confidence calibration within an inside-view framework or as a methodological shift toward base-rate dominance; (iii) how to weigh mechanistic structural models against historical base rates when they yield divergent point predictions; and (iv) whether long-horizon forecasts are best evaluated ex post by outcome accuracy or by audit of reasoning process.

Third, a cruxes section listing at least three sub-questions such that resolving them would move at least one party. For each crux, specify: the concrete case, dataset, or formal argument both parties agree is relevant (for example, the record of expert forecasting tournaments in geopolitical and technological domains; the predictive performance of structural macroeconomic models versus naive historical extrapolation during documented regime changes; or a specific nested-forecasting instance where class-selection criteria determine the predicted range); and each side's explicit prediction or judgment about that case, recorded in advance. Treat the nested-forecasting regress—whether iterative calibration can resolve class-selection dependence without circularity—as a central crux, with each side predicting whether this dependence is benign or fatal to outside-view autonomy.

Fourth, a brief concluding assessment of whether any portion of the dispute is merely verbal rather than substantive—for instance, whether "modesty" denotes confidence-level adjustment or methodological deference—explaining precisely which portion, if any, dissolves under analysis and why the remainder does not.

Write in formal, neutral prose. Do not include bibliographies, citations, reading lists, or requests for them. Do not invoke obscure theories, named figures, or technical results that a well-informed generalist would not recognize; rely on standard concepts in forecasting methodology, epistemology, and decision theory.
