I'm drafting the take-home final for my graduate seminar on metaphilosophy and the politics of knowledge, and I need an essay question that operates deep in the logic of what we have been calling ascription universality — the structural mechanism by which a particular epistemic standpoint is masked as universal not through simple error but through institutional control over the conditions of articulation and validation. I want the prompt to be unanswerable by anyone who merely summarizes familiar critiques of Western-canon bias; students must instead confront a specific reflexive paradox. If the dominant standpoint is genuinely particular, then labeling it false universalism seems to presuppose exactly the standpoint-independent criterion of universality that the critique itself aims to dismantle. To complicate this further, I introduced in lecture a position I term reflexive particularism: the claim that the only non-dominating form of universal aspiration is one that explicitly indexes its own conditions of production and treats translation failure across standpoints not as a defect to be overcome but as a constitutive feature of the claim itself. The exam question should ask students to do four things. First, articulate why ascription universality is structurally different from mere false generalization. Second, diagnose the self-undermining risk in standard critique and explain how it threatens to collapse into either quietism or a covert reinstatement of dominance. Third, reconstruct reflexive particularism without allowing it to slide into either relativism or a hidden universalism. Fourth, apply this framework to a concrete case — specifically, the global imposition of individual autonomy in bioethical frameworks upon communal care traditions that do not partition agency, responsibility, or bodily integrity in that way. Please write the exam question itself, a model answer of approximately 700 words, and a marking scheme that allocates points across these four must-hit criteria, specifies the marks for each criterion out of a total of 25, names the precise conceptual move that separates an A answer from a B answer in each case, and lists the characteristic misunderstanding or omission that typically costs students marks on each point. The level is upper-division graduate, and the time allowance is 90 minutes of writing.
