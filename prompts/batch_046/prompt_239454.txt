Two members of a philosophy-of-population-ethics reading group—let's call them Maya and Jonah—got stuck in an exchange about the procreation asymmetry, and a third member asked me to referee. Here is how it went.

(1) Maya opened with the standard asymmetry: we have weighty reason not to bring into existence a child who will suffer severely, yet we have no parallel reason to bring into existence a child who will flourish, because non-existence is not a harm and mere existence is not a benefit to the merely possible.

(2) Jonah replied that this only looks compelling if one accepts a "person-affecting" restriction. If one allows impersonal evaluation—counting value or disvalue regardless of for whom—then failing to create a flourishing life seems like leaving value unrealized, just as creating a suffering life is realizing disvalue.

(3) Maya conceded the point about impersonal evaluation but introduced a distinction between counterfactual harm and counterfactual benefit. Harming, she said, requires lowering a baseline of well-being that already belongs to someone; benefiting requires raising a baseline for someone. Since the non-existent have no baseline, neither applies symmetrically—yet suffering remains intelligible as bad for whoever experiences it, whereas happiness is not "good for" a non-existent person who is never actualized.

(4) Jonah countered that this restates rather than explains the asymmetry. He proposed an alternative framing he called "ontological debt asymmetry": bringing someone into existence activates a special creator-to-created normative relation that functions like a debt of justification, triggered only by negative outcomes. This, he argued, explains why we feel answerable for suffering we cause but not for happiness we fail to cause, without appealing to mysterious baselines.

(5) Maya said this framing smuggles in the conclusion. It treats existence as a normatively neutral default that generates obligations only when it goes wrong—which is precisely what's contested. She added that Jonah's view cannot explain why we do not think we fail existing people by not having additional children: if there were a genuine ontological-debt structure, one might expect obligations to maximize happy existences, not merely avoid miserable ones.

(6) Jonah responded that the debt is relational and activates only upon actualization. Failing to actualize creates no debtor, hence no failure. He concluded that the asymmetry survives, but for relational rather than metaphysical reasons about counterfactual baselines.

Please adjudicate move by move: rate each of the six moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with brief justification for each rating. Then say who is more right overall, identify what both are missing—especially regarding whether Jonah's "ontological debt" framing genuinely explains the asymmetry or merely renames it—and note whether either side has adequately addressed the challenge that an impersonal evaluation seems to erase the asymmetry entirely.
