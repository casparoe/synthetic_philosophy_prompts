Here's an argument I cooked up for a discussion on Scanlon's contractualism and now it smells fishy because it ends up saying that even saving a drowning child is unjustifiable if the rescuer gets their shoes wet. (1) Scanlon says wrongness is failure of justifiability: a principle is wrong if anyone affected can reject it, and that rejection can't be dismissed just by pointing to benefits for others. (2) Now here's a twist I added: suppose we formalize "can reject" through a rejection-score funtion — each affected person gets a score equal to their burden minus their share of the total benefit, and if anyone's score is positive, the principle is vetoed. (3) Take the principle "save one child from drowning at the cost of ruined shoes for the rescuer". The rescuer's score is high (ruined shoes) minus near-zero share of the benefit (the child lives, not them). So the score is positive; veto. (4) Take the principle "let the child drown so shoes stay dry". The child's score is catastrophic minus near-zero share of saved shoes; veto. (5) So both principles get vetoed. (6) Apply this to any burden-sharing: in every case there's someone with a positive rejection-score, because benefits are always distributed differently than burdens. (7) Therefore no principle ever survives the score test, and contractualism collapses into total paralysis. (8) Obviously that's wrong — but is the error in (2), where I invented the score funtion, or in (1), where I assumed rejection is always available regardless of proportion? Or maybe there's a hidden quantifier shift in (6) where I go from "for each principle there's a veto" to "there's a veto for all principles"? I really want to keep the anti-aggreagation intuition behind (1) but I need to know what I'm paying if I drop the score idea in (2).
