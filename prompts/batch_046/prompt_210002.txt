The debate over what Descartes’ cogito actually proves has persisted since the first published objections to his Meditations, and in contemporary discussion it has crystallized into three stable positions: the inferentialists, who hold that the move from thinking to being is an enthymeme requiring a suppressed major premise that whatever thinks exists; the intuitionists, who hold that the cogito expresses an immediate, non-inferential awareness of one’s own existence in the very act of doubting; and the performativists, who hold that the first-person pronoun does not denote a pre-given substance but is constituted in the act of thinking itself, so that the formula demonstrates not an underlying self but the inseparability of thought and its subject. In my own study group these three readings have coexisted for years without convergence—everyone grants the textual evidence, everyone can reconstruct the others’ arguments, and yet no one abandons their position. My working hypothesis is that the stalemate is not really about Descartes’ text but about competing standards of philosophical proof: whether proof must take discursive form, whether it can be immediate and intuitive, or whether it can be performative; and whether first-person reference operates like demonstrative identification or like a pure logical function. Is this diagnosis correct, or is there a hidden substantive disagreement about the ontology of the self—perhaps over whether the thinker must be a substance, a bundle of perceptions, or a grammatical fiction—doing the real work beneath the methodological clash? I want a diagnosis that engages what each camp actually claims, together with an honest assessment of whether any conceptual distinction—say, between de re and de dicto certainty, between epistemic and metaphysical priority, or between logical and existential quantification—could break the deadlock, or whether the disagreement is irremediable because it ultimately rests on bedrock intuitions about what it means to be a subject of thought.
