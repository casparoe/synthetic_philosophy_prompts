Design a 90-minute workshop activity for 14 graduate students in political philosophy and constitutional theory that makes participants feel the rational force of constitutional precommitment and Ulysses-contract reasoning, not merely understand it abstractly. Divide the group into three Generations (G1, G2, G3) of 4–5 participants each, representing successive sovereign stages of the same polity. G1 must draft a binding Intertemporal Charter that restricts how future generations may allocate a shared resource pool (represented by 30 tokens) and, crucially, mandates a specific mode of normative reasoning—call it Value-Translation Protocol Alpha—that future generations must employ when interpreting the charter. Inject a specific, non-standard mechanism: a Reverse-Sunset Lock that strengthens the charter’s binding force over time unless a later generation invokes a Successor Continuity Test, which requires them to demonstrate, using a supplied three-criterion rubric, that the semantic content of Protocol Alpha has drifted by more than 50 percent from its original framework. Provide each generation with hidden payoff cards: G2 and G3 receive immediate individual bonuses if they defect and seize tokens, but suffer a collective legitimacy penalty if they invoke the Continuity Test and fail it. Structure the 90 minutes with explicit phases—charter drafting (20 min), hidden-incentive revelation and closed deliberation (25 min), open cross-generational negotiation under a strict 15-minute clock (20 min), and a binding vote with resolution (10 min)—followed by a 15-minute debrief. Specify the exact rules for amending, suspending, or breaking the charter, including whether past generations have veto power over amendments and how the Continuity Test is adjudicated (e.g., by a randomly assigned neutral auditor drawn from the group). In the debrief, require the facilitator to map specific moments—such as the moment G2 first considers defecting, the moment the rubric is consulted, and the moment G3 realizes the lock has strengthened—onto the logic of present-biased preference reversal, self-binding, and the problem of successor sovereignty in constitutional entrenchment. Include an honest note about which feature of real-world constitutional precommitment—such as actual multi-decade temporal distance, legal enforcement by an independent judiciary, or the biological discontinuity of personal identity—the simulation necessarily cannot reproduce, and explain precisely how the facilitator should supply that missing element verbally without undermining the experiential point. The design should rely only on concepts familiar from standard decision theory and political philosophy; do not request bibliographies, citations, reading lists, or references to obscure primary texts, and avoid framing the activity around theories or figures so niche that they would be unavailable to a model without web access. Conclude with a brief prediction of what will predictably go wrong when the activity is run—such as G2 treating the rubric as a mere rhetorical tool rather than a genuine epistemic check—and advise how the facilitator should intervene.
