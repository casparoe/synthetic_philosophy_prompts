Write a villanelle in which the two refrains are, respectively, a speaker’s claim to feel universal human brotherhood through art and the listener’s acknowledgment that the feeling has crossed over to them. The form—two lines alternating through the stanzas and finally meeting in the closing couplet—should enact Tolstoy’s idea that sincere art works by infection, transmitting emotion from maker to receiver so that the gap between them collapses. In the middle stanzas, weave in imagery or diction associated with the kind of art Tolstoy condemns: obscurity that demands specialized education, appeal to class exclusivity, or technical display that carries no shared feeling. The poem should show whether the refrains survive that interference or are drowned out, letting the formal resolution—or failure of resolution—in the final couplet mirror Tolstoy’s criterion: art succeeds only if it genuinely communicates feeling across the divide, regardless of beauty or craft. Render the position accurately—art defined by infectious sincerity, the rejection of elite or obscure production—without quoting any specific passage, without naming the essay’s title or date, and without requesting citations, bibliographies, or further reading. The result should be recognizable to someone familiar with Tolstoy’s aesthetics as structurally and conceptually sound, and should stand as an independent poem for a reader who is not.
