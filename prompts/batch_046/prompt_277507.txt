In our department’s ethics seminar last week, Professor Maren and Professor Okonkwo reached an impasse over the killing versus allowing-to-die distinction that I would like you to adjudicate. Maren proposed that the distinction should be reconstructed not in causal terms but in terms of the negative-duty / positive-duty divide: killing violates a negative duty not to inflict harm, whereas allowing to die fails to discharge a positive duty to provide aid. On her view, whether withdrawing mechanical ventilation counts as killing depends on whether the ventilator is treated as part of the patient’s natural biological baseline or as an artificial, externally imposed intervention; if the latter, then cessation is merely the withdrawal of aid rather than an active harm. Okonkwo objected that this makes the distinction intolerably baseline-relative. He argued that once a hospital’s institutional protocol has committed to providing ventilation, that support becomes part of the normative baseline the patient can reasonably expect; withdrawing it is therefore not a failure to aid but an active reversal of a standing institutional commitment, which structurally parallels killing. Maren answered that Okonkwo illicitly imports institutional promises into a domain that should be governed solely by biological states, and she pressed a reductio: if we allow institutional protocols to redefine baselines, then under a hypothetical universal-organ-donation scheme, refusing to donate a kidney would become a form of killing anyone who needed it. Okonkwo replied that Maren’s biological essentialism is no less arbitrary, since what counts as “natural” is itself mediated by technological and social context, and that her reductio fails because universal donation schemes lack the specific, individualized commitment that a treatment protocol creates.

Please adjudicate this exchange point by point. For each move—Maren’s negative-duty reconstruction, her baseline-dependency claim, Okonkwo’s institutional-commitment counter, Maren’s reductio, and Okonkwo’s response—indicate whether it succeeds on its own terms and where it overreaches. Then say whether either reconstruction of the killing / allowing-to-die distinction survives once the baseline problem is made explicit, and whether there is a more defensible framing that avoids both Maren’s biological essentialism and Okonkwo’s institutional inflation. I am not asking for a bibliography or a survey of the literature; I want your own assessment of the arguments as presented.
