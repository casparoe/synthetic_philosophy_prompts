In a philosophy of death seminar, a classmate dismissed concerns about premature mortality by invoking Epicurus: "Death, therefore, the most awful of evils, is nothing to us, seeing that, when we are, death is not come, and, when death is come, we are not." She took it to settle not only that we should not fear death, but that death is never genuinely bad for the one who dies—no deprivation, no harm, no asymmetry with never having been born. I want to see exactly how much philosophical work this single sentence can be made to do, because it feels like it is being asked to carry several different arguments at once.

Can you lay out the distinct precise theses that "death is nothing to us" might express? I am especially interested in separating: (a) the claim that death cannot be an experienced harm (because awareness is absent); (b) the claim that there is literally no subject for whom death is bad (the "no-subject" problem); (c) the claim that death is not a deprivation of future goods, so the deprivation account of death's badness fails; (d) the claim that fearing death is rationally groundless, regardless of whether death is bad; and (e) any symmetry argument comparing posthumous non-existence with prenatal non-existence. For each reading, say whether it is a near-platitude, whether it is substantive and defensible, and whether it is actually false. Then tell me which of these readings the slogan was probably intended to support in the argument I heard, and which ones it genuinely supports versus which ones are smuggled in by ambiguity—particularly with respect to the interaction between the absence of awareness and the possibility of being harmed at a time when one does not exist. I would like the answer structured as a menu of readings with verdicts, not as a defense of one interpretation.
