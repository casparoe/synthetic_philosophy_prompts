I'm writing up my philosophical profile for a seminar on cross-cultural epistemology, and I'd like a consistency check before I present my view. My position draws on classical Nyāya but applies it to contemporary philosophy of science, and I've been trying to articulate exactly where I stand on the four pramāṇas and how they should be weighted in modern scientific practice.

Here is where I have landed: (1) I take perception (pratyakṣa) to be foundational in the sense that inference, comparison, and testimony all ultimately depend on it, but I do not think they are reducible to it — each pramāṇa is genuinely autonomous. (2) I think testimony (śabda) yields direct knowledge when conditions of speaker competence and sincerity are met, without requiring the hearer to perform any independent inference about reliability; at the same time, the replication crisis in psychology and medicine has convinced me that in current scientific practice we should treat published findings as default-suspect and require direct replicative verification — what I think of as "replicative perception" — before granting them the status of testimony-based knowledge. (3) I hold that comparison or upamāna is irreducibly distinct from inference: in scientific modeling, analogical reasoning works by establishing structural resemblance relations (for instance, comparing atmospheric dynamics to fluid dynamics) that license predictions without requiring the strict presence-in-the-subject condition that Nyāya inference demands of its middle term. (4) I think scientific instruments should be understood as extending perception rather than mediating testimony: once calibrated, a spectrometer or electron microscope presents data directly, not as a report from an expert. (5) At the same time, I believe that which structural analogies count as valid in a given field is partly shaped by disciplinary conventions — conventions that are themselves transmitted and maintained through testimony — yet I deny that this makes comparison reducible to either testimony or inference. (6) Finally, I am not a cultural relativist about epistemology: I think the four pramāṇas are universal, but I also think that modern scientific norms have distorted the balance among them by over-weighting testimony and under-weighting direct verification.

I'm especially worried that my defense of comparison's autonomy conflicts with my admission that analogical validity depends on conventional norms, and that my call for replicative verification undermines my defense of testimony's independence. I also want you to check whether there is an apparent contradiction — perhaps between treating instruments as perceptual extensions and insisting on perception's foundational directness — that actually turns out to be harmless once the conditions for assisted perception are spelled out.
