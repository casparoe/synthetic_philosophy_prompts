Having spent a career in experimental physics before turning to philosophy, I find the literature on embedded agency compelling yet curiously silent on what I will call instrumental embedding: the phenomenon in which measurement devices, protocols, and automated filters do not merely assist an agent but become constitutive elements of her deliberative loop, generating conceptual instability about where the agent ends and her environment begins. Within the philosophy of embedded agency, which open lines of inquiry should now take priority? I see three candidates: first, developing criteria that distinguish when an external artifact is genuinely part of the agent rather than merely a causal influence upon her; second, assessing whether such embedding erodes the autonomy conditions traditionally required for moral responsibility; and third, analyzing how technologically mediated collaboration should redistribute attributions of credit and blame. I ask for a defended ordering rather than a survey: which question is upstream of the others, which is tractable through conceptual analysis alone, and which, if resolved, would least alter the broader framework? I am preparing a long-form essay and wish to concentrate on the two or three problems whose settlement would most significantly reshape our understanding of agency in technologically dense practice.
