I run a philosophy workshop for about ten advanced undergraduates, meeting for a single ninety-minute session on decision theory. We have been discussing rational choice under external prediction, and I want an in-class game that makes participants feel the internal tension of Newcomb's problem before anyone names it or hears a lecture. To keep the idea from being something they have seen a hundred times, inject the following framing: the predictor is not an oracle but a machine-learning classifier trained on each player's visible deliberation patterns from prior rounds; players know that changing their reasoning strategy—not merely their box choice—can shift the classifier's forecast, but altering that strategy costs them a fixed number of points deducted before payout.

Design the complete session: the physical or digital materials, the round structure (including how many rounds, whether choices are revealed, and how the classifier updates), the payoff scheme using points convertible to a small tangible reward, and the rules that keep the prediction mechanism structurally real yet opaque enough that players must discover its logic through play. Arrange the incentives so that participants run into the conflict between causal independence and evidential correlation themselves—ideally constructing the one-box versus two-box distinction through their own strategic adjustments—before you name it in the debrief. Then provide the debrief: map specific in-game moments—when a player tried to out-deliberate the classifier, when a group collectively misread the feedback loop—onto the classic debate. Say what will predictably go wrong the first time this is run. Finally, be frank about which feature of the original one-shot problem—specifically the causal independence of the two boxes once the predictor's state is fixed—this repeated-round, reasoning-cost version necessarily distorts, and note exactly what you will have to supply verbally at the end.
