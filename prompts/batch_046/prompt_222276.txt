I have just concluded a long argument with a close friend about whether analogy-making is indeed the core of cognition, as Hofstadter and Sander propose in Surfaces and Essences. She maintains that elevating analogy to this status collapses the boundary between productive metaphor and mere category mistake, and she pressed me on whether we possess any non-circular criterion for distinguishing the two. Next Thursday I am delivering the opening address at an interdisciplinary workshop—‘Conceptual Boundaries: Analogy in Science and Art’—hosted at our university’s centre for cognitive studies. The audience is split evenly between cognitive scientists and philosophers of mind on one side, and practising artists with no formal training in philosophy on the other. I need a twelve-minute address that treats analogy-making as a genuine cognitive mechanism, uses the notion of porous conceptual boundaries to frame the workshop’s purpose, yet respects the structural limit my friend identified: that without a non-circular account of when boundary-crossing becomes error, I cannot claim analogy solves the problem of conceptual innovation, only that it raises it. The speech must work for experts without alienating the artists, and it must be shaped by this tension—what the view allows me to claim about cognition, and what it forces me to leave unresolved—rather than treating the philosophy as decoration.
