I have been reading Don Quixote on my own and keep returning to the philosophical problem of verisimilitude—not merely realism in art, but the deeper question of how a representation can be so lifelike that it becomes indistinguishable from reality, and whether that closeness is a cognitive virtue or an aesthetic trap. Quixote is driven to madness by chivalric romances that are verisimilitudinous enough to live inside, yet Cervantes also satirizes anyone who demands that fiction mirror the world exactly. Could you read the novel through this lens: where does verisimilitude explain why Quixote mistakes an inn for a castle, or books for lived experience, and where does the text resist being reduced to a simple warning about “too much realism”? I am especially interested in the scene where the priest and barber examine Quixote’s library—does the novel suggest that verisimilitude is a property of the text, of the reader, or of the gap between them? Please separate what is fairly settled in the philosophical debate about verisimilitude from what is genuinely contested among people who know the topic, and do not present a contested view as the consensus. I am preparing notes for a casual reading group, not an academic essay, so I want a reading that takes both the book and the idea seriously without distorting either.
