Consider the view, inspired by functionalist thought experiments about brain preservation, that consciousness does not require biological tissue or real-time internal dynamics. Specifically, imagine that Einstein's complete neural state-transition graph is printed across millions of indexed volumes in a library. A team of archivists, following strict protocols, updates the volumes and cross-references them in exactly the sequence that Einstein's brain would have executed. The claim is that this distributed, temporally dilated book-system is not merely simulating Einstein's consciousness but literally instantiating it, because what matters is only the preservation of the causal-topological pattern, not the speed, substrate, or locus of execution. Play this straight: what would have to be true for this conclusion to be wrong? Which premises—about the sufficiency of functional topology, about the irrelevance of temporal integration, about the possibility of distributed realization—are actually load-bearing, and for each one, what observation, argument, or formal result would genuinely undermine it rather than merely shift the example? In particular, distinguish between defeaters that the view's defenders could absorb by qualification (e.g., requiring a faster clock speed) and those that would force abandonment of the core claim. I want an honest assessment of how exposed this position actually is: is there any empirical or conceptual finding that would decisively settle the matter, or has the view been insulated such that every apparent counterexample can be redescribed as a different kind of realization?
