Assume the role of a professional historian of economic thought, specializing in eighteenth-century British political economy and the history of imperial administration. Before responding, please take a moment to outline mentally the central distinctions in Adam Smith's analysis of empire: the fiscal versus commercial accounting of colonies, the difference between political dominion and free commerce, and his specific judgments about North American independence versus the administration of territories in South Asia under chartered monopoly.

A colleague has summarized Smith's position as follows: "Smith thought the American colonies were an enormous net profit for Britain because they supplied cheap tobacco and sugar and bought British manufactures at monopoly prices, but he supported independence anyway because he believed all imperial dominion violates natural liberty. Once independent, he expected them to maintain preferential trade with Britain out of habit and affection. He also argued that European empires in Asia were necessary to spread commercial society and that Britain should expand its territorial empire in India rather than relying on the East India Company's private rule."

Please provide a careful accuracy audit of this characterization. Go claim by claim and indicate: (a) which elements a careful reader of Smith's major work on political economy would recognize and accept; (b) which involve subtle misrepresentations—such as conflating mercantilist arguments with Smith's own, attributing to him a rights-based moral framework he largely avoids, or inverting his assessment of territorial empire and chartered monopoly; (c) whether the colleague's implicit conclusion—that Smith's opposition to empire was purely ethical and therefore in tension with his economic system—survives once the misrepresentations are corrected. Where relevant, distinguish between Smith's reasoning about North American colonies and his reasoning about the East India Company's administration. Do not provide a bibliography or reading list; rely only on your knowledge of the arguments. If you refer to specific passages, paraphrase them without quotation marks.
