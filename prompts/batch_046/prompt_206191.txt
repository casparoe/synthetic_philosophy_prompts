A philosopher of mind I work with has articulated a finely graded scale of moral status running from insects to thermostats, with explicit verdicts on boundary cases. She holds that adult humans possess full moral status; cows possess substantial but clearly sub-human status; octopuses possess moderate status, below cows but well above fish; fish possess minimal but genuine non-zero status; individual honeybees possess negligible, technically non-zero status—killing one is not wrong, though aggregating harm across thousands is; a bee colony, despite collective problem-solving that exceeds any individual bee’s capacity, possesses no emergent collective status beyond the summed status of its members; a Portuguese man o’ war, though composed of specialized zooids functioning together, possesses minimal status lower than that of a single fish because it lacks centralized neural integration; a contemporary large language model possesses exactly zero status regardless of fluency or parameter count; and a thermostat maintaining temperature via feedback loops possesses exactly zero status, with no intermediate gradation possible. She must now advise on a synthetic neuromorphic chip that replicates the complete connectome and real-time dynamics of a honeybee brain in silicon, lacking biological tissue, evolutionary history, or sensory embodiment, yet functionally equivalent to the biological original at the circuit level. Predict whether she will assign it the same negligible status as the biological bee or deny it all status whatsoever. Then identify the two most plausible underlying rules that jointly accommodate all her earlier verdicts, show precisely how they diverge on this synthetic case, argue for the prediction that best fits her implicit framework with reasons, and propose one further test case—perhaps a biologically derived neural organoid lacking global integration, or an artificial system with unified workspace architecture but no neural replication—that would most clearly adjudicate between those rules.
