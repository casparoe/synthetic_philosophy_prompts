Having spent my career in experimental biology and only recently begun studying philosophy of medicine, I am trying to articulate a coherent position on health and disease for a small discussion group. Before I present, I would like a rigorous consistency audit. Here are my commitments, stated as precisely as I can manage them:

(1) I hold a naturalist, biostatistical view: a condition is a disease when some internal part or process operates with functional efficiency below the species-typical range for the relevant age and sex, evaluated independently of social values or subjective preferences.

(2) Drawing on evolutionary medicine, I distinguish two failure modes. First, design dysfunction: a mechanism fails to perform the task for which it was evolutionarily selected. Second, ecological misfit: a mechanism operates exactly as designed, but the modern environment is so different from the environment of evolutionary adaptedness that the interaction produces sustained homeostatic failure. I think both should count as diseases, though for different reasons.

(3) For mental conditions, I maintain that a state qualifies as mental illness only when there is an identifiable design dysfunction in neural, endocrine, or stress-response circuitry, together with clinically significant harm or impairment. Purely reactive suffering that tracks rational responses to adverse social circumstances is not, by itself, pathological.

(4) I deny that congenital deafness or autism spectrum traits are diseases, even though they involve statistically atypical sensory or neural functioning, because they are stable variations rather than failures of internal mechanisms; they do not disrupt homeostasis in a way that impairs survival, reproduction, or goal-directed functioning.

(5) I reject strong normativism—the claim that disease is constituted entirely by social values—but I also reject the WHO’s expansive conception that equates health with complete physical, mental, and social well-being, on the grounds that it conflates health with happiness and makes health empirically unattainable. At the same time, I think health is partly relational: an organism is healthy when its internal mechanisms maintain homeostasis in interaction with the environment it actually inhabits, not merely when it matches an abstract statistical norm.

Please examine these commitments for joint tenability. Which pairs are genuinely in tension, which conflicts are only apparent and dissolve on closer inspection, and how severe are the real ones? Rank the tensions by severity rather than treating them as equally damaging. For each genuine tension, identify the least costly revision that restores consistency and specify what that revision costs me conceptually. Then tell me which single change to my position set would eliminate the greatest number of problems with the smallest overall loss. Please write your answer in American English, in a formal and polished style.
