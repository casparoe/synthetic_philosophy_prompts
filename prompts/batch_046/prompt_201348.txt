I'm working through my own intuitions about when political obligation genuinely binds and when civil disobedience is warranted, and I find that my verdicts on concrete cases don't align neatly with the standard frameworks. Here are my judgments.

1. The conscientious draft resister who refuses induction into a war he regards as aggressively imperial: I think his legal-political obligation to serve is not merely outweighed but genuinely canceled, and his disobedience is fully justified.

2. The juror who nullifies a drug-possession statute because she judges it unjust: I think she violates a specific institutional duty she voluntarily assumed, so her disobedience is wrong, even if the statute itself is bad law.

3. The low-level bureaucrat who leaks classified documents revealing an illegal domestic surveillance program: justified. I also think she retains general obligations in other domains, so this is targeted cancellation, not wholesale rejection of authority.

4. The taxpayer who withholds a calculated fraction of income tax specifically to protest defense spending: not justified. The link between her act and the wrong feels too diffuse, and democratic channels haven't been exhausted, though I don't think the wrongness is mainly about the procedural-exhaustion point.

5. The prison guard ordered to carry out a capital sentence he considers barbaric: justified to refuse. Yet this feels closer to case 2—an institutional role with explicit contrary duties—than to case 1, and I'm unsure whether I'm treating roles inconsistently.

6. The climate activists who blockade a functioning coal terminal for three days without having tried electoral or legislative remedies on this specific facility: I think they are justified, despite lacking the "last resort" feature standard accounts usually require. The directness and urgency of the harm seem to matter more to me than procedural exhaustion.

7. The citizen who refuses to answer a mandatory census question about household immigration status, believing the data will be used for deportation: justified, but structurally this feels almost identical to case 3, except that no classified disclosure is involved and the refusal is passive rather than active.

My tentative principle is that disobedience is justified when it directly prevents or exposes a serious rights-violation, and the agent's institutional role either permits resistance or fails to generate a specific contrary duty. But case 5 undermines this, since the guard's role seems to generate exactly such a contrary duty. Case 4 undermines it too: leaking documents is indirect—the bureaucrat doesn't stop surveillance herself—yet I endorse it, while tax withholding, also indirect, I reject. Case 6 undermines any "last resort" clause I might add.

Is there a coherent principle—perhaps turning on proximity, role-specificity, or the distinction between active and passive refusal—that saves cases 1, 3, 5, and 6 while excluding 2 and 4? Or must I drop one of my verdicts? In particular, how should I classify the case I keep returning to: a public-school teacher who refuses to administer a state-mandated standardized test she believes harms her students, accepting that she may be fired? Is that closer to case 2 (a voluntary role-duty violated) or to case 5 (a moral refusal within role)? And what does the best principle say about case 7 relative to case 3—does the absence of classified information or active disclosure make the citizen's refusal weaker, or is the moral structure the same?
