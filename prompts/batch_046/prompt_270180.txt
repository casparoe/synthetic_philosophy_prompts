I'm trying to identify whether there's an established label for a particular failure mode of introspective reporting that I've noticed in discussions of self-knowledge and emotional awareness. The pattern runs like this. A person initially experiences and reports an affective or cognitive state using only a coarse conceptual vocabulary—say, describing a complex reaction to another person's success as simply "feeling bad," or describing a recurrent pre-event tension as "being nervous." Later, they acquire a more fine-grained distinction—perhaps between envy and resentment, or between a specific anticipatory dread tied to identity-threat and general anxiety. After this acquisition, two things happen together. First, their present introspection becomes more differentiated: they now report the finer-grained state. Second, and more importantly, they retrospectively revise their autobiographical reports, claiming that prior episodes, which they originally described with the coarse term, "really" contained the now-distinguished quality all along. The prior, genuinely undifferentiated phenomenology becomes inaccessible; they don't merely say "I used to call it X but now I call it Y," but rather "I was experiencing Y then, I just didn't have the word for it." This produces an illusion of continuity in the conceptual content of their mental life, even though the differentiation was introduced by the conceptual change itself.

I've seen variants of this in contexts ranging from clinical descriptions of emotional granularity to philosophical debates about whether learning theoretical frameworks alters one's access to prior mental states. It seems adjacent to the theory-ladenness of introspection, to confabulatory reconstruction of past reasons, and to conceptual-change phenomena familiar from philosophy of science, but none of these captures the specific temporal structure: the backward projection of a newly available introspective category onto prior episodes that were genuinely reported differently at the time, combined with the present inability to recover the earlier, coarser experience.

Does this configuration have an established name—perhaps within research on introspective accuracy, autobiographical reconstruction, or the conceptual mediation of self-report? If not, how should it be carved out from nearby phenomena such as hindsight bias applied to mental states, source-monitoring errors about one's own past judgments, or the mere linguistic relabeling of an unchanged underlying experience? I'm less interested in a bare term than in a precise delineation: what exactly belongs inside the category, what lies outside it, and where its boundaries sit with respect to related failure modes of self-knowledge.
