I am preparing a seminar handout on the special composition question and want a diagnostic decision tree that sorts positions by their underlying metaphysical commitments rather than by the standard philosophical labels. Design the tree so that every internal node asks about a specific feature of composition: first, whether there is any genuine relation of composition or only derivative co-location; second, if there is such a relation, whether it requires a unifying principle (contact, cohesion, functional integration, or another) and whether that principle operates collectively or pairwise; third, whether the principle is gradable or all-or-nothing; fourth, whether the resulting whole possesses causal powers that are not reducible to the arranged powers of its parts; and fifth, whether the presence of the relation depends on an observer-independent fact about the parts or can vary with an explanatory framework. At each split, anchor the question in a concrete case — a protoplanetary disk gradually coalescing, a modular swarm robot collective with redundant nodes that can lose members without losing function, a human body undergoing complete cellular turnover over years — phrased so that a respondent can choose without already knowing which named view the answer implies. After laying out the tree, give a residue report: which pairs of leaves the tree cannot cleanly separate, whether the ambiguity lies in the branching criteria or in the hybrid positions themselves, and whether threshold pluralism collapses into restricted organicism or remains distinct. Keep the emphasis on the criteria and their motivating cases, not on historical exegesis. Finally, say how confident you are in the tree's architecture and identify specifically the branch or distinction about which you are least certain — particularly the boundary between threshold pluralism and restricted composition.
