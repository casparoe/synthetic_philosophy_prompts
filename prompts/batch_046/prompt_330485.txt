Read William Golding's Lord of the Flies through the lens of the unilateralist's curse, but apply it specifically to the erosion of democratic authority rather than to standard physical collective-action problems. On this reading, each boy who abandons Ralph's conch is not simply defecting for personal advantage; instead, each reasons independently that his own refusal to obey will not, in isolation, collapse the democratic order, and that he can share in the benefits of Jack's hunt without being the decisive cause of the breakdown. The curse takes hold because every boy reaches this identical unilateral judgment at roughly the same time, producing a collective outcome—the silencing of the conch and the ascendancy of violence—that no single boy intended or caused alone. Center your analysis on the scene in which Ralph is left with only Piggy and the twins: how does Golding dramatize the moment when unilateral reasoning has already hollowed out democratic authority before any formal vote dissolves it? Resist, however, reducing the novel to a mere illustration. Do Jack's charismatic authority and the boys' shared fear of the beast represent causal dynamics that the curse framework overlooks? Finally, ask what the novel's closing image—Ralph weeping for the darkness of man's heart—contributes to the philosophical debate that a formal model of collective defection, focused on rational calculation, cannot supply.
