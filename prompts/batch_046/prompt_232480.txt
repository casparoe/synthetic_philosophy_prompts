For an upper-division seminar on rational choice and decision theory (about 14 students, 90 minutes), design a structured, multi-round classroom game that makes participants feel the specific tension in ambiguity-sensitive choice between wanting information for its own sake and maintaining dynamic consistency when ambiguity is resolved in stages. Rather than reproducing the standard single-urn Ellsberg demonstration, inject the following niche structure: in one round participants must choose whether to pay a fixed number of points (drawn from a shared pool that determines a small collective reward, such as a snack budget or a charitable donation in their name) to learn the color-composition of an ambiguous urn before placing a bet, but the rules contractually bind them to the same bet regardless of what they learn; in a subsequent round the identical informational resolution is embedded as a compound lottery rather than offered as an explicit purchasable option. The activity should make participants discover that their willingness to pay for resolution collapses or reverses across these two framings, even though the physical outcomes are identical. Include: the physical materials and setup; explicit rules for all rounds; roles if any; a minute-by-minute timeline; a point-based payoff scheme requiring no real money; a debrief that maps each round onto the formal distinction between ambiguity aversion, non-instrumental information preference, and the reduction failure of compound lotteries; an honest prediction of what will go wrong in the first run and how to handle it; and a frank note on which feature of smooth ambiguity models or maxmin expected utility the physical game cannot reproduce and the facilitator must supply verbally. Do not ask for bibliographies, citations, or reading lists. Begin with a single sentence stating the core mechanism and the visceral insight it targets, then present the complete design—setting, rules, rounds, timeline, payoffs, debrief, predicted failures, and limits—immediately after, with no preamble, no commentary, and no restatement of the prompt.
