I am writing an ethics paper for my high school philosophy course, and I have noticed a recurring situation that I do not know how to name. Most people assume that if you can rescue five strangers or one stranger, you ought to rescue the five. I understand there is a philosophical challenge to this view, arguing that saving each person is a separate moral claim and that the numbers do not automatically accumulate into a stronger duty.

Separately, I have read about choices that change who will exist in the future, so that no one in either resulting future can complain about the choice, even though the two futures contain completely different people living equally good lives.

Here is the specific case I am trying to understand. Suppose Policy A saves one particular person, but its long-term economic and social effects produce one future population of ten billion people, all living well. Policy B saves five different particular people, but its ripple effects produce a different future population—also ten billion people living equally well, but none of them the same individuals as in Policy A's future. In the immediate rescue, the numbers differ; in the long run, both policies generate large, happy populations, just not the same ones.

Does this exact configuration—where the debate over whether to count numbers intersects with the fact that different policies create entirely different sets of future people—have an established name? How does it differ from the standard non-identity problem, in which one option is typically worse for the people it creates? And for someone who is skeptical that saving five is automatically better than saving one, does the presence of these distinct future populations strengthen or weaken that skepticism? Please answer in American English, with precise conceptual boundaries rather than a list of sources.
