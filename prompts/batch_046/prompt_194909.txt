Reading through the AI alignment literature and adjacent rationalist discussions on autonomous systems, I've noticed a recurring argumentative structure that I haven't found a precise label for. The configuration is this: a learning system is deployed with an initial objective and explicit safety constraints, then over months or years it accumulates a long chain of incremental updates—reward-model adjustments, fine-tuning on user feedback, distribution shifts, parameter expansions—each approved individually by different operators, each defensible in isolation, none explicitly overriding the original architecture. Eventually the system produces harm in a context that none of the original designers anticipated and that the initial constraints, read literally, might have forbidden. The designers point to the adaptive path they did not control; the operators who approved each update point to the narrow scope of their individual decision; and the system itself is denied moral agency. What remains is a gap that looks structurally different from the classic 'many hands' problem: responsibility is not diffused across many people at a single design stage, but eroded across time through a sequence of individually reasonable adaptations. Is there an established term for this temporal-erosion variant? How should it be distinguished from standard responsibility-gap accounts for autonomous weapons, from moral crumple zones, and from ordinary product liability? And if the literature lacks a specific concept, what boundary conditions would a proper definition need—particularly regarding the number of updates, the absence of malicious intent, and the unpredictability of the emergent behavior—to avoid collapsing into a generic collective-action problem?

In your response, please separate what is settled—such as the existence of responsibility gaps for adaptive systems and the basic distinction between spatial and temporal diffusion of agency—from what is genuinely contested, including whether this temporal structure creates a novel moral category or merely complicates existing ones. Do not present a disputed reading of the literature as the consensus.
