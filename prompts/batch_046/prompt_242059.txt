In my philosophy of history seminar, a classmate summarized Nietzsche's essay on the uses and disadvantages of history for life like this. Monumental history, she said, works by holding up great figures as direct models for imitation, so that studying the past becomes a kind of hero-worship that drives present action. Antiquarian history she described as purely nostalgic preservation—keeping traditions and archives intact with no regard for whether they serve life now, which makes it harmless but essentially useless. Critical history, she claimed, is the dangerous one: it judges and destroys past values to free the present, and Nietzsche considers it the worst of the three because it severs life from its roots. She concluded that Nietzsche ultimately sees all historical consciousness as a burden and advocates an ahistorical state of pure creative becoming, almost like a perpetual present tense of the will. I suspect she is conflating inspiration with imitation and overstating his hostility to history, but I am not sure where the line falls. Is this a fair summary? Please distinguish what accurately reflects Nietzsche's distinctions among the three modes, what are standard simplifications or subtle distortions—particularly regarding whether critical history is meant to be purely destructive or whether it serves life when properly bounded—and whether her conclusion that Nietzsche prescribes a fully ahistorical existence actually follows from his argument about the necessity of forgetting.
