For a grad seminar I'm running on metaphysics of science I'm trying to write down my own commitments on chaos and determinism so the students can tear them apart. Here's roughly where I've landed, but I want a consistency audit before I present it. I hold: (1) hard physical determinism — same laws plus same initial conditions yield identical trajectories, period. (2) Chaotic dynamics are fully deterministic but show sensitive dependence, so forecasting breaks down exponentially even with near-perfect measurements. (3) The familiar 'in principle predictable / in practice unpredictable' split is a mistake once you take embedded observers seriously — any predictor is part of the system, so there's no external vantage point (no Laplace demon) that escapes chaos constraints. So 'predictability in principle' is basically a confused idea for universes like ours. (4) Still, I think chaos actually strengthens determinism, since it explains apparent randomness without needing quantum indeterminacy. (5) I reject free will entirely, but I think chaos generates real 'epistemic openness': agents inside the system can rationally treat branching futures as live options, even though ontologically there's only one actual path. (6) This openness isn't just subjective illusion — it's structurally unavoidable — but it also doesn't ground agent-causation or libertarian freedom.

The tension I feel is between (3) and (2): if I collapse the in-principle/in-practice gap, am I still entitled to frame chaos as making forecasting fail specifically 'in practice'? And between (4) and (3): if chaos undermines the demon thought-experiment, doesn't that weaken a classic motivation for determinism? Meanwhile (5) sems like I'm trying to keep determinism but smuggle in something like practical openness that sounds suspiciously close to the free-will position I reject — except I do reject it in (5), so maybe (5) and (1) pull apart? Can you audit these pair by pair?
