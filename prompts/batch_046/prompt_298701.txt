I sit on the exam committee for our doctoral program in philosophy of science and metaphysics, and I need one 90-minute essay question for this year's comprehensive exam—worth 25 marks—on the tension between ascription universality and epistemic dominance. The question should be genuinely unanswerable by anyone who has only memorized standard survey material on universal properties or situated knowledge; to ensure that, I want you to build the prompt around a specific, self-contained conceptual framework that you define within the question itself: call it the "domain-transitive ascription thesis" (DTAT). According to DTAT, if a predicate P applies to entities in domain D based solely on structural-functional criteria C, then P applies with equal force to entities in any distinct domain D* that also satisfy C, regardless of differences in our epistemic access to D and D*. Against this, introduce an "epistemic-dominance constraint" (EDC): whenever our identification of C in D* is mediated by instruments, theories, or analogical projections developed within D, the ascription to D* is not structurally independent but conceptually colonized by D's framework, undermining the universality claim. Ask candidates to evaluate the following argument: (1) DTAT requires that satisfaction of C be detectable independently of the epistemic framework used to detect it; (2) but the EDC shows that detection of C in D* is always framework-dependent; (3) therefore DTAT either collapses into a mere analogy or requires an impossible "view from nowhere"; (4) since the view from nowhere is impossible, DTAT must be abandoned; (5) but abandoning DTAT forces us into domain-relativism about all cross-domain ascriptions, including the ascription of intentionality or computation. The prompt should require students to identify which step is weakest, propose a modified "restricted universality" principle that accommodates the EDC without collapsing into analogy, and apply this modification to a concrete case—say, whether predictive-processing structures warrant ascribing "belief-like states" to both biological organisms and large language models when only the former have evolutionary histories. Give a model answer that makes five specific conceptual moves, assign marks to each (totalling 25), and include a one-line note for each on the typical failure mode. Do not ask for citations, bibliographies, or references to specific texts; the assessment should depend entirely on reasoning with the framework as defined in the prompt.
