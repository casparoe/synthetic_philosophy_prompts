I'm a journalist covering science and ideas, and I'm preparing a feature on how historians and philosophers assign explanatory weight to non-human forces—pathogens, climate regimes, and volcanic eruptions—without reducing them to mere backdrop or inflating them into full human agents. I need a rigorous sorting instrument for intellectually serious but non-specialist readers.

Please take the role of a professional philosopher of history with expertise in historiography, philosophy of science, and the ethics of explanation. Before you build anything, spend a moment brainstorming: list the distinct stances historians have taken toward pathogenic, atmospheric, and tectonic forces, paying special attention to the difference between episodic "event-actors" (a pandemic, a volcanic winter) and persistent "regime-actors" (endemic disease ecologies, multi-decade climate patterns).

Then construct a decision-tree taxonomy that sorts positions on this question: What explanatory and narrative standing, if any, should pathogenic, climatic, and volcanic forces hold in historical explanation?

Your tree should have five decision nodes, each phrased as a concrete claim or case judgment—not as "do you like determinism?" The root should start from a specific diagnostic case, such as whether the Black Death should be narrated as reshaping feudal labor relations through its own dynamics, or whether the 1815 Tambora eruption and its climate aftermath should be credited with shaping the rebellions and artistic movements of 1816, or whether long-term regimes like the Little Ice Age should be treated as actors of a different kind from sudden pandemics.

Structure the branches as follows:
- Node 1: Do these forces ever possess explanatory priority independent of human institutional responses?
- Node 2: If yes, is that priority pure causal efficacy (no agency, no narrative presence), or does it involve distributed or relational agency without full human intentionality?
- Node 3: If pure causal efficacy, should episodic event-actors be treated differently from regime-actors?
- Node 4: If distributed agency is granted, does that necessarily entail environmental determinism—that human choice and institution-building are epiphenomenal?
- Node 5: Regardless of determinism, is there a decisive moral or political peril—specifically, that emphasizing these forces exculpates human agents such as colonial administrators or fossil-fuel economies—that should block attribution?

Each leaf should carry a name (use standard labels where they exist, such as "strict humanist historiography," "actor-network-style historiography," or "unnamed" if the combination is unoccupied) and exactly one sentence describing its most awkward commitment—the conclusion its defenders must swallow but usually avoid.

Also flag any pair of leaves your tree cannot cleanly separate, with a brief diagnosis: is the gap merely verbal, incoherent, or unexplored?

Keep the tone formal and polished, present the tree in readable indented or labeled form, do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations, and do not quote any text verbatim—paraphrase any thinker you mention. Write entirely in American English, and make sure the instrument sorts people by what they actually believe about cases and explanations, not by which label they prefer.
