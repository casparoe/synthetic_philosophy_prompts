At a philosophy of action reading group I help organize, two regular participants—Jordan and Riley—got deadlocked on a modified Frankfurt case and whether it genuinely undermines the principle that moral responsibility requires the ability to do otherwise. I want an outside adjudication of their exchange, move by move, not a summary of the literature.

Here is how it went.

(1) Jordan opened with a standard Frankfurt-style scenario: an agent, call her Sarah, is about to vote for Candidate X. A neuroscientist has implanted a device that will force her to vote X if she shows any neural precursor of voting otherwise, but Sarah deliberates freely and votes X on her own; the device never activates. Jordan said this is enough to show that alternative possibilities are unnecessary for responsibility—Sarah is clearly responsible, yet she could not have done otherwise.

(2) Riley objected with the familiar flicker-of-freedom reply: Sarah could still have shown the precursor sign, so a minimal alternative possibility remains present. Jordan conceded that technically yes, but argued that such a flicker—an involuntary neural blip that never reaches the level of action—is too insubstantial to ground the kind of robust “could have done otherwise” the principle demands.

(3) Riley then proposed a refinement meant to seal even that flicker. Imagine a “preemptive blocker” that does not wait for a precursor but structurally suppresses the very formation of any counterfactual deliberative pathway. Sarah can still introspect that she is deliberating, but she has no meta-cognitive access to the absence of the “not-X” pathway; from the inside her deliberation feels fully open. The blocker never activates because the alternative never arises. Riley asked whether this finally yields a clean case of responsible action with zero alternatives. Jordan pushed back hard: he claimed the blocker either eliminates Sarah’s agency altogether—making her a deterministic automaton—or it fails to close every structural flicker. His sharper charge was that Frankfurt-style arguments are circular: they preserve agency in the original case only by leaving some flicker untouched, and whenever you try to remove the flicker you remove agency too. So we never actually observe responsible action without alternatives.

(4) Riley countered that Jordan is conflating metaphysical openness—whether there is a physically available pathway to not-X—with phenomenological openness, the felt experience of deliberation. The blocker removes metaphysical alternatives while preserving phenomenological openness, which is all that sourcehood-based responsibility requires. If Jordan is right that sealing metaphysical alternatives necessarily kills agency, then compatibilism about free will is false across the board, not just in Frankfurt cases. Riley said Jordan’s circularity charge misses the point: the case is meant to isolate whether responsibility tracks being the source of the action rather than having open paths.

Please adjudicate their exchange point by point. For each of the four moves above, rate it from clearly mistaken to clearly correct and briefly justify the rating. Then say what both are missing—particularly whether there is a coherent middle position on the relationship between sourcehood and the absence of even structural flickers, and whether phenomenological openness can do the work Riley assigns it. Finally, give an overall verdict: who is more right, and on what specific issue does the disagreement actually turn?
