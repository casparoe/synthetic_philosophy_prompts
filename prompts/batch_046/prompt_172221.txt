I'm writing an essay for my seminar on rational agency and decision theory, and I would like comments on the argument sketched below.

Notes and thesis: The standard reading of Buridan's ass treats it as a failure of preference completeness—if the donkey is exactly equidistant from identical hay and water, rational-choice theory predicts paralysis, yet Buridan allows the will to break the tie arbitrarily. Rather than treating this as a mere patch, I want to argue that it exposes a deeper structure: in cases of perfect symmetry, preference is not prior to choice but constructed through it. I call this retroactive preference formation—the agent chooses without a pre-existing reason, then generates a justification that makes the choice appear reason-responsive. To test whether this is a genuine feature of agency or just a descriptive quirk, I draw an analogy with institutional tie-breaking protocols in emergency triage, where arbitrary rules (e.g., first-arrived-first-served) are adopted precisely to prevent the kind of paralysis Buridan describes. The analogy suggests that rational agency may depend on pre-commitment mechanisms or constructed decision rules rather than on fully articulated preference orderings.

Questions for you: First, is my characterization of Buridan's position accurate, or do I misrepresent his view of the will's arbitrariness by treating it as retroactive justification rather than as a direct exercise of volition? Second, does the triage analogy illuminate the philosophical point, or does it conflate institutional design with individual psychology? Third, is there a stronger objection—perhaps that if preferences are genuinely constructed post-hoc, then the normative force of choosing for reasons collapses into mere rationalization?

The essay is intended to be conceptual rather than historical; I am not asking for further readings or citations.
