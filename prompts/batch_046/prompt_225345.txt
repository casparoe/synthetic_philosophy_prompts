Recently I found myself facing a concrete personal decision—whether to lock myself into a long-term charitable pledge that I anticipate will feel irrational once the funds actually leave my account—which brought me back to Derek Parfit's hitchhiker scenario and the broader problem of rational irrationality. From this I have formed the view that in genuinely non-repeated cooperative settings, rationality can require adopting credible self-binding mechanisms that render ex post defection impossible, even when maintaining those mechanisms carries a net cost and even when the cooperative partner cannot retaliate, because the capacity to make credible commitments is itself part of rational agency rather than a mere instrumental add-on. I take this to apply not only to interpersonal promises but to institutional design and, more speculatively, to the architecture of artificial agents that must bind their own future weights.

What would genuinely count against this position? I want a structured assessment of its vulnerabilities: which empirical results in behavioral economics or decision theory, which formal distinctions (for instance, between diachronic and synchronic rationality, or between reasons-responsiveness and utility-maximization), and which conceptual objections would weaken or overturn the core claim rather than merely requiring adjustment? Please distinguish decisive defeaters—things that should make me abandon the view—from those that are only awkward. Specifically, address whether the position risks unfalsifiability if every observed failure of commitment is explained away as a defective mechanism rather than a rational choice to defect, and whether that insulation is a theoretical strength or weakness for a normative thesis. I also want to know what proponents of rational self-binding themselves concede as damaging, not only what critics allege. Include, if relevant, under-discussed angles—such as the implications for distributed algorithmic systems in which an agent pre-commits its future computational state—rather than standard repetitions of the prisoner's dilemma or generic critiques of expected utility theory. Please write your answer in American English.
