I'm working on the axiology of well-being, specifically tranquilist views that treat only the absence of craving and suffering as intrinsically valuable and regard all positive mental states as at best instrumentally so. I want to understand a conceptual difficulty that arises once we distinguish between what I will call "structural absence"—the non-arising of a craving because the psychological conditions for it are not present—and "achieved absence"—the cessation of an existing craving through suppression, satiation, or extinction. Does tranquilism possess the conceptual resources to treat these two modes of absence as normatively identical, or does the need to prefer achieved absence (e.g., the extinguished craving of a practitioner) over structural absence (e.g., the craving-free state of a being that never developed the capacity) smuggle in a positive valuation of the process, discipline, or residual mental structure that produces it? Furthermore, if tranquilism insists that only absence matters and cannot differentiate these cases without appealing to positive goods, does this collapse the view into negative hedonism, or does it instead expose an ambiguity in using "absence" as a normative primitive? Please explain this tension with precision, and indicate whether you think the ambiguity is resolvable within a purely negative axiology.
