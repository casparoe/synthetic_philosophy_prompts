I am presenting at a joint philosophy-of-medicine and clinical neuropsychology seminar next week. My thesis is that pain asymbolia is not best explained by a standard sensory-affective dissociation, but by a disruption in what I call practical salience mapping—the process by which a nociceptive signal acquires action-guiding significance without necessarily altering its hedonic tone. I draw from this that the badness of pain is not an intrinsic sensory quality but a mode of embodied urgency, which implies that clinical intervention should target integrative failure rather than sensory suppression alone.

The audience is split: about half are neurologists and pain clinicians who are skeptical of philosophical representationalism that does not change treatment protocols; the other half are philosophers of mind committed either to the IASP definition of pain or to affective essentialism, the view that pain is necessarily unpleasant. They value clinical utility and patient autonomy, and they are quick to dismiss metaphysical framing that lacks direct clinical payoff.
