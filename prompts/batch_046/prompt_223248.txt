I'm writing up my methodological commitments for a comparative-political-economy seminar and want a consistency audit before I present them. Here is my package: (1) I accept the broad institutionalist claim—associated with Acemoglu and Robinson—that inclusive institutions are the dominant long-run driver of national prosperity, but I reject settler-mortality-style instrumental-variable strategies because the exclusion restriction fails: colonial settlement patterns influenced health and education infrastructure directly, not solely through institutional channels. (2) I think geographic determinism of the Sachs variety—latitude, malaria ecology, soil quality—has been empirically undermined by the reversal of fortunes among colonized societies and by the divergence of North and South Korea, so geography is at best a background constraint, never a sufficient cause. (3) I think culture—norms of generalized trust, time preference, and social cooperation—plays a genuinely causal role, but only as a mediator that institutions shape and reproduce, never as an exogenous driver with independent explanatory power over macro-level growth paths. (4) I think we can have knowledge of macro-historical causation without randomized experiments, provided we treat our causal claims as counterfactual-stability judgments evaluated within a restricted reference class of structurally comparable societies, rather than as universal laws or as point-identified potential-outcomes statements. (5) Because institutional change is path-dependent with feedback loops—institutions shape culture, which then reinforces or undermines institutions—I think standard potential-outcomes notation is inadequate for evaluating counterfactual institutional reforms; we need dynamic structural causal models that allow bidirectional edges over time. (6) Despite all this macro-level causal talk, I am a methodological individualist about explanation: institutions matter only because they alter individual incentives, culture matters only because it alters individual beliefs and preferences, and the macro-regularities are real but ontologically reducible to aggregated individual-level mechanisms. Do these hang together? Specifically, does rejecting the main identification strategy in (1) leave me without evidential support for the institutionalist thesis I still want to defend in (4)? Is (3) stable once (5) allows culture to feed back into institutions, which seems to blur the mediator/confounder distinction I insisted on? Does (6) commit me to a reductionism that undermines the autonomy of the macro-causal claims in (4), or can the two be reconciled? And is there an apparent tension—perhaps between (2) and (4)—that dissolves once we distinguish background constraints from causal explanations? Please rank the genuine tensions by severity, identify the cheapest revision for each, say which single revision would clean up the most inconsistencies, and flag any commitments that only look incompatible but are not.
