I was listening to two colleagues in my philosophy of risk seminar argue about the unilateralist's curse — the idea that when any member of a group can take a risky action alone, the member with the lowest risk threshold effectively decides for everyone. They had reached an impasse over whether the curse applies to purely informational releases, like publishing a potentially dangerous algorithmic technique, or only to physical interventions like deploying a geoengineering project.

Maya argued that informational releases are the purest case of the curse. Once the information is out, it cannot be recalled; even if the original publisher deletes the paper, mirror sites and memory preserve it. Because publication is irreversible in practice, a single researcher with an unusually high tolerance for risk can force the collective into a dangerous state, regardless of how cautious everyone else is. She added that this is especially severe under what she called threshold divergence with private priors: if the risky actor genuinely assigns near-zero probability to catastrophe, no social signal of others' reluctance will update her belief, so the standard remedy — observing that others haven't acted — fails entirely.

Leo countered that this mischaracterizes informational acts. He said that in any realistic scientific community, knowledge is redundantly distributed; if one lab suppresses a result, others are likely to discover it independently within a short window. Therefore, publishing is not truly unilateral — it doesn't change the collective outcome in the counterfactual world where the publisher stays silent. He granted that physical deployment is different because it creates a novel, observable state of the world. But for information, Leo claimed, the curse collapses into a general problem of collective epistemology, not a special problem of unilateral action. He also pointed out that if Maya is right, then any attempt to regulate publication is itself a unilateral informational act — the regulator's decision to suppress is also irreversible and made by a single agent with her own risk threshold, so we haven't escaped the curse; we've just relocated it to the censor. Maya responded by distinguishing direct irreversibility from derivative irreversibility. Even if others would eventually rediscover the technique, the specific timing and framing of the first publication can shape the trajectory of development — for instance, by directing funding and talent toward a risky path before safeguards are built — so the publisher does change the collective outcome in a causally relevant way, even if the information eventually becomes common knowledge. She also challenged Leo's regulatory point: institutional barriers aren't made by a single agent with private priors, but by deliberative bodies with diverse membership and sunset clauses, which structurally raise the effective threshold. Leo wasn't convinced. He said that Maya's distinction smuggles in a normative claim about the value of delaying discovery, which is exactly what's being disputed. If the only harm is that development happens faster, then the curse is just disagreement about the optimal speed of progress, not a structural failure of collective choice. He concluded that unless Maya can identify a harm genuinely contingent on the specific act of publication — not just on the eventual existence of the knowledge — she hasn't shown that informational releases fall under the curse at all.

Please adjudicate this disagreement point by point. Where is Maya right about the structure of the curse? Where does Leo correctly identify a collapse or relocation problem? Is Maya's distinction between direct and derivative irreversibility a genuine refinement or a normative smuggling? And does Leo's challenge about the optimal speed of progress successfully dissolve the curse for informational acts, or does it miss something about collective agency that Maya is trying to capture? Give a clear verdict on who is more right overall, and say what both are missing — perhaps about how collective reasoning or institutional design interacts with the curse when many independent actors face asymmetric information.
