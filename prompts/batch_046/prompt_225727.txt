I have been trying to determine my own commitments regarding origin essentialism—specifically, whether I could have come from a different sperm and egg—by examining my intuitive reactions to concrete scenarios rather than relying on standard thought experiments alone. Here is where I land. If my parents had conceived naturally a month earlier, with a different sperm fertilizing the same egg, I am quite sure the resulting child would not have been me. The same holds, I think, for an IVF case in which my mother’s egg was fertilized with donor sperm and then implanted: even though my mother gestated the child, the different paternal origin means it is not me. So far, the specific gamete pair seems decisive.

Yet the pattern becomes murkier when I vary the origin in more subtle ways. In actual monozygotic twinning, I feel there is no hidden fact about which twin “got” my identity—had the other embryo been the one implanted, that twin could just as well have been me, despite our being causally distinct cell lines with near-identical genomes. By contrast, imagine a hypothetical technique of artificial gametogenesis in which scientists derive sperm and egg from my parents’ induced pluripotent stem cells, fertilize them, but select a different recombination pairing that happens to yield exactly my nuclear genome; despite the genomic match, I am inclined to say that child is not me, because the particular sperm-egg pairing was not the one that actually occurred. Meanwhile, in three-parent IVF involving pronuclear transfer—where my parents’ nuclear pronuclei are moved into an enucleated donor egg with foreign cytoplasm—I feel that replacing only the cytoplasmic origin does not change whether the child is me, so long as the nuclear DNA traces back to my parents’ original fertilization. I am therefore pulled between thinking that what fixes my origin is the specific fertilization event, and thinking that it involves the causal continuity of the nuclear genome, since I allow either twin to be me in the splitting case but reject an identical genome when it arises from a different pairing.

Is there a single principle—perhaps centered on the causal origin of the nuclear genome, or on the specific fertilization event, or on something like the continuity of a particular developmental trajectory—that can accommodate all five of these verdicts without ad hoc adjustments? If not, which of my reactions should I treat as the outlier and revise? And applying whatever principle survives: in the pronuclear-transfer scenario I mentioned—where the reconstructed embryo carries my parents’ nuclear DNA but develops inside cytoplasm from an unrelated donor—would that child be me? And does your principle also predict whether, in monozygotic twinning, both twins could be me if implanted in different surrogates, or whether at most one can be?
