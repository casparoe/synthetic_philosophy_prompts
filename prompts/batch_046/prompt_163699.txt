Question: The problem of universals is about how different particulars can share a property, like two red apples both being red. Realists say there's one universal in both; nominalists deny that. One newer angle treats universals as 'structural slots' that a particular fills only relative to a descriptive framewrok, not as entities sitting inside things. How does that relate to the classic one-over-many problem? Can it dodge the standard realist objection about an instantiation regress, and the nominalist objection that resemblance theories are circular?

Student answer:
The one-over-many problem is that if redness is literally the same thing in apple A and apple B, we need to explain how one thing can be in two places at once. Platonic realists put universals outside space and time, but then you need a participation relation, and that can start a regress — you need a relation to relate the relation. Aristotelian immanent realists put the universal inside each particular, but they still need an instantiation relation, and some philosophers say that relation has to be a primitive non-relational tie or else you get the same regress.

The structural slot view says there's no single redness entity shared by both apples. Instead, being red is just filling the 'red-slot' in the descriptive framework we use. So it's closer to nominalism, since it denies there's one shared thing. But unlike resemblance nominalism, it doesn't say apple A is like apple B in some primitive way. Instead, both fill the same slot in the framework. That means it avoids the circularity problem with resemblance, because we never need to invoke a universal of similarity — the framework does all the work.

Also, since there's no universal being instantiated, there's no instantiation relation, and therefore no regress. The particular just stands in a 'filling-relation' to the slot, and that relation is part of the framework, not a metaphysical tie. So in a way this view borrows Aristotle's idea of a primitive tie but applies it to the framework-particular link, which lets it dodge both the realist and nominalist objections at once.

That said, I think the view doesn't fully solve the one-over-many problem. It explains why we call both apples red, but it doesn't explain why they are genuinely the same in nature — it just pushes the sameness into the framework. So it's more of a semantic solution than an ontological one. But as an ontology, it's basically Aristotelian realism moved up a level: the slots are just immanent universals that live in the framework rather than in the apples. So it doesn't really avoid multiplying entities; it just hides them in the descriptive system.
