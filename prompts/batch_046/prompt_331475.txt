Take on the role of an experienced philosophy podcast interviewer preparing a rigorous colloquium-style Q&A. Your guest is an unnamed philosopher who defends a restricted variant of David Benatar's asymmetry argument. On her view, the familiar asymmetry—where the presence of suffering is bad and its absence is good even for non-existent people, while the presence of pleasure is good but its absence is not bad—applies with special force in what she calls "high-variance reproductive contexts." These are situations in which prospective parents can predict with reasonable epistemic confidence that a future child would experience severe suffering, but cannot predict with comparable confidence that the child would experience proportionate well-being. She argues that in these contexts the asymmetry generates a decisive presumption against reproduction, and she uses this to defend mandatory genetic-counseling protocols that treat foreseeable severe harm as morally prohibitive while treating the prospect of great pleasure as morally inert.

Before drafting the interview, briefly brainstorm three pressure points on this position: first, whether the claimed epistemic gap between predicting suffering and predicting pleasure is genuine or merely an artifact of how we measure and report distress versus flourishing; second, whether her restricted view actually depends on Benatar's metaphysical asymmetry at all, or whether it collapses into ordinary risk-averse consequentialism that weighs probable harms more heavily than probable goods; and third, what her view implies for parents who, after conception but before birth, learn that the fetus carries a condition strongly associated with suffering—does the asymmetry then generate a duty to terminate, or does the shift from potential to actual personhood alter the moral calculus?

Then draft a sequence of eight to ten interview questions that move from clarification to direct pressure on these three points. For each pressure point, include at least one direct question and one follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give—for instance, if she appeals to an asymmetry in our evidential access to suffering versus pleasure, press whether that same evidential humility undermines her confidence in forecasting suffering; if she insists the metaphysical asymmetry is doing independent work, ask what normative difference it makes that risk-weighting alone could not capture. The questions should avoid generic biographical openings, should not request bibliographies, reading lists, or citations, and should treat the guest's position with philosophical precision rather than rhetorical dismissal. End with a single concluding question asking what kind of empirical finding or conceptual argument—about consciousness, about value comparison, or about the metaphysics of potential people—would cause her to abandon the restricted asymmetry entirely. Think carefully about the logical structure of her view and anticipate her standard replies before writing each follow-up.
