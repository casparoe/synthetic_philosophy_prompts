Here are preliminary notes for an essay I am drafting: I want to examine whether 'Middle Ages,' 'Renaissance,' and 'Scientific Revolution' denote real historical structures or are retrospective constructions, but I want to avoid the usual real-versus-invented dichotomy. Instead, I propose borrowing Richard Boyd's homeostatic property cluster (HPC) theory from philosophy of biology. On this view, the 'Scientific Revolution' might stabilize around a cluster of practices—mathematized natural philosophy, institutionalized peer critique, and printed dissemination—without possessing an essence, so that figures like Paracelsus share the temporal location but fall outside the cluster. The 'Renaissance,' by contrast, seems to fail the HPC test: its supposed cluster of humanist philology, artistic naturalism, and civic republicanism does not co-occur stably across Italy, Northern Europe, and Iberia, which suggests it functions less as a descriptive kind and more as a Kantian regulative ideal—a normative horizon that directs how we should read discontinuities rather than describing an actual regime. The 'Middle Ages' presents the opposite difficulty: enormous temporal span coupled with weak internal coherence, perhaps operating as a residual category defined negatively against classical antiquity and modernity. My central question is whether this framework genuinely resolves the periodization debate or merely relocates it: if these labels are HPC kinds, then the question of their reality becomes whether their clustering properties support counterfactual inferences about cultural change. I worry that the biological analogy may smuggle in ontological commitments that working historians reject, and that treating the Renaissance as a regulative ideal risks conflating classificatory practice with evaluative judgment. Would you please comment specifically on three things: whether the HPC analogy holds or collapses under historiographical pressure; whether I have understated specialist objections to treating these three periods as clusters rather than narratives; and whether the normative-horizon reading of the Renaissance introduces a confusion between how we classify and how we evaluate? I am not seeking a bibliography, but conceptual sharpening.
