Having retired from a long career in experimental physics and only recently begun a serious study of philosophy, I have been working through F. H. Bradley's Appearance and Reality, particularly the chapters concerned with the unity of the proposition and the status of relations. Bradley argues, as I understand him, that if a relation is treated as a distinct entity linking terms—say, R connecting A and B—then R itself must be related to A and to B by further relations, and those relations in turn require still others, producing an infinite regress. From this he infers that relations cannot be real as independent entities and that genuine unity is an undivided whole in which terms and connections are not externally mediated.

What I cannot clearly see is whether this regress is logically unavoidable or whether it depends on an unstated assumption that I find suspicious in light of my scientific background. I am inclined to call this assumption a binding postulate: the claim that any composite structure requires a discrete, separately identifiable mediator to unite its parts. In physics one often encounters unified phenomena—coherent coupling between oscillators, for example—where correlation holds without any intervening linking mechanism being independently isolable. This leads me to ask: does Bradley's regress begin only if one accepts this binding postulate? Could it be avoided not by denying relations altogether, but by treating relational connection as intrinsic to the terms, so that no external binder is required and the hierarchy of mediators never arises?

I would be grateful for help in understanding whether Bradley's argument necessarily presupposes such a postulate, or whether his reasoning proceeds independently of it. If the postulate is indeed essential, does that mean his conclusion about the unreality of relations is weaker than it appears, leaving conceptual space for a form of relational unity that does not collapse into an infinite regress of mediators?
