I'm working through an argument for my distributive justice seminar, and I keep returning to a chain that seems to show Derek Parfit's prioritarian view collapses into the very leveling-down position it was meant to avoid. The instructor emphasized that prioritarianism weights benefits by a concave function of absolute well-being but denies that equality itself has intrinsic value; I think my reconstruction respects that distinction, yet it reaches a conclusion that looks indistinguishable from radical egalitarian harm. Here is the argument as I have reconstructed it. (1) Prioritarianism evaluates social states solely by a weighted sum of individual well-being levels, where the moral weight of a benefit is a strictly decreasing, continuous function of the recipient's baseline level. (2) From this I derive what I will call the Well-Being Equivalence Thesis: if two distinct interventions produce exactly the same change in the total weighted sum, they are normatively interchangeable, regardless of whether the change is achieved by transferring goods, altering obligations, or reducing one person's well-being with no compensating gain elsewhere. (3) Consider a two-person society with A at well-being level 90 and B at level 10. Because the weight function is continuous, there exists some reduction r in A's level—say, from 90 to 70—whose negative contribution to the weighted sum is exactly matched in magnitude by the positive contribution of raising B by some corresponding amount g—say, from 10 to 20. (4) By the Well-Being Equivalence Thesis, the state in which A is leveled down to 70 and B remains at 10 is therefore morally on a par with the state in which A stays at 90 and B is raised to 20, since the two states yield identical prioritarian values. (5) But this means that actively harming the best off, with no one made better off, can be exactly as good as genuinely improving the worst off—precisely the leveling-down objection that prioritarianism was introduced to escape. (6) Hence, if prioritarianism accepts both its weighting structure and the continuity that makes (3) possible, it must treat pure leveling-down as sometimes required or at least permissible. (7) Therefore, prioritarianism either abandons its core aggregation rule or surrenders to the leveling-down objection.

I feel the slip is somewhere between (3) and (6), possibly an equivocation between arithmetic equality in the social welfare function and normative equivalence across different causal structures, or perhaps a hidden quantifier shift from "there exists some matching pair" to "this holds generally for all reductions." Yet (2) looks like a direct consequence of treating the weighted sum as the sole criterion of evaluation, and rejecting (4) seems to require smuggling in an independent, non-prioritarian concern for how the distribution was reached. If I keep (1) and (5), which step should I reject, and what does giving it up cost me—does it save prioritarianism only by making it ad hoc, or does it push me back toward a form of utilitarianism that ignores absolute levels altogether?
