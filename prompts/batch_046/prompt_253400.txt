I've been reading popular and some archaeological accounts of the so-called collapses of the Bronze Age Mediterranean, the Classic Maya lowlands, and Easter Island, and I'm struck by how the same material—abandoned ceremonial centers, soil erosion, disrupted trade—gets read either as catastrophic societal failure or as gradual political reorganization and population redistribution. One camp seems to treat the disappearance of particular monuments and dynasties as proof that these societies imploded under ecological or internal pressure; the other insists the people never disappeared but simply moved, changed political forms, or adapted to new conditions, and that labeling this "collapse" imposes a modern narrative of failure onto what was really transformation. The disagreement has persisted for decades without either side conceding much ground, and my working theory is that the deadlock is not primarily about new excavations or radiocarbon dates but about what continuity of a society actually requires—whether it means the survival of a specific state apparatus, stable population levels, continuous cultural practices, or merely the biological and linguistic persistence of the same people under different institutions. I want a diagnosis rather than a survey of collapse theories: is this a verbal dispute over the word "collapse," a hidden normative disagreement about whether change counts as failure, or a substantive empirical disagreement that could in principle be settled by demographic modeling or settlement-pattern analysis? Is there a concealed formal question—perhaps about how to measure societal complexity across discontinuities—doing the hidden work? And if the impasse is mainly conceptual, what specific distinction, reframing, or change of question could move it forward, or is the framework itself ill-posed?
