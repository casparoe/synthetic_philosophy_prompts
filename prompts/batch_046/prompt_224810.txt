Draft the joint statement that an Integrationist camp and a Separatist camp could both sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration on Tolstoy's aesthetics, as developed in his major essay on art. The Integrationists hold that Tolstoy's communicative definition of art—art as the infection or transmission of feeling from artist to audience—is conceptually inseparable from his moral evaluation, so that what he terms counterfeit art is not merely bad art but art that either fails to genuinely infect or infects with feelings he judges vicious, with no stable intermediate category between successful good infection and failure. The Separatists hold that Tolstoy offers a purely descriptive account of art as emotional transmission and then applies an independent normative filter, so that a work can be descriptively successful art—genuinely infecting its audience—while being normatively bad, or normatively admirable while being descriptively weak.

The document should contain:
1. A numbered section of agreed claims about Tolstoy's framework: that he defines art through emotional infection rather than through formal properties; that he requires sincerity in the artist; that he distinguishes real art from counterfeit art; that he evaluates works like religious icons positively and works like Wagner's operas negatively; and that he intends his account to apply across media.
2. The precise disagreements, each phrased in wording that its own camp would accept. One formulation should capture the Integrationist claim that the moral criterion is built into the conditions for successful infection, and another should capture the Separatist claim that infection and moral evaluation operate on independent axes.
3. The cruxes—sub-questions whose resolution would move at least one camp. Include at least one crux about whether Tolstoy's sincerity condition functions as an epistemic check on the artist's actual mental state or as a moral filter on the feeling's content; and at least one about whether there can be successful infection with vicious feelings that Tolstoy still classifies as art rather than as counterfeit.
4. For each crux, specify a concrete artistic case or conceptual scenario both camps agree is relevant—for example, a technically unsophisticated religious image that moves viewers profoundly, or a brilliantly executed work that evokes destructive pride—and record in advance what each camp predicts Tolstoy would classify it as.
5. A brief analysis of whether part of the dispute is merely verbal, particularly regarding whether counterfeit art denotes a distinct ontological failure of transmission or is simply Tolstoy's term for art that succeeds descriptively but fails morally.

In drafting, inject the following somewhat niche conceptual distinction not usually central to standard commentary: the problem of second-order emotional awareness, in which an audience's recognition that they are being emotionally infected either amplifies or dissolves the infection. Ask both camps whether Tolstoy's infection model can accommodate this reflexive layer without collapsing, and whether his evaluation of a work changes if the infection operates at this second-order level.

The response should state how confident it is in its reconstruction of Tolstoy's views and should single out the specific point about which it is least sure—likely either the role of second-order awareness in Tolstoy's framework or the classification of one of the concrete cases.

Write in formal, polished prose. Do not provide a bibliography, reading list, or citations. Do not include direct quotations from Tolstoy's text unless you have fetched and verified the passage; otherwise paraphrase without quotation marks. Do not depend on specific edition details.
