The problem: hedonism claims that pleasure is the only thing that makes life better, but pleasures seem wildly different—eating something sweet, finishing a hard puzzle, feeling relief when a headache stops. Philosophers call this the heterogeneity problem: if these experiences share nothing, then pleasure is not one thing, and hedonism collapses. Most discussions I have seen try to solve this by searching for a single hidden feel common to all pleasures. I want to take a different angle. I propose splitting pleasure into two independent dimensions that people usually conflate: (1) an immediate sensory-affective quality (the raw feeling), and (2) a broader evaluative stance that something is good or welcome right now (a kind of this-is-right-for-me judgment or pro-attitude). My core question is whether the heterogeneity problem actually dissolves once we separate these, or whether it just relocates—specifically, whether hedonism can survive if it counts only one dimension as the currency of well-being, and whether that choice makes hedonism either implausibly narrow (missing the satisfaction of solving puzzles) or implausibly broad (counting mere relief from pain).

Please structure the proposal as follows:

1. A precise statement of the research question.
2. Why existing answers—the common-feel view and the pro-attitude view—leave this open, without relying on obscure recent literature.
3. The method: what I would actually do week by week over three months. This should include careful conceptual analysis of well-being texts, constructing original thought experiments comparing subjects who have sensory pleasure without evaluative satisfaction and vice versa, and mapping the logical space of possible hedonist responses.
4. What a successful result looks like (a clear taxonomy showing which dimension hedonism needs, with explicit costs and benefits of each option) and what failure looks like (the distinction collapses, or it turns out to be purely verbal with no practical consequence).
5. An honest assessment of scope: why this is doable in three months without experiments, and what could make it too big.
6. A brief argument for why a non-specialist foundation should fund this—specifically, how clarifying what feeling good means could improve public-health well-being metrics that currently treat pleasure as a single score.
