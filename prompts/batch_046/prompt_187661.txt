I've recently been working on evaluation protocols for reasoning in large language models, and I've found myself defending a position stronger than I initially intended: that Dennett's intentional stance, when applied to these systems, is not merely a predictive convenience but explanatorily indispensable for a specific and limited domain—namely, the normative structure of how models correct or maintain consistency across extended contexts when confronted with contradictory premises. Lower-level descriptions, whether in terms of weights, attention dynamics, or token-level probabilities, seem able to reproduce the outputs but fail to capture why certain contradictions are resolved in particular directions and others are ignored; attributing belief-like representations with propositional content appears to be the only framework that predicts these selective repair patterns with the right kind of generality. I want an honest map of what would count against this claim—what specific results from mechanistic interpretability, what patterns of model failure, or what conceptual arguments about representation would show that the stance is only instrumentally useful and misses nothing essential?

Please sort the potential defeaters by how decisive they would be, from fatal to merely awkward: for instance, would it be decisive if researchers demonstrated that every instance of apparent consistency-repair could be reconstructed from low-level feature correlations without invoking propositional content, or merely awkward if the stance turned out to apply only to certain architectures or training regimes? I also want to know what defenders of this view—including, presumably, myself—would concede as genuinely damaging versus what they would dismiss as missing the point: perhaps the absence of any stable cross-prompt representational structure, or perhaps the failure of belief-attribution to generalize to out-of-distribution contexts. Finally, is the view vulnerable to being qualified into unfalsifiability—say, by retreating to the claim that the stance applies only wherever it works—and if so, does that undermine its status as a claim about explanation rather than prediction?
