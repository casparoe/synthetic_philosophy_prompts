I'm taking a course on practical ethics and we got into this argument about whether choosing between incommensurable goods is always a loss, or if sometimes the choice itself creates something new. My prof mentioned Ruth Chang and Bernard Williams but then kind of moved on, and I keep thinking about this idea I made up — or maybe read somewhere? — called "value residue accumulation." The idea is that when an institution picks option A over B, and A and B are genuinely incommensurable (not just hard to compare on one scale), the value of B doesn't just vanish; it sticks around as a kind of moral debt or structural obligation that shapes future decisions. So if a university demolishes an old community center to build an accessible one, the accessibility win doesn't cancel out the loss of historical continuity — it just means the institution now owes something to that lost value in its future planning.

Can you describe the most important implications of this "value residue accumulation" framing for how institutions should actually make hard choices? I'm especially interested in: (1) whether it changes how we think about regret and compensation after a decision, (2) if it gives us a reason to avoid hard choices altogether by trying to split the difference, and (3) whether small organizations with limited budgets face a different kind of residue problem than big ones.
