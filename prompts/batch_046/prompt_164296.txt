Imagine that a state legislature enacts an "Algorithmic Originalist Protocol" for its appellate courts: every judicial opinion must begin with a binding interpretation generated by a machine-learning system trained exclusively on state constitutional texts and judicial records predating 1868, which human judges may modify only with a legislative supermajority. Proponents argue that this restores the original public meaning of the Constitution and prevents arbitrary judicial innovation; opponents contend that it substitutes an automated, pre-emancipation legal ontology for the living constitutional judgment that citizens must actively maintain, thereby undermining the substantive promise of the Reconstruction Amendments. Drawing on Frederick Douglass's well-known intellectual trajectory—from his early view of the Constitution as a pro-slavery instrument to his later conviction, especially after the Thirteenth, Fourteenth, and Fifteenth Amendments, that the federal government must actively protect citizenship rights through evolving moral and legal interpretation—predict how he would likely assess this protocol. Specifically, would he see the AI-mediated restriction on post-1868 interpretive development as an act of constitutional fidelity, or as a mechanized evasion of the moral obligations he believed the Reconstruction framework placed upon judges and citizens alike? Address in particular whether his insistence that natural rights precede and constrain positive law would lead him to reject the protocol's implicit claim that textual fidelity can be automated without human moral judgment.
