I have recently joined the governing board of a cooperative founded in 1923, and I am now in regular conflict over whether to abolish an elaborate membership-vetting procedure whose purpose is now obscure to everyone on the board. To address this, I want to lead a single thirty-minute session for my fellow board members—mostly scientists and educators, none with philosophical training—explaining why Burke's idea of prescription and Chesterton's fence are not appeals to nostalgia but address a specific epistemic problem: we may not be able to reconstruct why an institution contains a particular feature. I need a teaching plan rather than a lecture script. Specify the order in which the conceptual pieces must arrive so that Burkean conservatism is not mistaken for a blanket prohibition on reform, identify the one carrying example that should illustrate both visible costs and hidden institutional dependencies and explain exactly what properties it must possess and what it must lack, map the misconceptions this audience will predictably generate—especially that Chesterton demands absolute stasis, that Burke opposes all change, and that the argument is merely risk aversion—and state the precise point in the sequence where each should be defused, propose a concrete check at the end that reveals whether they have grasped the principle rather than merely assented politely, and state explicitly what should be left out at this level. Give committed answers throughout: take definite positions on the sequence, the example, and the misconception map, with reasons, and do not offer me a menu of pedagogical options.
