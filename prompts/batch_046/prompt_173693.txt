ok so i've been playing with this somewhat niche read of Machiavelli where virtù isn't just personal guts or charisma but specifically the skill of building instutional structures that soak up fortuna's shocks — less like wrestling the river and more like building the banks and levees. the idea is that a ruler with real virtù doesn't just surf luck; he structurally lowers the varianc in things like tax collection or logistics, pushing fortuna out of whole policy domains rather than just taming it case by case. so machiavelli comes out less fatalist, more like he thinks design can actually quarantine randomness.

what'd count against this? not the usual handwaving about fortune governing half our actions — i want the defeaters specific to this institutional-virtù version. are there spots in the Discourses or The Prince where he treats institutions as just as brittle as people? historical examples he uses where well-built systems still collapsed because some deeper contingency got through? and outside the texts: what'd actual state-building patterns have to look like to undermine the claim that design can isolate luck?

rank them: fatal if institutional virtù collapses back into personal virtù, awkward if it just needs boundary conditions. also flag whether defenders of this read would concede any of these or just reinterpret them away. and be honest — does this make machiavelli sound too much like a modern institutionalist, and if so is that a real problem for the interpretation or just an aesthetic one? also i keep wondering if there's a hidden circularity: we call the design virtuous only when it succeeds, so is the view unfalsifiable? i want that addressed too.
