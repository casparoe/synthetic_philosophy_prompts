Benjamin Franklin pursued moral perfection by rotating through thirteen virtues in weekly cycles, recording failures in a private grid, and accepting that perfect attainment was impossible but the pursuit itself ennobling. Suppose he encountered today’s quantified-self ecosystems—wearable trackers, AI behavioral nudges, and social-media reputation scores that automatically monitor and reward virtues like frugality or humility. Would he regard algorithmic virtue-optimization as a natural extension of his chart method, or as a corruption of the voluntary, self-directed struggle he considered essential? Address specifically how he might adapt his rotational system to what we could term “attention-economy moral fatigue”—the erosion of tranquility caused by constant external evaluation—and whether such fatigue reveals a limit in his original framework.
