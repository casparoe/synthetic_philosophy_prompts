I am writing a novel set in a city with long-standing rent stabilization, and I have become preoccupied by a specific institutional design that my characters must navigate. Under the regime I am depicting, a residential lease may be transmitted to a surviving spouse or child upon the tenant's death—an inheritance of occupancy—yet the same lease may not be assigned to an unrelated party for consideration, even if both parties freely consent. Simultaneously, the statute caps annual rent increases well below inflation, but permits landlords to recover capital improvement costs through amortized surcharges calculated on a fixed schedule of useful life, as though the building were a depreciating capital asset rather than a social entitlement. The practice therefore treats the dwelling as both an inalienable hereditary benefit and a revenue-producing investment, with no explicit theory reconciling these two frames. What view of property, contractual freedom, and intergenerational obligation does this institutional architecture implicitly presuppose? Is it coherent with any familiar theory—perhaps a distinction between use-rights and exchange-rights, or a conception of housing as a primary good—without contradiction? And if one were to replace its implicit philosophy with a uniform welfarist calculus that treated every unit purely as a flow of welfare to be maximized, which concrete provisions—the succession rules, the amortization schedules, the prohibition on assignment—would become philosophically indefensible, and what institutional replacements would be required?
