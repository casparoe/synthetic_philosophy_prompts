Critics of hedonism frequently invoke Nozick's experience machine through an analogy with immersive virtual reality: both substitute a designed internal experience for an externally caused one, and the widespread reluctance to enter either is said to demonstrate that well-being requires more than favorable mental states — specifically, some form of genuine contact with reality. Lay out the correspondence table precisely: what corresponds to the machine's one-time entry decision, its irreversible wiring, the designer's script, the absence of other people inside, and the user's pre-existing projects? Then identify the disanalogies that matter: virtual reality is typically reversible, intermittent, and socially shared, whereas the machine is total and isolating; does this difference undermine the intuition being borrowed? Next, ask whether the analogy smuggles in a hidden normative premise — that well-being must be modally robust, stable across counterfactual breaks in the causal chain — which a hedonist can reject without contradiction. Conclude by judging whether the anti-hedonist argument survives once these breaks and hidden premises are exposed, or whether the analogy was carrying the conclusion all along.
