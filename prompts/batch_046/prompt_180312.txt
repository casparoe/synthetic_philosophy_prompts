Design a 90-minute seminar activity for about 12 philosophy graduate students that makes them feel why the standard conditions of deception, coercion, and incompetence fail to capture a deeper problem in consent theory: that the very framing of options can structurally shape or undermine the competence required for valid consent, and that stripping away one frame always installs another. Structure it as a role-play with hidden incentives, not a lecture. Divide participants into pairs: one plays a “protocol designer” who must present a fictional high-stakes choice (enrollment in an experimental treatment with genuinely ambiguous risk profiles) so that the other can consent; the other plays the “potential consenter,” who receives only what the designer discloses but also holds a private “vulnerability card” (e.g., a hidden cognitive-load condition or trauma trigger that makes certain informational formats incapacitating rather than clarifying). Designers do not know their partner’s card. Give each designer three point rewards—speed of agreement, completeness of disclosure, emotional neutrality—but assign hidden “institutional mandate” cards that weight these rewards differently, so no designer can maximize all three. After 20 minutes of negotiation, pairs record whether consent was given and on what terms. Only then reveal the mandates and vulnerability cards. The debrief should map specific moments onto the three standard conditions: where framing seemed neutral but exploited a hidden vulnerability (competence), where full disclosure overwhelmed decision capacity (a coercion-like effect), and where institutional incentives forced omission or emphasis (deception). State clearly what will predictably go wrong on the first run—designers will hoard information to protect points, partners will experience the hidden cards as unfair—and how to handle it. Finally, be explicit about which feature of structural framing the activity reproduces well—the inescapability of framing choices under institutional pressure—and which feature it cannot reproduce without a longer timescale: the sedimentation of framing norms into apparently objective background conditions that later generations take as given. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations.
