I have recently been forced to examine the architecture of my own decision-making under sustained professional pressure, and I have come to hold a specific view about free will and mechanical rigidity—one that attempts to avoid both libertarian metaphysics and eliminativist despair. The view, which I will call graded sphexish functionalism, holds that free will is not a global property of persons but a local property of particular actions, determined not by consciousness or causal origin but by the presence of what I term open-loop corrective branching: the capacity of a cognitive sub-routine to be revised in real time by feedback that was not part of its original trigger conditions. Sphexishness, on this account, is not mere behavioral simplicity but closed-loop stereotypy—fixed sequences that run to completion regardless of intermediate environmental variation. Moral responsibility, I maintain, should attach only to actions that exhibit open-loop branching, and this relocates the free-will problem from ontology to the taxonomy of cognitive architectures.

What would count against this view? I want an honest map of its exposure, not a defense. Which empirical findings about human cognition—concerning the actual flexibility of expert performance, the rigidity of moral reasoning under stress, or the extent of unconscious preprocessing—would be decisive rather than merely awkward? Which philosophical arguments—about the necessity of alternative possibilities for responsibility, about the coherence of attaching moral status to action-types rather than agents, or about whether any branching can be genuinely open if the revision rules are themselves determined—would force me to abandon or severely qualify the position? What does this view predict that rival accounts (for instance, global compatibilism, sourcehood theories, or eliminativism) do not, and have those predictions already been implicitly tested? I am particularly concerned with whether the distinction between open-loop and closed-loop architectures can be maintained without collapsing into either a behaviorist taxonomy or a disguised version of the very libertarianism it seeks to avoid. If defenders of this view—including myself—are tempted to insulate it by redefining “branching” whenever a counterexample appears, is that insulation a theoretical virtue or a fatal methodological vice? Please rank the defeaters: those that would kill the view outright, those requiring substantial revision, and those that are only embarrassing. And indicate which of these I, as a committed holder, should concede as genuinely damaging rather than dismissible, including whether the view has already been qualified into unfalsifiability and whether that is a problem for a position of this kind.
