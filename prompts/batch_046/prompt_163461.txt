I've been taking this course on bounded rationality and we talked about Herbert Simon's idea of satisficing, which my prof described as picking the first option that clears some internal good-enough bar rather than hunting for the global best. I think I mostly understand it, but I keep wondering if its really just a symptom of cognitive limits. Like, humans have small working memory and slow processors, so we set these aspiration levels as a kind of shortcut. But now that we have LLMs and large-scale optimization algorithms that can evaluate thousands of branches in milliseconds, doesnt that make satisficing obsolete? I'm writing an essay arguing that bounded rationality is essentially a biological hardware constraint, and once you port decision-making to silicon with enough compute and parallel evaluation, the good-enough threshold should naturally dissolve into full maximization. My roommate said something about how aspiration levels can adapt based on past experience and arent just static shortcuts, but I dont see why that matters if the underlying reason for them was always just computation cost. So is there actually a principled reason to keep satisficing in artificial systems, or is Simon's framework just a temporary patch for brains that cant run exhuastive search?
