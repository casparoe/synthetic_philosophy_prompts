Consider the position that a system of rules can coherently contain a genuinely self-amending provision — one that authorises its own alteration — without falling into paradox, provided self-reference is treated as procedural scheduling rather than semantic self-assertion. On this view, the rule does not claim its own current validity; it merely initiates a revision protocol that takes effect at the next logical step, thereby avoiding liar-style contradiction. This is offered as an improvement on hierarchical solutions (Tarskian meta-languages) and on Peter Suber's Nomic, in which mutable rules permit self-amendment but leave ambiguity about whether the amending act is evaluated under the old or the revised rule. Play it straight: what would have to be true for this procedural-scheduling account to fail? Which features of legal or logical practice — for instance, retroactive legislation, instantaneous adjudication, or constitutional entrenchment — would expose it? Distinguish decisive evidence (a demonstrable paradox arising within the scheduling framework itself) from merely awkward evidence (historical cases of legal chaos that defenders could attribute to poor drafting). What does the view predict that hierarchical or game-theoretic rivals do not? Is it genuinely exposed, or has it been insulated by the qualification that "proper" scheduling avoids trouble? Finally, state what its defenders ought themselves to concede as counting against it, and say whether you regard that concession as damaging. Please answer in British English.
