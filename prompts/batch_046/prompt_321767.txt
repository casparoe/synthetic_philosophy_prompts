I've been working through the standard decision-theoretic responses to Pascal's mugging and have settled on a stronger claim than the usual skeptical conclusion: that the mugging doesn't merely expose an unrealistic feature of unbounded utility functions, but reveals a structural defect in how the independence axiom handles compound lotteries when at least one branch contains outcomes of unbounded magnitude. On this view, bounding utility or applying epsilon-contamination doesn't solve the problem so much as relocate it—either by arbitrarily truncating the value space or by smuggling in a discontinuity that lacks independent justification—while risk-averse transformations like exponential utility merely dampen the symptom without addressing why the axiom forces an agent to aggregate tail risks linearly across independent trials. I find this persuasive partly because it connects the mugging to deeper issues about whether rational preference should be closed under arbitrary convex combinations in spaces where value isn't finitely bounded, though I'm aware I might be overfitting a single intuition about ignoring astronomically unlikely promises.

What would actually count against this position? I'm not asking for the standard catalog of fixes—bounded utility, risk aversion, the ignoring-small-probabilities heuristic—unless they genuinely undermine the claim that the independence axiom is the real culprit rather than just a convenient framing device. Specifically: which formal results or conceptual distinctions would show that the problem is indeed localized to unbounded utility and dissolves cleanly once that feature is removed, without leaving a residual tension in the axiom itself? Are there decision-theoretic frameworks—perhaps ones that abandon independence but preserve other rationality constraints—that handle the mugging case without generating worse paradoxes elsewhere, and what do they concede about the cost of that abandonment? And from the other direction: is there empirical or theoretical work on how actual agents, or well-designed artificial agents, treat sequential low-probability high-payoff offers that suggests the mugging conclusion is descriptively accurate in some domain, which would make my resistance to it a bias rather than a principled objection?

I'd like an honest map of where this view is genuinely exposed—what it predicts that its rivals don't, whether it risks unfalsifiability by redefining structural defect too broadly, and which defeaters its defenders would themselves concede are serious rather than merely awkward. In particular, I'm interested in whether there's a live argument that the independence axiom survives intact precisely because compound lotteries over unbounded spaces are not the right formal model for the sequential, epistemically mediated offers involved in real-world mugging scenarios, and if so, whether accepting that argument collapses my position or just narrows its scope in a principled way.
