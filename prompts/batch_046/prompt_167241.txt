I'm preparing a feature for a publication that covers science and ideas, and after a long discussion with a historian of medicine and a clinical psychiatrist—both of whom insisted they were talking about the same thing—I realized that the disagreement over psychiatric classification may hinge less on empirical findings than on the analogies each side uses to frame them.

In debates that invoke Thomas Szasz's claim that mental illness functions as a myth in the specific sense that medical language misdescribes problems of living and social deviance, defenders of the diagnostic model frequently compare psychiatric classification to epidemiological tracking: just as epidemiologists identify risk clusters and predict outbreaks without requiring that every case share a single biological cause, psychiatric manuals group co-occurring symptoms and predict treatment responses regardless of whether discrete brain lesions exist. Critics respond with a different analogy, comparing psychiatric diagnosis to the moral assessments embedded in Victorian poor-law administration: both rely on expert judgment to classify individuals as deserving or undeserving of liberty and public support, both present normative decisions as technical discoveries, and both generate institutional realities that confirm the categories they claim merely to describe.

Audit both analogies—the epidemiological model and the poor-law model—by specifying the exact correspondence between source and target in each case, identifying which structural features are genuinely shared and which are projected, distinguishing the disanalogies that matter for arguments about involuntary commitment and welfare conditionality from those that are merely rhetorical, and determining whether either analogy can genuinely license the conclusion that mental-health screening should be integrated into social-benefit eligibility frameworks, or whether the debate collapses into a verbal dispute about whether the word illness applies.
