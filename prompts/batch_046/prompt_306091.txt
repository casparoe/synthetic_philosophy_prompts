My philosophy-of-science colleague has ruled as follows on reduction versus emergence: diamond hardness is reducible to carbon lattice geometry; the evolutionary function of the heart is emergent, not reducible to molecular biology; the statistical entropy of an ideal gas is reducible to mechanics; the market price of a commodity is emergent, not reducible to individual preference; the superconducting transition is reducible to BCS pairing theory; and the ecological role of a keystone species is emergent, not reducible to population genetics.

Consider a new case: a synthetic network of abiotic lipid vesicles engineered with synthetic transcription circuits that collectively exhibit quorum-sensing behavior—releasing and detecting synthetic signaling molecules to coordinate gene-expression-like responses—without any evolutionary history, implemented purely through chemical diffusion and engineered DNA.

Predict her verdict, identify at least two plausible underlying rules that jointly accommodate all six earlier judgments but diverge on this synthetic system, state what each rule predicts here, give your best-guess verdict with reasons, and specify one further case that would best distinguish the rules. In doing so, separate what is broadly settled in philosophy of science from what remains genuinely contested among specialists, and do not present any contested interpretation of reduction or emergence as the consensus view.
