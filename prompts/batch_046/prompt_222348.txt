Please construct and defend the thesis that the three canonical failures of Athenian direct democracy—the abrupt reversal in the Mytilene debate, the catastrophic decision to invade Sicily, and the trial and execution of Socrates—are not best understood as discrete pathologies (respectively, emotional volatility, imperial hubris, and philosophical intolerance). Rather, argue that they are manifestations of a single institutional deficit: the absence of procedural mechanisms—what might be called deliberative buffers—that separate the formation of collective judgment from its immediate execution under conditions of high rhetorical pressure and affective intensity. In your argument, explain how this structural feature accounts for the speed and irreversibility of each decision, address the objection that such buffers are conceptually incompatible with the classical Athenian conception of democratic sovereignty, and defend the further claim that Socrates’s trial represents the limiting case of this deficit rather than an anomalous episode of personal vendetta.
