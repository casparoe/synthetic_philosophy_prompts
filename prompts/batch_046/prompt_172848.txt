Here is the argument, with each step numbered.

(1) If realism about universals is true, then every genuine similarity between concrete particulars is explained by their literally sharing an entity—an immanent universal.

(2) If nominalism is true, similarities are brute and unexplained; but then we have no principled way to distinguish a genuine similarity (all red things) from an accidental conjunction (all red things that are also round) without invoking a universal of similarity.

(3) So nominalism either collapses into unexplained bruteness that cannot ground our actual distinctions, or it secretly reintroduces a universal.

(4) Trope theory avoids both by giving each particular its own particularized property—its trope. Two particulars are similar when their tropes are exactly similar.

(5) But if trope similarity is explained by a shared feature of the tropes themselves, that shared feature is a universal instantiated by the tropes, which reinstates realism at the level of tropes.

(6) If trope similarity is instead brute, then trope theory collapses back into the nominalism of step (2), now applied to tropes rather than particulars.

(7) There is a fourth option I am calling constitutive comparison: similarity is not discovered in the world nor instantiated in entities, but constituted by a comparative mental or conceptual act that groups particulars together without requiring any shared entity, immanent or transcendent.

(8) However, the act of comparison itself must either instantiate universals (for example, the universal of being-a-comparative-act) or be a brute particular.

(9) If the act is a brute particular, then its capacity to group reds together rather than reds-with-rounds is unexplained—exactly the problem nominalism faced in step (2).

(10) If the act instantiates universals, then constitutive comparison collapses into realism about those universals, and we must ask whether those universals are immanent in the act or transcendent.

(11) Therefore every position—realism, nominalism, trope theory, and constitutive comparison—either collapses into unexplained bruteness or into realism.

(12) Unexplained bruteness is inadequate because it cannot justify the stable, intersubjective distinctions we actually make (red versus blue, human versus stone).

(13) So realism must be true. But immanent realism requires that universals exist in every particular that instantiates them.

(14) If a universal exists in every actual particular of a kind, then in any counterfactual situation where a particular of that kind exists, the same universal must exist there as well.

(15) But if the universal of being-human, say, exists in counterfactual humans who have no causal or spatiotemporal connection to actual humans, then its existence does not depend on the actual particulars; it is an abstract object capable of existing uninstantiated.

(16) Therefore immanent realism is impossible: any universal that can be shared across actual and counterfactual particulars is transcendent.

(17) Transcendent realism, however, requires a relation of participation or instantiation linking abstract universals to concrete particulars.

(18) That participation relation is either a universal (which generates a third-man regress) or a brute particular (which leaves participation unexplained).

(19) Hence transcendent realism also fails.

(20) Consequently, no coherent account of shared properties is available; the problem of universals has no solution.
