I am a novelist invited to deliver the opening remarks at the inaugural retreat of the Meridian Institute for Physics and Philosophy, to be held in the reading room of a decommissioned observatory. The audience will be divided almost evenly between theoretical physicists—several of whom have publicly adopted the dismissive stances associated with Weinberg, Hawking, and Feynman regarding philosophy's contribution to scientific work—and philosophers of science who defend its indispensability. There will also be graduate students and family members present. The organizers have asked me not to lecture but to frame the weekend's conversations, and I am neither physicist nor philosopher.

Please write a formal address of roughly eight minutes. The speech must allow the debate over philosophy's utility to science to do genuine structural work: it should determine what I can and cannot claim from the podium, shape my acknowledgment of both sides, and prevent me from pretending to adjudicate a technical dispute I am unqualified to settle. Because the specialists in the room will recognize the references to Weinberg's critique of philosophical interference, Hawking's declaration that philosophy is dead, and Feynman's impatience with abstraction, I need the address to treat these positions with precision rather than caricature, yet remain fully accessible to the non-specialists.

To prevent the address from collapsing into a familiar summary, please weave through it a conceptual frame drawn from my recent fiction: the notion of 'negative space' in conceptual architecture—the unacknowledged void that appears when a discipline removes its philosophical scaffolding, leaving it eventually to reconstruct first principles it no longer possesses. This idea should explain why the scientists' dismissals contain partial truth (philosophy is not a laboratory instrument) while also showing how that very dismissal creates a hidden dependency that eventually collapses. Do not include citations, bibliographies, or reading lists; the speech must stand entirely on its own reasoning. Avoid verbatim quotations from actual texts, and do not invoke any highly specialized technical results beyond what a well-read generalist could be expected to understand.
