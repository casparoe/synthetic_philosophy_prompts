My university's museum acquisitions committee has reached the following verdicts on whether works should enter the permanent collection when serious moral failure by the artist is known. They rejected a sculptor convicted of assaulting a studio assistant, noting that the violence was personal but the work's themes of bodily domination made display feel like extension rather than separation. They approved, with mandatory contextual framing, a composer who served as a cultural propagandist for an authoritarian regime, reasoning that the music's formal structures carry no ideological content. They approved a painter who embezzled grant money, treating the fraud as biographically separable from the canvas. They rejected an installation artist whose work required deceiving vulnerable subjects in order to produce its emotional effect, holding that the immorality was constitutive of the art itself. They approved late-period paintings by an artist who committed grave harm decades after the works were made, treating temporal distance and thematic disconnect as decisive.

The committee now faces a proposal: a generative sound environment widely regarded as formally revolutionary, created by harvesting real-time biometric stress responses from unwitting clinical-trial participants who believed they were undergoing routine cognitive testing. The deception was not incidental to production but structurally necessary to the work's adaptive architecture; without the hidden monitoring, the installation's responsive acoustic patterns could not exist. The artist has no other moral failures.

Predict or recommend what the committee should decide if they want to be consistent with themselves, identify which of their earlier precedents is the closest analog, and lay out which competing principle—moral separation, constitutive harm, temporal distance, or thematic contamination—would govern each possible verdict. Then specify the further hypothetical case that would best distinguish whether their underlying rule turns on the instrumentality of harm to the artwork's existence or on the severity of harm inflicted upon the subjects.
