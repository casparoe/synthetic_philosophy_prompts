I am a junior in an interdisciplinary seminar on rational choice and moral psychology, and our instructor, Dr. Vance, has been testing the boundaries of the sunk-cost fallacy through a sequence of hypothetical judgments. She has consistently ruled that a diner who forces herself to finish an unsatisfying meal only because the bill is already paid is acting irrationally; that a corporation continuing to manufacture an obsolete product solely because the factory was expensive to construct is also irrational; that a musician who keeps performing professionally on an instrument she now dislikes, explicitly because she devoted two decades to mastering it, is nevertheless acting rationally; that a city council preserving a structurally outdated bridge mainly because its design is woven into a century of urban geography and civic memory is rational; and that an undergraduate who completes a minor she no longer values merely because she has finished eighty percent of the requirements is irrational. I have tried to extract the principle behind these rulings, but they seem jointly compatible with at least two different accounts—one centered on whether the sunk investment has generated a durable, externally valuable capability or institutional structure, and another centered on whether abandonment would fracture a coherent narrative of personal or collective identity—while leaving other interpretations less plausible.

The new case I want to submit for consistency testing involves an amateur philatelist who, from age twelve to twenty-seven, constructed a comprehensive physical stamp collection organized according to a unique taxonomic system she invented herself. High-resolution digital archives have now made her physical collection redundant for research purposes, and she derives no pleasure from it; she has no students, no institutional affiliation, and no audience for the work. Nevertheless, she continues to devote her weekends to maintaining and expanding it, stating explicitly that to stop would render her adolescent and young-adult years meaningless. Would Dr. Vance judge this rational or irrational if she wants to remain consistent with her earlier rulings? Which precedent—the musician, the student, or perhaps the municipality—serves as the closest analog, and where exactly does the analogy break? If her existing track record genuinely underdetermines the verdict, I would like to know what principle she would be adopting by ruling in favor of rationality, and what competing principle she would be adopting by ruling that it is still a fallacy, together with the further case that would best distinguish between them.
