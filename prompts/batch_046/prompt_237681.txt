I have recently been revising the syllabus for an advanced seminar on the philosophy of temporal consciousness, and I find myself returning to a debate that seems to have reached an almost complete impasse: the interpretation of the colour phi phenomenon in relation to the distinction, familiar from the philosophy of mind, between Orwellian and Stalinesque revisions of experience. The phenomenon itself is well known — a dot that changes colour as it traverses a display is reported as having changed colour at an intermediate point where only the original hue was physically present — but the disagreement concerns whether the subject's conscious episode genuinely contains the second colour at that intermediate temporal location, with the earlier portion of the trajectory being retroactively overwritten (the Orwellian reading), or whether the original perceptual sequence remains intact and only the subsequent memorial or reportive access to it is corrupted by postdictive binding (the Stalinesque reading). What puzzles me is that both sides appeal to the same neurophysiological literature on temporal integration windows and both concede that there is no direct introspective access to micro-temporal structure; yet they draw diametrically opposed metaphysical conclusions about the revisability of phenomenal content.

My own inclination is that much of the deadlock stems from an unstated ambiguity in the phrase the experience — whether it denotes the instantaneous phenomenal state available at a given neural latency, or the temporally extended, narratively accessible episode that becomes available for reflective judgement — but I am reluctant to dismiss the disagreement as merely verbal without first examining whether there is a deeper substantive divergence about the transparency of consciousness or the conditions under which perceptual error can occur. Could you diagnose the structure of this disagreement? Specifically, do the two positions differ about empirical facts that further experimentation on temporal binding could resolve, about conceptual criteria for attributing content to consciousness, or about bedrock intuitions concerning whether phenomenal states can be genuinely revised rather than merely misremembered? Furthermore, given your diagnosis, what kind of development — a refined conceptual distinction between phenomenal and access consciousness, a novel experimental paradigm isolating pre-reportive awareness, or perhaps an argument that the question is ultimately ill-posed — would constitute genuine progress rather than mere repetition of the existing stalemate?
