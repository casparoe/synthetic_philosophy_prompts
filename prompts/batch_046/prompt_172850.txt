For more than half a century, the Fischer controversy over German responsibility for the outbreak of the First World War has persisted despite broad agreement on the primary sources: historians on both sides cite the September Programme, Bethmann Hollweg's memoranda, and the Austro-Hungarian correspondence of July 1914, yet the divide between those who see a deliberate German drive for hegemony and those who see a structural collapse of alliance systems and bureaucratic momentum remains essentially unchanged since the 1960s. My working theory is that the disagreement has stalled because the opposing camps are operating with incompatible, largely unacknowledged philosophical frameworks—one treating national responsibility as requiring a unified collective intention analogous to individual mens rea, the other treating it as a distributed, structural property of state apparatuses that can generate culpable outcomes without a singular national decision. Is this diagnosis accurate, or is there a concealed empirical dispute—perhaps about the counterfactual dependency of the conflict on specifically German choices—actually driving the impasse? Might the two sides in fact be answering different questions, one about causal-historical explanation and one about moral attribution, under the single heading of "war guilt"?
