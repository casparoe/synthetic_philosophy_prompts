In philosophical and interfaith discussions, Jain anekāntavāda is routinely explained through the parable of the blind men and the elephant: each man touches a different part—tusk, leg, ear, tail—and reports a different shape, so each is partly right and the full truth requires combining all partial reports. The analogy is often used to present Jainism as an ancient anticipation of standpoint epistemology or scientific model pluralism, licensing the conclusion that contradictory descriptions can be equally true partial perspectives on one complex reality and that the tradition is therefore fundamentally a doctrine of tolerant pluralism. Audit this analogy precisely. Provide an explicit correspondence table: what plays the role of the blind men, of blindness itself, of the elephant, of the parts touched, and of the implied observer who could see the whole? Then identify where the mapping breaks against the actual structure of Jain epistemology—specifically syādvāda (conditional predication) and the saptabhaṅgī (sevenfold formula)—and against Jain metaphysics, particularly the doctrine of anekānta (the manifoldness of reality) and the status of naya (standpoint) as an epistemic instrument rather than an ontological slice. Does the parable smuggle in a synthesizable totality or a single correct integrated view that Jain ontology denies? Does it conflate epistemic limitation with ontological multiplicity? Separate the disanalogies that matter for the pluralist conclusion from those that do not. Finally, say whether the conclusion—that Jainism is best read as a form of modern pluralism or relativism—survives once the disanalogies are in view, and if not, propose a better analogy (or a correction to the elephant story) that captures syādvāda without importing the hidden assumptions.
