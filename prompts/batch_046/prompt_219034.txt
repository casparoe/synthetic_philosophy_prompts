I have no formal background in philosophy, but I have been working through Daniel Dennett’s brain-transplant puzzle—“Where Am I?”—in which his brain is removed, duplicated, and he is left wondering which version is the real him. To make sense of it, I turned to John Locke’s Essay Concerning Human Understanding, published near the end of the seventeenth century, where he argues that personal identity consists not in the sameness of substance or soul but in the continuity of consciousness and memory. As I understand Locke’s point, a person remains the same person across time not because she keeps the same atoms, but because she can remember her earlier thoughts and actions as her own; if that consciousness could be moved into a different body, the person would move with it.

I want to apply this to Dennett’s case, but I have introduced a variant that I do not recall seeing discussed in popular accounts. Suppose the brain is not copied in a single instant, but is gradually replaced, neuron by neuron, over several years. During this transition, both the original biological brain and the synthetic replacement remain active and continuously exchange information, so that at every stage there is one stream of consciousness that is partly hosted in each location. If Locke is right that identity follows consciousness, does this mean that “I” am already distributed across both locations before any final “cut” is made? And if so, does Dennett’s question—“Where am I?”—cease to be about a single point and become instead a question about the shape of a trajectory through time?

I am not looking for a bibliography or reading list; I only want help seeing whether Locke’s criterion actually resolves Dennett’s worry or makes it worse, and whether my gradual-replacement scenario introduces a problem that Locke’s original framework was not built to handle.
