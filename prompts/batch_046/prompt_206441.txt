I was argueing with my roommate about group decisions after skimming some papers on judgement aggregation. We both get the basic discursive dilemma — three people vote on premises A and B and on whether A and B imply C, and the majority ends up accepting A and B but rejecting C, so the group is inconsistent. But then I started wondering about a version where people don't just vote yes/no, but rate confidence in each premise from 0 to 1. The rule is: a premise is collectively accepted if the average confidence is strictly above 0.5, rejected if strictly below, and if it's exactly 0.5 we apply a tie-breaker that picks whichever option makes the overall set of premises consistent with the conclusion.

Now here's the thing I keep playing with. I can set up three people with scores that sit right near the boundary, so that if Person 2 lowers their confidence in B from 0.52 to 0.48 — just barely crossing the line — B gets flipped from accepted to rejected. But because of the tie-breaker, that flip changes how the 0.5 case for premise A gets resolved, which in turn flips A, and suddenly the group accepts neither A nor B but still accepts C, or some other weird combination where the collective view is consistent but matches nobody's individual view. It's not exactly the standard doctrinal paradox, because the paradox here is driven by these tiny shifts across an arbitrary cutoff and the cascading tie-breaker, not by straightforward majority disagreement on premises versus conclusion.

Does this specific failure mode have a name in the literature? I know about non-monotonicity in social choice, but I'm asking about the particular pattern where a continuous confidence scale gets chopped at 0.5 and the discrete jumps cascade through consistency constraints. If it doesn't have an established concept, can you name it and draw the boundary: what distinguishes it from ordinary discursive dilemma, from Condorcet-style cycling, and from simple measurement error near a threshold? I'd also like to know whether philosophers treat this kind of boundary-sensitivity as a serious objection to graded aggregation rules, or if it's usually dismissed as an artifact of the cutoff.
