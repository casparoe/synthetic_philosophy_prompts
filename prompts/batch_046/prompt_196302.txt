hey, so i retired from biophysics a few years back and lately i've been trying to wrap my head around philosophy of mind stuff. in my little reading group we got stuck on gödel's first theorem and what it actually means for whether a computer could think like us. everyone in the room - including me - accepts the math: any consistent formal system strong enough for arithmetic can't prove all arithmetic truths, and it can construct a sentence that says "im unprovable" which is true but unprovable inside. no dispute there.

the split is this: one camp (lets call them the jumpers) says this kills any fixed-algorithm model of human math, but not because we're magic - just because our minds are like a sequence of formal systems, each one jumping to a stronger one whenever we hit a gödel sentence. they call this "axiom plasticity" or reflective ascent, and they think its still mechanistic in a broad sense, just not static.

the other camp (the closure folks) says no, thats missing the point. they say gödel shows we have direct epistemic access to the truth of the gödel sentence that no syntactic system can mirror, even a jumping one, because the jump always happens after we've already seen the gap. for them, the disagreement isnt about computation speed or layers, its about whether recognizing the gap requires a non-formal mode of knowing.

we've been at this for months and nobody budges. we all agree on the formal result, we all agree humans can extend their axiom sets, we even agree that a "turing jump hierarchy" describes the jumper view. so what's actually breaking? is it just a verbal fight over whether "mechanical" includes dynamic self-modification? is there a hidden empirical claim about whether human axiom revision is algorithmically predictable? or are we answering different questions - one about causal implementation, one about epistemic justification - and calling them both "what gödel proves about the mind"?

and if your diagnosis is right, is there anything that could actually move this? a formal result about the limits of reflective ascent, a conceptual distinction that splits "mechanism" cleanly, or should we just admit its bedrock intuition about what understanding feels like?
