In the autumn of 1982, after seven people in the Chicago area died from cyanide-laced Tylenol capsules, Johnson & Johnson withdrew roughly thirty-one million bottles from shelves across the country, accepting a direct loss exceeding one hundred million dollars and the near-destruction of its brand in over-the-counter pain relief. Treat this not as a simple morality tale about corporate responsibility, but as an episode to be read through Herbert Simon’s framework of bounded rationality and satisficing—particularly through the distinction between procedural rationality, which evaluates a decision by the adequacy of the process given hard cognitive limits, and substantive rationality, which evaluates it by proximity to an optimal outcome, and through the dynamics of aspiration-level collapse when an environment shifts suddenly from routine commerce to existential moral crisis. What could the executives actually compute in the compressed time available, and what institutional structures truncated or shaped their search? Does the recall decision function as a classic satisficing solution—meeting a drastically lowered aspiration threshold that prioritized institutional survival and human welfare over profit maximization—or does it break the model, since no bounded algorithm searching within normal business constraints could have produced such a financially catastrophic choice without an external moral heuristic overriding the search? Specify which aspects the theory explains well—the narrowing of options under pressure, the reliance on simple rules, the adaptation of aspiration levels to a changed environment—and which it fails to capture, such as the symbolic and ritual dimensions of the recall, the role of media framing in restructuring the decision space in real time, and the possibility that moral shock introduces a form of reasoning that satisficing does not describe. Conclude by assessing what the episode returns to the theory: is it a confirming instance that rational actors adapt their aspiration structures to match crisis conditions, or is it an anomaly that exposes the silence of bounded-rationality models precisely where moral stakes are highest?
