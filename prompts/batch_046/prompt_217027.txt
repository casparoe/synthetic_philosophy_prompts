I am a doctoral student in philosophy of science embedded in an interdisciplinary peer-review board that screens behavioral and social-science study protocols before data collection begins. In our weekly meetings we keep encountering the same structural failure: a researcher observes a property in one population, time-slice, or measurement context and then projects it onto a different target using a predicate that looks stable but may conceal the kind of index-dependent divergence Goodman illustrated with grue—that is, predicates that coincide in all observed instances yet split in unobserved ones purely because of a hidden temporal or contextual parameter. I want to turn the core logic of the problem of induction, especially the criteria for projectibility and the asymmetry of definability, into a reusable fifteen-minute audit protocol that my board can apply without requiring formal logic training.

The deliverable should be a step-by-step decision procedure: ordered questions the reviewer asks in sequence; what counts as a sufficient answer to each; explicit stop-points where the protocol returns a negative judgment on the proposed projection; and explicit hand-off points where the remaining issue is one of values—such as whether demographic similarity is ethically adequate—rather than epistemic fact. You must preserve the conceptual machinery—predicate entrenchment, resistance to arbitrary temporal indexing, and the risk of symmetric-definition-with-divergent-extension—while stripping away notation. At the same time, please incorporate a distinction our unit has begun using but which is not standard in the literature: separate entrenchment, understood as the Goodmanian criterion of past linguistic and scientific use, from mechanistic anchoring, understood as whether the predicate tracks a feature whose stability is independent of the original measurement context. Treat these as independent audit axes, flag conflicts between them, and include a small set of test protocol descriptions that a reviewer can apply at each stage. Be explicit about which steps are genuinely algorithmic and which are judgment calls disguised as procedure. Then apply the protocol to three proposals: first, a cognitive-science study that measures cognitive flexibility via a specific bilingual task in children and projects the result to monolingual adults in high-stress occupations; second, an epidemiological model defining resilience in a post-industrial urban cohort and applying it to predict outcomes in rural agrarian populations facing climate disruption; and third, a case designed to produce an unwelcome result—specifically, a study that uses an implicit-association measure calibrated in one demographic group to project implicit bias as a stable individual trait across culturally distant hiring contexts, where entrenchment in the literature is high but mechanistic anchoring is weak. For each case, indicate exactly where the procedure stops, where it defers to values, and what remains unsettled. Finally, state clearly what the protocol cannot accomplish: whether it ever licenses genuinely novel predicates, whether it eliminates all grue-like risk, and where domain expertise must supplement the checklist.
