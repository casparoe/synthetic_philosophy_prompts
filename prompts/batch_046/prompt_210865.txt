I've recently found myself mediating a dispute between two communities whose moral frameworks treat land in fundamentally incompatible ways—one regards specific territories as inalienable and sacred, the other treats all territory as fungible economic resource—and I notice that I judge proposed compromises inconsistently. Here are my verdicts. A deal allowing Group A limited, revocable ceremonial access in exchange for Group B permanently protecting adjacent watersheds strikes me as a legitimate gain from trade: each side concedes something peripheral while safeguarding its core value. A deal in which Group A accepts a one-time cash payment to permanently waive all territorial claims strikes me as corrupt, not a trade. A deal in which Group B agrees to recognize the land as sacred only during ritual hours while retaining full development rights strikes me as worse than no agreement, because it instrumentalizes the other side's values. Yet a deal establishing shared co-management, in which both sides surrender exclusive sovereignty but gain joint decision-making authority, strikes me as genuinely right, even though sovereignty is precisely what each side considers non-negotiable in principle. The case I keep revisiting is this: Group A agrees to halt its pipeline protest in exchange for Group B funding an independent environmental monitor, where the funding is drawn directly from pipeline revenue. I cannot decide whether this is a principled compromise or a moral corruption. Is there a coherent principle—perhaps about which commitments can be traded without betrayal, or about the formal structure of reciprocity—that captures this pattern? If so, state it precisely, explain what it predicts for a compromise I have not listed, and say honestly which of my verdicts it requires me to overturn.
