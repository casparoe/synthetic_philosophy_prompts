I am preparing a discussion document for an advanced seminar in epistemic ethics, and after a long exchange with colleagues I realized that the standard framing of this debate collapses distinctions that need to be kept separate. I need a structured adversarial-collaboration statement that two opposing camps could both sign: one camp defends the position associated with William James’s classic essay on belief, holding that when a hypothesis is live, forced, and momentous, an agent may be permitted—sometimes even required—to adopt belief on practical or passional grounds that outrun the available evidence; the opposing camp defends the evidentialist position associated with W.K. Clifford’s classic essay, holding that believing anything on the basis of inadequate evidence is morally impermissible in every case, regardless of practical urgency or psychological vivacity.

Please produce the joint statement these camps could issue after good-faith collaboration. Open with a numbered section of agreed claims that both sides can sign without qualification: shared commitments about the nature of belief, about the normative relevance of evidence, about the distinction between belief and suspension of judgment, and about what makes a hypothesis live or forced. Then provide a disagreements table in which each row states one precise residual disagreement, phrased in wording that the camp owning that view accepts for itself, paired with the opposing formulation in wording that camp accepts; refer to the sides neutrally as the Jamesian camp and the Cliffordian camp. Next, identify the cruxes—sub-questions such that resolving them in one direction would move at least one side. I expect at least three: (i) whether belief is sufficiently voluntary for moral appraisal to apply; (ii) whether the ethics of belief should be evaluated at the level of individual psychology or at the level of institutional and social belief-formation practices; and (iii) whether the concept of epistemic risk can be analogized rigorously to decision-theoretic expected utility without collapsing James’s practical defense into Clifford’s evidential calculus. For each crux, specify a concrete case, thought experiment, or argument both camps agree bears directly on it, and record in advance what each camp predicts the correct analysis should yield. Finally, include a brief section identifying any disagreement that now appears largely verbal, specifying which terms are being used differently and explaining why the underlying substantive dispute, if any, lies elsewhere. Write this as a formal, polished document suitable for seminar circulation. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations to secondary literature; the statement should stand on its conceptual merits alone.
