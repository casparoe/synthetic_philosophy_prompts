In a seminar on philosophy of history I have been tracking a long-running disagreement about overdetermination and multi-causal explanation that seems to have reached a peculiar kind of stalemate. The central case is always some large-scale historical transition—participants often invoke the collapse of imperial orders or the onset of widespread conflict—where multiple factors, such as economic strain, institutional decay, and contingent decisions by particular agents, each appear sufficient, in combination with background conditions, to bring about the outcome. One faction treats the question as empirical: they ask whether, had one factor been absent, the outcome still occurs, and they treat disagreement as a dispute over the correct counterfactual history. A second faction insists that the real problem is metaphysical or conceptual: if you remove one cause, you may no longer be describing the same event, since historical events are individuated partly by their causal pathways; on this view, asking whether the collapse was overdetermined is like asking whether a painting would still be the same painting if every brushstroke were different. A third faction, meanwhile, argues that both sides illegitimately import a scientific-causal model into a domain where explanation is narrative and holistic, so the very notion of isolating variables is a category mistake.

What frustrates me is that these three positions do not seem to be answering one another. The counterfactualists accumulate case studies and historical analogies; the conceptualists refine identity conditions for events; the narrativists remind everyone that historians do not cite covering laws. Yet after years of this, no one appears to have shifted ground, and the literature seems to cycle through the same examples without resolution. Is the disagreement primarily about hidden empirical commitments—about what counts as a permissible counterfactual in an open, non-repeatable system—or about the aims of historical explanation itself? Or might there be a more formal issue that neither side has articulated, perhaps concerning how explanatory redundancy behaves when causes are not independent but structurally coupled, so that removing one factor reshapes the others in ways that make simple counterfactual testing impossible? Given your diagnosis, what would actually count as progress: a clarification of what historical explanation is for, a formal framework for counterfactual reasoning in open systems, or an argument that the framing question is ill-posed?
