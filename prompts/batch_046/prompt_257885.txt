I have been trying to understand Scanlon's contractualist idea that wrongness is defined by principles no one could reasonably reject, and I keep running into the aggregation problem: if each person has an individual claim not to be sacrificed, then saving five lives instead of one seems impossible to justify, because the one person's rejection is just as reasonable as any single one of the five. I want to set an exam for an advanced undergraduate seminar—ninety minutes, worth thirty percent of the grade—that forces students to apply this tension rather than just name it. The exam should present a concrete institutional scenario: a public health board must allocate a single stockpiled treatment that can cure either one patient with a unique condition or five patients with a common condition, and the board's founding charter was explicitly drafted in contractualist terms, requiring that every affected party be able to reasonably accept the allocation rule. The prompt should ask students to determine whether the charter can justify giving the treatment to the five, or whether contractualism blocks aggregation entirely in this case. I need three things: first, the exam question itself, written for students who have read the basic literature but should not be able to answer it by summary alone; second, a model answer that shows the specific moves a strong response must make—distinguishing pairwise justification from collective counting, explaining why the one patient's reasonable rejection does or does not block the principle that saves the five, and clarifying whether Scanlon's framework allows weighted reasons or must treat each claim in isolation; and third, a marking scheme that awards marks for correctly identifying the contractualist crux and penalizes answers that confuse this problem with utilitarian aggregation or with Rawls's original position. Please do not include bibliographies, citation requests, or references to obscure secondary debates that a student without library access could not verify; keep the task self-contained.
