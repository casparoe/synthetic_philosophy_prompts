After a long and somewhat exhausting argument with a friend about whether Daniel Dennett's three stances are genuinely distinct explanatory strategies or merely pragmatic shortcuts we cannot abandon, I want a satirical piece that demonstrates real familiarity with the philosophy rather than caricaturing it from the outside. Please write a deadpan mock regulatory memo—approximately 600 words, formatted with official headings, numbered clauses, and bureaucratic subsections—from a fictional "Bureau of Ontological Posture," which mandates that every interaction with an entity must be conducted under exactly one of Dennett's three stances: the physical stance, the design stance, or the intentional stance. The memo should establish enforcement protocols for ambiguous cases: a learning thermostat, a corporation facing negligence litigation, a large language model undergoing safety evaluation, and a wild ecosystem being managed for conservation. The satire should arise from exaggerating the actual philosophical tensions—how the design stance offers predictive leverage at the cost of ontological commitment, how the intentional stance becomes unavoidable in legal and moral contexts despite being "just" a heuristic, and how the physical stance remains theoretically sovereign yet practically useless for anyone who needs to file a report by Friday. Include a "Real Pattern Certification" section that parodies Dennett's conditions for pattern reality—compressibility, predictive projectibility, and independence from arbitrary observer framing—by translating them into bureaucratic checklists that determine an entity's tax bracket, liability shield, and eligibility for intentional-stance protections. The humor must be internal: a reader who knows the literature should recognize that the absurd regulations are faithful extensions of Dennett's own arguments, not misunderstandings of them. Keep the voice relentlessly bureaucratic; never wink or signal irony explicitly. The target should be the institutional fantasy that philosophical distinctions can be cleanly operationalized without ontological residue, not Dennett as a person. Do not include citations, bibliographies, reading lists, or requests for further sources, and avoid naming any specific living commentator. The piece should remain legible to someone without specialist training but reward a philosopher of mind with the recognition that every absurd clause tracks a real problem in the literature.
