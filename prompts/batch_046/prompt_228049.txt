I'm on the museum's Committee on Public Memory and Exhibition Ethics, and we're meeting thursday to vote on funding a proposed permanent gallery called "Defeat as Foundation." The curator wants to put three cases in one room — the Confederate Lost Cause, the Masada narrative, and the Kosovo Field myth — and frame them all as examples of how a militarily defeated group can turn that defeat into the moral foundation of a later nation. Half the board says this is exactly the kind of comparative work we should be doing; the other half says it's false equivalence that puts defenders of slavery on the same plane as religious martyrs or anti-colonial resistance. The deeper issue, I think, is whether there's a principled distinction between what I'm calling "corrective defeat" — where losing was the just outcome of an unjust cause — and "constitutive defeat," where the loss actually formed the group's identity regardless of whether the cause was righteous. But I'm genuinely unsure whether that distinction holds up under pressure, and I need a recommendation, not a seminar.

Please draft the short written opinion I'll circulate before the meeting. Take a definite stand; don't lay out options and leave me to choose. Start the memo with your bottom-line recommendation in the very first sentence, then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
