Compare Plato's account in the Meno — according to which knowledge differs from true belief because it is tied down by an explanatory grasp of why the belief is true, rendering it stable and recoverable — with what we can call a counterfactual-resilience account of epistemic value. On the latter view, knowledge is better than mere true belief not primarily because the knower possesses a causal or rational account of the belief's truth, but because the belief is structured so that it survives a specific range of nearby counterfactual perturbations: the knower distinguishes the actual situation from closely adjacent error-possibilities without the belief collapsing. First, explain how each account accounts for the stability that distinguishes knowledge from lucky true belief. Second, assess whether the resilience account requires the kind of explicit, articulable reason that Plato demands, or whether implicit discriminatory capacities suffice; and state what this implies for the comparison. Third, consider whether Plato's commitment to recollection as the source of knowledge creates an asymmetry that prevents the two accounts from addressing the same phenomenon. Fourth, examine a belief that is resilient — surviving nearby errors — yet false: does either account have the resources to exclude this as knowledge, or does resilience risk over-inclusion? Finally, argue which account gives a more satisfactory explanation of why knowledge is practically superior — that is, why it guides action better — and whether that practical superiority derives from the belief's internal anchoring or from its structural resistance to misapplication.
