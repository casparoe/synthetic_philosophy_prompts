I'm trying to understand the philosophical logic behind modern data-governance regimes. In Germany, constitutional doctrine treats personal data as an extension of personality, requiring explicit consent for collection and granting citizens a right to informational self-determination; in the United States, freedom-of-information laws treat government records about citizens as presumptively public, and openness is treated as a check on power, while private-sector data practices are regulated more lightly through market mechanisms. Meanwhile, China's social-credit infrastructure aggregates private transactional data into state-readable scores, treating visibility to the state as a civic duty. What strikes me is that liberal democracies seem to operate under an unstated principle I might call 'asymmetric legibility': citizens demand that the state be transparent (readable) to them, but insist that they themselves remain opaque (illegible) to the state and to each other, except in narrowly defined public roles like voting or property ownership.

What philosophical commitments about the self, citizenship, and knowledge does this asymmetry presuppose? Does it require a specifically liberal conception of the person as an autonomous agent whose identity is constituted partly by control over information, or can it be reconstructed on republican grounds as a safeguard against domination? Is the practice consistent with treating some domains—say, financial transactions or health records—as genuinely private while making others—criminal records, campaign donations—public by default? Or does the distinction rest on a hidden theory of which parts of life are 'political' in the relevant sense? Finally, if we replaced this implicit framework with a view that treats all data about citizens as either equally public or equally private—perhaps along the lines of a fully transparent society or a fully encrypted one—what institutional practices would have to change, and which parts of the current regime are genuinely necessary versus merely historically contingent?
