Two members of my seminar on collective moral responsibility have been arguing about whether the First World War can be blamed on "Germany" in a meaningful moral sense, assuming for the sake of argument that the Fischer thesis about deliberate German escalation is substantially correct. One, call her Mara, insists that if the German state pursued a premeditated policy of expansion—backed by a bureaucratic and military apparatus that made that policy robust against the replacement of individual leaders—then "Germany" is properly the bearer of collective blame, not merely a collection of guilty officials. She argues that collective agency requires institutional continuity, and that holding a nation responsible is analogous to holding a corporation liable: the entity persists, benefits, and acts through its structures, even if individual citizens dissent. The other, call him Jonas, counters that this slides illicitly from institutional culpability to national moral guilt. He grants that specific decision-makers and the state apparatus can be blamed, but says that attributing blame to "Germany" as a nation distributes guilt across millions of conscripts, dissidents, and civilians who had no meaningful input into foreign policy. He proposes a counterfactual robustness test: if replacing Bethmann Hollweg and Moltke with similarly positioned actors would not have altered the outcome because of systemic pressures, that shows the system was culpable, not that every constituent of the nation shares in the guilt. He further argues that in 1914 Germany was an authoritarian empire, not a representative democracy, so citizens cannot be treated as tacit co-authors of state violence. Mara replies that Jonas's robustness test actually supports her view—if the outcome is structurally overdetermined by German institutional arrangements, then the collective entity, not just its parts, is the appropriate target of blame. She also claims that refusing to attribute blame to the collective makes nonsense of historical accountability practices like treaty obligations. Jonas retorts that treaty obligations are legal-political instruments, not moral verdicts, and that using Versailles-style liability to justify collective moral blame is circular. He accuses Mara of conflating "the German state in 1914" with "the German nation," and says that without democratic authorization, the representational link needed for collective blame is broken.

As someone listening to this exchange, I want a point-by-point adjudication. For each round of argument—Mara's collective-agency analogy, Jonas's distributive-objection and robustness test, Mara's structural-overdetermination reply, and Jonas's distinction between legal liability and moral guilt—say who is right about the specific claim being made and where the reasoning fails or succeeds. In particular, address whether Jonas's counterfactual robustness test actually undermines Mara's position or inadvertently supports it, whether Mara's corporate-analogy holds given the non-democratic nature of the German Empire, and what both disputants are missing about the difference between attributing blame to an institutional structure versus attributing it to a nation as an enduring moral subject. Do not simply declare a winner; identify exactly which premises each party should concede and which they should defend, and note any hidden assumption—such as a theory of collective intentionality or a model of democratic representation—that both sides are relying on without defending.
