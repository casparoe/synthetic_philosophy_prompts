I'm studying how institutions and movements respond to dangers that lack any identifiable agent or enemy—systemic climate shifts, ecological tipping points, or drift in critical algorithmic infrastructure—where no single actor is deliberately causing the harm and no localized intervention clearly stops it. I keep encountering a particular configuration, and I'm unsure whether it has an established name beyond generic "goal substitution" or Goodhart's law.

The pattern runs as follows. Because the real danger is causally diffuse and hard to organize around, people construct a concrete, measurable stand-in: for instance, "carbon intensity per unit of economic output," "a specific demographic parity metric in automated hiring," or "a biodiversity index score for a region." This surrogate is adopted not as one lever among many but as the operational definition of addressing the threat. Then, and this is the part I want to isolate, people begin to defend the surrogate with the full moral urgency that properly belongs to the original danger. They speak and act as if achieving the proxy just is reducing the risk, even when the causal link is tenuous or unknown. Finally, if someone demonstrates that optimizing for the surrogate does not actually reduce the underlying threat—say, that carbon intensity can fall while absolute emissions rise, or that parity metrics can be satisfied without altering harmful outcomes—the defenders rarely treat this as a reason to abandon the proxy. Instead, they interpret the disconnect as an implementation failure and double down on refining the measurement, preserving both the organizational tractability of the surrogate and the moral authority borrowed from the acausal threat.

I've seen versions of this in environmental governance debates and in discussions of AI safety and accountability, but I want to know whether there is a concept that captures exactly this combination: a distributed or agentless danger, a quantifiable proxy adopted for organizational tractability, the proxy inheriting the moral weight of the original threat, and a characteristic resistance to discarding the proxy even after its disconnect from the threat is shown. Is this merely Goodhart's law in risk-management clothing? A variant of what some call "moral substitution"? Or does it have its own literature and precise boundaries? If there is no established term, I would want a careful coinage that distinguishes it from ordinary bureaucratic inertia, from cases where a proxy is knowingly used because it is genuinely the best available causal lever, and from simple measurement error. In particular, I want to know whether the transfer of moral urgency from threat to surrogate is essential to the phenomenon or only a frequent accompaniment, and whether the resistance to abandoning the proxy is better explained by institutional sunk costs or by something distinctive about how acausal threats structure practical reasoning.
