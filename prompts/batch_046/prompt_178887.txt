As a novelist who has spent several years attempting to write fiction that refuses mere decorative neutrality, I have come to hold something very close to W. E. B. Du Bois's view that all art is propaganda and that the claim to aesthetic autonomy—beauty pursued for its own sake—is either an evasion or a luxury that the systematically oppressed cannot afford. More precisely, I hold that every representational choice in a work (whose interiority is rendered visible, which social contradictions are permitted to appear, what emotional register is assigned to suffering, which formal harmonies are pursued) is never neutral framing but always serves some political function, and that art addressing Black life bears a particular obligation to tell the truth about that life rather than to pursue formal perfection that obscures it. What would count against this position? I am not asking for the standard rebuttals—indirect moral change, the failure of didacticism, the value of ambiguity—unless you can show they genuinely damage the core claim. Rather, I want the defeaters mapped and ranked from fatal to merely awkward: which specific aesthetic intuitions (for example, about the irreducible value of formal beauty that exceeds its message), which historical cases of politically committed work that failed precisely because its propagandistic purpose overrode its artistic means, and which conceptual distinctions (such as the difference between propaganda as intentional strategic distortion and propaganda as unavoidable positional framing) would force a defender to concede that the thesis is either trivially true or demonstrably false? Which of these would a committed Du Boisian concede as genuinely damaging, and which would they dismiss as missing the point? I also want an honest assessment of whether the position risks unfalsifiability: if "all art is propaganda" is saved by defining propaganda so broadly that even an abstract lyric about a flower necessarily serves some racial politics, does that preserve the view or empty it of content? If my framing conflates propaganda with testimony, or collapses aesthetic judgment into moral judgment in a way that misrepresents Du Bois's actual argument, I want you to say so plainly rather than accommodating the confusion. Please be direct: if this task rests on a false premise about Du Bois's view, or about the possibility of separating form from political content, tell me outright instead of proceeding politely.
