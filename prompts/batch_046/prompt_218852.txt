I am writing an essay for my third-year political philosophy course on policing and the state’s monopoly on legitimate violence. I want to analyse the Minneapolis City Council’s 2020 pledge to dismantle the Minneapolis Police Department, issued in the wake of George Floyd’s death, together with the city’s 2021 ballot measure proposing a Department of Public Safety, as a case study that tests Weber’s thesis about the state’s monopoly on physical force.

Rather than treating the monopoly as a single claim, I wish to work with a distinction between institutional exclusivity — the concentration of lawful coercive power in one agency — and normative legitimacy — the moral justification for the state alone, regardless of organisational form, to exercise that power. Please analyse the Minneapolis episode through both lenses: did the council’s pledge actually threaten institutional exclusivity, normative legitimacy, or neither? How should we classify the proposed Department of Public Safety — as a fragmentation of the monopoly, a rebranding of it, or something else entirely? What does the defeat of the measure indicate about whether public resistance was directed at the loss of institutional form, at the perceived erosion of normative authority, or at something else?

Then reverse the analysis: does the episode confirm Weber’s view that modern states resist the fragmentation of coercive authority, or does it suggest that the monopoly is more durable in normative form than in institutional form? Where does the theory fail to capture the distinction between defunding as budgetary reduction and abolition as functional disaggregation — for instance, separating mental-health crisis response and traffic enforcement from armed policing? And where is the theory genuinely silent: does it help us understand whether voters rejected change out of fear of disorder, attachment to the symbol of the police, or an inability to imagine coercion without a uniform?

Please keep the answer disciplined: identify the relevant actors (the council, abolitionist activists, state-level authorities, and the electorate), specify the options actually available, and state clearly where the mapping between theory and case breaks down rather than forcing the theory to speak.
