Notes for a short essay on conflicts between collective memory and the historical record. I want to distinguish three layers at which a community's memory and the archival record can come into conflict: first, factual disagreement about what occurred; second, categorical disagreement about what kind of event it was—for instance, whether a particular episode should be classified as civil conflict, genocide, or systemic failure; third, normative disagreement about what obligations, if any, the present inherits from the past event. The standard framing asks which source should "give way," but I think this assumes that resolving the first layer resolves the others, which it does not. I propose instead a framework of "layered authority": the historical record should have governing authority over factual claims, but collective memory retains legitimate authority over categorical framing and over the inheritance of moral obligations. I allow one exception: when a categorical framing depends not merely on interpretation but on factual premises that are demonstrably false and constitutive of the category itself, so that retaining the framing requires denying the factual record not interpretively but structurally. I would welcome comments on three points. First, does this taxonomy miss a fourth layer—perhaps affective or aesthetic framing—that operates independently of the categorical and normative ones? Second, is the exception I allow for categorical framing too broad, and does it risk collapsing the framework back into archive supremacy? Third, is there an established concept in the philosophy of history or in memory studies for this kind of non-competitive, functional separation of authorities, or am I effectively reinventing an existing distinction under new labels?
