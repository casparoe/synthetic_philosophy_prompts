I'm a student in an upper-division philosophy course on causation and agency, and I also intern part-time with a municipal parks department reviewing incident reports. Each week I must decide whether a staff omission—a failure to act—counts as a cause of harm, which determines whether we update protocols or leave the case to values-based judgment. I have roughly twenty minutes per file, no legal background, and a supervisor must sign off on any non-routine finding. Based on our course discussion—that omissions cause only when they represent a deviation from a determinate baseline of normal functioning, when the harm is counterfactually robust across nearby worlds rather than merely actual, and when no redundant safeguard renders the omission causally redundant—please construct a step-by-step decision procedure my team could run consistently. For each step, specify the question, what counts as a sufficient answer, where the answer stops with "omission is not causal," and where it must be referred to a person because the issue is normative rather than factual. Then apply the procedure to three cases: (1) a ranger did not repair a gate latch that had been flagged, and a cyclist was injured; (2) the department did not stock an antidote for a toxin present in one plant species, and a visitor was poisoned; (3) three staff members each failed to check a shared weather alert, and campers were injured in a storm, though any single check would have triggered evacuation. Include at least one case where the procedure gives a counterintuitive result, and state explicitly what kinds of omissions the procedure is not designed to assess.
