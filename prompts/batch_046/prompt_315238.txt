Hey, I spent thirty years in atmospheric physics and only started reading philosophy in retirement, so forgive me if I'm mixing up terms. I was thinking about the lottery paradox and the preface paradox together, and I built this argument that seems to show scientific modeling is either irrational or not objective. Here are the steps:

(1) The lottery paradox tells us that if belief is rational with each ticket, it still doesn't close over thousands of conjunctions — we can rationally think ticket 1 loses, ticket 2 loses, etc., without thinking "all lose."

(2) The preface paradox is the same shape but for an author: she can rationally belive every claim in her book and still rationally believe at least one claim is wrong, because she distibutes a kind of global error occurence allowance.

(3) So rational belief seems to allow a "global error budget" B across a set S: you never individually disbelieve any member, but you reserve B for the set as a whole.

(4) Now take a big climate model with, say, 10,000 paramterized sub-routines. The scientist assigns each a 99.9% confidence, leaving a global budget of about ten expected errors spread across the set.

(5) By the budget logic, she should not believe any particular sub-routine is correct, since each carries its share of B.

(6) But she has to treat every one as correct to run the simulation; she can't suspend judgment on all of them.

(7) So she's rationally committed to both using each as true and not believing any specific one is true — contradiction, unless belief is context-relative.

(8) If belief is context-relative, then there's no real fact about whether she "believes" the parameters, and scientific objectivity collapses.

(9) So either scientific practice is irrational, or the budget model is wrong.

But I feel like something slipped between (4) and (5), maybe about how probabilities aggregate? Or maybe I'm misusing "belief" versus "acceptance"? I can't tell. Which premise or step should I reject, and what does giving it up cost me? I'd prefer to keep the idea that scientists can be globally humble without being locally paralyzed.
