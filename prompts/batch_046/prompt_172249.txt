In a graduate seminar on self-reference I have been co-facilitating, a debate about Hofstadter's strange-loop model of the self has reached a stable equilibrium that nobody can break. One camp—call them the recursive-symbolists—reads the account literally: they hold that the "I" is a discrete representational token, an explicit self-denoting symbol embedded in a syntactic architecture analogous to Gödel numbering, sustained by feedback loops of narrative prediction rather than formal proof. They insist that without such a token there is merely distributed self-monitoring, not a strange loop in the proper sense; the loop, on their view, lives in the symbol's semantic content, not in the dynamics that carry it. The opposing camp—the attractor naturalists—grants that brains construct robust self-models but denies that any token literally refers to the system containing it. For them, the "I" is a stable high-dimensional attractor in a continuous predictive hierarchy: the system predicts its own predictions, but there is no syntactic symbol with self-referential content, only a retrospective construction of a center of narrative gravity from flowing dynamics. Both sides accept the same neuroscience of default-mode activity, both reject any Cartesian theater, and both concede that an artificial system could in principle instantiate whatever counts as self-reference; yet for over two years the arguments have circled without concession.

My own suspicion is that the dispute is partly verbal—a disagreement about whether "strange loop" requires a discrete representational vehicle—and partly a mismatch between an ontological question about mental representations and a phenomenological question about the unity of experience, all conducted under a single heading. But I want a sharper diagnosis: is there a hidden formal question about whether continuous dynamics can realize genuine self-reference without a discrete symbol? Are the sides differing over standards of explanation—whether a model must identify a specific representational vehicle—or over bedrock intuitions about what "I" must feel like from the inside? Or is the impasse a sign that the question is ill-posed, asking for a single structure behind phenomena that need not share one?
