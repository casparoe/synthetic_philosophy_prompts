I am a doctoral student in philosophy of religion and moral philosophy, and I am preparing a seminar module on karma understood specifically as a theory of moral desert rather than as a cosmological mechanism or a psychological incentive structure. The conceptual core I want students to inhabit is this: moral desert is non-fungible and diachronic—it accrues to a subject who persists across biological lives—yet there is no intersubjective ledger, no psychological continuity that allows the subject to recognize prior actions, and no central authority to verify that a particular outcome precisely matches a particular prior deed. For an advanced graduate seminar of about ten to twelve students, with seventy-five minutes available, I need a runnable in-class activity that makes them feel the force of this idea through the rules and incentives themselves, not through a lecture that names it afterward.

Design a structured simulation or role-play with explicit rules, materials, roles, and a non-monetary payoff scheme—perhaps decision rights over a shared resource, reputation tokens, or the power to set rules in a final round—so that participants must allocate “karmic outcomes” to anonymous “future selves” represented only by stripped-down action profiles from prior rounds. The mechanics should force them to attempt precise moral accounting across discontinuous identities: for instance, they draw action-cards in round one, then in round two receive outcome-cards assigned to anonymous profiles with no identifying link, and must collectively decide whether justice has been satisfied. The rules should be arranged so that the group runs into the problem themselves—discovering that they cannot stabilize agreement about whether desert has been discharged without invoking an unverifiable authority, and noticing that pooling or transferring desert violates the intuition that each subject gets exactly what she deserves.
