For a seminar on epistemic modesty I am formalizing a normative claim about the interaction of inside-view reasoning and outside-view calibration in philosophical judgment. The claim is this: if a philosopher has carefully evaluated the inside-view arguments for some contested thesis P—for instance, the denial of libertarian free will—and finds them subjectively compelling, yet she knows that (1) the relevant expert community divides roughly fifty-five to forty-five against P, and (2) she has previously adopted and then abandoned three other substantive philosophical positions after encountering statistical or sociological data about expert disagreement in those domains, then rationality requires her to cap her credence in P at no more than 0.45, irrespective of the apparent strength of her current reasoning.

Please state your actual credence—on normative grounds, not descriptive psychology—that this claim is correct. Decompose your answer into at least three terms: (A) the weight that outside-view disagreement statistics should carry relative to first-order philosophical reasoning in domains with persistent, stable expert split; (B) the relevance of one's personal revision history to present calibration, specifically whether prior abandonment of positions under outside-view pressure licenses stronger discounting now; and (C) whether the specific numerical cap of 0.45 is approximately the right normative output, or whether the correct cap should lie substantially elsewhere. Provide a specific number between 0 and 1 for each term with one line of justification, then combine them into a final credence. Finally, indicate which single term your final answer is most sensitive to, and specify what kind of argument or empirical finding would move that term by at least 0.20.
