I've been arguing with friends in rationalist-adjacent forums about whether deplatforming and collective ostracism should be analyzed as punishment, and one analogy keeps surfacing to short-circuit the normative debate before it begins: collective social sanction, the claim goes, functions like an adaptive immune system. The community is the body; harmful actors or ideas are pathogens; withdrawal of platform or association is the isolation of infected tissue; outrage and norm-updating are inflammatory signaling; and the institutional memory of who has been sanctioned—revised lists of discredited figures, updated community norms—corresponds to memory cells that allow a faster, stronger response upon re-exposure. The analogy is deployed specifically to license aggressive, low-procedural-fairness sanctions: just as we do not hold a trial before an antibody binds to a virus, society should not demand courtroom-level proof or individualized culpability assessments before cutting access. The intended conclusion is that social sanction is hygienic maintenance rather than retributive justice, and therefore false positives are an acceptable cost of systemic health.

I want a rigorous audit of how far this mapping actually holds. Could you lay out an explicit correspondence table—what in the social domain plays the role of the pathogen, the antigen-presenting mechanism, the inflammatory cascade, the memory cell, and the autoimmune failure mode—and then separate the disanalogies that genuinely undermine the normative conclusion from the ones that are merely cosmetic? In particular, does the analogy smuggle in the assumption that the social body has a unified, non-contested interest, that threats are exogenous rather than endogenous dissent, and that inflammatory response is automatically calibrated to threat magnitude? I am especially interested in whether the immune framework quietly substitutes a consequentialist calculus—minimizing total infection—while suppressing the retributivist intuition that sanction must be proportionate to culpability rather than to risk. Does the conclusion that procedural safeguards should be relaxed survive once these breaks are visible, or was the analogy carrying the entire normative load? If it collapses, is there a structurally better analogy—perhaps social sanction as distributed error-correction in a noisy signaling network—that preserves the idea of collective learning without importing the biological baggage of a single organism?

One further complication: in the discussion I am drawing on, someone introduced a somewhat niche distinction between epistemic hygiene and moral hygiene—using the immune analogy either to filter false beliefs or to expel bad character—and I am not convinced that distinction is stable once you press on it. Please say how confident you are in each part of the analysis, and explicitly single out the point you are least sure about—whether it is the biological-to-social mapping, the normative translation, or the stability of that hygiene distinction.
