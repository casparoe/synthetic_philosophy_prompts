Argue for the position that Gödel's first incompleteness theorem, when transported outside mathematics, is almost always misinterpreted through a category error, yet retains a defensible analog that applies not to human cognition or empirical science but specifically to self-authorizing normative frameworks. Begin by stating the theorem's actual conditions—recursive axiomatizability, consistency, and sufficient expressive power to encode primitive recursive arithmetic—and show why these conditions fail to be satisfied by scientific theories as actually practiced and by cognitive architectures, so that familiar claims about inevitable "Gödelian gaps" in physics or about the irreducibility of human minds to algorithms rest on a false analogy rather than on the theorem itself. Then defend a narrower, structurally parallel claim: any normative system—such as a comprehensive theory of practical reason or a closed legal order—that attempts to internalize its own criteria for revision, authority, and exception-making will encounter an instability arising from self-reference in its governing rules, not from arithmetic representation. Distinguish this analog precisely from Gödel's result: specify what is preserved (the impossibility of complete self-grounding within the system's own resources), what is lost (the specific syntactic construction via Gödel numbering and the arithmetization of syntax), and why the analog remains philosophically significant despite being technically different. Conclude by explaining what this corrected application genuinely reveals about the limits of formalism in ethics or jurisprudence, and—just as importantly—what it explicitly does not show about the uniqueness of human cognition or about the necessary incompleteness of natural science.
