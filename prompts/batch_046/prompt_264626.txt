I am preparing for a graduate philosophy of law seminar and need a rigorous adjudication of a disagreement that broke out in my study group; please act as an expert philosopher of law specializing in jurisprudence, think through each argumentative step carefully, and begin with a brief analysis of the conceptual stakes before delivering your verdict. The dispute concerns a hypothetical statute—call it the Retroactive Intent Act—enacted in 1945 with perfect procedural form according to the state's rule of recognition. The Act criminalizes a specific form of collaboration that was technically legal under the prior regime, but applies only to defendants who acted with malicious intent not mentioned in the original law, and it applies retroactively to actions taken five years earlier. Two members of my group debated whether this is genuinely law.

The first member, Alex, argued from a legal-positivist position: the Act is valid law because it was enacted according to the correct procedural rules. Calling it not law because it is unjust or retroactive, Alex said, confuses the question of what law is with the question of whether law is good. Even monstrously unjust rules can be law; that is precisely why we need a concept of legal validity separate from moral evaluation.

The second member, Blake, defended a natural-law view inspired by Fuller: extreme retroactivity violates the internal morality of law—specifically the principles that laws should be promulgated and should not be retroactive—so severely that the Act fails to be law altogether, not merely unjust law. Blake insisted this is not moral criticism disguised as conceptual analysis; it is a claim about the conditions under which a norm can perform the function of guiding conduct that makes something law rather than arbitrary command.

Alex replied that Fuller's eight principles are best understood as conditions of efficacy, not as moral conditions on legal validity. A retroactive statute may be terrible at guiding behavior, but it still functions as law—courts apply it, officials enforce it, citizens can know it exists. If efficacy is the standard, then the Act is still law, just inefficient or dangerous law.

Blake countered that if a failure of procedural morality is severe enough, the norm does not guide conduct in the relevant way at all—subjects cannot orient their behavior by it—so the efficacy-based defense collapses. The distinction between bad law and not law is not sharp, Blake said, but that does not mean it does not exist; some failures are so total that legal status is undermined, not just legal quality.

Alex then invoked Hart's idea of a minimum content of natural law—the claim that any functioning legal system must contain basic protections for life and property—to show that Hart already acknowledged a necessary connection between law and morality. So, Alex argued, the positivist can accommodate the intuition that law cannot be entirely disconnected from morality without accepting Fuller's stronger claim about internal procedural morality.

Blake responded that Hart's minimum content concerns the substantive rules a society needs to survive, not the internal morality of the lawmaking process itself. Therefore it does not address Fuller's actual argument, which is about how law must be made—prospective, general, promulgated—not just about what it must contain.
