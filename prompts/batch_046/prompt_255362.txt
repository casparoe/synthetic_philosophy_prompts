I run a graduate seminar in Indian epistemology with about eight doctoral students, meeting for a single ninety-minute session. I want them to feel, from the inside, the distinctive tension in classical Nyāya between śabda (testimony) and anumāna (inference) as independent pramāṇas—specifically, the claim that authoritative verbal testimony can yield knowledge of imperceptible entities in a way that is not reducible to an inferential chain from perceptual data.

Design a structured in-class deliberation—part role-play, part forced-choice game—that makes the students encounter this tension through action rather than exposition. Invent a concrete, somewhat niche scenario that is not the standard textbook example: do not use the classic fire-on-the-hill inference case or the standard self-knowledge debate. Instead, construct a hypothetical dispute about an alleged imperceptible causal substrate—call it a binding field—revealed only through conflicting expert reports and indirect measurement traces that do not by themselves entail its presence. Divide participants into small groups, each assigned one pramāṇa as their exclusive epistemic resource: direct perception of observable traces, inference from those traces to an unobserved cause, comparative reasoning by analogy to known mechanisms, or authoritative testimony from specialists who claim direct acquaintance with the substrate. Give them a scoring scheme using points, not real money, that rewards collective coordination on a judgment about whether the binding field exists, but penalizes any group that draws on reasoning methods outside their assigned source.

Arrange the rules so that the testimony-only group and the inference-only group naturally arrive at incompatible conclusions, and so that hybrid strategies are structurally discouraged: for instance, allow cross-group negotiation only if each side can translate its conclusion into the other side’s permitted vocabulary, which should prove impossible without loss of content. The participants should discover for themselves that testimony cannot be collapsed into inference without forfeiting its distinctive authority, yet that maintaining its independence produces a coordination breakdown—before anyone names this as the problem of śabda-svātantrya.

After the activity, provide a debrief that maps each moment onto specific elements of Nyāya theory: which group embodied which source, where the coordination failure mirrors the classical argument for testimony’s independence, and where the frustration with fragmentation mirrors the critics’ worry about epistemic disunity. Be explicit about what the activity cannot reproduce—specifically, the metaphysical grounding of testimony in the speaker’s veracity and the hearer’s comprehension of sentence-meaning, and the Nyāya distinction between linguistic competence and inferential reconstruction—and tell me exactly how I must supply that missing element verbally after the exercise.

Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it: students will likely smuggle inferential reasoning into the testimony group, or they will interpret authoritative testimony as merely well-confirmed scientific report. Explain how to intercept these moves without breaking the simulation, and identify which feature of the idea—namely, the non-psychological objectivity of testimony’s validity conditions—must be introduced by me rather than generated by the rules.
