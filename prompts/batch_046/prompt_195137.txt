I've been trying to formalize a tension in Scanlonian contractualism that turns the aggregation problem into a reductio, and I want to know where the slip is. Here is the argument, with a small twist: instead of treating every complaint as flat, I weight each person's complaint by a structural exposure coefficient—the degree to which the principle systematically assigns that burden to people in her exact position. (1) Contractualism requires that principles be justifiable to each affected person; a principle is wrong if anyone could reasonably reject it. (2) To apply this, we compare the burden a principle imposes on an individual against the burden she faces under alternatives, scaled by her coefficient. (3) In the classic rescue case—save five strangers or save one—the principle "maximize lives saved" imposes death on the one; her coefficient is 1 and her complaint is maximal, so she can reject it. (4) By the same token, if we apply "protect whoever is closest" or "choose randomly," each of the five faces a high coefficient-weighted chance of death; each can reject those principles too, since her own exposure remains severe. (5) Thus contractualism seems to entail that no principle for distributing an unavoidable death among the six is justifiable to all, producing moral paralysis in forced-choice rescues. (6) Extend this to risk: a policy that saves 100 lives by imposing a 0.01 fatal risk on each of 10,000 people assigns every affected party a non-zero coefficient; each can reject the policy ex ante, because she might be the one killed, and the aggregate benefit to others is not her benefit. (7) If every risk-bearing policy is rejectable by each affected party, then collective action—vaccination, infrastructure, even shared transit—is impermissible without unanimous consent. (8) To avoid paralysis, the contractualist must allow aggregation: either summing complaints across people or letting probabilities cancel within an individual's life-plan. (9) But once aggregation is permitted, the "reasonable rejection" test reduces to comparing total weighted burdens across outcomes, which is structurally identical to a rule-utilitarian calculus with a contractualist gloss. (10) So contractualism either collapses into utilitarianism or paralyzes all collective choice.

I'd rather keep (1) and (3) intact—there's something right about justifiability to each person and about weighting complaints by systematic exposure. Please lead with your bottom line in the first sentence and then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question. Which step should I reject? Is there a hidden equivocation between ex ante and ex post complaint, or between individual and collective justification? And if I reject the aggregation-blocking move in (8), do I pay the price of abandoning the anti-utilitarian core of Scanlon's view?
