I'm working through whether democratic backsliding names a real pattern or is mostly a partisan epithet for governments one dislikes, and I'm trying to fix my intuitions by testing them against concrete cases rather than by reading another taxonomy. Here are my gut verdicts, which I hope are mostly consistent but include at least one pair that pulls in opposite directions.

First, Hungary since 2010: constitutional amendments that weakened checks, consolidation of media ownership into loyalist hands, and electoral rules tilted toward the ruling party. Elections still occur and Fidesz wins them, but I think this counts as clear backsliding—the institutions have been re-engineered, not merely used aggressively. Second, Poland between 2015 and 2023: the disciplinary chamber for judges, capture of the constitutional tribunal, and refusal to recognize EU court rulings, all achieved through ordinary statutes and parliamentary majorities. Again, clear backsliding, and the fact that PiS initially won fair elections does not excuse the subsequent institutional capture. Third, Turkey after the 2016 coup attempt: mass purges of civil service and judiciary, the 2017 referendum centralizing presidential power, and the suppression of opposition parties like the HDP. This is backsliding, though it was partly ratified by referendum and began with democratic legitimacy. Fourth, the United States after 2020: state legislatures passed voting-access restrictions, state officials tried to overturn certified results, and a violent mob attacked the Capitol, yet power transferred and the opposition won competitive national elections in 2022 and 2024. I call this severe democratic erosion and norm-breaking, but I hesitate to label it backsliding proper because the competitive structure survived; if I applied the Hungary standard here, I think I'd be stretching the concept into a partisan weapon.

That hesitation creates my central tension. In Mexico from 2018 to 2024, the government weakened the autonomy and budget of the National Electoral Institute (INE), proposed popular election of judges, and used majoritarian rhetoric against independent agencies, yet competitive elections continued and the opposition won the presidency in 2024. Sometimes this looks to me like Hungary-lite—structural weakening of referees—other times like ordinary majoritarian politics in a developing democracy that still allows alternation. I also want to include a hypothetical that strips away partisanship: imagine a long-established democracy where the ruling party, without changing constitutional electoral rules, creates by statute parallel administrative oversight councils with veto authority over policies enacted by opposition-controlled regional governments, staffing them with tenured loyalists. Elections remain free nationally and locally, but governing power is decoupled from electoral victory. My intuition is that this is backsliding, but I am unsure whether my reason is that elections have been structurally hollowed out, or that a normative line—perhaps about institutional forbearance—has been crossed.

So my questions: Is there a coherent principle—distinguishing structural decoupling, normative erosion of forbearance, and partisan capture of referees—that delivers all of these verdicts, or must I drop some? Please state the principle precisely, indicate which clause of it is motivated by which case, and say what it predicts for a case I have not listed, such as Brazil's recent conflicts over electoral court authority or India's controversies around election commission appointments. Most importantly, be honest about which of my verdicts the principle cannot accommodate: does it force me to call the US a case of backsliding, to acquit Mexico, or to treat my hypothetical decoupling scenario as something other than democratic decay? And does the resulting account make backsliding analytically separable from a partisan label, or does it collapse into institutional change I disapprove of?
