I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy background. Last week I was talking with two colleagues—one with a physics degree and one who consults on applied ethics—about whether omissions can genuinely cause outcomes. We used a recent production incident as our example: a missing health-check in our deployment pipeline allowed a buggy build to roll out. The discussion escalated into a sustained philosophical disagreement, and neither would concede. I would like an outside adjudication.

The physics-trained colleague argued that causation requires some physical interaction or energy transfer, and since "not running the health-check" is literally the absence of an event, there is nothing in the world that did the causing. The buggy build was caused by the positive presence of faulty code and the positive presence of a deployment mechanism; the missing check merely failed to prevent it, which is an absence of intervention, not a cause.

The ethics consultant replied that this confuses metaphysics with analysis. She said that by standard counterfactual dependence—if the health-check had run, the bad build would not have deployed—the omission qualifies as a cause under interventionist criteria. She also noted that in law and moral practice we routinely assign responsibility for failures to act, and it would be strange if omissions were causally inert in every domain except our blame practices.

The first colleague pushed back that counterfactual dependence alone generates an infinite list of omissions for every event. For instance, his own absence from the server room is also counterfactually linked—if he had been present and stopped the deployment, the incident would not have occurred. So a selection principle is required. He claimed that any such principle, such as "normal expectations" or "role-based duties," is normative rather than physical. Therefore omission-causation either collapses into an unmanageable infinity or smuggles values into metaphysics under the label of causation.

The second colleague agreed that selection is necessary but denied that it undermines causation. She argued that physical causation is already selective—we do not cite every prior state of the universe when explaining why a glass broke—so demanding an extra normative filter specifically for omissions is unfair double-counting. She added that even in non-agentive systems, such as a pipe with a missing seal, we correctly say the missing seal caused the leak, with no agent or duty in sight.

The first colleague responded that the missing seal is not an omission but the absence of a physical part, which is categorically different from failing to perform an action. He maintained that omissions only arise in contexts where a prescribed process, protocol, or functional role exists—like a health-check script or a security patrol—so they are always already embedded in normative or structural frameworks. Consequently, one cannot separate omission-causation from those frameworks.

The second colleague's final move was that even if omissions are structurally embedded, that does not make them non-causal; it only shows that some causes are relational rather than intrinsic. She concluded that denying omission-causation altogether forces us to say that no one ever harms another through neglect, which is plainly false.

They then asked me to adjudicate. Specifically: is counterfactual dependence sufficient to establish omission-causation, or does the selection problem defeat it? Is there a clean metaphysical distinction between a missing physical part and an omission of action, and does that distinction matter here? And what, if anything, are both parties missing in their exchange?
