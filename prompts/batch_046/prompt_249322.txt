ok so i was trying to push Good's theorem into epistemology and now i have an argument that sounds like it kills all reasoning. (1) Good's theorem: in any decision problem, if you can observe a signal at zero cost before acting, then observing weakly dominates acting blind, regardless of prior. (2) Forming a belief or running an inferrence is an action in a decision problem: you select premises, apply rules, and output a conclusion, with getting it right as the utility. (3) Before any specific inferrence, there's always at least one free observation you haven't made—maybe a quick consistency check, a counterfactual test case, or just a ten-second delay in case a better argument occurence to you—that could in principle alter your premise-selection. (4) Therefore, for every reasoning-decision, Good's theorem applies: you should gather that observation first. (5) But applying (4) recursively means you should gather observations about whether to gather observations, ad infinitum, so you never actually infer anything. (6) Hence either Good's theorem is false, or rational belief-formation is impossible. Which step should i reject? I want to hang on to (1) and (2) if possible, but i'm worried the problem is in (3) treating internal checks as free signals within the same decision frame that Good requires. Or maybe (5) sneaks in a modal slide?
