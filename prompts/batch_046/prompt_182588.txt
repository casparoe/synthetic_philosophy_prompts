I've been running a grad seminar on the paradox of tragedy for like five years now, and every semester we hit the exact same wall. You've got the old Hume-style hedonists saying we enjoy sad art because we take pleasure in the craft — the painter's skill, the actor's delivery — and then you've got the negative-emotion camp saying no, we genuinely feel grief or fear but we value that feeling for moral rehearsal or emotional calibration. Then there's a smaller group, mostly influenced by recent affective neuroscience, who talk about "wanting" versus "liking" and suggest the paradox only exists if we assume enjoyment means positive affect, which might be wrong. Lately I'm convinced the debate is stalled not because nobody's smart enough, but because the sides are answering seperate questions: one group is doing psychology of reward, another is doing normative aesthetics about what we ought to seek out, and a third is doing phenomenology of emotional ambiguity. So I want a diagnosis, not a survey of Aristotle-to-now. Is this disagreement verbal — about what "enjoyment" or "art" means — substantive but hidden behind different disciplinary standards, or just bedrock intuitions about whether suffering can ever be part of a good experience? And here's the part I care about: is there any move — a conceptual distinction, an empirical result, a change in framing — that could actually end the stall, or is this one of those philosophical loops we recycle? Be honest about where your reasoning is weakest. Write in American English, please. And tell me specifically: what argument or finding would change your mind about whatever diagnosis you give?
