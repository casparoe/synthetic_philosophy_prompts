hey, i'm a retired physicist who got bored after leaving the lab and started reading popper and some philosphy of history. there's one pattern i keep seeing in arguments about whether history actually has laws like physics does, and i can't tell if it's just sloppy reasoning or if there's a proper name for it.

first example: someone claims there's a law that every rapid technological adoption is followed within about 20 years by a demografic collapse in the regions that adopted it first. then people bring up the green revolution in india — tech spread fast, no collapse. the defender doesn't drop the law; instead they say agricultural tech doesn't count as technological adoption in the relevant sense, but they never said that before. then another case shows a collapse that had nothing to do with tech, and suddenly they widen the law to cover any rapid systemic change. so the kind-boundaries slide both ways — shrinking to dodge counterexamples, expanding to grab confirming ones.

second example: a historian argues all true maritime empires decline within 150 years of reaching peak naval tonnage. when rome's naval peak lasted longer, they say rome wasn't a pure maritime empire because it had big land armies. but then carthage, which also had land forces, fits the timeline perfectly, and suddenly carthage gets called a pure case. the taxonomy seems rebuilt around each data point to keep the law untouched.

i know popper talked about historicism being unfalsifable, and i know about ad hoc saving in science. but this feels different — it's not just tacking on an extra clause. it's reshuffling the category names so the law never actually touches the ground. is there an established concept for this exact two-way boundary sliding? and if not, can you name and define it with clear edges, saying what it is not — not plain equivocation, not motte-and-bailey, not just popper's poverty-of-historicism critique? i don't want a bibliography, just want to understand the carving.
