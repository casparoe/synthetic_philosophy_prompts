I work as a software engineer without formal training in academic philosophy, but a recent discussion about ethical guidelines for autonomous systems led me to sketch the following notes on moral status. I would appreciate critical feedback on whether the reasoning holds together, whether I have mischaracterized any standard philosophical positions, and whether the technical analogy I use actually clarifies the problem or merely obscures it.

Notes toward a gradient account of moral status:

In software architecture, we rarely treat components as either fully critical or fully disposable. Instead, we assign reliability tiers, latency budgets, and graceful-degradation priorities based on how extensively a failure in one node propagates through the system. I wonder whether moral status might be structured similarly, as a continuous gradient rather than a binary property.

Consider three cases arranged by informational integration. A human adult maintains a complex self-model with long-term planning and emotional coherence, which I take to correspond roughly to a high-level control plane with extensive state management. An insect possesses rapid sensory-motor loops but minimal internal modeling; this seems closer to a stateless microservice that reacts to inputs without retaining a persistent self-representation. A thermostat implements a simple feedback loop with no representational content at all, analogous to a basic PID controller that adjusts output based solely on an error signal.

If moral consideration tracks the richness of this internal integration, then we might replace the question "Is this entity sentient?" with a scalar moral weight coefficient, perhaps ranging continuously from near zero for pure feedback devices up to full status for richly integrated agents. This avoids the awkwardness of drawing a sharp boundary between, say, a complex invertebrate and a simple vertebrate.

However, I see an immediate aggregation worry. If an insect carries a coefficient of 0.001 and a human carries 1.0, then one thousand insects collectively outweigh one human. That seems counterintuitive, yet in distributed computing we do sometimes aggregate small failures into system-wide outages. I am unsure whether the aggregation of tiny moral harms should be treated analogously, or whether there is a philosophical argument that moral status is non-aggregable in this way.

Another concern is that a purely continuous model might erode the special protections we usually grant to persons. Perhaps one needs a discontinuity or a critical threshold to preserve the idea that certain violations are categorically different, not merely larger in magnitude. I understand that some philosophers defend sharp boundaries while others accept gradations, but I do not know how to adjudicate between them without simply importing my engineering intuition about continuous metrics.

Could you comment on whether this gradient framing is philosophically defensible, whether my comparison to system-reliability tiers is more misleading than helpful, and whether there is a standard response to the aggregation problem that I have missed? I am particularly interested in whether treating moral status as continuous forces one to accept that harming large numbers of very simple entities can be worse than harming one complex entity, and if so, whether that result is actually a reductio or just an unfamiliar implication.
