I'm presenting a workshop paper defending what I call Nozick's implicit threshold view of animal moral status. The thesis is that if we take both the experience-machine objection and the utility-monster argument in Anarchy, State, and Utopia seriously, Nozick is committed to a non-utilitarian, rights-based boundary for animals: beings capable of framing a long-term life-plan possess an inviolable separateness that blocks aggregative trade-offs (the Kantian side of his view), while beings lacking that capacity—most non-human animals—are protected not by distributive welfare rights but by a negative liberty constraint against being treated as renewable resources within human projects. On this reading, factory farming is wrong not because suffering is bad in an aggregative calculus, but because it instrumentalizes animals within precisely the kind of trade-off logic Nozick rejects for persons. The audience is mostly virtue ethicists and care ethicists who think relational context and emotional attunement matter more than abstract rights, one analytic political philosopher who knows the libertarian literature cold, and a cognitive ethologist studying goal-directed planning in corvids who will push hard on where the empirical boundary falls. Draft my Q&A prep sheet: list the objections I will actually face, ordered by how damaging they are and by who will raise them, each phrased in the vocabulary of that subgroup, with a concise reply I can deliver in about two minutes, a frank note on where that reply is weakest, what I should concede upfront about Nozick's sparse direct textual engagement with animals, and the one objection I'm hoping nobody asks—namely, whether corvid food-caching behavior counts as framing a life-plan in Nozick's sense. Keep the argument self-contained: no citations, bibliographies, or external references needed, since the case should stand on conceptual grounds anyone in the room can assess without looking anything up.
