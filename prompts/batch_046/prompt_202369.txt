For a graduate seminar on collective responsibility, design a decision-tree taxonomy that sorts positions on whether a nation can bear guilt for wrongs committed by prior generations. Use Jaspers’s fourfold division—criminal, political, moral, and metaphysical guilt—as one branching axis, but build the tree primarily from concrete diagnostic cases rather than from named theories. Each internal node should pose a specific scenario: for instance, a state that has replaced nearly all of its population through immigration and adopted a constitution explicitly repudiating the predecessor regime, or a state in which present citizens enjoy structural benefits from past expropriation without having authorized it. The leaves should include at least six clearly characterized positions—e.g., strict individualism, Jaspers-style political liability, inherited collective culpability, structural-complicity without guilt, reparative-duty-only, and metaphysical solidarity—located in the logical space of answers rather than listed in advance. Identify any unoccupied combinations (e.g., a view that affirms metaphysical guilt while denying all institutional continuity) and briefly diagnose why they may be incoherent or merely unexplored. Flag pairs of leaves the tree cannot cleanly separate, especially where pure individualism and unqualified inherited guilt overlap in practical prescription despite different metaphysical commitments. Keep the instrument self-contained: no bibliographies, no citation requests, and no dependence on obscure texts a student could not reason through from the cases alone.
