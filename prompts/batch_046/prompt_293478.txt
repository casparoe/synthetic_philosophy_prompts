Make an argument for retaining the common prior assumption in formal epistemology despite the standard objections from disagreement and heterogeneous priors. Do not treat the common prior as an empirical claim about actual agents; defend it instead as a normative constraint on rational belief revision within a shared epistemic framework. Specifically, argue that once we distinguish first-order beliefs from second-order commitments to update rules—where the latter include structural invariants such as closure under conditionalization on a shared partition—the common prior applies only at this second-order level. Address two standard objections: first, that observed real-world disagreement demonstrates that priors are not shared; second, that allowing heterogeneous priors solves rational disagreement without collapsing into subjectivism. For each, show how the structural reinterpretation dissolves or at least significantly weakens the objection. Then introduce an additional consideration: in cases of partial awareness, where agents may not share an identical space of possibilities, the structural common prior must be defined relative to a minimal shared ontology rather than a maximal one. Argue that this relativization strengthens rather than undermines the normative force of the assumption, because it forces explicit articulation of ontological commitments before disagreement can be rationally assessed. Conclude by explaining what this view implies for modeling epistemic disagreement in interactive settings—specifically, whether two agents with different first-order priors but shared structural commitments can genuinely agree to disagree, and why that result differs in kind from the conclusion of the standard agreement theorem.
