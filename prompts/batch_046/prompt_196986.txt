After a long and somewhat exhausting argument with a close friend about whether grand historical narratives still deserve a place in serious historiography, I have tried to organize my objections into the outline of a short essay. I would appreciate rigorous comments on its conceptual coherence, its fairness to the authors it discusses, and whether its central framing is genuinely distinctive or merely a restatement of familiar criticisms.

The essay targets what I call the scale fallacy: the tendency in large-scale histories such as Harari's Sapiens and Diamond's Guns, Germs, and Steel to treat the transition from small bands to states and then to global empires as a problem of identifying the correct macro-driver—whether cognitive flexibility or geographic endowment—rather than treating each scale shift as a locally contingent reconfiguration of dependency networks. I argue that both authors implicitly make scale an explanatory variable instead of an emergent property that needs its own explanation.

I then draw on Graeber and Wengrow's The Dawn of Everything not primarily for its empirical diversity of early societies, but for a structural point: if social forms were genuinely discontinuous and reversible, then stage-based explanation collapses not because the data are messier, but because the very logic of necessary progression dissolves. From this I propose a framing I term discontinuity without progress, applying it specifically to agricultural adoption. Rather than viewing the Neolithic transition as a revolution driven by climate, population pressure, or cognitive breakthrough, I treat it as a prolonged field of failed and partial experiments in sedentism that only appear inevitable in retrospect because the survivors of those experiments controlled the material and textual archives. The question I pose is whether this framing genuinely escapes teleology, or whether it merely inverts the same narrative structure by privileging contingency over determinism.

I also worry that rebutting Diamond's geographic determinism without adequate care slides into a kind of voluntarist exceptionalism—the idea that societies freely choose their organizational forms regardless of ecological constraints. I want the essay to maintain that constraints matter, but that they underdetermine outcomes far more radically than macro-narratives allow.

My specific requests are these. First, is the scale fallacy a coherent conceptual distinction, or does it mischaracterize Harari and Diamond by conflating heuristic simplification with causal claim? Second, does discontinuity without progress add anything beyond Graeber and Wengrow's own arguments about seasonal flexibility and the rejection of evolutionary stages, or is it redundant? Third, am I being unfair to Harari's actual treatment of shared fictions as a mechanism for large-scale cooperation, which might be compatible with local contingency? Fourth, does the essay's framing of agricultural adoption as archival survivorship depend on archaeological specifics I have not verified, or can it stand as a conceptual claim about historiographic method?

Please treat this as a request for conceptual sharpening rather than for a bibliography or reading list; I am interested in whether the argument holds together, not in being directed to further literature.
