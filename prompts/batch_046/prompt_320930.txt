You are an expert in democratic theory and conceptual analysis, with particular attention to the boundary problem of who belongs to the demos. Before you design anything, take a few minutes to brainstorm the logical dimensions that genuinely separate inclusion criteria—especially those that become visible only when presence is mediated, deferred, distributed, or non-human, rather than direct and bodily.

Your task is to construct a decision-tree taxonomy that sorts positions on democratic membership. The instrument must be built from the logical space of answers, not from a list of named theorists. The audience is advanced undergraduates in political philosophy who have not memorized the literature; every branching node must present a concrete case with two or three answer options that can be chosen without knowing academic labels.

Design exactly seven branching nodes, each centered on one of the following cases:

1. A permanent resident who is fully subject to local law but holds no citizenship.
2. A future generation represented by an appointed trustee with binding veto power over long-term environmental policy.
3. A non-human ecosystem structurally coupled to democratic economic decisions, represented by an ecological proxy with standing in the assembly.
4. An algorithmic forecasting system whose outputs constrain legislative options but which lacks legal personhood and consciousness.
5. A citizen living permanently abroad who retains formal voting rights in national elections.
6. A temporary migrant worker present only for a single harvest season.
7. A collective of artificial maintenance agents that must approve budget allocations to keep critical infrastructure stable.

For each leaf reached through the tree, provide: (a) the conventional name of the position if one exists in democratic theory, or “unnamed” if none does; (b) one sentence stating its core inclusion criterion; and (c) its single most awkward commitment—the counterintuitive consequence of applying that criterion consistently across all seven cases.

After presenting the tree in clear hierarchical form, identify at least two answer-combinations that are logically possible but appear unoccupied in the literature, and diagnose briefly whether each gap stems from incoherence, unattractiveness, or mere neglect. Then flag any two positions that your tree fails to distinguish, explaining exactly why they collapse into the same leaf.

Constraints and framing: Do not request bibliographies, reading lists, or citations. Do not invoke highly obscure recent theories; work from widely shared conceptual resources in democratic theory, but apply them specifically to the mediated-presence framing above—where inclusion might depend on being affected, being subjected, being represented, being functionally coupled, or being capable of reciprocal accountability. Think step by step, and ensure the tree sorts respondents by what they believe about these cases rather than by which label they prefer.
