Design a take-home examination problem for a final-year philosophy of logic course on Lewis's triviality results for indicative conditionals. The problem should require approximately 1,200 words and carry 40 marks; allow 90 minutes. Do not ask students for bibliographies, reading lists, or citations, and do not frame the task as a literature review.

To prevent answers that merely recite the standard derivation, embed the following constructed scenario: a philosopher proposes that indicative conditionals are evaluated only within a restricted formal language L, in which all atomic propositions are probabilistically independent except for a single designated pair (P, Q) that are permitted to covary. The philosopher claims that, within L, Adams's Thesis holds for every conditional in the language and that a weakened probabilistic independence condition—applying only to propositions outside the designated pair—also holds. Ask students to determine whether Lewis's triviality result still applies in L. They must identify the precise step in the proof that either fails or remains intact, explain whether the restriction genuinely blocks the derivation or merely relocates the independence failure to the designated pair, and assess whether the philosopher's proposal saves the probabilistic analysis of indicative conditionals from triviality.

Provide, together with the problem, a model answer and a marking scheme. The model answer should make explicit the three analytical moves required for full credit: (1) the factorization of joint probabilities that underwrites the derivation; (2) the application of the independence assumption to the antecedent and the conditional itself; and (3) the diagnosis of how the designated-pair exception interacts with steps (1) and (2). The marking scheme should allocate specific marks to each move and should list, for each deductive error, the corresponding misconception that should cost marks—e.g., conflating the triviality result with the claim that indicative conditionals are material conditionals; treating the language restriction as automatically dissolving the need for probabilistic independence; or failing to see that the exception for (P, Q) reintroduces dependence precisely where the proof requires independence.

The difficulty must come from applying the logical machinery to a modified case, not from recalling labels. Ensure the prompt is self-contained: a student without web access should be able to attempt it using only standard knowledge of the triviality result and basic probability theory.
