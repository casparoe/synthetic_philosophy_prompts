The philosophical debate over whether everyday conventions—tipping in service contexts, queuing in public spaces, observing local norms about jaywalking—constitute genuine moral obligations, and whether temporary visitors are bound to follow them, has reached an impasse that looks empirical but is not. On one side, the coordination view holds that these practices are stable equilibria solving collective-action problems; defection is inefficient and perhaps rude, but not morally wrong, and a visitor owes only prudential compliance, not moral allegiance, since she never entered the implicit contract that sustains the scheme. On the other side, the recognition view holds that conventions embody structures of mutual acknowledgment; to benefit from them while disregarding them is a form of free-riding that exploits cooperative relationships, and visitors acquire obligations precisely because they step into the domain of shared expectation. Both camps agree on the descriptive sociology and on the fact that defection can cause harm; neither denies that conventions vary across cultures. Yet the arguments have cycled for years without movement.

I want a diagnosis rather than a verdict. Is this disagreement substantive—perhaps about whether implicit consent can bind temporary beneficiaries who never signaled acceptance—or is it verbal, turning on whether "moral obligation" means "social expectation backed by sanction" versus "duty arising from cooperative fairness"? Or are the sides answering different questions masked by one heading: one asking whether the convention itself possesses intrinsic normative force, the other asking whether an outsider who temporarily enjoys its benefits acquires a derivative duty not to undermine the scheme?

To test your diagnosis, apply it to the specific distinction between equilibrium-compliance, where one follows a rule because it is the stable coordination point, and recognition-compliance, where one follows it as an expression of acknowledgment of others as co-participants. Does this distinction dissolve the debate, sharpen it, or reveal that one side is conflating two separate normative relations? And tell me what would count as progress: a conceptual clarification, a reframed thought experiment involving a visitor who explicitly opts out, or an argument that the entire framing rests on a category error between social expectation and moral duty?
