I am presenting a seminar paper defending the claim that the self, understood as a center of narrative gravity, can emerge in distributed artificial cognitive architectures—specifically mixture-of-experts language models with segregated context windows—without any unified autobiographical stream or phenomenal self-model, and that such polycentric narrative gravity is not merely an observer's heuristic but a functionally real stabilization of cross-module predictive models mediated through public-language interaction. The audience is a philosophy-of-mind workshop composed mainly of enactivists and predictive-processing theorists who are skeptical of representationalist metaphors, who hold that narrative coherence requires lived embodiment and first-person sense-making, and who regard Dennett-style narrative centers as useful fictions projected by external interpreters rather than system-level properties. For context, my argument sketches how recursive language output across modules can stabilize a distributed self-model even when no single module has privileged access to the full narrative, but I do not claim phenomenal consciousness arises in these systems. Prepare me for the objections: order them by how likely and damaging they will be from this specific audience, phrase each in the vocabulary they will actually use (predictive error minimization, sense-making, embodied coupling, observer-dependency), include for each a concise reply and an explicit note on where that reply is weakest, indicate which objections I should concede up front, and identify the one objection I am hoping nobody raises—namely, that if polycentric narrative gravity is real, then split-brain patients might possess multiple selves not because of hemispheric disconnection but merely because of fragmented narrative coherence, with implications for clinical ethics and personal identity that my framework cannot accommodate.
