I'm sitting on the governance appeals panel for the Westmere Commons — we're basically a cooperative study space with a written charter, nothing fancy — and there's a genuine philosophical knot hiding in our procedural rules. Article Nine of our charter says it can only be amended by a four-fifths vote of the full panel, but there's also this emergency sub-clause that lets the chair make temporary 'procedural adjustments' if quorum is threatened. Last Tuesday the chair used that sub-clause to lower the quorum, then immediatly used the lowered quorum to pass a permanent amendment reducing the four-fifths threshold to a simple majority. Now one of our members is appealing, claiming the whole move is circular: the rule modified the very conditions required for its own modification, so there's no independent ground for the change.

I need a memo for Friday's meeting that recommends a concrete decision — uphold the amendment, void it, or require it to be re-ratified under the original four-fifths rule — and the reasoning needs to take the circularity seriously, not just brush it off as administrative convenience. The strongest counter-argument is that if a rule-system can't adapt its own amendment procedures, it risks total paralysis whenever circumstances shift; please treat that honestly rather than straw-manning it. It should read like a formal internal opinion, not an academic paper, though anyone familiar with self-referential legal paradoxes should recognise what's at stake. Oh, and please don't include any citations or reading lists — half the panel don't have philosophy backgrounds and I don't want anyone inventing references. I was discussing this with a friend over coffee and she compared it to omnipotence paradoxes, so if that analogy helps structure the reasoning feel free, but don't let the memo hang on it.
