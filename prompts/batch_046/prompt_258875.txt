I was arguing with my roommate about Quine's gavagai example — you know, the one where a linguist can't tell if the word means rabbit or undetached rabbit part — and we got into this idea about how social pressure can lock you into a wrong interpretation. So, write a short story about Mara, a field linguist documenting Kavi, where the only remaining speaker is an old engineer who keeps using gavagai in totally contradictory contexts.

In this culture, translation disputes aren't settled by pointing or asking questions; instead they run a kind of social auction where every interpretation you propose costs you a favor owed to the community, and once you've staked one you have to defend it in every subsequent conversation or permanently loose face. Mara accidentaly stakes a small favor on rabbit, then finds herself locked into that reading even when the speaker applies gavagai to a broken machine, a shadow, and a family recipe. I want the story to feel like it's really about escalation of commitment rather then just a language puzzle, and I'd like the ending to stay ambiguous about whether Mara understands the language or has just won the auction by being too stubborn to back out. Maybe set it on a decaying research station so there's nowhere else to go, and try to keep the tone kind of quiet and claustrophobic.
