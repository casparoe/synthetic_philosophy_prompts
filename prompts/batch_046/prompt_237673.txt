I'm working through a philosophy of science course that recently paired Hume's problem of induction with the formal no-free-lunch results in statistical learning theory. The instructor framed the pairing as potentially transformative: if every predictive strategy is matched by an equally valid anti-strategy across the space of all possible worlds, then asking why we should expect the future to resemble the past may need reformulation rather than straightforward solution. Before I try to assess particular answers, I want to map the terrain precisely.

Please write the desiderata: what any philosophically adequate theory of inductive justification must deliver if it takes both Hume's challenge and the no-free-lunch theorems seriously. For each requirement, state the case or consideration that motivates it — for example, whether the theory must explain why scientists successfully converge on particular inductive biases without circular appeal to past success; whether it must preserve a meaningful distinction between rational belief revision and arbitrary preference; whether it needs to account for why NFL results don't seem to paralyze actual scientific practice; whether it must treat the selection of a hypothesis space as an epistemic achievement rather than a given; and whether it must accommodate the formal result that uniform weighting over all possible target functions yields no learning advantage. Flag which desiderata you take to be genuinely non-negotiable and which are preferences of particular camps (such as demanding determinism, demanding purely a priori justification, or demanding that induction be reducible to deductive inference). If some subset of these requirements is jointly unsatisfiable — say, if explaining convergence without circularity conflicts with accommodating the full generality of NFL — say so explicitly; that tension is what I most want to see articulated. Keep the weight on the criteria and their motivating cases rather than on scoring particular philosophers, though you may briefly indicate where a broadly Bayesian, a reliabilist, and a pragmatic account stand against the list.
