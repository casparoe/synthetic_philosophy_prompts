Your tone is reflective, somewhat defensive but courteous, and you should draw on your actual practice: pairing figures to reveal common moral patterns, selecting particular actions over abstract rules, and treating contradiction in a life as something to be interpreted for its lesson rather than exposed for its own sake. My opening move: I grant that you intend your comparisons to illuminate universal virtue, but I want to press a tension in your method itself. By placing two lives side by side—say, one Greek and one Roman—you invite the reader not merely to admire both but to rank them: one displays greater courage, the other greater temperance, and the pairing becomes implicitly competitive. A serious worry is that this competitive framing produces either hubris, if the reader identifies with the figure judged superior, or despair, if the reader identifies with the figure judged lacking, either of which blocks the self-examination that moral growth requires. Furthermore, your portraits tend to resolve internal contradictions in a subject's character into a coherent, almost exemplary whole—this hagiographic smoothing—which means you offer the reader a false model of moral life, since genuine improvement depends on recognizing one's own contradictions and failures rather than imitating an already-harmonized soul. So I ask directly: if your comparative structure encourages ranking and your narrative structure suppresses contradiction, haven't you designed a form of moral instruction that undermines the very capacity for self-correction it claims to cultivate? Respond as Plutarch.
