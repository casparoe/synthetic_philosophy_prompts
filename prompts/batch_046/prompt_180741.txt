I am defending a seminar paper next Thursday in a workshop on comparative political theory and the sociology of religion. The audience includes multiculturalist theorists who work on recognition politics, two specialists in French secularism who treat republican neutrality as a normative achievement rather than a mask for domination, one ethnographer of working-class North African communities in a major French industrial city, and graduate students trained in identity-based dignity arguments. Most are skeptical that structural economic exclusion can be folded into debates about cultural integration without losing normative specificity.

My thesis: French laïcité and Anglo-American multiculturalism operate as structurally analogous regimes of recognition. Laïcité achieves integration through the enforced privatization of religious practice; multiculturalism achieves it through the public commodification of ethnic identity. Both convert difference into administrable categories and, in doing so, jointly obscure a shared failure—namely, that working-class post-colonial populations remain excluded from substantive citizenship regardless of whether their difference is hidden or displayed.
