I have recently been appointed to advise a heritage-ethics board that must decide whether contemporary international human-rights instruments should classify all ritual practices involving human remains—including those attested in colonial-era ethnographies of indigenous societies—as universal crimes irrespective of cultural context. In preparing my contribution, I have returned repeatedly to Montaigne’s essay on cannibals, not for its conclusions but for its comparative method: he juxtaposes European religious violence with Tupinambá ritual consumption in order to destabilize European moral self-certainty rather than to idealize the other. What I need is not an exposition of that essay, but a predictive reconstruction of his probable reasoning. Suppose Montaigne were confronted with the modern anthropological thesis that reports of cannibalism were largely a colonial projection deployed to justify domination and dispossession—a thesis that undermines the empirical foundation of his own comparison. Would he have integrated it as a deeper vindication of European bad faith in reporting, or resisted it because dissolving the concrete cultural otherness would weaken the very mirror he holds up to Europe? And if he then turned that same method to our present juridical problem—balancing cultural relativism against universal prohibition in human-rights law—how would his reasoning likely proceed? Trace the internal steps, indicating precisely where his commitment to experiential sympathy over abstract judgment propels him forward, and where it collides with unstated assumptions about natural law or shared human commonality that his framework never fully examines. Do not supply a bibliography or citations; I require only the reconstructed argument, with explicit markers for the points at which a sixteenth-century framework strains against these post-colonial and legal questions.
