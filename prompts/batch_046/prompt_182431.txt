I'm presenting at a philosophy of science and science-policy workshop my thesis that in high-uncertainty, low-base-rate domains—specifically the governance of gene-drive technologies—the standard forecasting prescription to average inside-view and outside-view estimates is epistemically incoherent. I defend an "inside-first, coherence-bounded" model: the outside view should not contribute independent evidential weight but should act solely as a structural consistency check on the causal mechanisms posited by the inside view. If the reference-class base rate conflicts with the inside-view mechanism, the conflict indicates either a mis-specified reference class or a hidden dependency in the causal model, not a reason to regress the forecast toward the base rate. The audience comprises philosophers of science who specialize in model-based reasoning and are suspicious of deference to statistical aggregates when mechanistic understanding is available; decision theorists familiar with Tetlock-style forecasting tournaments, who treat the outside view as a hard prior; and science-policy scholars skeptical of rationalist epistemology, who worry that dismissing base rates licenses unwarranted technological optimism. They care about epistemic modesty, reference-class selection, and institutional accountability. Draft my Q&A prep sheet: the six to eight objections I will actually face from these three camps, ordered by how damaging they are to the coherence-bounded model, each phrased in the vocabulary of the camp that raises it, with a concise reply suitable for about two minutes, an explicit note on where that reply is weakest, what I should concede up front to preserve credibility, and the single objection—most likely from the decision-theoretic camp—that I most hope nobody asks.
