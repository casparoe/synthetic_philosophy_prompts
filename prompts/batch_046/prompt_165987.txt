Next month you present at a joint workshop on mechanistic interpretability and philosophy of science. Your thesis is that in mechanistic anomaly detection, two implementations should count as the same mechanism not when they share isomorphic causal graphs or interchangeable parts, but when they converge on identical dynamical attractors in their state-space dynamics—even if every physical component and every causal edge has been replaced or rewired. Your audience is split between ML researchers who operationalize mechanisms as circuit subgraphs and philosophers of science working in interventionist and mechanistic-explanation traditions. Draft your speaker-prep sheet: list the objections you will actually face, ordered by likelihood and damage, phrased in the vocabulary each subgroup uses. Include the single objection you are hoping nobody raises—specifically, the charge that your attractor-based criterion collapses mechanism identity into bare functional equivalence, thereby erasing the explanatory distinction between mechanistic understanding and black-box prediction—and prepare the reply you would have to give if it comes. Make the sheet practical and ordered for defense, not for exploration.
