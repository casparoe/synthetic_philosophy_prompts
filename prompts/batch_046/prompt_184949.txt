i've been stewing on parfit's hitchiker case and i think it can be twisted into an argument that rational people should be bad at philosophy. my study partner says i'm overreaching but can't pinpoint why. here's our chain:

(1) in the hitchhiker case, it's rational ex ante to want to be disposed to pay the driver after rescue, even though ex post the payment is irrational.
(2) the reason is instrumental: the driver predicts the dispostion, so having it gets you rescued, and that's worth the cost.
(3) apply the same structure to belief: it's instrumentally valuable to be disposed to form beliefs that motivate you, even when evidence is weak — like believing your cause is just or that you'll succeed.
(4) so ex ante it's rational to want a belief-forming mechanism that isn't fully evidence-responsive.
(5) but philosophical reasoning just IS being fully evidence-responsive about abstract claims, regardless of motivation.
(6) therefore ex ante it's rational to want to be disposed to reject philosophical conclusions that don't serve your interests.
(7) but if you actually have that dispostion, you can't trust your own rejection of this very argument.
(8) so either rationality requires distrusting rationality, or the hitchhiker pattern doesn't extend to belief.

where does this go wrong? i really want to keep (1)-(3) since they both feel right, but (6) smells like it has a hidden equivocation between "being disposed to act against interest" and "being disposed to form beliefs against evidence." is that the problem, or is it deeper — maybe something about doxastic voluntarism or a scope shift with "rational ex ante"? tell me which premise to drop and what it costs me, especially if i want to save the idea that rational agents can rationaly cultivate irrational dispositions.
