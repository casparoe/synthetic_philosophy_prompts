In a working group I run on moral cognition and normative ethics, we have been stalled for over a year on how to interpret the cross-cultural literature on folk morality—specifically the well-documented finding that outside WEIRD populations, loyalty, authority, and purity violations are widely treated as genuinely moral transgressions rather than as conventional or prudential failures, while WEIRD respondents tend to restrict the moral domain to harm and fairness. The descriptive pattern itself is not in dispute; everyone accepts the data. The disagreement concerns what it implies for moral objectivity.

One faction—the Divergence Objectivists—argues that because the boundary of the moral domain shifts predictably with cultural environment, there cannot be culture-independent moral facts. At best, they say, there are locally stable norms that track evolved cooperative strategies, and the predictive stability of these differences across generations shows that moral judgment is causally anchored in contingent social history rather than in independent reasons.

The other faction—the Universalist Structuralists—grants the surface divergence but insists it reflects parameter variation within a single universal moral grammar. All cultures, they claim, possess the same underlying modules; cultural institutions determine which modules are chronically activated, which violations become salient, and how affect is calibrated. They point to priming studies in which WEIRD subjects can be induced to weight loyalty or authority more heavily, and to shared neural correlates of moral evaluation across populations. The Divergence Objectivists reply that parameter-setting explanations only push the problem back: if the parameters are set by contingent history and are not themselves responsive to independent moral reasons, then the judgments are causally explained but not justified, and objectivity is lost at the level of calibration, not merely application. The Universalists counter that calibration is precisely where moral reasoning operates—objectivity does not require identical first-order outputs but convergence under shared procedural conditions.

The stalemate is frustrating because both sides accept the same empirical findings and the same logical relations, and yet neither has moved. I want a structural diagnosis rather than an adjudication of who is right. Is this disagreement actually hidden empirical—say, about whether moral psychology supports a sharp distinction between domain-specification and parameter-calibration, and whether that distinction can be experimentally isolated? Is it about competing standards for what counts as data in moral philosophy—predictive stability of cultural differences versus shared underlying architecture? Or is it a bedrock clash about what moral objectivity requires: specifically, whether objectivity demands that correct moral judgment be invariant across cultural environments, or only that there exist correct procedures by which any culture could, in principle, revise its parameters?

Furthermore, if your diagnosis is that the sides are answering different questions—perhaps one about whether moral properties are causally independent of culture, the other about whether moral cognition is universally structured—what conceptual distinction or staged thought experiment could expose that divergence and either resolve it or show that it should not be resolved? If, on the other hand, you think the impasse is structural and that no new result or distinction could move it, explain why, and say what that implies for how a working group with these commitments should proceed.
