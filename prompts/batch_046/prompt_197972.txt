I have just come away from a lengthy argument with a friend about illusionism concerning phenomenal consciousness; she insisted that if phenomenal experience is an illusion, there must be a real subject who is deceived, which she took to collapse the view into self-contradiction or tacit dualism. I need a teaching plan for an interdisciplinary reading group—philosophers of science and cognitive psychologists with no specialist background in consciousness studies—to explain how illusionism can deny the reality of phenomenal properties without denying that there is experience. Present your response as exactly five numbered items, each one or two sentences long, with no introductory or concluding remarks. First, specify the dependency order in which the components must be introduced so that the inversion of the explanatory gap—treating the gap as evidence of introspective misrepresentation rather than an ontological deficit—feels earned rather than contrived. Second, identify the single carrying example that should anchor the session, describing what properties it must possess and what it must lack in order to block the misconception that an illusion requires a homuncular observer. Third, map the predictable misconceptions—the self-refutation objection, the conflation with eliminativism about access consciousness, and the assumption that illusionism denies subjective feeling altogether—to the precise points in the sequence where each should be headed off. Fourth, propose a brief check for understanding that demonstrates whether the group has grasped the misrepresentation thesis rather than merely retaining that consciousness is mysterious. Fifth, state explicitly what must be omitted at this level, such as detailed neurophenomenological findings or technical debates about phenomenal concepts, and acknowledge that omission plainly.
