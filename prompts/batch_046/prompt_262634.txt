From the standpoint of a doctoral researcher in philosophy of religion examining the afterlife of Fear and Trembling in contemporary bioethical discourse, I want to test whether Kierkegaard's concept of the teleological suspension of the ethical can be coherently extended beyond its original Abrahamic frame. Consider a hypothetical case without direct nineteenth-century precedent: a prospective parent, after receiving comprehensive polygenic risk profiling, chooses to implant an embryo carrying a high-probability genotype for exceptional cognitive capacity coupled with a severe, lifelong psychiatric vulnerability—explicitly against the unanimous recommendation of clinical ethics boards—because they understand themselves as singularly called to nurture that specific potentiality regardless of the universal moral obligation to minimize foreseeable suffering. If Johannes de Silentio were asked to evaluate this decision, would he read it as a genuine repetition of Abraham's paradox—faith suspending the ethical for a higher telos—or as a category error that collapses the knight of faith into aesthetic self-justification? In your answer, do not invoke secondary literature, citation lists, or bibliographies; instead, reconstruct the evaluation solely from the internal logic of the pseudonymous authorship, the structure of the aesthetic, ethical, and religious stages, and the specific paradox of Abraham's silence. Be precise about where the analogy holds, particularly regarding the incommunicability of the call and the isolation of the individual before God, and where it breaks down, especially with respect to the absence of a direct divine command and the presence of calculative, predictive rationality in the parent's reasoning. Finally, indicate whether Silentio would regard the parent's refusal to justify the act to the ethics board as a legitimate form of silence analogous to Abraham's concealment from Sarah, Eliezer, and Isaac, or as a failure of the ethical duty to speak that reveals the absence of true faith.
