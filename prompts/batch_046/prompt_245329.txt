As a retired experimental physicist now reading philosophy, I have been struck by an analogy that seems to fail precisely where it ought to hold. In physical systems, perfect symmetry between two potential wells is never genuinely stable; an infinitesimal fluctuation or hidden bias inevitably breaks it, yielding a determinate outcome. Buridan's ass, by contrast, is stipulated to face two perfectly equivalent bales of hay with no perturbation whatsoever, yet we still ask how the donkey might choose rather than starve. Please explain the conceptual issue: is choice without preference structurally different from physical symmetry breaking because it requires a form of determination that does not depend on any causal differentiator, even one below the threshold of observation? Or can the philosopher's notion of arbitrary selection be reconceived as merely an unmeasured cognitive fluctuation? I seek not a historical survey but a clear, gut-level clarification of why rational agency under perfect equilibrium might resist reduction to a hidden-variable physics of decision.
