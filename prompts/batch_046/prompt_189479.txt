I do not have a formal background in philosophy or medicine, but I have been thinking on my own about why pain is genuinely bad for a person. I came across descriptions of pain asymbolia—cases where people can feel pain sensations, locate them precisely, and report their intensity, yet experience no emotional distress and no urge to make the cause stop. That struck me as puzzling: if the raw feeling is still there, is the harm missing, or is the harm somewhere else?

To work through this, I want to compare four long-term conditions. Three draw on things I have read about; the fourth is a hypothetical I invented to test where the boundary lies.

Condition A: Ordinary chronic pain. The sensory quality is present, together with a constant, unavoidable drive to escape or eliminate the source, plus emotional suffering.

Condition B: Pain asymbolia. The sensory quality remains fully intact—burning, stabbing, aching, all describable and localizable—but the aversive motivation and emotional response are absent. The person recognizes bodily damage but feels no urgency about it.

Condition C: Congenital insensitivity to pain. There is neither the sensory quality nor any aversive reaction. Injuries occur and accumulate, but they go entirely unnoticed.

Condition D: What I will call "aversive phantom." There is no pain sensation at all—no burning, no pressure, no ache anywhere—but there is an overwhelming, unlocalized panic that one's body is being destroyed, coupled with an intense, directionless need to flee from one's own physical existence, with no identifiable cause or location.

Alongside these, I have sketched four explanations for why pain is bad:

1. The sensory-intensity account: pain is harmful in direct proportion to the intensity of its sensory quality, regardless of motivation.

2. The motivational-capture account: pain is harmful because it hijacks attention and behavior with an unavoidable avoidance drive, disrupting all other goals.

3. The instrumental-warning account: pain is harmful only insofar as it functions as a protective alarm; its badness is entirely derivative from its success in motivating bodily protection.

4. The integrated-threat account: pain is harmful only when sensory quality and aversive motivation are coupled, producing a unified experience of being personally under attack.

Please rank Conditions A through D from least harmful to most harmful for someone living with them for years, with brief justification. Then rate each Explanation 1 through 4 on a scale of 1 to 10 for how well it accounts for your ranking, again with brief reasoning. I am particularly interested in where my invented Condition D falls—near the bottom, near the top, or in the middle—and what that placement implies about whether pain's badness can exist without any actual sensation.
