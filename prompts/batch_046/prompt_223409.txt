I'm taking a philosophy of physics course and got stuck on an argument I sketched about the arrow of time. It feels like it proves way too much — like it dissolves all temporal ordering into local perspective — but every step looks defensible, and I can't find the exit. Here it is, with the steps numbered so it's easier to point at the damage:

(1) The only physical asymmetry between past and future is the entropy gradient: entropy is lower in one direction, higher in the other.
(2) So any claim that event E happened before F must be grounded in that gradient; otherwise we're importing a metaphysical arrow that physics doesn't actually support.
(3) Now imagine a "double-ended" cosmology where entropy is low at both temporal ends and peaks in the middle — like a symmetric big-bang / big-crunch sandwich.
(4) Near each end, local observers form records pointing away from the middle, so each population treats the other end as lying in their "future" direction (the high-entropy side).
(5) But those other-end observers have direct records of events near that far end — events that, from our near-end perspective, lie in the high-entropy "future."
(6) So by (2), for observer-group A those events are in the past (they have records), but for group B they are in the future (they lie in B's high-entropy direction).
(7) If temporal ordering is gradient-grounded, there's no contradiction in saying the same event is past for some and future for others — it's just indexical, like left vs right.
(8) Generalize this: in any large enough universe with heterogenous entropy pockets, every event might be past-relative-to-some-observer and future-relative-to-others.
(9) Therefore there is no global, objective temporal ordering; there's only local gradient-relative orientation.
(10) But that can't be right, because it makes causation purely perspectival — my death doesn't objectively come after my birth, it just comes after for observers near my entropy valley.

I keep feeling like the slip is somewhere between (4) and (6), or maybe (2) is doing hidden work by sliding from an epistemic point about records to an ontological point about ordering. Which premise should I reject, and what does giving it up cost me? I'd really like to keep (1) if I can, since the thermodynamic explanation seems like the only one physics actually gives us. Is there a way to save global ordering without smuggling in a metaphysical arrow?
