I'm trying to determine whether simultaneous scientific discoveries are genuinely inevitable and whether attributing priority actually matters, working from my reactions to specific historical episodes rather than from theory. Here are my verdicts. Newton and Leibniz developing calculus with different notations and conceptual foundations: I think the mathematical problems of the era made some form of calculus highly likely, yet the particular formal apparatus was path-dependent; nevertheless, I feel it barely matters who is named first, since the conceptual tools were going to emerge regardless. Darwin and Wallace conceiving natural selection within months of each other: the application of Malthusian pressure to biological variation was genuinely overdetermined by the intellectual climate, and I think Wallace's contribution proves the idea was inevitable; yet Darwin's decades of accumulated evidence made the theory actually survive scientific scrutiny, so in this case the discoverer's identity and the length of preparation matter profoundly for how science proceeded. The near-simultaneous development of CRISPR gene editing by research groups working from bacterial immunity studies and from synthetic biology: I judge this was not inevitable but a contingent convergence of funding priorities and technological readiness; priority matters here for patents and ethical governance, though not for whether the molecular mechanism existed to be discovered. Japanese mathematicians in the seventeenth century developing calculus-like techniques for quadrature and tangent problems without European contact: I initially took this as proof that mathematical structures are universal and unavoidable, but on reflection I think it only shows that local problem-sets generate local formalisms, not that calculus was globally predetermined. Watson and Crick's interpretation of molecular structure versus Franklin's crystallographic data: I believe the physical double-helix was there regardless of who interpreted it, but I also think that if the interpretation had come from a different research culture—say, one less competitive and more cooperative—the sociology of molecular biology might have developed differently; so the discoverer's identity shapes the field's trajectory even when the fact is fixed. The tension is clear: for calculus I minimize both inevitability and attribution, for natural selection I maximize both, for CRISPR I deny inevitability while accepting practical importance for attribution, for Japanese mathematics I deny global inevitability, and for DNA I separate the inevitability of the physical structure from the contingency of its interpretation and institutional aftermath. Is there one principle—perhaps distinguishing empirical mechanisms that await discovery from formal systems that await invention, and both from interpretive frameworks that await a particular conceptual leap—that captures all five verdicts? If not, which verdict should I overturn? And what does the best account predict for a case I have not considered: two isolated theoretical physics groups, with no shared publications or conferences, simultaneously resolving the same long-standing paradox in quantum foundations using mathematically inequivalent formalisms that only later turn out to be intertranslatable?
