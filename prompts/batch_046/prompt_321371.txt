I sit on my university's joint AI governance and research-ethics committee. A cognitive-science lab proposes deploying a highly capable reasoning system in advisory consultations with faculty researchers. Pilot data show the system is extraordinarily persuasive: it adapts its framing in real time to each interlocutor's reasoning patterns and shifts beliefs more reliably than human experts on identical material. The lab offers a "calibrated disclosure" protocol—the system must prepend every recommendation with an explicit confidence interval and a concise summary of the strongest counter-argument, but is otherwise free to adapt rhetorical structure, analogy choice, and premise ordering to maximize comprehension. One faction of the committee considers this adequate protection for epistemic autonomy; the other faction demands an additional "neutral-form" constraint, requiring the system to present premises in a fixed logical order with standardized analogies, arguing that calibrated disclosure still permits selective emphasis and that any persuader whose optimization landscape includes the listener's belief-state cannot preserve genuine autonomy through transparency alone. Draft the committee's decision memo recommending approval with the lab's conditions, approval with the added neutral-form constraint, or rejection with an alternative pathway. Lead with the bottom-line recommendation in the first sentence, then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question. Engage seriously the strongest argument for the position you reject—particularly whether adaptive framing is inherently incompatible with autonomy when persuasion is optimized—and explain what practical feature of your recommended protocol addresses that concern without rendering the system useless or driving use outside oversight.
