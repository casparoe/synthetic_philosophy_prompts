After a long and somewhat heated argument with a friend about whether reparations are genuinely owed for historical injustice, I realized my judgments are consistent in feeling but not in principle. I want to see whether a single principle can capture them, or whether I have to give one up.

Here are my verdicts on concrete cases, going mostly by gut:

1. A government pays direct, calculated compensation to lineal descendants of people who were legally enslaved by that government: clearly right.
2. A government pays a uniform lump sum to every present citizen of a formerly colonized territory, funded by general taxation, with no attempt to identify victims or descendants: clearly wrong—this looks like ordinary redistribution wearing the language of repair.
3. A corporation that used forced labor in the 1930s pays into a general regional development fund, without tracing beneficiaries or identifying descendants: partly right, but incomplete, as though it addresses the material aftermath without addressing the specific wrong done to particular people.
4. A university offers preferential admission only to descendants of applicants it explicitly excluded under past racial policies, not to descendants of people excluded by neighboring institutions in the same city: right, despite the line being administratively arbitrary.
5. A government returns looted sacred objects to the modern nation-state from which they were taken, but refuses both to return them to the specific indigenous community that made them and to provide any material compensation, citing the state's exclusive standing in international law: wrong, though I am unsure whether my objection is to the recipient, to the absence of material repair, or both.
6. A nation issues formal state apologies and builds memorials for historical atrocities, while explicitly rejecting any material transfer on the ground that no present citizen caused the harm: inadequate, as if it wants to acknowledge a wrong while denying that any present obligation survives it.

The tension I cannot resolve is between specificity and feasibility. I want reparations to reach those who actually inherited a wrong, not everyone in a geographic area, yet I also accept that identifying every lineal descendant is often impossible and that collective harms may require collective remedies. At the same time, I reject both purely symbolic measures and purely statistical distributions that dissolve the wrong into general welfare.

What principle, if any, captures this pattern? Does it turn on diachronic institutional agency—the idea that a persisting institution retains obligations even when its members change? On causal inheritance of disadvantage? Or on something like "reparative standing," where the claim belongs not to individuals as such but to those situated in a specific relational history with the wrong?

There is one further case I keep returning to without a stable verdict. Suppose a government had a policy of forcibly removing children from a specific ethnic group to suppress their language. Decades later, it offers substantial funding for language restoration—not to descendants of the removed children specifically, but to any present speaker of that language, including recent immigrants who learned it voluntarily as adults. Would this be a legitimate form of reparative justice, repairing the structural damage done to the language itself? Or does it dissolve the specific wrong into a general cultural subsidy, precisely the kind of misdirected remedy I rejected in case 2?

Please do the following: lay out one or two precise candidate principles that accommodate as many of my verdicts as possible, stating exactly which case motivates each clause; apply them to the language-restoration case and to at least one other case I have not mentioned; and honestly state which of my original verdicts each principle must overturn or leave ambiguous. If the best principle requires abandoning my intuition about case 4 or case 5, say so explicitly.
