So I've been working through this idea that the separateness of persons objection to utilitarianism is usually sold short. Most people say it's just about not being able to justify harming one person to help five, but I think the deeper version is what I'd call the boundary-preservation claim: moral reasons generated inside one person's life-plan can't be legitimately pooled or traded across persons unless there's an explicit consent-based or relational bridge. Utilitarianism fails not just because it allows trade-offs, but because it treats welfare as a kind of fugible currency that ignores these boundaries. I find this pretty convincing, especially when you think about cases where someone is asked to bear a massive cost so that strangers get tiny benefits—there's something weird about saying the reason applys to them in the same direct way.

But I want to stress-test it. What would actually count against the boundary-preservation claim? I'm looking for arguments, thought experiments, or maybe formal results that show these boundaries either don't exist or aren't morally decisive—things that defenders of the view themselves would have to concede are real problems, not just critics talking past us. Also, has this kind of position been qualified so much that it's basically unfalsifiable? Like, if every counterexample gets answered by saying well that case had a hidden relational bridge, does that make the view vacuous, and is that a fatal flaw or just how normative theories work? I'd expecially like to know if there's decisive evidence versus merely awkward evidence, and whether there are developments in moral psychology or decision theory that should make me update. I was arguing about this with a friend over coffee and realized I couldn't name the thing that would actually change my mind, so help me map out where this view is actually exposed.
