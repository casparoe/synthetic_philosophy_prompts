I encountered a summary in a recent philosophy-of-science essay that applies Gödel’s incompleteness theorems to formal normative theory. The author writes that Gödel’s first theorem proves that any consistent, recursively axiomatizable system capable of expressing Peano arithmetic contains sentences that are true in the standard model yet unprovable within the system; extending this, the author claims that any normative framework with comparable logical strength—say, one that can formalize quantitative welfare comparisons—will necessarily include moral truths that cannot be derived from its axioms. The summary then invokes the second incompleteness theorem to argue that such a system can never demonstrate its own consistency, which means moral realists who adopt formal axiomatizations must treat consistency as an external, non-derivable article of faith. Finally, the author notes that because Gödel’s original proof required omega-consistency, a normative system that deliberately avoids omega-inconsistency can escape incompleteness altogether, and concludes that Gödel thereby demonstrates formal ethics to be either permanently incomplete or resting on unprovable commitments.

This sounds technically informed, but I am unsure how much survives careful scrutiny. Could you audit the summary? Please separate what is technically accurate about the theorems themselves, what counts as a standard simplification that omits necessary conditions (such as recursive axiomatizability, the syntactic-versus-semantic distinction, or the role of the standard model), and what is a misapplication or overstatement—particularly regarding omega-consistency and Rosser’s improvement, the conditions under which a system genuinely escapes the theorems, and whether these mathematical results place any substantive constraint on moral or normative theory. I want to know whether the conclusion that formal ethics is impossible survives once the summary is corrected, or whether the errors are serious enough to undermine the argument entirely.
