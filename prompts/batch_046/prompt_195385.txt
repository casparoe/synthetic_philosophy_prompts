As a longtime reader of rationalist forums who teaches a graduate seminar in value theory, I am drafting a 90-minute exam essay that tests whether students understand negative utilitarianism structurally rather than being able to reproduce the familiar asymmetry between suffering and happiness. The crux should be a tension that arises when that asymmetry is embedded in a risk framework: if suffering carries lexical or near-lexical priority, then any trajectory with a non-zero probability of generating unprecedented suffering seems categorically prohibited, even when it prevents vastly more baseline suffering. To prevent students from reaching for pre-canned trolley-problem templates, I have constructed a specific comparative case. Program Alpha eliminates a well-understood form of suffering across a very large population with near-certainty, but carries a 0.01% risk of producing a qualitatively different, more intense suffering-state that is not commensurable with the baseline; Program Beta eliminates a smaller, well-understood suffering-state with absolute certainty and zero tail risk, but does not scale. I want students to determine whether negative utilitarianism can adjudicate between Alpha and Beta without either covertly importing a positive value of scale or knowledge, or collapsing into decision paralysis in which any catastrophic tail risk dominates regardless of baseline benefits. Please write the exam question suitable for a 90-minute response of approximately 800 words; a model answer that works through the adjudication step by step; and a marking scheme built around five must-hit analytical moves totaling 20 marks. For each move, specify its point value and include a one-line note on the characteristic failure mode—such as treating the risk as a simple expected-value problem, resolving the tension by denying incommensurability, or importing an unstated positive commitment to knowledge production. The prompt should assume familiarity with suffering-focused ethics, but it must not ask for citations, bibliographies, reading lists, or specific author attributions beyond the conceptual framing itself.
