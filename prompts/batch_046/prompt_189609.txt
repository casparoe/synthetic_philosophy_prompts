I hold the view that the three canonical failures of Athenian direct democracy—the assembly's initial vote to execute all Mytilenean men and enslave the women and children, its authorization of the Sicilian Expedition despite Nicias's explicit strategic objections, and the jury's conviction of Socrates—reveal a structural defect in unmediated popular sovereignty rather than mere contingent misfortune. Specifically, I argue that when a single deliberative body exercises legislative, executive, and judicial authority without an independent constitutional filter—such as the diminished Areopagus after Ephialtes's reforms—procedural legitimacy can be converted into substantive catastrophe by rhetorical escalation faster than institutional memory can correct it. I take this to challenge contemporary arguments for sortition-based citizen assemblies or expansive direct-democratic mechanisms, since the Athenian case suggests that error-correction requires institutional separation, not merely broader participation.

What would have to be true for this position to be wrong? I want an honest assessment of its exposure. Which underlying assumptions—about the causal role of rhetoric in the Mytilene reversal, about whether the Sicilian vote reflected broad strategic consensus rather than emotional dynamics, about whether Socrates's trial was genuinely democratic or an aberration driven by specific post-war trauma—could fail, and what evidence or arguments would demonstrate that failure? Are there interpretations of these episodes that preserve the structural critique but relocate the defect away from democratic procedure itself, perhaps onto imperial overreach, elite factionalism, or wartime psychology? Conversely, is there evidence that Athenian democracy possessed functioning internal error-correcting mechanisms—such as the second-day reversal at Mytilene or the graphe paranomon—that actually worked, and if so, does that defeat my claim or merely qualify it?

Please distinguish between what would be decisive against my view and what would merely be awkward. For instance, would it be decisive if historians showed that the Sicilian decision emerged from sustained strategic deliberation rather than from Cleon-style emotional manipulation? Would it undermine the position if Socrates's trial turned out to be procedurally regular by Athenian legal standards, with no procedural irregularity? I am especially interested in whether my thesis risks being qualified into unfalsifiability—say, by redefining every democratic success as not truly direct and every failure as structurally inevitable—and whether that insulation is itself a problem for an argument of this kind. What predictions does my structural-defect thesis make that rival explanations, such as the view that Athenian failures were products of imperial overreach rather than democratic procedure, do not make? And are there live escape routes, analogous to hidden-variable loopholes, that defenders of direct democracy could take without abandoning the core commitment to popular sovereignty? Finally, what should I personally find most persuasive: a historical demonstration that Athenian institutions corrected themselves effectively, or a conceptual argument that modern direct-democratic designs have solved the separation problem I identify?
