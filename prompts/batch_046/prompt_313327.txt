Consider the following argument, which I have been trying to repair without abandoning its core intuition about moral worth.

(1) If an action possesses full moral worth, then the agent performs it for the right reasons—that is, the reasons that make the action right are also the reasons that actually move the agent.

(2) To be moved by a reason R, an agent must represent R in some form and take it as favoring the action.

(3) If an agent represents R and takes it as favoring the action, then the agent is in a position to know that she is acting on R.

(4) Once an agent knows she is acting on R, her motivation is necessarily partly constituted by a second-order concern: she wants to be the sort of person who acts on R, or she wants to maintain her self-conception as responsive to R.

(5) Therefore, any agent who is self-aware enough to be moved by R is also partly moved by this second-order concern.

(6) But a second-order concern about one's own moral character is not the reason that makes the action right; it is a self-regarding reason.

(7) So in any case where an agent knows what she is doing and why, her motivation is mixed: part right-reason, part self-regarding reason.

(8) If a motivation is mixed in this way, then the agent does not act purely for the right reasons.

(9) Hence, no self-aware agent ever acts purely for the right reasons, and therefore no self-aware action ever possesses full moral worth.

(10) The only actions with full moral worth, then, are those performed in ignorance of one's own motives—either by agents who lack self-awareness or by agents who have not yet recognized the normative force of R.

(11) But moral education aims precisely at producing agents who are self-aware about the normative force of R and responsive to it.

(12) Therefore, successful moral education systematically undermines the possibility of full moral worth.

The conclusion seems absurd: it implies that we should either avoid moral education or celebrate moral ignorance. Yet each premise seems defensible—(1) captures a Kantian or Arpalian intuition, (2)-(3) seem like minimal conditions on being moved by a reason, and (4) draws on familiar observations about the corruption of virtue by self-consciousness. Where does the chain break? Identify the specific premise or inferential step that should be rejected, explain why it initially looks plausible, and then price the rejection: what must a defender of moral worth say about self-awareness, about mixed motives, or about the conditions on being moved by a reason? In particular, discuss whether the argument illicitly shifts between "being partly motivated by" and "being moved solely by," and whether there is a stable position that allows an agent to be fully responsive to the right reasons while remaining aware that she is so responsive.
