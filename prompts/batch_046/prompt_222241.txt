I hold the view that the Cartesian theater is not just empirically unsupported but conceptually incoherent: positing a single inner stage on which consciousness "happens" merely relocates the observer problem rather than dissolving it. As an alternative, I accept a strong version of the multiple drafts model — specifically, that there is no canonical, final-version experience occurring at a determinable neural moment, but only distributed, competing narrative fragments that achieve temporary coherence through probing and editorial selection. What would genuinely count against this position? I want its exposure mapped precisely: which empirical results — for example, about global workspace integration, cross-modal binding in split-brain subjects, or the temporal dynamics of reportability — would be decisive defeaters rather than merely awkward accommodations; which conceptual costs (say, about diachronic personal identity or the authority of first-person report) defenders of the view should themselves concede; and whether the model risks being qualified into unfalsifiability, such that any observed neural synchronization can be redescribed as draft convergence without ever allowing for a true theatrical presentation. In particular, is there an observation that could distinguish the absence of a central stage from a distributed stage that functions identically, or does that very distinction collapse the view into a notational variant of its rival? Rank the defeaters from fatal to cosmetic, and be honest about whether the position is well insulated — and whether that insulation is a virtue or a vice for a theory of consciousness.
