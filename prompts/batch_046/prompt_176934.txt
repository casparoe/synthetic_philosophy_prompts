Question: In elections where both main candidates advocate rights-violating policies, philosophers sometimes describe an 'expressive trap': voting for the lesser evil expresses complicity, while abstaining expresses indifference. One proposed resolution distinguishes 'threshold endorsement' (voting crosses a line into complicity regardless of comparison) from 'gradient endorsement' (complicity scales with the harm you enable relative to alternatives). Does this distinction actually resolve the trap? Discuss with particular attention to whether abstention escapes complicity altogether.

Student answer (approx. 400 words):

The distinction is useful because it shows complicity isn't binary. Under gradient endorsement, choosing Candidate A (who wants to restrict speech) over Candidate B (who wants to ban it) puts you lower on the scale, since you enable less harm. This is basically the utilitarian calculation: minimise damage. Jeremy Bentham developed the idea of threshold endorsement in his political writings on representative government, arguing that any vote for a rights-violating regime crosses a moral line no matter the alternative. So the threshold view is Benthamite, while gradient endorsement is closer to modern consequentialism. Abstaining avoids the trap entirely because it involves no selection, so there's no endorsement to scale. Of course, abstention might still have instrumental effects — your vote could have stopped the worse candidate — but that's a separate point about efficacy, not expression. Overall, the distinction resolves the expressive problem for voters, though I think abstainers are still in a cleaner position morally because they haven't actively placed themselves on the gradient.
