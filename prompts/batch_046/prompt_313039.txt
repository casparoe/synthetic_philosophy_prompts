Here's an argument I cooked up after skimming some Ash'arite kalam and now it feels like a reductio that hits too hard. (1) Al-Ghazali denies necessary connections between created things: fire doesn't burn cotton, God just pairs them habitually. (2) The usual definintion of a miracle is an event that violates necessary natural law. (3) If occasionalism is right, there are no necessary laws linking fire and cotton—only regularities God keeps up out of habit. (4) So there's literally nothing for a miracle to violate; breaking the pairing isn't breaking a law, since there was never a law. (5) Therefore occasionalism makes miracles conceptually impossible. (6) But Al-Ghazali uses occasionalism specifically to defend miracles like the sun not rising or dead bodies reviving. (7) So the view undermines the very thing it's built to protect. (8) The escape is to say miracles violate divine habits, not natural laws—but then those habits are doing all the work of laws, and occasionalism collapses into theism-with-exceptions, i.e. standard physicalism plus God. Where's the mistake? I'd like to keep (1) and the possibility of miracles, but I think (2) is importing a mechanist framework Al-Ghazali rejects. Is that the exit, or is there a hidden slide between habits and laws that breaks the argument?
