Student answer: In Ecce Homo, Nietzsche argues that philosophical insight depends on physiological conditions, presenting his own diet—light, Italian-influenced food, little alcohol, and strict climate selection—as a necessary precondition for the clarity that made his critique of Christian morality possible. He extends this analysis to Wagner, whose sedentary habits and heavy German diet, he claims, directly produced the emotional excess and theatricality of his music. For Nietzsche, then, bodily regimen is not merely background but a form of philosophical argument: to change one's metaphysics, one must first change one's digestion. At the same time, he insists that intellectual discipline—particularly the selection and avoidance of books—functions as mental nutrition, so philosophy involves both bodily and cognitive cultivation. The view avoids crude reductionism because it allows interaction between body and mind, yet it remains vulnerable to circularity, since Nietzsche uses the same concept of decadence to describe Wagner's body and his art without providing independent criteria for either.
