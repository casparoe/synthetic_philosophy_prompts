I am preparing an interview for a philosophy-of-logic podcast with an unnamed defender of what she calls “domain-locked restrictivism” about Gödel’s incompleteness results—the view that the theorems apply only within precisely graded conditions of formalizability and do not license broad conclusions about human cognition, artificial intelligence, or other non-arithmetical domains. She distinguishes three grades: weak formalizability (computational capacity without explicit axiomatization), strong formalizability (recursively axiomatized representation of primitive recursive arithmetic), and reflective formalizability (explicit inclusion of consistency statements about oneself). I want eight to ten questions that press where the literature actually presses—on the Lucas-Penrose argument, on self-certifying architectures, on the syntactic requirements for a literal Gödel sentence, and on the patchwork defense of mechanism—each with a follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give.

1. Popular expositions claim that Gödel’s first theorem shows any system “complex enough to do arithmetic” is incomplete. You argue that “complex enough” must be parsed through your three grades. Can you specify exactly which conditions—effective axiomatizability, omega-consistency or mere consistency, and representability of primitive recursive functions—are jointly necessary for the first theorem to apply? And why does a neural network that implicitly learns arithmetic but possesses no explicit axiom set fall into weak rather than strong formalizability?

Follow-up: If you concede that an implicit representation can always be extracted into an explicit axiomatization by an external observer, doesn’t your weak/strong distinction collapse in practice? What empirical feature of a cognitive architecture—say, the absence of a globally accessible proof-checking procedure—would convince you it genuinely remains weak?

2. The Lucas-Penrose argument moves from “this formal system F cannot prove Con(F)” to “the human mind can recognize Con(F), therefore the mind is not F.” You reject this on the grounds that it assumes human cognition is a single, consistent formal system. But critics reply that the argument needs only local consistency about arithmetic, not global consistency. How do you respond without begging the question against the mechanist?

Follow-up: If you respond by distributing cognition across a social or evolutionary process rather than an individual mind, does that distributed system itself become formalizable at a higher level? And if so, haven’t you relocated the Gödelian limit rather than dissolved it?

3. Self-certifying AI architectures attempt to verify their own consistency within a stronger meta-system. You treat this not as an escape but as a “level shift” governed by the second incompleteness theorem. Is your objection conceptual—about the impossibility of reflective closure—or practical—about an infinite regress of meta-systems?

Follow-up: If your objection is conceptual, then does your view entail that any reflective AI—one that can represent its own proof procedures—is in principle incapable of trustworthy self-assessment? Or is there a principled break in the hierarchy, perhaps at a non-computable oracle level, that allows genuine closure?

4. You reject the claim that Gödel reveals “mathematical truths absolutely unprovable by humans,” insisting that undecidability is always relative to a specific formalization. Defenders of mathematical platonism cite Tarski’s undefinability result to argue that arithmetical truth cannot be defined within arithmetic itself, implying access to a non-formalized realm. How do you reconcile your domain-locked view with the apparent need for a meta-language to define truth?

Follow-up: If you concede that defining arithmetical truth requires stepping outside any given formal system, why doesn’t that concession grant the platonist exactly the kind of extra-formal cognitive access they claim human mathematicians possess?

5. Your taxonomy places biological cognition in the weak-formalizability grade, partly because brains lack the syntactic transparency required for self-reference. Recent neuro-symbolic AI attempts embed explicit logical representations within neural architectures. If such a hybrid achieved transparent self-reference about its arithmetic component, would it be promoted automatically to your reflective grade, and would Gödel’s second theorem then apply?

Follow-up: If yes, then doesn’t your view predict that successful neuro-symbolic integration will hit a hard consistency ceiling? And if that empirical prediction fails—if the hybrid system remains reliable—would you abandon the grade distinction or revise what counts as reflective?

6. The standard mechanist rebuttal to Lucas-Penrose is that the mind is not a single Turing machine but a patchwork of subsystems, none meeting Gödel’s conditions individually. You call this the “patchwork defense” and charge that it saves mechanism only by making it unfalsifiable. Is your charge primarily about unfalsifiability, or does it depend on a specific unity condition for personal identity?

Follow-up: If the mechanist accepts your challenge and proposes a global-workspace architecture that integrates subsystems without unifying them into a single axiomatization, does that architecture fall under your strong or reflective grade? Can you show that Gödel’s theorems still fail to apply?

7. Some philosophers construct Gödel-style sentences for ethics or legal theory by encoding normative propositions into arithmetic. You treat these analogies as category errors, not because the domains lack structure, but because they lack the specific syntactic coding relation—representability of primitive recursive functions—that Gödel’s proof requires. Can you specify the minimal structural feature that must be present for a literal Gödel sentence to exist, and why ethics necessarily lacks it?

Follow-up: If someone designed a formal deontic logic with explicit Gödel numbering of its own formulas, satisfying your coding condition, would you then accept the resulting undecidable normative statement as genuinely analogous to the arithmetical case, or would you still insist on a domain-specific block?

8. Assuming your restrictivism is correct, what should change in verification protocols for high-stakes AI? Should we abandon internal consistency proofs and rely solely on external auditing? And if external auditors are themselves cognitive systems—perhaps collective human institutions—aren’t they subject to the same grade-based limits?

Follow-up: If you recommend external auditing, how do you avoid an infinite regress of auditors auditing auditors? Is there a practical stopping point—say, democratic oversight or empirical stress-testing—that does not pretend to be a formal consistency proof but is sufficient for justified trust?
