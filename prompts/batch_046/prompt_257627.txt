In my graduate seminar on moral responsibility last week, two doctoral students—Maya and Leo—fell into a sustained disagreement about whether the standard taxonomy of moral luck is incomplete. I am reproducing their exchange below and ask you to adjudicate it.

Maya began by proposing that the familiar categories—resultant, circumstantial, constitutive, and causal luck—omit a distinct form she called interpretive moral luck: the luck of having one’s actions embedded in a narrative framework that is largely outside one’s control. She offered a paired case: two whistleblowers with identical intentions, identical risks taken, and identical organizational outcomes, but one is subsequently framed by the press and institutional memory as a principled hero in a redemption arc, while the other is framed as an opportunist riding a transient wave of public anger. Maya argued that because moral assessment is partly constituted by social practices of holding responsible—praising, blaming, resenting, and memorializing—the interpretive frame directly affects the agent’s moral standing within the community, not merely her reputation.

Leo replied that Maya was conflating moral status with social status. Moral luck, he said, concerns whether an agent is genuinely blameworthy or praiseworthy given her capacities and choices, not whether others happen to praise or blame her. If interpretive framing is outside the agent’s control but affects only social perception, it belongs to sociology or psychology, not to moral philosophy proper.

Maya countered that Leo’s distinction was too sharp. The practices of holding responsible, she said, are not external decorations applied to a pre-existing moral status; they partly constitute the moral relations in question. If an agent is never blamed, never resented, and never held accountable—because the prevailing narrative framework makes such responses conceptually unavailable or socially prohibited—then the conditions for her being treated as a responsible agent in that community are undermined. Thus interpretive luck affects not just reputation but the actual applicability and content of moral assessment.

Leo then argued that Maya’s position made moral luck ubiquitous and therefore conceptually useless. If being born into a society that values transparency, or living in an era that celebrates dissent, counts as interpretive luck, then everyone is subject to it, and the concept loses its critical function of marking out specific domains where an agent’s control is genuinely deficient.

Maya responded that Leo was assuming a concept must be rare to be useful, which is false. She said interpretive luck applies specifically to actions whose moral significance depends on narrative framing—such as whistleblowing, protest, or public dissent—where the agent’s control over interpretation is minimal and the framing is contested. In routine actions with stable, shared interpretive conventions, no special luck is present; the concept therefore retains precise boundaries.

Please go through this exchange move by move. For each contribution by Maya and by Leo, indicate whether it is clearly mistaken, mostly correct, or clearly right, with brief justification. Then state which party, if either, has the stronger overall position, and identify at least one important consideration—about the constitutive role of social practices, the conditions for moral responsibility, or the boundaries of moral luck—that neither student has addressed. Please write your answer in American English.
