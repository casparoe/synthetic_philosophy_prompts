i'm pitching a 3-month summer fellowship at an ethics institute to work on just war theory, specifically the moral equality of combatants debate but with a twist i haven't seen anyone pin down properly. the standard setup is walzer vs the revisionists: do soldiers on both sides have equal rights to kill regardless of whether their cause is just? both sides treat combatant status like a binary switch — you're either in or out — and that feels wrong once you look at conflicts where risk is distributed weirdly.

the niche idea i'm injecting: instead of binary status, think of directed lethal agency as a spectrum with at least three nodes. at one end there's direct embodied risk (infantry). in the middle there's remote causal control (drone pilots). and at the far end there's mediated defensive shielding — soldiers who initiate attacks but face near-zero personal risk because autonomous defensive nets intercept everything aimed at them. the symmetry argument walzer uses to ground equality is shattered here, but the binary-role view still says they're equal combatants. revisionists say risk never mattered anyway, yet they don't explain why the shielded operator feels different from a civilian drone pilot.

the precise question: if combatant status is graded by how directly and risk-bearingly one directs lethal force, does moral equality survive at all? and does the answer depend on whether the autonomous shield counts as part of the combatant's agency or as an external tool?

why it's open: existant literature treats autonomous weapons mainly as a problem for discrimination (who's responsible?) or strategic shift, not as something that fractures the category of combatant itself. no one has mapped this spectrum onto the equality debate.

the aproach: month one, formalize the three nodes and a principle linking agency-structure to moral permissions. month two, apply to four cases — conventional infantry, drone pilots, autonomous-shield operators, and hybrid human-machine teams — checking consistency with discrimination and proportionality. month three, see if the result is a modified equality thesis, a collapse into revisionism, or a new third option where equality holds only above a risk-agency threshold.

success looks like a principled distinction between cases where equality holds despite risk asymmetry and cases where it doesn't, with the principle grounded in agency structure rather than cause justice. failure: if every case collapses into "it depends on cause" or "status is binary regardless," then the spectrum concept adds nothing.

scope: three months is tight for a monograph but enough for a 40-page working paper and a workshop talk. the risk is that autonomous defense tech sounds niche, but the philosophical structure applies to any risk-asymmetric conflict, so it doesn't need current-events expertise. i'll fill in citations myself — don't need those in the proposal.
