Here is my opening move: I am a doctoral student in philosophy working on self-deception and well-being. I want to grant your distinction between bodily pleasure and intellectual happiness, but I want to press you on the illusion component. You advise cultivating false beliefs about the stability of human attachments so that study is not disrupted by emotional turmoil. Yet you also argue that women must free themselves from the prejudice that they are governed by unreason. This seems tense: if reason demands we eliminate false beliefs, and if women in particular are accused of being unable to face reality, then advising us to maintain illusions about love risks reinforcing the very prejudice that we are too emotional for serious inquiry. Furthermore, if my study depends on an illusion about my partner's constancy, and that illusion is shattered, my happiness collapses—so the happiness of the soul seems built on a fragility that reason should want to cure, not protect. Must this illusion be unconscious, or can a rational person knowingly maintain it? And how do you prevent your doctrine from becoming an excuse to keep women in a state of pleasant falsehood rather than in the light of reason? Respond to this directly, and defend your view.
