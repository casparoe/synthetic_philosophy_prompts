The long-running debate about Kant’s Formula of Universal Law (FUL) has stalled over whether the universalizability test is a genuine moral criterion or merely a logical consistency check that either permits too much—false positives for self-interested maxims that can be universalized only because they are probabilistically or collectively self-defeating—or prohibits too little—false negatives for maxims that seem permissible despite producing practical contradictions only under a thick reading of rational agency. Formalist interpreters maintain that the test concerns the logical form of a maxim conceived as a universal law of nature, and that once the maxim is specified with full precision about conditions, agents, and outcomes, counterexamples dissolve; they treat apparent failures as misspecifications rather than flaws in the test itself. Agency-centered and constructivist interpreters, by contrast, argue that without a substantive account of what a rational will can consistently endorse, the logical reading is either empty or covertly imports normative content through the notion of a practical contradiction. For decades both sides have traded refined cases—the egoist who universalizes exploitation only under conditions of contingent scarcity, the minor promise-breaker whose maxim yields no contradiction in conception, the collective-action maxim that is individually sustainable but universally unstable—without movement. Is this disagreement substantive, about the ontology of maxims and the nature of practical reason? Is it verbal, turning on competing definitions of universalization or contradiction? Or are the two sides pursuing different projects—one logical-formal, one normative-justificatory—under the single heading of universalizability? Diagnose the structure of the impasse by attending to what each side actually says about maxim specification and rational volition, and tell me what, if anything, would count as progress: a formal logic of maxims, a distinction between logical and practical contradiction with independent criteria, an empirical study of how agents actually universalize, or an argument that FUL was never intended to operate as a standalone decision procedure and the debate is therefore ill-posed.
