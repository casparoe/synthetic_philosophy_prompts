After a long argument with a friend about whether supertasks are genuinely paradoxical or merely misdescribed, I have agreed to design a single ninety-minute session for our reading group — mostly physics and mathematics graduate students with almost no formal philosophy background — focused specifically on Thomson's lamp and the logic of infinite task completion. I want the pedagogical architecture, not the lecture itself. First, the dependency order: which conceptual pieces must be in place before the lamp is introduced, so that the paradox feels structurally inevitable rather than contrived? Second, the carrying example: identify the one concrete scenario that should anchor the entire session, explaining what it must contain and what it must omit to work for this audience, and specifically address how I should integrate the comparative case I sketched in the argument — a reverse lamp that begins in an unspecified state and is subjected to the same toggle sequence in backward temporal order — without letting that comparison collapse into confusion about causation. Third, the misconception map: this audience will likely conflate mathematical convergence with physical completion, treat the absence of a final toggle as automatically dissolving the state question, and assume that infinite divisibility of time entails infinite divisibility of state change. For each, specify the precise moment in the sequence where it should be intercepted and defused. Fourth, the check for understanding: propose one specific question or miniature thought experiment that would reveal whether they have grasped the conceptual dependency rather than memorized the paradox formula. Finally, what should I openly refuse to explain or leave out at this level — for instance, should I avoid analogizing to Zeno's dichotomy, should I decline to adjudicate whether infinite physical accelerations are possible, and should I avoid invoking formal models of temporal logic? I do not want a bibliography, citation list, or suggested readings; I need only the teaching plan.
