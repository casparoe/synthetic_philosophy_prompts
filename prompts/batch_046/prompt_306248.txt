I am drafting a historical novel set in the early fourteenth century, and one scene requires a Dominican theologian to argue with a secular jurist about whether it is ever lawful to kill a tyrant. To keep the dialogue from collapsing into modern platitudes, I need to understand how Aquinas actually distributes the moral authority to resist. At a dinner with a medieval historian and a political philosopher, I heard them debate this very question without reaching agreement, and I need an adjudication to shape the scene with philosophical integrity.

The historian argued that Aquinas permits the removal of a tyrant only through a genuinely public authority—either the corporate body that originally instituted the ruler or a superior power—and that private assassination is always forbidden. She emphasized that Aquinas distinguishes a usurper, who never holds legitimate title, from a ruler who abuses a legitimate office, but in neither case does an individual citizen acquire a private right to kill; for Aquinas, she said, the disorder and sedition that follow secret murder are almost always a greater evil than the tyranny itself, so the prohibition is robust and not merely tactical. The jurist replied that in the case of a pure usurper there is no superior authority to invoke, which means the people themselves, acting as the retained sovereign body, may depose and even kill him, and that this is collective rather than private action. He added that Aquinas's framework must accommodate extreme cases—when every court, council, and ecclesiastical avenue has been deliberately destroyed—because then collective action is practically impossible, and a ruler who governs solely by brute force can be resisted by individuals under the natural law of self-defense. The historian objected that this reads modern popular-sovereignty theory back into Aquinas; for Aquinas, authority derives from God and natural law, not from a social contract that the people can reclaim, so the notion of "retained sovereignty" is anachronistic. She also insisted that Aquinas's comparison of a tyrant to one who rules by violence rather than reason describes the tyrant's moral degradation, not a license for private citizens to become executioners. The jurist conceded the anachronism in part but maintained that even within Aquinas's own terms, the common good requires that resistance remain possible; to deny any individual recourse when all institutions are annihilated is to make Aquinas's natural law implausibly rigid.

Please adjudicate their exchange point by point. Who is right about Aquinas's distinction between usurpation and the abuse of legitimate authority? Is private killing ever permitted, or only collective removal by a public body? Does the total collapse of institutional mechanisms alter the moral calculus, or does Aquinas's fear of sedition remain absolute regardless? Are modern sovereignty concepts genuinely foreign to Aquinas's framework? And what, if anything, are both disputants missing—perhaps the role of prudential judgment, the difference between active resistance and passive endurance, or specific conditions Aquinas sets out in his treatise On Kingship and in the Summa Theologiae? State who is more right overall. End your answer with a line beginning exactly Verdict: that summarizes your judgment in a single sentence.
