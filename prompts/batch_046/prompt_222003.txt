I'm trying to determine whether vagueness is always a feature of our representations or whether some boundaries are genuinely sharp in reality, and I want to see whether my intuitions about the sorites paradox and its analogues hang together under one principle. Here are my verdicts on concrete cases.

On physical aggregation—the classic sorites setup—a collection of 10,000 grains of sand is a heap; one grain is not. Removing grains one by one, I believe there is no specific count, say 3,142 versus 3,141, at which heap-status is lost. The same for baldness: 20,000 hairs is not bald, none is bald, and I reject the idea that hair number 8,437 is the hidden cutoff.

On institutional classification, my intuition reverses. I think there is a sharp boundary for democracy. Once a state's chief executive is appointed by hereditary succession rather than selected through competitive election, that state is definitively not a democracy, regardless of how intact its courts or legislatures remain. There is no ontologically vague intermediate zone.

On biological termination, I think death is similarly sharp. There is a precise moment when integrated brain function permanently ceases, even if our instruments sometimes fail to detect it exactly.

But on social and evaluative properties, I revert to thinking vagueness is real in the world, not merely in language. A net worth of $50 million makes one wealthy; $500 does not. Yet I do not think there is a hidden precise figure—say, $1,847,293.50—that separates the wealthy from the non-wealthy. Similarly, a 400-page monograph on modal logic counts as a work of philosophy; a grocery list does not; and I do not think there is a hidden page count or conceptual-density score that resolves every borderline case.

These judgments are in tension. If sharpness tracks natural kindhood, democracy (seemingly institutional) should differ from death (biological), yet I treat them alike. If sharpness tracks practical importance, wealth should be sharp, yet I treat it as vague. Is there a coherent account—epistemicist, supervaluationist, ontic vagueness, contextualist, or otherwise—that delivers all six verdicts? State the principle precisely, show which clause is motivated by which case, and tell me what it predicts for an unconsidered case: a municipal ordinance defining a large public gathering as 500 or more persons, applied to 499 people dispersed across a plaza versus 500 packed into a single subway car.

Finally, be honest about which verdict your best principle must overturn: my intuition that democracy and death have sharp boundaries, my intuition that sand and hair do not, or my intuition that there is a principled distinction between the institutional/biological group and the social/evaluative group.
