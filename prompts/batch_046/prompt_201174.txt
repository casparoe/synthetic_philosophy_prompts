Design a 60-minute workshop activity for about 14 philosophy graduate students that makes them feel the reliability problem in introspection—specifically, the gap between having access to the content of one’s judgment and having access to its causal etiology—without lecturing them on it first. To avoid a standard replay of well-known confabulation studies, inject a masked normative-framing mechanism: the same formal argument is embedded in two subtly different affective contexts, and participants must publicly justify their evaluations under a reward scheme (social credit or symbolic stakes only) that rewards coherence rather than accuracy. Specify the rules, roles, materials, and payoff structure; describe a debrief that maps specific moments in the room onto the philosophical distinction between judgment-access and cause-access; predict what will go wrong on the first run and how to handle it; and say frankly which part of the problem—for example, the transparency thesis about mental states or first-person authority claims—the exercise can reproduce on its own and which part the facilitator must supply. The group should leave having experienced why introspective reports can be both sincerely held and systematically wrong about origins.
