A fellow student summarised lexical-threshold negative utilitarianism for me in these terms: "The position asserts that pleasure and pain are metaphysically incommensurable—there is no common metric, cardinal or ordinal, that can map suffering onto well-being. Because suffering possesses lexical priority, any quantity of pleasure, no matter how enormous, is ethically outweighed by even the slightest pain. From this it follows immediately that sentient existence is always a net harm: since no life is entirely free of suffering, and since pleasure can never compensate for it, the optimal state of affairs is one in which no conscious beings exist at all. The claim about incommensurability is therefore primarily an empirical thesis about human phenomenology, not a normative one, and the anti-natalist conclusion is derived purely by logical deduction from that empirical premise."

This summary strikes me as partly convincing but possibly too quick. I would like you to examine it carefully. Which sentences fairly represent the commitments of lexical-threshold negative utilitarianism, and which subtly misrepresent the view? In particular, please distinguish: (i) the thesis that suffering and pleasure are not intersubstitutable on a single scale (lexical ordering); (ii) the stronger claim that they are metaphysically incommensurable; and (iii) the empirical claim about phenomenological comparison. Does the anti-natalist conclusion genuinely follow without smuggling in further premises about risk, the impossibility of suffering-free lives, or the moral status of potential beings? Reach a clear verdict on whether the summary is a fair exposition or a distortion, and justify your assessment.
