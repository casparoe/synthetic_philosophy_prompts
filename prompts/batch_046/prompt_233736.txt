Thomas Malthus died in 1834, before the demographic transition, synthetic nitrogen fixation, or modern integrated climate-resource modeling existed. Present to him, as a counterfactual interlocutor, the contemporary philosophical debate over whether predictive theories can be "right but early"—falsified in the medium term by temporary suppressing variables that eventually exhaust themselves, leaving the underlying mechanism intact for a much longer horizon. Focus on two posthumous developments. First, the demographic transition, in which rising per-capita income systematically suppresses fertility, apparently contradicting his claim that population always presses against subsistence regardless of temporary wealth. Second, current climate-resource models that embed carrying-capacity dynamics inside endogenous technological feedback loops, projecting renewed resource stress after the mid-22nd century once one-time fossil-energy and agricultural-innovation bonuses dissipate. Ask Malthus to adjudicate—using only the conceptual apparatus of his Essay on the Principle of Population and its major revisions—whether these developments (a) falsify his mechanism outright, (b) restrict its domain without falsifying it, (c) suspend judgment until the long run is actually observed, or (d) rescue it through auxiliary hypotheses about temporary suppressors. Require him to draw a sharp distinction between "the conditions for the mechanism never obtained" and "the mechanism is dormant but will eventually activate," and to state whether injecting endogenous technology into the model preserves his core insight about geometric population growth versus arithmetic subsistence growth, or transforms it into an unrecognizable theory. Have him specify which distinction is decisive, and whether a prediction that remains empirically false for two centuries can still claim support from a projected 22nd-century confirmation. Keep his reasoning in his own vocabulary—positive checks, preventive checks, geometric versus arithmetic growth—without asking for modern citations, bibliographies, or reading lists.
