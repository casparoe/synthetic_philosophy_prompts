I'm delivering the opening remarks at my school's annual interdisciplinary symposium on consciousness and physical science. The audience includes physics teachers, philosophy students, parents, and one visiting researcher; I need the address to work for all of them without either dumbing down the concept or leaving the non-specialists behind. I want to structure the speech around a specific, unconventional framing: if panpsychism holds and phenomenal experience is distributed through fundamental physical fields rather than localized in brains, then ethical obligation might track not cognitive sophistication but the coherence and density of integrated phenomenal structure within a system—so that certain highly ordered non-biological processes could, in principle, warrant moral consideration, while simple organisms lacking such structure might not. This claim must do real structural work: it should block the usual human-centered consolation about unique consciousness, require me to respect empirical boundaries without retreating into mysticism, and prevent me from treating the idea as mere decorative metaphor. Please write a formal, approximately eight-minute address that navigates these constraints for a mixed audience, without appeals to authority through citation, bibliography, or named theorists.
