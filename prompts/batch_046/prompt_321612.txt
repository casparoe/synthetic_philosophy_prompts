I'm running a philosophy club and we're doing Shirley Jackson's The Lottery next month. I want to read it through the lens of information cascades and rational herding, but not the generic 'people copy each other' version. Specifically, I'm wondering if the villagers are in an epistemic cascade—where they actually come to believe the lottery is necessary because everyone participates—or a preference cascade, where they all privately hate it but stay silent because dissent is too costly. Tessie Hutchinson's late protest is the obvious moment: does her timing show dissent is only rational when you're the victim, or does it reveal how ritual suppresses private signals? There's this niche concept I ran into, 'informational externalities in collective rituals,' where the ritual itself becomes evidence that suppresses counter-evidence once the cascade is running. Does Jackson's story support that, or do details like Old Man Warner's defensiveness show people do have private doubts that just never get voiced? I'm teaching this to undergrads and definately don't want to reduce the story to a sociology diagram—so tell me where the literary texture resists that reading, and what the story adds about human conformity that a formal cascade model misses.
