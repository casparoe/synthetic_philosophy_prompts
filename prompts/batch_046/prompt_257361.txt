I am not an academic, but I have been thinking on my own about advance directives — the documents in which people record what medical treatment they wish to receive should they later lose the capacity to decide. The standard justification for honouring these documents is that the earlier self retains authority over the later self because both are one continuous person. I want to press a stranger framing, which I call the 'conditional-bequest' view: rather than treating the directive as a lasting command issued by an enduring self, we might treat it as a bequest made on the condition that the recipient — the future patient — remains within the same value framework that the bequest was designed to protect. If a state such as severe dementia or prolonged unconsciousness does not merely alter preferences but dissolves that framework entirely, then the authority of the earlier self might lapse not because the person has died, but because the conditions of the gift have failed.

Please describe the most important implications of taking this conditional-bequest view seriously. In particular, consider what it implies for clinicians and families when the present patient seems content despite having no recollection of the values that originally shaped the directive; whether relatives should be regarded as executors of the bequest or as advocates for the patient who now exists; and what this does to the broader idea that we hold a single, unbroken authority over our own future bodies.
