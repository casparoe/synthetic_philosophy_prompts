I am preparing interview questions for a philosophy podcast episode featuring an unnamed defender of a stringent, institutionally extended form of tranquilism. On this view—call it negative-state tranquilism—the absence of craving and suffering is not merely sufficient but exclusively constitutive of well-being; positive mental states such as joy, enthusiasm, aesthetic rapture, and loving attachment are treated not as neutral omissions but as active disturbances of equanimity that diminish value. The defender extends this from personal ethics to collective design, arguing that democratic deliberation should be structured to suppress partisan excitement and rhetorical enthusiasm, which she treats as cognitive pollutants structurally analogous to craving. Draft eight to ten interview questions that avoid asking her to restate this package and instead press on its internal architecture. Include at least one question on how she distinguishes benign positive affect from craving-driven excitement without circularly appealing to equanimity; one on whether her institutional proposal requires an implausibly robust epistemic authority to classify emotional states as pollutants; one on what her view says about creative or scientific achievement, which often appears to depend on the enthusiastic attachment she rejects; and one on how she responds to the objection that if absence alone is valuable, permanent sedation or death are not merely permissible but optimal. For each of these four, provide a follow-up keyed to her most likely reply: if she appeals to functional stability of the mind, ask where stability ends and stagnation begins; if she invokes a distinction between natural and cultivated affect, ask whether that distinction smuggles in a positive account of human flourishing; if she treats enthusiasm as instrumentally valuable only for survival, ask why that instrumental worth does not accumulate into intrinsic value over a complete life; and if she distinguishes absence of suffering from absence of consciousness to block the death objection, ask whether that distinction introduces a positive value she explicitly denies. The remaining questions should probe related tensions—such as how her view handles grief as the loss of a loved one, or whether a society engineered for equanimity can still generate the discontent that drives moral progress—each paired with a brief follow-up anticipating the standard defense.
