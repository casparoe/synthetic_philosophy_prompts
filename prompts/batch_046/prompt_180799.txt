I am preparing a feature for a magazine of science and ideas on democratic reform, and during my research I witnessed a rigorous exchange between Dr. Amara Okonkwo, a political theorist who advocates sortition-based assemblies, and Dr. Julian Reeves, a constitutional historian sceptical of anti-careerist reforms. They debated whether strict term limits combined with random selection can genuinely eliminate career politics without sacrificing institutional competence. Their disagreement unfolded over six moves.

Okonkwo opened by arguing that the core pathology of modern legislatures is professionalisation rather than corruption: representatives become careerists who optimise for re-election and donor access rather than deliberation. She proposed replacing elected chambers with citizens selected by lot for a single, non-renewable term of three years, arguing that this restores descriptive representation and severs the feedback loop between governance and fundraising. She acknowledged that institutional memory would diminish, but insisted that permanent civil servants—not elected politicians—already provide continuity, so the loss is overstated.

Reeves replied that this conflates administrative continuity with legislative judgement. In jurisdictions that have experimented with strict term limits, turnover does not weaken bureaucracy; it strengthens it, because inexperienced legislators delegate drafting and oversight to permanent staff and lobbyists who face no electoral check. He added that career politicians, for all their faults, accumulate tacit knowledge about coalition-building and procedural obstruction that cannot be captured in briefing documents.

Okonkwo then introduced a mechanism she calls rotating civic juries with embedded technical scribes. Under her model, citizens serve in staggered panels, but the technical staff supporting them are also drawn by lot from a qualified pool and serve only for that panel's term, preventing the formation of a permanent bureaucratic class. She argued that complex policy domains do not require years of personal expertise but rather structured access to disinterested expert testimony, which rotating panels can summon ad hoc without relying on career legislators as intermediaries.

Reeves countered with what he termed an apprenticeship legislature. Rather than eliminating careerists entirely, he proposed that every lot-selected citizen be paired with a retiring legislator who serves for one term as a non-voting mentor, transferring procedural and substantive knowledge without retaining formal power. He claimed this captures the epistemic benefits of experience while preventing the mentor from accumulating a personal constituency. He further argued that Okonkwo's rotating scribes are unrealistic for domains such as financial regulation or climate governance, where expertise requires sustained engagement with evolving datasets, not merely ad hoc testimony.

Okonkwo rejected the apprenticeship model as smuggling careerism back through informal influence. She contended that mentors would inevitably become shadow legislators, with their advice carrying disproportionate weight precisely because novices lack the confidence to dissent. She also maintained that Reeves overstates the time required for policy literacy: a well-designed civic jury can master a specific portfolio within months, provided the information architecture is redesigned around transparency rather than institutional memory.

Reeves made a final reply. He observed that Okonkwo's transparency architecture is itself a form of institutional design that requires long-term maintenance and revision, which rotating amateurs are ill-equipped to oversee. More importantly, he argued that she ignores party structures. In parliamentary systems, parties provide the continuity that individual legislators lack; if legislators rotate but parties remain, party elites become the true careerists, centralising power in party headquarters and making careerism more opaque, not less.

Please adjudicate their exchange move by move: rate each of the six moves on a scale from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with brief justification for each rating. Then state who is more right overall, and identify what—if anything—both parties are missing, particularly regarding the interaction between sortition, term limits, and party discipline in systems where collective responsibility rather than individual representation structures legislative behaviour.
