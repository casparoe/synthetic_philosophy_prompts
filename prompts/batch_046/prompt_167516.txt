A study partner walked me through the following argument about Bernard Williams's integrity objection, and I cannot locate the slip. (1) The one-thought-too-many case shows that when a husband saves his wife only because he calculated that doing so maximizes overall welfare, he has adopted a reasoning stance toward her that is incompatible with love; the fault lies not in the outcome but in the comparative, fungible structure of the reasoning itself. (2) Williams extends this into the integrity objection: if morality demands that I abandon a ground project — a lifelong craft, relationship, or commitment that gives my life its shape — then it demands that I treat what makes my life mine as exchangeable for other goods. (3) So to preserve integrity, ground projects must be shielded from any moral demand that would require their abandonment; they carry, in effect, lexical priority over conflicting obligations. (4) Yet Williams denies that integrity is absolute; he allows that one might justifiably sacrifice a project if the moral stakes are catastrophic, which implies integrity is not lexical but threshold-weighted. (5) Therefore integrity blocks ordinary moral demands but yields to extreme ones. (6) But locating the threshold requires comparing the importance of my project against the importance of the moral demand — precisely the detached, comparative weighing that the one-thought-too-many objection was meant to condemn. (7) So either integrity is lexical and morality can never legitimately override it (making it impossible, for example, that I should save strangers at catastrophic cost to my project), or it is threshold-weighted and Williams's own framework licenses the calculative reasoning he attacks. (8) The first horn makes integrity a kind of moral solipsism; the second collapses the objection into self-refutation. (9) I am committed to keeping both (1) and (5). Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? In particular, is there a coherent distinction between the weighing required to set a threshold for integrity and the weighing that the one-thought-too-many objection targets, or does that distinction dissolve under pressure?
