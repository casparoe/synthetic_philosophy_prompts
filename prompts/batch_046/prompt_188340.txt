What would a theory that preserves both the causal inertness of phenomenal consciousness and the reliability of first-person phenomenal judgments actually have to get right? Give me the full specification of adequacy conditions: the specific phenomena it must account for—such as the asymmetry between self-attribution and other-attribution of qualitative states, the apparent immediacy of introspective reports, the persistence of phenomenal judgment in blindsight-like dissociations, and the intuition that one can be wrong about one’s own experience without being globally unreliable—the distinctions it must preserve rather than collapse (for instance, between epistemic access and causal efficacy, between the content of a judgment and the mechanism that produces it, between localized introspective error and wholesale skepticism about phenomenal knowledge, and between having a phenomenal property and judging that one has it), and the logical or empirical constraints it cannot violate without forfeiting the very phenomenon it aims to save. For each desideratum, state the motivating case or argument that makes it genuinely non-negotiable rather than a partisan preference, and say whether any subset of these requirements is jointly unsatisfiable. Then identify the single requirement that eliminates the largest share of current responses to the paradox—whether causal-acquaintance theories, higher-order tracking accounts, or deflationary eliminativist moves—and briefly note where the main existing camps stand against the list. Keep the weight on the criteria and on whether the list itself reveals an internal tension, rather than on constructing a novel theory.
