In a recent philosophy of science seminar, my instructor repeatedly invoked the familiar slogan that you can't get an ought from an is to dismiss two very different arguments: first, attempts to ground norms of scientific rationality in purely descriptive accounts of how successful inquiry actually operates, such as evolutionary epistemology or the sociology of peer review; second, attempts to derive ethical obligations regarding ecological preservation from purely descriptive claims about biological interdependence and ecosystem stability. She seemed to treat the slogan as a single knock-down principle that settles both cases. I want to bring a disambiguation to the next session. Lay out the distinct precise theses that this slogan could express—distinguishing, at minimum, logical non-derivability (no valid inference without normative premises), semantic irreducibility (no normative vocabulary in purely descriptive premises), motivational independence (knowledge of facts alone cannot produce normative commitment), and ontological non-identity (normative properties are not reducible to natural properties). For each reading, indicate whether it is trivial, substantive and defensible, false, or overreaching. Then specify which of the two seminar arguments—normative methodology derived from descriptive success, or ethical obligations derived from ecological interdependence—each reading actually blocks, and which ones it fails to block. Present the result as a menu of interpretations with verdicts rather than defending a single reading.
