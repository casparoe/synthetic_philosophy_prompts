I constructed the following argument for a metaphysics seminar and now find it suspicious because it seems to prove far more than I intended. (1) If the principle of the identity of indiscernibles holds without restriction, then Black's familiar two-sphere universe—two spheres that share every intrinsic property and differ only in spatial position—is metaphysically impossible. (2) Because that scenario is at least conceivable, the principle must be restricted: numerical distinctness can be sustained by purely relational or spatiotemporal differences, even when all intrinsic properties are shared. (3) But if relational differences alone suffice for distinctness, then for any actual world there exists a possible duplicate that shares every intrinsic feature and differs only in the arrangement or location of its parts; hence no argument from the qualitative uniqueness of the actual world to its singularity can succeed. (4) Apply the same reasoning diachronically. At time t an object O possesses intrinsic properties P; at t' a qualitatively identical object O' possesses P and stands in temporal relations to O. If those temporal relations are external to the intrinsic nature of O and O'—just as spatial separation is external to the spheres—then O and O' are related only as the spheres are. (5) Consequently, without an unrestricted principle of identity, nothing binds O and O' into one persisting entity; every qualitative change produces a numerically distinct object, and diachronic persistence collapses entirely. (6) Yet persistence is a datum we should not abandon. (7) Therefore we must reject (2), restore the unrestricted principle, and treat Black's universe as genuinely impossible rather than a counterexample. Where does this argument go wrong, if it does? I would prefer to keep both (2) and (6), but I want to know precisely what I am paying if I do.
