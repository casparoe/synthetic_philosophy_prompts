I'm a novelist working on a book about a consciousness researcher, and I've been messing with this idea — heterophenomenology, where you treat first-person reports as texts describing a subject's heterophenomenological world rather than direct windows onto qualia. Here are my gut verdicts on cases, and I want to know if there's one principle behind them or if I'm just being incoherent.

One: my protaganist interviews a patient who describes a vivid orange glow after staring at the sun. The researcher logs it as data about his heterophenomenological world but doesn't think there's literally an orange glow in his brain. Feels right.

Two: same patient, under hypnosis, describes being abducted by aliens. The researcher still logs it — it's part of his world — but doesn't think it happened physically. That feels right too, but now I'm stuck: what's the real difference between the orange glow and the alien story? Both are false about the outside world.

Three: I wrote a scene where my blind character describes "seeing" colors through sound. I initially treated her first-person account as valid data, same as a real subject's report. Then I caught myself — she's fictional. So I think her report should be excluded. But I'm not sure why, since for real subjects all we ever have is the text of their report.

Four: two study subjects report opposite things about the same painful stimulus — one excruciating, one barely noticeable. I think both are equally valid data about two different heterophenomenological worlds. No contradiction.

Five: my researcher feeds prompts to an AI and gets back indistinguishable first-person descriptions of "experiencing" a sunset. I feel these should be excluded from heterophenomenological study, even if they read exactly like human reports.

So is there a coherent principle that gets all five? Or do I need to drop one? If I have to drop one, which verdict is the least defensible? And if my intuition that fiction and AI should be treated differently is just wrong — like maybe I'm smuggling in authorial intent where it doesn't belong — please say so plainly. Don't just politely agree with me; if the task as posed rests on a confused premise about what heterophenomenology can do, push back.
