I'm trying to design a weird applied exercise for my grad seminar on Pascal — we keep circling back to whether his justice/force distinction is just a cynical observation or if it can actually guide practice. So imagine you're a mediator on a municipal heritage board: you meet twice a month, you've got about forty-five minutes per dispute, and your recommendation goes to a city council that rubber-stamps you unless the politics are toxic. You have no police power; you can only advise whether custom should stand or whether legal title should be enforced. The board gets constant clashes between long-standing customary use and formal ownership — old footpaths, community gardens on technically municipal land, that sort of thing.

Can you turn Pascal's framework into a reusable decision procedre for this mediator? By that I mean a flowchart in words with ordered questions, clear "stop with no" points, and explicit flags for where the call is a value judgment rather than a fact. Ground it in his view that justice without force is powerless, force without justice is tyrannical, and that real justice is embedded in custum and convention rather than discovered in nature. Crucially, build in his distinction between the poltical order (where enforcement and convention apply) and the order of charity or mind (where persuasion and recognition apply) — flag when a dispute is being wrongly treated as spiritual justice. Then run the procedre on two cases: (1) neighbors blocking a footpath used for thirty years but technically on private property, and (2) a community garden maintained for two decades on land the city now wants to sell to a developer. Include one case where the answer is probably not what the mediator wants. Be honest about where it collapses and just smuggles in preference.

Also — and I genuinely don't want a bibliography or citations, I know you can't browse — please say what argument or consideration would change your mind about whether Pascal's framework can be proceduralized without betraying it, and flag where your reasoning is most likely to fail, especially on how those three orders actually interact when you're under time pressure.
