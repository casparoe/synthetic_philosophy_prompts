In debates over whether moral concern should extend to insects, the scale argument—that there are perhaps 10^19 individual insects alive at any given moment, so that even a very low prior probability of sentience yields staggering expected suffering—has been countered for decades by a response that treats moral patienthood as binary rather than probabilistic: without neural architectures capable of unified subjective experience, attributing sentience to an organism is a category error, regardless of how many such organisms exist. Despite broad agreement on the entomological data and on the normative premise that suffering is bad, the dispute has not moved. My working hypothesis is that the stalemate is not primarily empirical—no one expects a single experiment to resolve it—nor is it a conventional normative disagreement. Rather, it concerns whether moral risk should be governed by expected-value reasoning across populations or by evidential thresholds for kind-membership, with one side treating false positives about inner life as costly moral overreach and the other treating false negatives as catastrophic omissions at scale. Is this diagnosis correct, or is there a hidden empirical disagreement doing the work—perhaps an unstated conflict over whether global workspace dynamics are necessary, or merely sufficient, for consciousness? Give me a structural diagnosis supported by what proponents of the scale argument and its critics actually say about population size, neural complexity, and the symmetry of moral error. Then, given that diagnosis, specify what would actually constitute progress: a formal model of moral risk under deep uncertainty, an empirical criterion for unified experience in invertebrates, a conceptual distinction that dissolves the framing, or an argument that the frameworks are incommensurable and no resolution should be expected. I want the diagnosis and the prognosis, not a survey of positions.
