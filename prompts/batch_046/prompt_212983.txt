I am a doctoral student in philosophy of cognitive science working on conceptual categorization and artificial cognition. A colleague in my department recently summarized Douglas Hofstadter's Letter Spirit project in the following terms: the program operates by isolating a hidden geometric invariant—essentially a Platonic letter-form—that persists unchanged beneath all stylistic variation, and its network of "codelets" functions like feature detectors that converge on this invariant morphological core. From this she argued that every instance of an "a" belongs to the category "a" precisely because it instantiates this core, while stylistic differences are merely noise around the essential form; consequently, she concluded that Letter Spirit solves the problem of conceptual fluidity not by accommodating it, but by demonstrating that fluid appearance masks an underlying rigid essence fully accessible to computation.

This sounds suspicious to me—my impression was that the project was designed to model emergent, analogy-driven categorization without postulating any invariant core—but I am not expert enough in the system architecture to identify exactly where the distortion lies. Is this summary fair? Please distinguish what it gets right about the project's actual mechanics (for example, the role of analogical reasoning, gridfont generation, and distributed codelet activity), what counts as a defensible popular simplification, and what actively misrepresents Hofstadter's explicit anti-essentialist commitments. Most importantly, does the inference—that Letter Spirit vindicates a hidden-essence theory of letter categories—actually follow from anything the project demonstrates?
