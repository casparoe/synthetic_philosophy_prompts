The audience is deliberately divided: roughly half are classicists and democratic theorists who will recognise Thucydides' account of the Mytilene debate, the decision to invade Sicily, and the trial of Socrates without prompting; the other half are constitutional lawyers, civil servants, and institutional designers with little background in ancient history. In this framing, the Mytilene reversal is not an act of mercy but a failure of binding; the Sicilian Expedition shows the same logic projected across time rather than space; and Socrates' trial reveals presentist deliberation turned inward, treating philosophical dissent as an immediate threat to be eliminated rather than a future resource to be preserved.

Please include one genuinely unconventional observation—something that would surprise the specialists without being arbitrary—perhaps connecting the procedural haste of Socrates' trial to a feature of direct democratic accountability that is rarely discussed in classical scholarship. The tone should be formal but spoken, suitable for being read aloud, and it must refuse false consolation: no nostalgic claim that Athens was uniquely vicious, but also no reassurance that modern institutions have solved the problem. Write in British English, avoid any bibliographic references or requests for citations, and do not use verbatim quotations from ancient texts—paraphrase any specific claims from Thucydides or Plato instead. Structure the response in four named sections, in this order: first, 'Framing and purpose of the workshop' (approximately 150 words); second, 'The three failures as one pathology' (approximately 450 words), moving through Mytilene, Sicily, and Socrates with explicit reference to how presentism operates in each case; third, 'The uncomfortable parallel for modern bodies' (approximately 200 words), drawing out implications for contemporary deliberative institutions without anachronism; and fourth, 'Closing without consolation' (approximately 150 words).
