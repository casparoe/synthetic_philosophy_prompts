Construct a thought experiment for the material-constitution debate—whether a statue is identical to the clay that makes it up. Engineer this case: a clay statue is replaced molecule-by-molecule with a synthetic substitute physically and chemically indistinguishable from clay; the replaced molecules are not destroyed but immediately reassembled elsewhere into a second statue that tracks the first shape exactly. By the end, two statues stand, each composed of exactly half the original clay and half the substitute, with the original clay split evenly between them in an interleaved lattice rather than in spatial blocks.

The scenario must meet these constraints:
- No exotic physics, teleportation, or perfect duplication—only gradual replacement and reassembly possible in principle.
- The identity theorist (statue = aggregate of matter) must be forced to identify each statue with an aggregate that overlaps the other in membership, since both contain half the original clay.
- The constitution theorist (statue is constituted by but not identical to its matter) must distinguish the two statues by their constituting aggregates, yet must also explain why the original statue is one of them rather than the other without appealing solely to spatiotemporal continuity of the matter, because both share that same original portion.

Present the scenario in one paragraph. Then demonstrate it meets each specification: state the verdict each theory gives, identify the structural feature (interleaved shared matter with simultaneous replication) that forces the divergence, and note what happens if that feature is removed (e.g., the replaced clay is destroyed rather than reassembled). Finish by articulating how a defender of the identity view could attack the case—for instance, by denying that overlapping aggregates can each be identical to a distinct statue—and assess whether that defense saves the view or collapses it into the constitution view.
