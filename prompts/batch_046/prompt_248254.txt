I founded a design collective with three partners shortly after finishing graduate school. Over the following fourteen years, every original member left—some for competing firms, others for unrelated fields—and each departure brought in replacements who gradually altered our methods, client base, tools, and physical workspace. Today I am the sole remaining link to the founding group; the legal entity retains the original registration, but every employee, every piece of equipment, every project archive, and even the office address has been replaced at least once. One former partner, who departed eight years ago, now argues that recent revenue from techniques he originally developed belongs partly to him, on the grounds that our present collective is still the same organization he co-founded, merely evolved. The current members reject this, insisting that with total turnover of people and practices, only a hollow legal shell remains, and that moral obligations to departed founders dissolve once nothing substantive is left. I am caught between these views: the law treats us as continuous, yet intuitively it feels as though an original thing has disappeared and something else has taken its place. Is there a coherent philosophical account of organizational identity that can settle whether continuity survives complete internal replacement? And if so, does it support honoring old agreements, or does it license treating the present collective as genuinely new?
