Question: In Clark and Chalmers’s extended mind framework, mental processes are not necessarily confined to the brain. Consider a musician who wears a biometric feedback system that continuously translates her heart-rate variability into haptic vibrations on her wrist, allowing her to modulate emotional arousal in real time during a performance. Applying the extended mind thesis, analyze whether this loop constitutes an extended affective state. Your answer should address the parity principle, distinguish it carefully from the complementarity principle, and discuss whether emotional or affective processes fall within the thesis’s intended scope.

Student Answer: Clark and Chalmers argue that mental processes can extend into the environment when external resources play the same functional role as internal ones. This is the parity principle: if an external tool performs a function that, were it done inside the head, we would call it mental, then it counts as part of the mind. The classic comparison is between Otto, who uses a notebook to store beliefs, and Inga, who uses biological memory; since both serve the same behavior-guiding function, Otto’s notebook is part of his belief system. Applying this to the musician, the haptic loop regulates arousal continuously, is constantly available, and is automatically endorsed, just as internal interoceptive monitoring would be, so her emotional regulation extends into the wearable device. Clark and Chalmers also introduced the complementarity principle in their original 1998 paper, stating that external resources need not replicate internal mechanisms exactly but only need to complement them with different processes; in practice, parity and complementarity are essentially two formulations of the same functional-integration criterion. Although the thesis was originally presented with beliefs and memories in mind, Chalmers later clarified that it applies only to states with conceptual structure, which means raw emotional arousal—lacking propositional content—is automatically excluded from extension, though the cognitive monitoring of that arousal may still extend. Therefore, the loop functions as an extended cognitive monitor of emotion but not as an extended emotional state itself.
