I am drafting materials for a graduate seminar on philosophy of mind and AI governance, and I want to formalize a disagreement I have been exploring with a colleague. One of us defends a broadly Dennettian view: intentionality is derived rather than original, the intentional stance is a predictive heuristic with no metaphysical prerequisites, and there is no in-principle barrier to attributing full mental-state vocabulary to an artificial system that supports the appropriate functional patterns—Dennett's two-bitser scenario being the canonical illustration that derived intentionality can carry all the explanatory weight we need. The other defends a broadly Searlean view: original intentionality is anchored in biological causation and consciousness, derived attributions to artifacts or algorithms are always observer-relative metaphors, and ascribing literal beliefs or intentions to a language model is as ontologically misplaced as attributing thirst to a thermostat, regardless of predictive utility.

Please write the joint statement that these two camps should have produced for an adversarial collaboration, focused specifically on the institutional practice of using mental-state language—belief, desire, intention, understanding—in AI safety audits, regulatory filings, and system-card documentation. The document should contain: (1) a numbered list of empirically and philosophically agreed claims, clearly separating agreement about current architectures and predictive success from agreement about ontology; (2) a table of residual disagreements rendered in neutral wording that each side accepts as accurate to its own position, especially on whether such audit-language is a pragmatic predictive tool, a metaphorical shorthand, or a literally true description; (3) the cruxes—those precise sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one party—including whether functional isomorphism without biological substrate can ever justify literal mental-state ascription, whether the success of the intentional stance depends on its being literally true or only instrumentally reliable, and whether regulatory transparency requires ontological commitment; and (4) for each crux, a concrete case or evidential scenario both sides agree is relevant, such as a documented failure mode in a large language model where attributing an intention explains behavior that current mechanistic interpretability cannot yet account for, together with each camp's prior prediction about what that case demonstrates. The deliverable should be a constructive, signed-style document—not an adjudication—that separates disputes about words from substantive ones and makes explicit what the practice of mental-state attribution in governance actually presupposes about original versus derived intentionality.
