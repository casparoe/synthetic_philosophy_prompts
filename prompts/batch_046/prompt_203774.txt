I cover philosophy of mind and cognitive science for a science-and-ideas magazine, and I am commissioning a satirical companion piece to a feature I am preparing on first-person authority. Following a recent seminar discussion with colleagues—where we debated whether introspection could ever be calibrated like an external instrument—we kept returning to the absurd image of a startup treating self-knowledge as a subscription service. For the magazine's satire section, I would like an ironic product review, approximately 700 words, written in the deadpan, sincerely enthusiastic voice of a serious technology reviewer. The subject should be a fictional wearable headset called the Introspect Pro, marketed as consumer neurotechnology that eliminates introspective error by translating subjective reports into what the manufacturer calls "verified inner telemetry."

The satire should target the overconfidence in self-knowledge common to both popular psychology and certain analytic philosophy of mind—specifically the assumptions that mental states are fully self-intimating, that privileged access operates analogously to direct observation, and that first-person authority can be outsourced to an algorithm without epistemic loss. Rather than inventing straw positions, exaggerate real ones: present confabulation, blindspot bias, and the transparency illusion not as failures but as "calibration updates"; include a sidebar-style comparison table pairing classic introspective limitations with the headset's rebranded "corrected interpretations"; and seed the narrative with fictional specifications, tiered subscription pricing, and a customer testimonial from a user who now claims absolute certainty about her own unconscious biases because the device confirmed them. The piece must remain in character throughout, never breaking irony, so that the humor lands only for a reader who recognizes that outsourcing introspection to hardware is precisely the opposite of solving its reliability problem. Please avoid bibliographies, citations, reading lists, or references to specific editions and page numbers; the conceptual references—to privileged access, confabulation, and the transparency of experience—should be recognizable without scholarly apparatus, making the piece meaningful for a reader without web access.
