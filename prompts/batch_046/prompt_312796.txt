After watching the cooperative I helped found dissolve within eighteen months of rapid growth—passing through charismatic leadership, factional deadlock, and bureaucratic sclerosis—I have adopted a hybrid view of historical change. I hold that Polybius’s anacyclosis captures the structural lifecycle of institutions with genuine fidelity, and that Ibn Khaldun’s dynamics of asabiyyah explain the mechanism of renewal: cohesive groups displace decaying structures, then decay in turn. Yet I maintain that technological and epistemic accumulation proceeds linearly rather than cyclically, so that institutional form repeats while material capacity grows, giving history a spiral shape rather than a circle or a straight line. What would genuinely count against this? Please distinguish fatal evidence from merely awkward evidence; include observations, arguments, formal results, or institutional developments—things that have arguably already occurred—that should move me; say what defenders of this hybrid view would themselves concede as damaging; state what predictions this model makes that pure cyclicalism and pure linear progressivism do not; and assess honestly whether I have insulated the view from refutation by allowing linear technological progress to absorb every institutional failure. If the view collapses into one of its rivals under pressure, say so, and judge whether that vulnerability is a defect.
