As a journalist who writes on science and institutional ethics, I am preparing a feature on how philosophy seminars can stage conceptual conflicts that resist easy resolution. For this, I need a runnable classroom exercise—structured like a role-play with rules, hidden incentives, and a debrief—that puts about fourteen graduate students inside the tension between the duty to remember and the case for forgetting, as that tension is articulated in the work of Avishai Margalit and Philip Rieff. The session should last ninety minutes, and the theoretical framing should not be named until after the activity concludes.

The concrete scenario should inject a niche institutional problem not usually found in classroom debates: a scientific archive holds a dataset from a discredited mid-twentieth-century study in which subjects' full names are embedded in the raw records. Two redaction protocols are on the table. Commemorative retention keeps the names visible in the public record, treating the subjects as present moral witnesses to institutional harm; this tracks a Margalit-style commitment that shared memory within thick relations requires holding wrongs in collective view rather than dissolving them. Functional anonymization strips all personal identifiers not out of private sentiment but to prevent the dataset's reuse in contemporary predictive modeling; this tracks a Rieff-style diagnosis that modern institutions often pursue a therapeutic erasure, converting moral debts into administrative hygiene in order to secure present operational comfort.

Design four roles—an archivist committed to commemorative retention; a data-ethics officer advocating functional anonymization; a representative of the subjects' community; and an independent auditor empowered to block either protocol only if unanimous concerns are formally registered. Provide them with a one-page decision document (the redaction protocol) and a non-monetary payoff scheme: each role receives a scorecard tracking institutional credibility, community trust, and epistemic access, and the group receives a collective score that rewards either unanimous agreement or a particular weighted balance of values. Structure the timeline in three rounds: private position papers (ten minutes), negotiated revision (thirty-five minutes), and a binding vote with hidden payoff revelation (ten minutes). The rules must be arranged so that participants encounter the structural pull themselves—the archivist cannot maintain retention without damaging the community representative's trust metric, while the anonymizer cannot secure protection without eroding the auditor's credibility—so that moral argument gradually shifts into institutional risk management before anyone names the philosophy behind it.

After the vote, conduct a twenty-minute debrief. Map the private-paper round onto Margalit's distinction between forgiveness and remembrance; map the negotiation shift onto Rieff's critique of therapeutic culture and its tendency to dissolve the past for present comfort. Identify precisely where in the timeline participants typically abandon moral language for administrative language, and explain what that transition reveals about the idea. Be explicit about which feature of Margalit's or Rieff's account the exercise cannot reproduce—whether the intergenerational temporal depth of shared memory, or the metaphysical status of sacred commitment in Rieff's sociology of culture—and instruct me, as facilitator, on how to supply that missing element verbally.

Predict what will predictably go wrong the first time this is run: likely role confusion between the community representative and the auditor, premature convergence on a split-the-difference compromise, or participants importing modern data-privacy norms without questioning whether anonymization is genuinely therapeutic forgetting or an independent moral logic. Offer specific handling instructions for each failure mode.

Because this design requires an interpretive reconstruction of Margalit's ethics of memory and Rieff's concept of anti-culture, please conclude by stating how confident you are in the mapping you propose between the scorecard mechanics and the philosophical positions. In particular, single out the point about which you are least certain—whether it concerns Rieff's notion of therapeutic erasure, the fidelity of the payoff structure to Margalit's idea of thick relations, or the claim that functional anonymization in scientific archives genuinely instantiates a case for institutional forgetting rather than a different ethical framework altogether.
