Design a reusable 48-hour decision procedure for a pharmacovigilance compliance officer who evaluates roughly six internal safety signals per quarter and must determine whether each qualifies as "known adverse event" requiring mandatory regulatory filing, or remains an unconfirmed suspicion. Ground the procedure in post-Gettier epistemology: knowledge requires justified true belief plus an anti-luck condition, meaning the justification must be non-accidentally linked to the truth rather than merely coincident with it. Present it as ordered gates or tests with clear pass-fail criteria, mark every step that remains an irreducible judgment call rather than a mechanical rule, and specify where the procedure halts with a procedural "no" or hands the decision to human values. Apply it to three cases: (1) a cluster with correct causal tracking and matching justification; (2) a true harm rate detected only because instrument miscalibration accidentally produced readings that align with real biological damage—the belief is true and carries some justification, but the justification tracks the wrong feature; (3) a false harm belief supported by strong but mislabeled comparator data. Ensure case (2) yields a result the team will resist—likely that the signal does not count as knowledge and should not trigger filing—and state explicitly what the procedure cannot resolve, such as whether regulatory ethics ever demands disclosure of true beliefs that fail the anti-luck condition. Separate what is settled in epistemology about the Gettier challenge from what remains genuinely contested among specialists (for instance, whether safety, sensitivity, or virtue-theoretic conditions best capture the anti-luck requirement), and do not present any contested analysis as the consensus.
