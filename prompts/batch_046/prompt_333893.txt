Design a 90-minute seminar activity for 12 advanced undergraduates on Buddhist anatta and personal identity. The aim is for participants to feel—before the concept is named—how practical and narrative continuity can survive the absence of an enduring subject that owns successive mental states. To keep the exercise out of well-worn territory, frame it as a rotated-aggregate governance simulation: the five skandhas (rupa, vedana, samjna, samskara, vijnana) are assigned as functional roles that are randomly reshuffled among participants every 15 minutes. A fictional biography is staged across five decision points (making a promise, causing a harm, altering a preference, claiming a memory, facing death). At each point, the current holders of the five roles must reach a binding collective decision speaking only from the perspective of their assigned aggregate; once the decision is recorded, all roles rotate, and no participant may appeal to “I made that choice” or invoke a persistent self to justify continuity. The group earns coherence points for maintaining biographical consistency across rounds, but loses ownership points whenever anyone smuggles in a hidden “chairperson” or narrative ego to stabilize agency. Include precise rules for turn-taking, the rotation protocol, the point scheme, and a 20-minute debrief that maps each round onto one skandha and onto a standard philosophical objection to anatta (e.g., moral responsibility, first-person authority, diachronic memory). Be explicit about what the simulation can reproduce—functional continuity without substrate identity—and what it cannot: the phenomenological transparency of self-consciousness, which the facilitator must supply through guided reflection rather than rules. Note one predictable failure mode—students will likely invent an implicit narrator or executive self to resolve conflict—and specify how to counter it without breaking the rotation rule. Do not request bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; the activity must be self-contained and runnable without external sources.
