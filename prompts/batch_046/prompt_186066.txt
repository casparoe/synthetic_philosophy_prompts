I am a doctoral student in philosophy of language, and over the past year I have been immersed in the literature that follows Kripke's lectures on Wittgenstein's rule-following considerations. Despite extensive engagement, the debate between the skeptical challenger—who presses that there is no fact of the matter about whether one is adding or quadding—and the anti-skeptical camps (dispositionalist, primitivist, normative-pragmatic) appears to have reached a kind of perfect equilibrium: every objection is anticipated, every reply meets a counter-reply, and yet no side has yielded ground. I am increasingly persuaded that this stalemate is not a straightforward substantive disagreement about the metaphysics of meaning, but rather a case in which different parties answer distinct questions—some about metaphysical determination, others about epistemic access, others about the conditions for normative authority—under the single heading of "rule-following." Specifically, I wish to examine whether both sides implicitly share what I shall term the "determinacy-first" assumption: that semantic content must be grounded in conditions that determinately fix extension, with the only dispute being whether those conditions are internal dispositions, communal practices, or primitive norms. Is this diagnosis correct, or is there a hidden empirical disagreement—perhaps about cognitive architecture or the psychology of concept acquisition—that is actually driving the impasse? Furthermore, could anything genuinely resolve it: a conceptual distinction between semantic determinacy and normative authority, an empirical result about how children learn arithmetic rules, or a formal analysis of the structure of rule-systems? Please diagnose the kind of disagreement operating here, supporting your analysis with reference to what the principal sides actually maintain, and tell me honestly whether progress should be expected or whether the question is structurally ill-posed. In doing so, please separate what is well established in the literature—for instance, that unsophisticated dispositionalism faces both the finiteness problem and the error problem—from what remains genuinely contested among specialists, and do not present any single interpretation, whether the normative-pragmatic reading or a purely metaphysical one, as though it commanded consensus.
