I've been working through Friedman's F-twist in the context of agent-based epidemiological models that assume homogeneous mixing. If I take the insulation thesis seriously, the ontological falsity of an assumption—treating a spatially distributed population as uniformly interacting—seems epistemically neutral so long as the predictive outputs hold. But I'm stuck on a compounding problem: if I layer a second unrealistic assumption onto the same architecture—say, that infection risk is memoryless across repeated contacts with the same individual—and the joint model's forecasts actually improve, doesn't Friedman's logic require treating this stacked unrealism as a methodological virtue? It feels as though there's no internal stopping point for accumulating contradictory false premises as long as the aggregate error term shrinks. What's the correct criterion, within the F-twist framework, for distinguishing productive unrealistic simplification from destructive model incoherence? Or does the framework genuinely lack any constraint that would prevent contradictory assumptions from being rationalized away purely by predictive fit?
