I'm the senior editor for a world-history journal and we're recieveing way too many submissions that treat germs, climate swings, or volcanic winters as either decorative backdrop or as hidden puppet-masters that "caused" revolutions and famines all by themselves. I need a reusable decision procedure my copy desk can actually run on any draft: ordered questions that decide whether a non-human force gets real actor-status without sliding into environmental determinism. Include what counts as a sufficient causal link, the point where the procedure stops with a hard no because human agency got erased, and the points where it just hands the decision up to me because it's a values call disguised as a fact check. Be honest about which steps are really judgment calls in a checklist costume, and build in my "contingency gate" idea — that an environmental factor only earns full actor-status if its effect runs through a specific, avoidable institutional failure that human actors actually controlled.

Then run it on three submissions:
- One that assigns the Black Death actor-status for reshaping English labor markets in the 1300s, naming specific transmission mechanisms and institutional responses.
- One that claims the Little Ice Age caused the Thirty Years' War by ruining harvests and leaving princes with no choice but to fight.
- One about 1816 that gives Tambora's aerosol veil co-actor status with British colonial grain policy in the Bengal famine, and argues that without the volcano the famine wouldn't have occured at all.

Make sure one of these comes back with an answer I'll probably dislike. Also say clearly what the procedure can't settle, and flag where it misfires if the author hides human choices inside a "natural" mechanism.
