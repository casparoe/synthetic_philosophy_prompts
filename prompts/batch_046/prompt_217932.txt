Hey, I'm prepping a grad seminar on philosophy of science and I've hit this argumentative pattern in multiverse and string-theory debates that I can't quite place. You start with a theory that promises some concrete empirical signature -- maybe supersymmetry at a specific collider energy, or some distinctive imprint in the cosmic microwave background from bubble collisions. The experiments come back negative or ambigous. But instead of taking that as a serious strike, proponents pivot: suddenly the main evidence is that the theory is the only mathematically consistent quantum gravity we have, or that it explains the cosmological constant's value by distributing it across a landscape of possibilities. The empirical bar gets replaced by these meta-theoretical virtues -- uniqueness, explanatory breadth, consistency -- and the theory is treated as well-confirmed on those grounds.

It's not quite the degenerating problemshift Lakatos described, since the adjustments aren't ad hoc patches to save specific predictions; they're more like a wholesale migration of what counts as confirming evidence, triggered specifically when observation becomes inaccessible. And it's not just Bayesian updating on theoretical virtues, because there's this distinctive temporal structure: empirical ambitions first, then they fade, then a different kind of justification rushes in to fill the gap. Does this specific migration pattern have an established name? If not, can you distinguish it precisely from ordinary research-program defense and from straightforward non-empirical argument? I want the students to recognize the move without me just calling it 'moving the goalposts.'
