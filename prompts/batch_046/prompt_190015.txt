In evolutionary biology and philosophy of science seminars I attend, the long-running dispute over adaptationism and Gould and Lewontin's spandrels critique has settled into a frustrating pattern: all participants grant that pleiotropic correlations, architectural byproducts, and drift occur; all grant that selection can reshape them; yet whenever discussion turns to whether a particular complex trait — for instance, the observed correlation between linguistic recursion capacities and enhanced spatial-navigation modules — should be treated as a direct adaptation, an exaptation, or a genuine spandrel, the same moves recur without convergence. Adaptationists press for a functional narrative linking the correlation to differential reproductive success in ancestral environments; critics press for quantitative-genetic evidence that selection coefficients actually built the correlation rather than developmental constraint or genetic drift producing it as a byproduct. Each side charges the other with either Panglossian just-so storytelling or with an implicit denial that selection can integrate complex phenotypes, but in a year of meetings no position has shifted. Is the stall caused by a hidden empirical question — say, whether current quantitative-genetic architectures can discriminate adaptive integration from constraint-driven correlation — or by a deeper divergence over what an evolutionary explanation must deliver: a mechanistic developmental account, a statistical demonstration of selection, or a historical narrative of reproductive advantage? Or are the parties answering different questions — one about evolutionary origin, one about present functional maintenance — while using the single term adaptation for both? Diagnose the structure of this disagreement by attending to what each side actually demands as decisive evidence, and say whether any formal result, empirical finding, or conceptual distinction could break the deadlock, or whether the framing itself is ill-posed in a way that makes progress unattainable.
