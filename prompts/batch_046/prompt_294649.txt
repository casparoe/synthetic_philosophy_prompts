I am a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy, and in a study group with colleagues from our distributed-systems reading club I recently witnessed a disagreement about information cascades and rational herding that I have been unable to adjudicate on my own. The discussion began when one colleague—call her Maya—described a sequential code-review process in a large open-source repository: maintainers approve pull requests one by one, each able to observe prior approvals but not the private reasoning or confidence weights behind them. Maya argued that once two senior maintainers approve a subtly buggy change, junior maintainers with weak negative private signals will nevertheless approve, not because they have rationally updated on the seniors' beliefs, but because the social and reputational cost of being the sole dissenting voice outweighs their private evidence; she concluded that this is irrational herding disguised as Bayesian updating.

Her interlocutor, James, objected vigorously. He maintained that if the juniors genuinely possess noisy private signals and correctly infer that two approvals carry information about the seniors' hidden signals, then herding is precisely what rational Bayesian agents ought to do; labeling it irrational, he said, conflates individual rationality with collective inaccuracy. James further insisted that the cost of dissent is merely a component of the agents' payoff structure, and rational agents are defined by optimizing over their full payoffs—including social costs—so there is no paradox or irrationality in the cascade itself.

Maya then sharpened her position by introducing a feature specific to this repository: maintainers do not cast simple binary votes but attach graded confidence scores, and this creates a strategic distortion. A junior maintainer with a moderately strong negative signal may still publish a high-confidence approval because they anticipate that a low-confidence dissent will be ignored, while a high-confidence dissent will trigger a costly conflict; consequently, their public action does not reflect their posterior belief but rather a strategic manipulation of the very signal that later observers are meant to update upon. James responded that this demonstrates only that the observation channel is corrupted, not that herding is irrational; if later observers know the channel is corrupted, they should discount the approvals accordingly, and any failure to do so is a defect of meta-reasoning rather than of herding. Maya countered that if every agent knows the channel is corrupted and discounts it, yet still herds because the social cost of breaking the cascade remains, then the cascade is sustained not by belief updating but by something closer to equilibrium selection in a repeated interaction—perhaps even a form of coercion—rather than by the evidential logic that defines rational herding.

Neither would yield. I am asking for a point-by-point verdict: is Maya correct that strategic distortion of confidence scores transforms this case into something other than standard rational herding? Is James correct that individual rationality remains intact so long as agents maximize over complete payoffs? And what are both parties missing about whether such a cascade could be broken without altering the underlying payoff structure?
