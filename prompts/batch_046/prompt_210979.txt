I'm developing a framework for assessing moral worth specifically in terms of what it takes to act for the right reasons, and I'm trying to navigate between the usual stalemates—roughly, between Kantian demands for purity of motivation and more pluralist, quality-of-will approaches—without simply picking a camp. Before I commit, I want you to specify the full list of desiderata that any adequate theory in this area must meet. For each requirement, provide the motivating case or counterexample that makes it indispensable: don't just state the condition, but show what failure looks like in practice.

Please cover at least the following dimensions, though you may reorganize or supplement them: (a) the distinction between acting in accordance with right reasons and acting from them, and whether counterfactual dependence on the moral reason is genuinely required or merely a prejudice of one tradition; (b) the problem of motivational redundancy—cases where an agent possesses both a genuinely moral motive and an independent self-interested motive that would each suffice to produce the same action, and whether such redundancy vitiates moral worth or is harmless; (c) whether worth requires that the moral reason be psychologically dominant or decisive in some explanatory economy, or whether mere causal efficacy is enough; (d) how the theory treats agents who do the right thing but for conceptually malformed, bizarre, or only accidentally aligned reasons—what I will call moral blindness—without collapsing into the view that only philosophers can have moral worth; (e) consistency with our actual practices of praise, blame, and partial credit, especially in cases of genuinely mixed motivation. For each desideratum, mark it as non-negotiable (any theory violating it fails outright) or as a camp-specific preference, and explain the marking.

Crucially, I want you to identify whether any subset of these requirements is jointly unsatisfiable—perhaps the demand for pure moral motivation conflicts with the demand that worthy agents be psychologically realistic, or the redundancy condition undermines the dominance condition. If you see a hidden trilemma or tension among apparently compatible demands, lay it out explicitly; that is the part I value most. Finally, please state clearly what would change your mind: which specific counterexample—say, a refined case of redundancy, a new variant of moral blindness, or an argument about psychological realism—would lead you to reclassify one of these desiderata from non-negotiable to negotiable, or reveal that two of them actually conflict after all. Where is your reasoning most likely to fail?
