I am co-designing a museum exhibit about a small Alpine valley whose communal grazing contracts were renegotiated after the prolonged cold and failed harvests that followed the massive volcanic eruption of 1815. One of my co-curators wants to present the climate disruption not as a passive backdrop but as something that actively reshaped the valley's social arrangements—almost like a participant with its own weight in the story. Our advisory historians object that this makes the local leaders look like mere effects of temperature and ash, erasing the fact that they deliberately chose to rewrite the contracts rather than disperse. I want to defend a middle view: that the cold snap genuinely constrained and redirected what was possible, without being an intentional agent like a mayor or a merchant, and without rendering the leaders' choices inevitable. But I am unsure how to draw that line with philosophical precision.

There is an added wrinkle. During those same years, a crop-destroying blight—carried by a waterborne pathogen—devastated a valley just over the ridge but never reached ours. Should the fact that this germ-driven blight was geographically close yet causally absent change how I treat the cold snap as a genuine "actor" in our specific narrative? Or does comparing the present climate shock to an absent microbial threat confuse two different kinds of historical influence?

Could you help me articulate the conceptual framework I need—how to grant real causal weight to climate shifts, volcanic fallout, and germs without turning them into intentional agents or collapsing human decision into mere reaction—so that I can write the exhibit panels with rigor rather than vague metaphor? In particular, I want to understand whether counterfactual proximity of a non-occurring biological event should affect how I assign agency to the actual environmental forces that did shape our valley.
