I'm taking an advanced course on philosophy of mind, and in our discussions of consciousness and first-person authority my study group keeps noticing a specific argumentative pattern that seems to sit somewhere near debates about whether dreams are experiences—particularly the exchange between Malcolm and Dennett—yet doesn't quite match either side. I don't know if the pattern has an established name, and I'd like to understand exactly what it is, where its edges are, and what it is not.

Here is the pattern, described through two concrete instances. First, in the dream case: a subject reports upon waking that she experienced a vivid red triangle. We have no behavioral check available at the time of occurrence—only the retrospective report. Someone then argues that this report should not be treated as authoritative evidence for an experience, not because dreams necessarily lack phenomenal character, but because the report occurs in a context where it cannot be corrected by others: there is no concurrent observation, no ability to ask clarifying questions during the event, and no consistency check with other mental states at that time. The same person, however, will treat an awake report of seeing a red triangle as authoritative, not because they have direct access to the awake subject's phenomenology, but because the report is embedded in a web of correctability—others can point to the object, ask about its shape, check for consistency with the subject's other beliefs. The key move is that the epistemic authority of the report is grounded entirely in the surrounding social and behavioral correctability-conditions, rather than in any feature of the mental state being reported.

The second instance appears in debates about mystical or drug-induced experiences. A subject reports a sense of unity with the universe. Critics sometimes reject the report not by arguing that no such phenomenal state occurred, but by noting that the conditions for correcting or integrating the report—cross-checking with physical science, comparing with other observers, embedding in a stable life narrative—are absent or severely weakened. The state might be real, but its epistemic status as knowledge, or even as a veridical experience, is undermined purely by the absence of these external correctability structures. What I'm trying to isolate is the specific conceptual maneuver: using the presence or absence of intersubjective correctability conditions not merely as evidence for or against a mental state, but as partly constitutive of whether that state counts as an experience in the epistemically relevant sense. This feels different from ordinary verificationism, because it doesn't claim that unobservable states don't exist; different from externalism about mental content, because it doesn't say the content depends on the environment; and different from Malcolm's specific argument about the concept of dreaming, because it applies equally to waking mystical reports and doesn't depend on any claim about the logic of mental-state concepts. Does this configuration have an established name in philosophy of mind, philosophy of testimony, or the literature on dreams? If there is no precise term, please coin one with a clear definition and boundary conditions—specifically distinguishing it from verificationism, from social externalism about content, and from the thesis that experience requires conceptual articulation. Finally, please indicate how confident you are in your answer, and identify the specific point about which you are least certain—whether that is the identification with an existing term, the boundary with Malcolm's view, or the applicability to the mystical-experience case.
