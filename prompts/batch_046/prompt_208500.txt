Exam prompt for grading (Urban Philosophy, final):

Consider the claim that restrictive single-family zoning in high-opportunity neighborhoods violates a right to the city by excluding lower-income households from urban amenities and economic opportunity. Critics respond that local residents possess a democratic entitlement to control land use in their own neighborhoods, and that abrupt upzoning risks displacing existing communities. Evaluate whether state preemption of local zoning can be justified by appeal to this right, distinguishing three interpretations: (a) protection against displacement, (b) democratic self-determination over local space, and (c) equitable access to urban opportunity regardless of residence. Specifically, address whether linking upzoning to a community-benefits agreement that reserves 40 percent of new affordable units for current neighborhood residents, and that requires resident approval of the deal, resolves the tension between interpretations (b) and (c).

Student response:

The right to the city should be read first and foremost as democratic self-determination over local space. When residents enact restrictive zoning through local democratic processes, they are exercising that right rather than violating it. State preemption is therefore unjustified unless the local decision actively undermines the other interpretations. Exclusionary zoning does conflict with equitable access, since it bars outsiders from entering the neighborhood, but this conflict is resolved by a well-designed community-benefits agreement. By reserving 40 percent of new affordable units for current residents and giving those residents veto power over the agreement, the plan protects them from displacement—satisfying interpretation (a)—while allowing growth that eventually expands access—satisfying interpretation (c). Democratic control is preserved because residents must approve the deal, so interpretation (b) is also respected. The remaining 60 percent of units provide the necessary access for outsiders. Consequently, there is no genuine moral conflict: all three interpretations are harmonized, and state preemption is only legitimate if residents reject a deal that objectively meets all three criteria, in which case the state may impose the access component but should not override the resident veto.
