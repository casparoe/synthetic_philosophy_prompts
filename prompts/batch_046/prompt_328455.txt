I retired some years ago after a career in molecular biology, and lately I have been reading philosophy with the same mixture of fascination and methodological suspicion that one brings to an unfamiliar experimental protocol. In conversations with former colleagues—most of whom remain firmly in the empirical camp—I have found myself defending the idea that metaphysics can ask precise questions even when they outrun direct observation. One such question is the special composition question, familiar from the work of Peter van Inwagen: under exactly what conditions do several discrete objects come together to form a single further object? Yet in my own research, I was constantly confronted with a mirror problem that receives far less philosophical attention. I have begun to call it the special persistence question: given that a complex system—say, a tissue culture or a colonial organism—maintains its organizational identity despite near-total replacement of its material parts over time, what precisely determines that the same object persists rather than being replaced by a numerically distinct successor?

I would like you to compare these two questions—the special composition question and this proposed special persistence question—focusing especially on living and quasi-living systems where functional integration seems more relevant than spatiotemporal contiguity alone. Do the criteria that answer one tend to answer the other, or do they generate conflicting verdicts? For example, if one holds that parts compose a whole only when they are bound by a self-maintaining functional organization—an idea I have encountered in discussions of autopoiesis and synthetic biology—does that same organizational criterion govern persistence through part-replacement, or does persistence require an additional condition, such as the continuous trajectory of a particular material substrate? You might consider whether there can be composition without persistence, or persistence without composition, using illustrative cases like slime molds, regenerative organisms, or engineered biological constructs, without relying on bibliographic references or citations. I am particularly interested in whether the asymmetry between these two problems is metaphysically substantial or merely an artifact of how scientists conventionally track entities in the laboratory. Please develop the comparison with care, and note where philosophical analysis seems to clarify the scientific intuition, and where it appears to complicate it beyond recognition.
