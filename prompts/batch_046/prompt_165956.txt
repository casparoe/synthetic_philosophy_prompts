As a novelist accustomed to constructing worlds in which the ordinary rules of existence are suspended or reconfigured, I have become preoccupied with a philosophical question that bears directly on my craft: to what extent does my ability to conceive a scenario indicate that it is genuinely possible, not merely fictionally permissible? Over the past months I have accumulated a set of intuitive verdicts about particular cases, yet I cannot tell whether they are governed by a single principle or whether they expose an inconsistency in my own modal reasoning.

My judgments are as follows. I find that I can genuinely conceive of water being composed of XYZ rather than H2O, but only under conditions of chemical ignorance; once I grasp that water just is H2O, the idea that this very liquid might have had a different molecular structure strikes me as not merely false but genuinely impossible. I can also conceive, without any felt contradiction, of a being physically identical to me in every respect who nonetheless lacks any inner consciousness whatsoever—this seems to describe a real metaphysical possibility rather than a hidden logical impossibility. By contrast, I genuinely cannot conceive of 2 plus 2 equaling 5; the failure feels absolute, not a limitation of my imagination. In ethical matters, I can conceive of an entire civilization in which cruelty is systematically revered and compassion despised, with no internal incoherence in its institutions; this leads me to think that if moral truths exist, they are not metaphysically necessary. The difficulty arises with personal identity and narrative form. I feel I can conceive of my consciousness surviving a transfer into a silicon substrate, remaining genuinely me. Yet I can conceive with equal clarity of an atom-for-atom silicon duplicate of myself that behaves identically but possesses no consciousness at all. These two conceptions seem to pull in opposite directions: one suggests that substrate is irrelevant to the presence of consciousness, the other that it is essential to preserving my identity. Furthermore, in my fiction I have constructed scenes in which an omniscient narrator both asserts and denies the same occurrence within a single sentence, treating this not as an error but as an intentional feature of the story-world. I do not know whether this reveals that logical contradictions can be locally suspended in some metaphysically possible worlds, or whether it merely demonstrates that linguistic framing can disguise an underlying impossibility.

Is there a coherent principle—perhaps one that distinguishes logical possibility, metaphysical possibility, and what I might call authorial or narrative possibility—that captures the pattern of these verdicts? If no single principle can accommodate all of them, which judgments should I abandon in favor of simplicity? And whatever principle emerges, what should it predict about a further case I have not mentioned: can one genuinely conceive of time passing in the complete absence of change, not even in consciousness or in the positions of particles?
