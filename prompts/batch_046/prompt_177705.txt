I'm in a seminar on collective responsibility and have constructed an argument about Jaspers's distinction among criminal, political, moral, and metaphysical guilt that seems to force an absurd conclusion about inherited national guilt. Here are the steps:

(1) A nation-state is a continuous legal-institutional entity across generations; like a corporation, it inherits obligations and liabilities regardless of personnel change.
(2) Jaspers holds that criminal guilt is strictly individual and non-transferable, whereas political guilt attaches to citizenship in a state that commits wrongs, moral guilt to individual conscience, and metaphysical guilt to solidarity with human suffering.
(3) Citizenship is transmitted by birth and law, not chosen, so the status that grounds political guilt is inherited by descendants who played no part in the original acts.
(4) If institutional continuity transmits the conditions for political guilt, then present citizens inherit the very status that makes them politically guilty for historical atrocities committed before their birth.
(5) If inheriting that status is sufficient for being guilty in the political mode, then current citizens bear political guilt in exactly the same way as those alive during the atrocity.
(6) But Jaspers treats moral and metaphysical guilt as requiring personal involvement or solidarity; they cannot be assigned merely by legal membership. Political guilt, however, is assigned precisely by that membership.
(7) Therefore we face a dilemma: either deny that political guilt is genuinely moral guilt, reducing it to reparative liability without moral stain, or accept that guilt is an inescapable birthright tied to state membership—so a child born today is politically guilty for centuries-old violence, and only emigration or state dissolution can absolve her.
(8) The second horn proves far too much; it collapses collective responsibility into a hereditary moral status that no individual action can alter.

My instinct is to reject step (4) or (5), but I cannot tell whether the error is an equivocation between inheriting legal liability and inheriting moral guilt, or whether Jaspers's category of political guilt was never designed to survive full institutional continuity in the first place. Which premise should I abandon, and why does it only look plausible? If I want to preserve the idea that a nation can bear non-criminal responsibility for past wrongs without branding every present citizen guilty, what does that rejection cost me—must I give up institutional continuity, or can I save it by reconceiving political guilt as something other than inherited moral status?
