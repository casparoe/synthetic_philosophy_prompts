I am a retired physical chemist who, since leaving the laboratory, has taken up philosophy with the same systematic attention I once gave to molecular structures. Next Thursday I am to lead a single ninety-minute session for a reading circle of fellow retired scientists and engineers—none with formal philosophical training—on the heterogeneity problem for hedonism, that is, the question of what, if anything, all pleasures genuinely share in virtue of being pleasures. I do not want you to explain hedonism to me; rather, I need the pedagogical architecture for the session. Please set out the precise order in which the conceptual components must be introduced, indicating what depends on what so that the sequence is not arbitrary. For the carrying example, I wish to employ an analogy drawn from my own discipline: treating the pleasure of resolving a long-standing theoretical puzzle and the pleasure of feeling a cool breeze on sun-warmed skin as though they were chemical isomers—possessing an identical underlying formula yet arranged in structurally different configurations—to ask whether pleasures share one phenomenological “formula” or are as different in kind as distinct elements. Specify exactly what this analogy must illuminate, and, just as importantly, where it must be withdrawn before it misleads. Then map the misconceptions that scientifically minded newcomers are likely to import or form—such as assuming that shared neural activation proves qualitative identity, or that qualitative diversity necessarily proves there is no common nature—and state precisely at which point in your proposed sequence each should be intercepted and neutralised. Include one diagnostic question I can pose in the final ten minutes to reveal whether the participants have genuinely grasped the problem rather than merely adopted a position. Finally, state clearly what should be deliberately omitted at this introductory level—perhaps certain technical distinctions in contemporary philosophy of mind or particular historical formulations—and present the entire plan in British English, without bibliographic references, reading lists, or appeals to texts the group could not verify from first principles alone.
