I'm presenting at an economic history seminar a revised Braudelian argument I call resonant structuralism. Rather than treating climate as static geographic background—the mountains and seas of Braudel's longue durée—I treat multi-decadal climate oscillations, such as prolonged drought cycles, as slowly moving structures with their own periodicities. I propose that these cycles interact with what I term institutional memory span: the length of time an organization's decision-rules persist without generational turnover. When a climate cycle exceeds this span, the institution cannot adapt incrementally because the conditions that shaped its rules are forgotten before the cycle completes, producing structural rupture rather than gradual adjustment; when the cycle is shorter, smooth adaptation follows. I apply this to agrarian societies that collapsed after long drought intervals versus those with faster institutional turnover that survived comparable stress. The audience is mostly economic historians steeped in Braudel's Mediterranean but skeptical of deterministic mechanisms; several quantitative economic historians who will demand measurable correlations; and institutional historians who will object that institutional memory span is an anachronistic metric imposed on premodern societies. Draft my Q&A prep sheet: the six or seven objections I will actually face, ordered by how damaging they would be, each phrased in the vocabulary of the group that will raise it, with a concise reply, a note on where that reply is weakest, what I should concede up front, and what I must hold at all costs for the argument to survive. Include the weakest structural point in my own framework—the boundary case where climate periodicity and institutional span are approximately equal—that I should address head-on rather than hope they miss. End with the one question I'm hoping nobody asks: whether this framework implies that modern societies with compressed institutional memory are therefore immune to long-cycle climate risks.
