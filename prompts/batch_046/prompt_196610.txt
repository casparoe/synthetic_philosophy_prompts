After a long, unresolved argument with a close friend about Bernard Williams’s integrity objection—specifically the case in which a husband saves his spouse but only after weighing her life against a stranger’s in an agent-neutral calculus, revealing what Williams calls one thought too many—I have settled on a stronger structural reading that I want stress-tested. I maintain that the objection is not merely a psychological observation about moral motivation but a claim about the architecture of practical reason: agent-neutral moral theories conceptually require treating an agent’s ground projects as fungible inputs to an impersonal calculus, and this alienation is not contingent but built into the form of reasoning itself, violating the unity of practical reason that makes first-personal commitment intelligible. My friend replies that this overreads Williams, who permits ground projects to be overridden in extreme circumstances, and that I have illegitimately turned integrity into an absolute side-constraint rather than a weighted consideration within moral deliberation. I want an honest map of what would genuinely defeat my position. Which premises about practical reason, moral phenomenology, or the structure of first-personal reasons could fail without collapsing my view into pure utilitarianism on one flank or an implausible moral particularism on the other? Would robust empirical evidence that agents routinely perform agent-neutral calculation without experiencing alienation undermine the conceptual claim, or would it only show that the alienation is contingent? Is there a defensible intermediate account—one that distinguishes first-personal reasons from impersonal ones without granting the former veto power—that preserves the core force of Williams’s example without committing me to the stronger thesis that all moral arithmetic involves an unacceptable form of estrangement? Please assess how exposed this position is: distinguish evidence that would be decisive from evidence that would merely be awkward, and say whether the view risks being well-insulated to the point of unfalsifiability or whether it genuinely risks something.
