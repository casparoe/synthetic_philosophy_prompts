I'm planning a single seminar session for first-year philosophy students who have just read Locke on personal identity and are inclined to treat psychological continuity as the obvious answer to where the self goes. I want them to work through Dennett's "Where Am I?" narrative not as a survival puzzle, but as a demonstration that first-person point of view can come apart from bodily location without either being the "real" self. Design the explanatory arc: the conceptual distinction that must be in place before the story is introduced and why Locke's framework alone doesn't provide it; the specific episode in Dennett's narrative that should carry the session—why it needs the brain-in-the-vat in Houston, the radio-linked body in Tulsa, and the backup duplicate brain all present together, and what feature of that conjunction makes the split visible; the three misconceptions they will bring in sequence—first that the experiment asks for a factual location rather than testing an intuition, then that Dennett is defending a brain-based theory of identity, then that the narrative voice itself proves the brain is the self—and the exact point in the sequence where each should be interrupted and absorbed; the check for understanding that would reveal whether they have grasped the structural point about first-person authority versus metaphysical location rather than merely being unsettled by the science-fiction framing; and which parts of the subsequent literature—the split-brain cases, Parfit's reductionism—should be named only to be explicitly set aside at this level.
