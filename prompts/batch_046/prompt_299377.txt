Since I recently became the primary caregiver for a parent with advanced neurodegeneration, the question of whether suffering-focused ethics demands not merely anti-natalism but the active elimination of consciousness has shifted from seminar abstraction to an urgent practical boundary problem. I have constructed the following argument to test that boundary, and its conclusion strikes me as intolerable, yet I cannot locate the precise fracture.

(1) In suffering-focused ethics, only token instances of experienced suffering carry intrinsic negative value, while the absence of suffering carries intrinsic positive value only when it replaces an actual suffering-state.

(2) If an agent can prevent a token suffering-event E without generating a countervailing token suffering-event of equal or greater weight, and without violating an independent deontic constraint, then the agent is morally required to prevent E.

(3) A potential conscious being P, if actualized, will be the subject of a set of token suffering-events S_P. Preventing P's existence eliminates every element of S_P, and because no actual subject P exists to experience deprivation, this elimination produces no countervailing token suffering.

(4) Therefore, suffering-focused ethics requires that agents prevent the actualization of P whenever the expected weight of S_P is greater than zero.

(5) Now introduce an indexical boundary condition: an existing conscious being B is actually present, so moral evaluation regarding B must be indexed to B's actual phenomenal continuity rather than to merely modal capacities.

(6) Nevertheless, the logical structure governing (2) through (4) depends not on whether a suffering-capacity is potential or actual, but solely on the modal availability of token suffering-events that can be eliminated without producing replacement suffering.

(7) For any existing B, the set of future suffering-events S_B is available in exactly the same modal sense: they are not yet actual tokens, but they can be eliminated by terminating B's consciousness, with no subject remaining to suffer the loss of future goods.

(8) By transitivity, if preventing a potential capacity for suffering is obligatory, then maintaining an actual capacity through inaction is prohibited whenever elimination is available and survivors' suffering can be independently minimized.

(9) Hence suffering-focused ethics obligates the elimination of all actual and potential conscious capacities — in effect, the extinction of sentient life — a conclusion I reject, though I wish to preserve (3) and (4).

Which step should be rejected? Does (5) perform any genuine work, or is the slide concealed in the move from (6) to (7)? If I defend (5) by insisting on a rigid indexical boundary that protects existing beings but not potential ones, what consistency cost does that exact from the core motivation of suffering-focused ethics? And if the error lies instead in (2) — perhaps because it tacitly licenses the destruction of the very subjects whose suffering it aims to reduce — what remains distinctive about the view?
