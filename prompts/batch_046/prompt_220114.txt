Please explain a conceptual tension that arises when comparing Western liberal individualism with East Asian relational ethics—specifically, the distinction between ontological and normative forms of collectivism. In the standard framing, Western thought treats the individual as metaphysically prior to society, with community arising from voluntary association among pre-existing agents, whereas East Asian traditions (particularly Confucianism) are said to treat the person as constituted by social roles such that there is no intelligible self prior to relationships like parent-child or ruler-subject.

Yet this binary seems to collapse two distinct claims: (i) that relationships are constitutive of personal identity in an ontological sense, dissolving any boundary between self and community; and (ii) that relationships are normatively and developmentally necessary for human flourishing, without thereby eliminating a distinct locus of moral agency. The latter view preserves an interior space—evident in practices of self-cultivation (xiu shen)—where the individual engages in moral discipline that is not reducible to role-performance.

Your task is to clarify whether Confucian and related East Asian frameworks actually commit to (i), or whether they are better understood as holding (ii), and whether Western interpreters have systematically misread relational ethics by conflating the two. In doing so, explain how the concept of self-cultivation can cohere with relational constitution without reverting to atomistic individualism, and identify the precise conceptual point at which Western liberalism and East Asian relationalism genuinely diverge rather than merely talking past each other.
