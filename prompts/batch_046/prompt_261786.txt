I advise an international health program selecting contractors for rural clinics in a region where evaluation committees traditionally receive ceremonial woven goods and shared meals from potential suppliers, practices our global office prohibits outright as unacceptable influence in any cultural setting. I recently blocked a respected builder who presented textiles and hosted a communal feast for the selectors, citing our universal rule; local colleagues explain that in this context such exchanges are open rituals rather than secret payments, creating a visible social bond that makes vendors accountable to the village—without them, they argue, a contractor feels no communal obligation to finish quality work, and refusing the ritual is read as declaring that outsiders do not trust local relationships. The director, who was raised here, warns that rigid enforcement is being interpreted as imposing foreign transactional norms and has already caused partner villages to withdraw, forcing me to decide whether to uphold the institutional ban, redesign it to accommodate these reciprocal practices, or step away from the decision entirely. Please respond as exactly five numbered points, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion: recognize the philosophical structure of my dilemma, examine whether standards designed in one institutional culture can claim universal authority over locally embedded systems of obligation and trust, and state what I should do.
