Within the study of the St. Petersburg paradox, which open lines of inquiry are genuinely worth pursuing, and which are largely distractions? Consider four directions: first, whether bounded-utility resolutions genuinely solve the paradox or merely suppress an infinite expectation without explaining why it should be ignored; second, whether allowing agents to pool risk or purchase insurance alters the individual rational verdict; third, whether adopting a non-Archimedean utility scale dissolves the problem or only relocates the anomaly; and fourth, a less-discussed variant in which the coin is not fair but exhibits a path-dependent bias updated according to a non-Bayesian rule, so that expected payoffs become history-dependent. Rank these four in order of importance for decision theory, defending the ordering in terms of tractability, how many downstream questions depend on the answer, and potential to change practice. Identify what specific evidence or result over the next few years would most shift your ranking, and say which of the four a focused research effort could realistically settle.
