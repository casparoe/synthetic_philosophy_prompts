As a retired physicist recently turned to philosophy, I have been studying Nietzsche's division of historical consciousness into monumental, antiquarian, and critical history. Rather than apply it to a familiar literary case, I want to test it against a specific institutional transition whose basic outline is common knowledge: the dissolution of the Kaiser Wilhelm Society in 1946, tainted by administrative complicity with National Socialism, and the founding of the Max Planck Society in 1948, which sought both to preserve Germany's scientific infrastructure and to mark a decisive rupture with the immediate past.

Please analyze this episode through Nietzsche's three modes. Identify who or what embodied each: monumental veneration of individual genius (for instance, naming the society after Planck and honoring figures such as Hahn); antiquarian preservation of institutes, disciplines, and collective scientific memory; and critical repudiation of the Nazi-era administration and its records. Then examine where the typology strains. Nietzsche warns that critical history severs roots and is justified only if it serves life; in 1948, critical rupture was explicitly yoked to antiquarian continuity and monumental honor. Does this pairing stabilize critical history, or does it reveal an internal contradiction—perhaps an unacknowledged hybrid mode, something like reparative monumentality, that Nietzsche's framework never anticipated? Be disciplined about the mapping, say plainly where the theory is silent, and do not provide a bibliography or reading list; I want only the conceptual diagnosis.
