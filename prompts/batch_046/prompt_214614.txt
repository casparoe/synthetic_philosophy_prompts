I serve on the clinical ethics committee of a university-affiliated palliative care center. Our medical director has proposed an "Equanimity-First Palliative Protocol" for terminal patients that draws explicitly on Mahayana Buddhist ethics—specifically Śāntideva's practice of parātma-parivartana (the exchange of self and other) and the Abhidharmic distinction among duḥkha-duḥkha (pain-in-pain), vipariṇāma-duḥkha (pain-in-change), and saṅkhāra-duḥkha (pain-in-conditioned-formations). The protocol recommends that clinicians prioritize guided contemplation of anattā (non-self) and, in cases where a patient expresses strong attachment to bodily comfort, reduce opioid analgesia below standard palliative levels on the grounds that clinging to pleasant vedanā perpetuates saṅkhāra-duḥkha and obstructs upekkhā (equanimity), which the tradition treats as a deeper form of relief than analgesia alone. A patient's family has objected that this substitutes a clinician-imposed spiritual framework for the consequentialist obligation to minimize experienced suffering; they further argue that Śāntideva's exchange was a voluntary bodhisattva self-sacrifice, not a license to withhold care from a non-consenting patient. Our legal counsel notes that the center's "reasonable accommodation" policy protects patients' religious practice but does not authorize clinical protocols derived from a tradition's soteriology. In particular, address whether applying parātma-parivartana in this clinical setting commits a category error by converting a voluntary ascetic practice into a prescriptive clinical duty, and whether the Abhidharmic taxonomy of suffering can justify reducing analgesia without implicitly rejecting the patient-welfare commitments that make consequentialist ethics plausible in medicine at all.
