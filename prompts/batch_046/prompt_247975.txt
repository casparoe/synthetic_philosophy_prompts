I have recently begun working on the ethics of long-term institutional eldercare, and in that context I have come to accept a reconstructed care-ethical position that I want to stress-test. The view holds that the normative core of caring is not mutual recognition, emotional attachment, or voluntary commitment, but rather what I call asymmetrical reciprocity: a moral obligation of care arises whenever one party's basic flourishing is structurally entangled with another's sustained attentive labor, even if that labor is unacknowledged, non-voluntary, and not reciprocated in kind. On this account, institutional caregivers bear obligations not because they have entered a contract or formed an attachment, but because the residents' flourishing is constitutively dependent on their labor; conversely, wild animals and distant ecosystems fall outside the scope not because they lack sentience, but because no human attentive labor is structurally entangled with their flourishing in the same way. I want an honest map of this position's exposure. What specific observations, boundary cases—such as fully automated care environments, or human dependency mediated entirely by non-human agents—formal considerations in resource-allocation or attention economics, and intuitions about obligation would genuinely count against it? What does it predict that contractualist or consequentialist rivals do not, and where does it risk collapsing into one of them? Which of these vulnerabilities would a committed defender concede as damaging, and which would they dismiss as presupposing a rival moral framework? Please rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and say whether the position is well insulated or whether that insulation is itself a problem. Finally, please state how confident you are in this assessment and single out the point about which you are least sure.
