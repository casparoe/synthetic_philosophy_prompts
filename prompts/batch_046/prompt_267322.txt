I've been in this studdy group all semester arguing about free will and I think we've accidentally built an argument that eats itself. We're trying to defend compatibilism without giving up determinism, but here's the chain we ended up with:

1. Free action requires an ability to do otherwise that holds the actual past and natural laws fixed.
2. If determinism is true, then holding past and laws fixed, every bodily movement is unavoidable.
3. So determinism wipes out that ability for every action, not just the ones we call unfree.
4. The compatibilist response is that being able to do otherwise just means one would have acted differently if one had wanted to - that's the conditional analysis of ability.
5. But if determinism is true, whether she wanted X was itself inevitable given conditions before she was born.
6. So the compatibilist version of being able to do otherwise becomes: if she had been in a different mental state, which was impossible given the actual past, then a different result would have followed.
7. That counterfactual is satisfied by sneezes and reflexes too - if my roommate had had a different neural state she wouldn't have sneezed during her vow ceremony.
8. So the criterion applies to literally every event that has a mental cause, free or reflexive.
9. Therefore compatibilism doesn't distinguish free actions from unfree ones; it either makes every physical event free or empties the distinction.
10. But we want to keep both determinism and the idea that some actions are genuinely free while sneezes aren't.
11. One of these steps has to break, but I'm not sure which.

Can you tell me which premise is actually the weak link, why it seemed plausible at first, and what rejecting it costs? I'd really like to keep 1 and 4 if possible since without them the debate feels like it changes the subject entirely, but tell me honestly if that's not workable.
