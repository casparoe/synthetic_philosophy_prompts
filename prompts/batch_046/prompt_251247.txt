I have been working through a problem set in my philosophy seminar on population ethics, and I am trying to formalize my professor's implicit axiological framework before our next session. Over the past weeks she has endorsed the following judgments without further elaboration: first, that a world containing ten billion people each living at welfare level 100 is preferable to a world containing one hundred million people each living at 200, which suggests she weights aggregate welfare rather than average welfare; second, that bringing a new person into existence at welfare 50 constitutes a moral improvement, whereas bringing one into existence at negative welfare does not, even if the total sum across all lives would increase; third, that a hypothetical world populated by 10^20 people with lives barely worth living—welfare just barely above zero—is worse than a world of roughly ten billion people living flourishing lives, indicating that she rejects the extreme version of the repugnant conclusion; fourth, that if forced to choose between one billion people at welfare 90 and two billion people at welfare 85, the larger population is the better outcome despite its lower average; and fifth, that there is no general moral obligation to maximize the number of happy lives if doing so imposes severe costs on those already alive.

We now face a novel policy scenario that extends beyond the standard textbook examples: a long-term cosmic governance proposal would use substrate-independent computation to create 10^30 digital minds over millennia, each maintaining a stable welfare level of 1—just barely positive—while leaving the welfare of all existing biological humans unchanged at approximately 100. Would my professor endorse this proposal? I am less interested in a simple affirmative or negative answer than in identifying the two or three competing axiological rules—perhaps critical-level utilitarianism with different thresholds, person-affecting restrictions on creation, or range-sensitive prioritarian frameworks—that could jointly accommodate her five earlier verdicts, explaining what each rule predicts about this digital-mind case, offering my best guess as to which rule she is actually running with reasons for preferring it, and specifying what further hypothetical comparison—such as a small population of digital minds at welfare 150 versus an astronomically larger population at welfare 2—would most clearly adjudicate between the remaining candidates.
