Here is an argument I worked up for a philosophy discussion group and now find suspicious, because if it holds together it seems to erase distinctions I want to keep.

(1) On Scheffler's afterlife conjecture, much of the value in our present activities depends on the expectation that humanity continues after us—carrying forward projects, culture, and relationships—rather than ending in extinction.

(2) If extinction is uniquely bad, it is not merely because of the deaths involved, but because it severs the "afterlife" that gives prior lives their meaning.

(3) But if present value depends on the existence of a future humanity, it must also depend on that future's expected quality and duration: a brief, suffering-ridden future offers far less sustaining "afterlife" than a long, flourishing one.

(4) So any action that marginally lowers expected future flourishing—say, emissions that will degrade living conditions centuries hence—diminishes the present value of our lives in exactly the same kind of way as actions that risk extinction, only by a smaller magnitude.

(5) Therefore ordinary harmful acts and existential-risk acts lie on a single scalar continuum of "afterlife damage," and we ought to treat them with proportionally severe urgency.

(6) Yet this collapses the distinction between catastrophic harm and everyday imprudence, implying that a routine carbon-intensive choice should be regarded with nearly the same moral horror as developing an unaligned superintelligence.

(7) I want to preserve the claim that extinction is specially bad in a way present deaths are not—so I am inclined to defend (1) and (2)—but I cannot accept that every minor reduction in future quality is an existential-level wrong.

Where exactly does the inference from (2) to (4) fail? Is there a hidden equivocation between the binary presence of an afterlife and its graded quality, or does the error lie in treating value-dependency as linear? If I must reject one premise, I would prefer to reject (4) rather than (1), but I want to understand what that rejection costs.
