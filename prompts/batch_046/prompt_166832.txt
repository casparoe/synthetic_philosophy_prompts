Construct a decision tree for the debate over norms of assertion, intended for use in a graduate epistemology seminar. The tree should take a respondent from the starting commitment—assertion is governed by an epistemic norm that binds the speaker, not merely a pragmatic or moral constraint—to exactly one of eight terminal positions. The eight leaves must be: (i) the strict knowledge norm, (ii) the reasonable-belief norm, (iii) the belief-only norm, (iv) the truth-only norm, (v) the justification-only norm, (vi) the contextualist norm in which required standards shift with practical stakes, (vii) the pluralist norm in which different speech-act types carry different norms, and (viii) the eliminativist view that there is no unified epistemic norm of assertion.

Every internal node must be a concrete case or scenario that a respondent can evaluate without knowing the philosophical labels. The tree must include branches for at least these five stress cases: (A) a speaker asserts p with justified true belief that is not knowledge, in a Gettier-style situation; (B) a speaker asserts p with full knowledge, but the audience is systematically misled about the speaker's evidential basis; (C) a speaker asserts p sincerely and with adequate justification, yet p is false and the speaker is blameless; (D) a speaker asserts p in an institutional role—e.g., expert testimony or scientific collaboration—where the speaker lacks personal belief or direct verification but holds institutional warrant; and (E) a speaker asserts p while in a state of second-order ignorance, unsure whether their first-order justification meets the relevant standard.

Build the tree from the logical space of answers rather than from a list of author names: first specify the combinations of answers the cases allow, then locate each of the eight named positions in that space. Identify any combination of answers that corresponds to no named position, and briefly diagnose whether it is unoccupied because it is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Explicitly flag any pair of leaves that your tree cannot genuinely separate, and indicate what further case would be needed to split them. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; the instrument should be usable by someone familiar with the debate but without access to external texts.
