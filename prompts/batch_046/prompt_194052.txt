I am preparing a long-form piece on the ethics of engineered well-being, and in an interview a science writer offered me a compact summary of David Pearce's hedonistic imperative that struck me as suspiciously tidy. According to this account, Pearce's position is essentially utilitarianism pushed to its biological limit: suffering is the worst feature of conscious existence, so we are ethically required to use genetic editing and designer psychopharmacology to eliminate negative affect entirely and replace it with uniform, high-intensity pleasure across all sentient life. The writer maintained that Pearce draws no meaningful ethical distinction between human and non-human welfare, which means wild ecosystems ought to be redesigned so that predators and prey alike inhabit states of constant euphoric reward, and that objections rooted in evolutionary necessity or the value of authenticity amount to little more than appeals to the naturalistic fallacy. The summary further claimed that Pearce aims specifically to overcome the hedonic treadmill — the evolutionary baseline of subtle, chronic dissatisfaction — and that this commitment implies an immediate, global research program in germline modification to enhance endogenous opioid signaling, on the grounds that any delay perpetuates avoidable harm.

Before I rely on this framing, I need a careful audit. Please work through the characterization sentence by sentence: which elements accurately reflect the abolitionist project's core commitments, and which subtly misrepresent Pearce's technical distinctions — for example, between gradients of bliss and uniform euphoria, or between the informational and affective dimensions of pain? I am particularly concerned that the summary may conflate Pearce's long-term speculative program with an urgent policy prescription, and that it presents his response to evolutionary objections as a single dismissive fallacy charge rather than a more nuanced argument about the contingency of our current reward architecture. Would a defender of the view accept that wild ecosystems should be engineered for constant euphoric reward, or does that formulation import a biological uniformity that Pearce's actual proposal avoids? And does the summary's conclusion — that opposition collapses into the naturalistic fallacy — survive once the misrepresentations about urgency, uniformity, and the scope of redesign are corrected? Give me the correspondence and the breaks explicitly, so I can decide whether to quote the summary with corrections or discard it.
