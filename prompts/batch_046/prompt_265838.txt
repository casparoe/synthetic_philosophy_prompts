I am giving the opening address tomorrow at a small interdisciplinary symposium honoring the retirement of Dr. Elena Vasquez, who spent forty years studying self-referential systems in cognition. The room will be split between neuroscientists and philosophers of mind who know Hofstadter's strange-loop account of the self intimately, and her family and former students who do not. The address must work structurally for both groups, not merely decorate a standard tribute.

What makes this personally urgent rather than ceremonial is that I have just served for six months as her temporary medical proxy and guardian after a stroke disrupted her autobiographical memory. Watching the pattern she called I fragment and partially reassemble through others' recognition has made Hofstadter's metaphor feel simultaneously indispensable and inadequate.

Please write a ten-minute address that takes the idea of the self as a strange loop — a symbol that refers back to itself through hierarchical patterns — and uses it to shape what I can and cannot claim about her presence in the room. Specifically, explore the tension between two readings: that the loop is substrate-independent and might persist in reconstructed narratives held by others, and that it requires an active first-person symbol that cannot be outsourced. Do not resolve this neatly.

To keep the idea from being a generic citation, inject the following conceptual twist: treat the loop not as enclosed within one skull, but as distributed across what I will call recognition nodes — the people who hold fragments of her patterns and feed them back to her. Ask whether this distribution dissolves the loop or extends it, and let that question determine the emotional arc of the speech. Do not include bibliographies, citations, or reading lists, and avoid ultra-niche formalisms that depend on precise technical recall. The speech should honor her work without pretending her current state is either tragic absence or seamless continuity, and it should feel like a real occasion rather than a lecture.
