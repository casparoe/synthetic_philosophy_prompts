Write the desiderata that any adequate philosophical account of personal identity must satisfy if it takes the Buddhist doctrine of anattā—radical no-self—seriously rather than treating it as a merely negative claim. Specify the phenomena the theory must explain, the cases it must get right, and the distinctions it must preserve, with the motivating consideration behind each requirement. At minimum, address: (1) continuity of moral responsibility across mental-stream configurations without a persisting substance or substrate; (2) the coherence of first-person reports of memory and anticipation without an owner of those mental states; (3) the distinction between conventional designation of a person and ultimate non-existence of that person, and how a theory avoids collapsing the conventional into the ultimate or vice versa; (4) the possibility of ethical progress and karmic continuity despite the doctrine of momentariness; and (5) the specific case of a practitioner who reports the dissolution of self-boundaries in deep meditative absorption yet retains deliberate ethical discrimination—what conditions of continuity, if any, must hold for this to be intelligible rather than contradictory, and whether any hidden self-substrate must be smuggled back in to preserve agency. Flag any pair of desiderata you suspect are jointly unsatisfiable, particularly between radical momentariness and the demands of practical moral attribution, and indicate which requirement you take to be non-negotiable and which might be relaxed if coherence demands it. Finally, briefly indicate how a reductionist Abhidharmic stream-theory, a conventionalist Madhyamaka two-truths approach, and a modern narrative-constructivist interpretation fare against these criteria, but keep the emphasis on the list of requirements and their motivating cases rather than on the scoring.
