I serve on the editorial board of an interdisciplinary journal that recently adopted an internal charter informed by neopragmatist and antirepresentationalist commitments—specifically, that theoretical vocabularies function as practical tools for coping rather than as mirrors of an independent reality, and that disputes between frameworks are often differences in purpose rather than straightforward errors of correspondence. Our copy-editors now face a recurring practical problem: submissions in neuroscience, climate science, and sociology routinely frame empirical claims in representationalist language—speaking of models that reveal the true structure of a phenomenon, of brain regions that are the seat of some mental function, or of social mechanisms that really cause an outcome—while our charter asks us to treat such framing as a heuristic choice rather than a metaphysical commitment. I need a reusable decision procedure, not a philosophical essay, that a senior copy-editor can run in about fifteen minutes on a manuscript passage before it reaches peer review. The procedure should present ordered questions, state what counts as a sufficient answer at each step, identify clear stop-points where substituting neutral language is safe, flag points where substitution changes the substantive claim, and mark explicitly where the call is one of editorial values rather than linguistic analysis. It should also specify what kinds of disagreements lie outside its scope.

Please construct this procedure and apply it to three submissions currently under review. The first is a cognitive neuroscience paper describing the anterior insula as the seat of subjective feeling and arguing that fMRI data directly reveal the neural reality of emotion. The second is a climate assessment report stating that ensemble projections show what the atmosphere will actually do over the next century. The third is a sociology article writing that the real cause of urban poverty is structural disinvestment rather than individual choice within an explicitly normative policy argument. For each case, show where the procedure stops with a concrete recommendation for revision or approval, where it hands the decision to the board because values are in play, and whether it permits the author’s original language. Include at least one case where the procedure yields an answer—perhaps allowing representationalist phrasing—that our board will likely find uncomfortable, and explain precisely why the procedure allows it despite the charter. Finally, state exactly what this procedure cannot do: for instance, whether it can adjudicate between genuinely incommensurable research programs, or whether it can distinguish an author’s deliberate rhetorical strategy from an unexamined metaphysical assumption. Constraints are that the editor has no philosophy training beyond this charter, must decide quickly, and needs the board’s sign-off only on value-laden cases.
