A friend presented me with the following chain of reasoning about coherent extrapolated volition and asked me to locate the error. I want to keep steps (1) and (3) if possible, but I am willing to abandon others.

(1) Coherent extrapolated volition (CEV) defines an agent's true interests as the preferences that agent would endorse after achieving greater knowledge, cognitive coherence, and empathetic understanding.

(2) The idealization process strips away biases that depend solely on contingent limitations—ignorance, weakness of will, or arbitrary indexical attachments.

(3) One such bias is temporal discounting: valuing present welfare more than future welfare merely because it is nearer in time. A fully idealized agent therefore weights future persons according to their actual moral significance, not their temporal distance.

(4) By the same reasoning, spatial distance and social proximity are also indexical properties without intrinsic moral weight; an idealized agent does not favor those near her in space or in her social network.

(5) So far the scope of concern extends to all actually existing persons, regardless of where or when they live.

(6) But if the criterion for inclusion is "not being excluded by an arbitrary indexical," then the boundary between actual and merely possible persons is also an indexical boundary—actuality is just the property of being in this world rather than another.

(7) Consequently, a fully coherent CEV must extend equal consideration to all possible minds across modal space, weighted only by the probability of their existence and the intensity of their welfare.

(8) This implies that my present moral obligations are dominated by long-range, high-leverage actions—such as directing nearly all resources toward existential-risk mitigation—because the expected number of future possible minds dwarfs the present population.

(9) Yet this conclusion seems to dissolve every local, particular obligation—to family, to community, to present projects—into a single, totalizing calculus, which conflicts with the intuition that morality permits agents to lead meaningful lives anchored in specific relationships.

(10) Therefore, the move from (5) to (7) must contain a hidden slide.

The friend insists the flaw is not in (6) but in the inference from (7) to (8)—that even if possible minds matter, probability-weighting does not necessarily swamp present duties. I think the deeper problem is elsewhere. Which step should be rejected? Explain precisely why it looked plausible, what kind of error it involves—whether an equivocation on "arbitrary," a scope shift in quantification, or a modal slide—and what each of the main alternatives costs: giving up (2), giving up (6), or giving up the inference in (8).
