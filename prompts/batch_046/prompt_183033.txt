I have been co-teaching an interdisciplinary seminar on long-term societal change, and over the past month the discussions have made the concept of "collapse" feel personally unavoidable. We have been examining the Bronze Age palace economies of the eastern Mediterranean, the Classic Maya abandonment of the southern lowlands, and the ecological transformation of Rapa Nui—cases traditionally narrated as failures of governance or environmental management. Yet the more I read in archaeology and paleoclimatology, the more it seems that these societies did not so much fall as reconfigure: trade networks dispersed rather than vanished, populations relocated rather than disappeared, and monumental practices shifted rather than ceased. The tension I am left with is whether "collapse" names an objective rupture or merely a change that later observers, attached to particular institutional forms, fail to recognize as continuity.

For our final session, I would like to close with a poem that holds this tension open rather than resolving it into either tragedy or complacency. I am drawn to the sestina, because its rotating end-words seem structurally suited to a process that returns upon itself in altered form rather than ending. Please write a sestina using these six end-words in this order for the first stanza: fall, stone, change, root, dust, remain. In the second through fifth stanzas, rotate them according to the standard sestina pattern, so that the sixth word of one stanza becomes the first of the next, with the internal sequence cycling through positions 6-1-5-2-4-3. The envoi should bring together all six words, ideally with fall and remain in the final lines, to ask whether the two are distinguishable after all.

The poem should treat the three cases with enough specificity that a specialist would recognize them—the palace bureaucracies of the Late Bronze Age, the cessation of monument-building in the Maya lowlands, the deforestation and continued moai carving on Rapa Nui—without treating any of them as a simple morality tale. Rather than praising resilience or lamenting loss, let the form do the work: the repetition and permutation of the end-words should suggest that what looks like an ending is also a redistribution, and that collapse might be the name we give to a root that has only moved. The tone should be contemplative, not sermonizing; I want the seminar participants to leave with the conceptual question intact, feeling that the poem has given shape to an ambiguity they have been living with all semester.
