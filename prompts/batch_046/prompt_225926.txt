I'm involved in drafting ethical protocols for a technically plausible clinical framework governing long-term neuroprosthetic trials, in which a patient's biological neural tissue is incrementally replaced with synthetic analogues over months or years, while the patient remains conscious and retains legal personhood throughout the transition. Within these proposed guidelines, there is a specific institutional safeguard: the trial must be suspended and the post-replacement subject administratively reclassified—potentially assigned new legal standing—if sustained discontinuities emerge in autobiographical narrative, for instance, if the patient consistently fails to recognize prior personal records or describes themselves as a successor entity rather than a direct continuation. Curiously, the same regulatory framework treats rapid whole-brain emulation or destructive scanning-based uploading as falling entirely outside this safeguard, categorizing such procedures as replication rather than preservation of identity, and therefore not requiring narrative-continuity monitoring at all.

What implicit philosophical theory of personal identity does this institutional distinction presuppose? Does the practice commit to a psychological-continuity account modified for synthetic substrates, to a narrative-constitution view of the self, or to a biological-continuity criterion that has been tacitly abandoned in the gradual case but inconsistently retained elsewhere? Reconstruct the implicit framework as precisely as you can, assess whether the boundary drawn between gradual replacement and instantaneous uploading is philosophically defensible or merely a bureaucratic artifact designed to manage liability, and then specify what clinical and legal practices would need to be redesigned if we replaced this underlying view with a strict substrate-independent functionalist criterion—one that identifies persistence solely with the uninterrupted operation of a particular cognitive architecture, regardless of narrative coherence, the speed of substrate transition, or the presence of autobiographical rupture.
