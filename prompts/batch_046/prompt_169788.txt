Take the rapid adoption and subsequent collapse of recovered-memory therapy in American clinical psychology—roughly 1980 to 1995—as a case study in information cascades and rational herding. In the first phase, therapists adopted hypnosis and suggestive interviewing after observing peers report dramatic breakthroughs, despite noisy private clinical evidence and weak external corroboration; in the second, the cascade reversed as false-memory research and prominent legal reversals made non-adoption observable. Apply the standard rational-herding framework: identify the actors (clinicians, patients, expert reviewers, institutions), the private signals versus observable actions, and the payoff structures. Then answer in both directions. First, does the theory predict that adoption was individually rational, and does it explain the velocity of diffusion? Second, where does it fail—does null evidence naturally unwind a cascade, or does the collapse require an exogenous institutional shock that the model treats as outside its scope? Third, since this is an expert epistemic community rather than a market, do reputational and moral payoffs alter the conditions for initiation and collapse, and does this extend or undermine the theory's generality? Finish by saying what the episode reveals about applying cascade logic where the herded "good" is truth rather than profit.
