I want to press the statue-and-clay case in a direction that connects material constitution to artifact ontology, because I think there's an unresolved tension that standard treatments gloss over. Here is my reasoning, and I want you to identify the exact misstep. At any time t, the statue and the clay share every proper part arranged in the same spatial configuration. If we apply Leibniz's law without restriction, they must be identical. To block this, constitution theorists invoke modal differences: the clay can survive being squashed into a ball, while the statue cannot. But I think this move confuses ontological persistence with our sorting practices. The scattered matter survives as "clay" only because we continue to apply that sortal; there is no independent object with different persistence conditions. So there is never more than one material entity present, and "the statue" is just a temporary form-phase of the clay, analogous to "being a teenager" being a phase of a person. The problem is that this seems to make artifacts ontologically second-rate—if squashing destroys only a phase, not a distinct object, then art vandalism is metaphysically harmless, like forcing someone out of adolescence. Is my error in treating modal properties as sortal-dependent rather than intrinsic, in assuming that co-located entities must have distinct persistence conditions, or in inferring that being a "phase" entails lacking genuine reality? Take a definite stand: either the statue is genuinely distinct from the clay and you should show how to avoid both the Leibniz contradiction and the collapse into mere phase-descriptions, or you should defend the phase-view and explain why our intuition that vandalism destroys something real is systematically mistaken—but do not just list options; defend the position that survives scrutiny and show precisely where my premises fail. I also want you to say whether a better analogy than "phase of matter" is available for understanding how artifacts relate to their substrates, and whether that analogy saves or undermines the intuition about destructive harm.
