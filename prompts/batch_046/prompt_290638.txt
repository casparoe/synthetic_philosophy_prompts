You are grading a graduate seminar exam in twentieth-century philosophy. The exam question reads: Frank Ramsey maintained that the question "What is the meaning of life?" is not a pseudo-question in the logical-positivist sense, but requires philosophical reconstruction. Explain Ramsey's reconstruction in terms of objective value and rational preference ordering, paying particular attention to his distinction between first-order commitments to intrinsic goods and second-order coherence conditions on those commitments. Does Ramsey hold that transcendental or metaphysical justification is necessary for a life to count as meaningful? Justify your answer with reference to the role of coherence in his account.

Then identify the subtle errors: check whether the student conflates coherence as an epistemic or structural condition with coherence as the constitutive ground of value, and check whether the student correctly represents Ramsey's reason for rejecting transcendental justification (rooted in the internal accessibility of objective goods within experience) or mischaracterizes it as deriving from a blanket empiricism that rejects all metaphysical hypotheses as unnecessary. Student answer:
Ramsey does not treat the meaning-of-life question as meaningless, unlike Carnap or the early Wittgenstein. Instead, he reconstructs it as asking whether a particular life is organized around goods that are objectively valuable. For Ramsey, value is not reducible to desire or pleasure; it is an objective feature of activities such as knowledge, friendship, and aesthetic appreciation. These function as first-order intrinsic goods.

But a life is not meaningful merely by containing these goods in aggregate. Ramsey adds a second-order coherence condition: the agent's preferences among these goods must form a rationally ordered structure, such that local commitments do not undermine the global framework. This coherence is not merely psychological consistency; it is a normative requirement that the preference ordering remain stable under rational reflection. Importantly, this condition does not require universalizability in the Kantian sense—Ramsey applies universalizability to ethical judgments, not to life-plans—though he does maintain that particular ethical judgments must themselves be universalizable.

Because value is objective, a life can participate in meaning without transcendental backing. One does not need God, a cosmic telos, or metaphysical absolutes to justify one's commitments, because the goods themselves are already accessible within ordinary experience. The coherence of one's preference structure provides all the justification that is needed; any further appeal to the transcendent is redundant. This rejection of transcendental justification follows directly from Ramsey's broader empiricism, which treats metaphysical entities as unnecessary hypotheses in the manner of Ockham's razor.
