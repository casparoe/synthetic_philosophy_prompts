I have just spent the evening in a protracted argument with a friend over whether destroying wild habitats can ever reduce net animal suffering by preventing overpopulation, starvation, and disease, and I now need to turn that disagreement into something I can actually use at work. I serve as a senior planning officer for a regional land-use authority; I review development proposals that fragment or destroy ecosystems roughly twice a month, and I must submit a welfare-impact assessment within two hours of each review, signed off by both an ecologist and an ethics adviser. I want a reusable decision procedure—ordered questions, stopping conditions, and explicit markers where empirical fact ends and value judgement begins—derived from the philosophical debate on wild-animal suffering and ecosystem welfare, but reduced to what a non-specialist can apply without searching literature. The procedure should incorporate two distinctions that kept arising in our argument: first, between occurrence-weighted suffering (actual present distress in existing individuals) and capacity-weighted suffering (potential future distress across replacement generations); second, the trophic replacement problem, whereby reducing herbivore habitat may lower herbivore suffering but increase predator, parasite, or scavenger suffering elsewhere in the network. For each step, specify what evidence suffices, where the procedure terminates with a negative determination, and where it must defer to human judgement because no empirical resolution exists. Apply the procedure to three cases: draining a wetland to prevent recurrent waterfowl botulism; clearing woodland edge to reduce deer density ahead of winter starvation; and constructing a solar farm on invertebrate-rich grassland that eliminates adjacent agricultural pesticide use. Include at least one case where the procedure produces a recommendation that conflicts with common conservation intuition, and clearly state what the procedure cannot do—especially regarding cross-taxa sentience comparisons and long-term evolutionary dynamics. Please write the answer in American English.
