Consider the following argument drawn from Descartes' correspondence with Henry More, where he answers the charge that treating animals as automata denies them all inner life. He grants that beasts display complex behaviors—fleeing harm, seeking nourishment, nurturing offspring—but insists these can be fully explained by the mechanical disposition of organs, analogous to the movements of a clock driven by weights and springs. He then adds that even if one conceded some form of sensory reception in animals, this would not establish genuine cognition, because true thought requires not merely receiving impressions but reflectively grasping them as belonging to oneself. From this he concludes that there is no reason to attribute to animals anything beyond corporeal machinery.

I am trying to determine whether this passage supports a narrower interpretive claim that has recently been floated: that Descartes denies animals not phenomenal consciousness in general, but only a specific cognitive architecture—namely, the capacity for indexical self-reference, or representing a state as belonging to oneself. On this reading, an animal might have unreflective sensations without possessing the conceptual machinery to own them. Does Descartes' appeal to clockwork mechanism and to the requirements of genuine cognition commit him only to the absence of self-referential representation, or does it entail the stronger thesis that there is literally nothing it is like to be an animal? Please help me understand how to read the passage on this point, whether Descartes himself draws any distinction between lacking self-reference and lacking consciousness altogether, and which interpretation makes better sense of his comparison to mechanical devices.
