I'm composing a novel set in the years following the Tambora eruption of 1815, and during a residency I encountered a fellow writer who summarized what she called the new materialist history of climate and disease. According to her account, recent scholarship demands that historians treat volcanoes and pathogens not as passive backdrops but as genuine historical agents possessing directed intentions—forces that actively drive revolutions, famine, and imperial collapse. She insisted that the only intellectual danger is environmental determinism, which she defined as the error of stripping these forces of agency entirely, reducing them to inert conditions. From this she concluded that once we admit Tambora caused the catastrophic harvest failures and the subsequent unrest, we are compelled to attribute to the climate a form of will; otherwise, she argued, we have merely relapsed into determinism by treating the volcano as a dead mechanism.

Her formulation has an almost literary appeal—treating the eruption as a character with motives—which is precisely why I want to check its philosophical footing before I build a narrative around it. Where does her summary confuse causal efficacy with intentional agency? Does granting volcanoes or germs a role as historical actors truly require attributing will to them, and is her characterization of environmental determinism accurate, or does it misrepresent that position as denying causation rather than overclaiming it? I want to know which parts of her account survive careful scrutiny, whether her conclusion about Tambora follows from anything sound, and exactly where the line falls between recognizing non-human forces as actors and falling into the determinism she warns against—without asking for a bibliography of the specific monographs she had in mind.
