Consider the institutional practice—found in classical humanist education, religious formation, and modern professional-school ethics curricula—of using extended biography as a primary vehicle for moral learning. The practice takes several forms: the study of Plutarch's paired lives in Renaissance schools, the devotional reading of saints' lives in Catholic formation, and, more recently, the assignment of "flawed exemplar" biographies in medical and legal ethics, where students study lives that combine serious moral failure with professional achievement in order not to imitate the subject but to diagnose the conditions under which virtue collapses. Across these variants, sustained narrative immersion in a single life is treated as a distinctive mode of moral education, different from case analysis, rule-learning, or abstract argument.

What philosophical commitments about moral knowledge, character, and the relation between narrative and virtue does this family of practices presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory: does treating biography as pedagogy require that moral knowledge is particular and embodied rather than universal and propositional? Is the practice compatible with a Kantian model of moral education centered on autonomy and universal law, or does it depend on an Aristotelian framework of habituation and practical wisdom? What does the shift from devotional hagiography—which suppresses failure and invites identification—to the modern "diagnostic" biography—which highlights failure and demands critical distance—reveal about whether the tradition rests on one consistent view or on rival ones? Are Plutarch's comparative structure, which seems to invite judgment between lives, and hagiography's demand for admiration philosophically compatible within the same pedagogical logic? Finally, distinguish what this practice strictly requires from what it merely accommodates: could a coherent version of narrative moral learning be built on a non-virtue-theoretic foundation, or is exemplarism inescapable? And if the underlying theory were replaced—say, by a view that moral formation happens only through rule-governed reasoning or through direct participation in practices—what specific elements of the biographical curriculum would have to change or be abandoned?
