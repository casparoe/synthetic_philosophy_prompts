I am a retired experimental physicist who, over the past several years, has turned to philosophy of religion and moral theology. I want to avoid textbook summaries and instead produce a rigorous, good-faith adversarial-collaboration document on Augustine’s ethics of language—specifically, on lying. Please draft the joint statement that two opposing interpretive camps could both sign.

Name the parties precisely:
- Party A, the Metaphysical Absolutists, hold that Augustine’s writings establish an exceptionless prohibition on false assertion, grounded in his metaphysics of language as ordered to divine truth and in his psychology of the will.
- Party B, the Epistemic-Contract Contextualists, hold that Augustine’s prohibition is indexed to malicious intent and to violations of an implicit epistemic contract between speaker and listener, thereby allowing mental reservation or strategic omission when literal disclosure would cause grave moral harm.

The document should contain the following, formatted as a numbered agreed-claims section followed by clearly demarcated disagreement and crux sections:

1. Agreed claims both parties can sign. Include: (a) the textual loci in Augustine’s corpus where mendacity is treated (without citing editions or page numbers); (b) the core metaphysical claim that human speech participates in divine veritas and is naturally ordered to manifest truth; (c) the psychological claim that lying corrupts the speaker’s interior disposition regardless of external outcome; (d) the shared acknowledgment that Augustine distinguishes falsehood spoken in jest or pedagogy from falsehood spoken with intent to deceive; and (e) a novel analytical framing both sides accept for heuristic purposes—namely, that every speech act establishes an implicit epistemic contract specifying what the speaker warrants and what the listener is entitled to expect, analogous to calibration protocols in empirical measurement. Both sides should agree this framing is useful even if they disagree on its normative weight.

2. Precise formulations of residual disagreement, each phrased in wording that its own party would accept. Articulate the dispute not only about Augustine’s intent but about the ontology of deception: is lying a privation in the utterance itself, independent of consequence, or is it a relational disorder in the contract between speaker and audience?

3. The cruxes—sub-questions such that resolving them would move at least one party. I take there to be at least two:
   (a) Whether Augustine’s notion of duplex cor applies only to explicit false assertions or extends to strategic omissions in high-stakes communicative contexts.
   (b) Whether the moral evaluation of a deceptive utterance depends solely on the speaker’s interior will-to-deceive, or also on whether the listener’s epistemic entitlement has been violated.

4. For each crux, present a concrete case or argument both parties agree bears on it, with each side’s prediction recorded in advance. Do not use standard textbook examples. Instead, employ this shared niche test case: a clinical researcher running a trial for a fatal disease possesses noisy preliminary data suggesting the treatment is ineffective, but premature disclosure could cause patients to abandon the trial and lose access to palliative care. The researcher states, “The trial continues and data are being analyzed”—a technically true utterance—while deliberately omitting the preliminary negative finding, knowing patients will interpret the statement as hopeful news. Both sides should treat this as structurally analogous to the dilemmas Augustine considers. Each side should record in advance: (i) whether this counts as a lie under Augustine’s definition; (ii) whether the speaker’s will is thereby corrupted; and (iii) whether the epistemic contract with the patients has been violated.

5. A brief section identifying any purely verbal disagreements—disputes that dissolve once terms are precisely defined—and explaining which portion of the dispute remains substantive.

Please paraphrase Augustine’s positions without verbatim quotation marks; do not include bibliographies, reading lists, citation requests, or references to specific editions. The deliverable should be constructible by reasoning alone, without web access.
