After spending the last year assisting a close relative through repeated disability-benefits reassessments—encounters in which the abstract rhetoric of the undeserving poor became administratively concrete—I can no longer read the philosophy of welfare conditionality with detachment. I am not asking for a bibliography, citation list, or reading plan; I need a defended agenda for conceptual work.

Within the normative and epistemic architecture of the modern welfare state—specifically the logic of workfare, the concept of benefit fraud, and the moral ontology of deservingness—which open questions or lines of inquiry are genuinely upstream of others? I want a dependency-aware prioritization for roughly three years of focused philosophical work, not a survey.

Please treat the following as interdependent nodes rather than separate literatures. First, the ontological priority problem: is desert in welfare contexts a moral property that claimants possess prior to bureaucratic encounter, or is it an artifact generated by compliance rituals? Second, the epistemic structure of fraud: are state evidentiary practices for detecting fraud structurally biased toward false positives, and does the concept of fraud collapse into circular non-compliance with administratively arbitrary conditions? Third, the design-level framing: does conditionality function primarily as an economic incentive mechanism or as a ritual of moral membership, and does resolving this change normative justification?

For each, defend its rank: which resolution unlocks answers to the others; which is tractable for conceptual philosophy rather than empirical sociology; and which, if settled, would actually alter how a policy designer should think about conditionality rather than merely refining vocabulary. I am particularly interested in work that sits at the boundary of moral philosophy and the philosophy of bureaucracy—questions about administrative burden, epistemic injustice in assessment procedures, and the teleology of verification rituals—that do not reduce cleanly to welfare economics or conventional political theory.

Be explicit about dependencies that fail: if resolving the ontological priority problem has little downstream effect on fraud-detection design, name that. Be explicit about what you deprioritize and why. What should matter most, and why does the rest matter less?
