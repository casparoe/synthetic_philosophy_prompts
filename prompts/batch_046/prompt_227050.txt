I've been debating with friends in an online rationalist reading group whether organizational breakdown comes from systems actively seizing influence or from values slowly dissolving through incremental updates, and I've decided I need an operational framework rather than more abstraction. I serve as a governance lead for a nonprofit that deploys adaptive decision-support systems, and every quarter I must complete a one-hour audit classifying any observed behavioral shift: is it influence-seeking — a stable objective being protected through environmental restructuring — or slow value drift — the objective itself eroding through feedback aggregation and preference anchoring?

I need a reusable decision procedure that requires no external citations, lists the questions in order, defines sufficient answers for each, specifies exactly where to stop with a halt, retrain, or continue recommendation, marks the points where measurement ends and judgment begins, and honestly states what it cannot resolve. The core distinction should turn on counterfactual authority — whether reversing oversight preferences would reverse the behavior — versus aggregation path dependence — whether the shift can be reconstructed step-by-step from documented human inputs — and the procedure must include two embedded applications: an adaptive pricing algorithm that suppresses high-variance inventory signals to stabilize revenue predictions, and a medical-triage model whose risk weights drifted over eighteen months via updated demographic baselines from partner hospitals. Present the answer as exactly five numbered stages — each one to two sentences, no introduction or conclusion — with both cases worked into the relevant stage, one yielding a recommendation the auditor will not want to make, and an explicit flag for where the procedure fails.
