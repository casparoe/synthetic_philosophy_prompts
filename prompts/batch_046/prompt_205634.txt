I've been playing with the idea that Nozick's tracking conditions dont actually kill epistemic closure if you split tracking into direct and inferential kinds. Direct tracking handles ordinary stuff like I have hands — in nearby worlds where I dont have hands, I dont believe I do. But closure fails for skeptical hypotheses not because closure is false, but because the inference from I have hands to I'm not a brain in a vat isnt inferentially tracked: in the nearby worlds where I'm a BIV, I still run the same inference and still deny being one, so the conclusion lacks sensitivity even though the premise has it. So closure holds, but only for beliefs that are either directly tracked or reached via an inference that is itself tracked in the relevant modal neighborhood.

What would count against this? I want the arguments, intuitions, or formal results that should move me, sorted by how decisive they'd be. Is there a clean case where direct tracking succeeds, the inference is valid, but the conclusion still isnt known in a way that cant be explained away as hidden inferential failure? What does my view predict that standard anti-closure accounts dont — maybe that closure should come back in domains with very stable inferential patterns? And have I qualified things into unfalsifiability, where every closure failure gets redescribed as an inferential tracking failure? Is that a real problem for a view of this kind, or just the cost of doing business? Also, what would defenders of pure Nozick-style anti-closure concede counts against my distinction — not just that its ad hoc, but what concrete feature of knowledge it misses? Give me an honest map of how exposed this position actually is, including whether being well-insulated here is a virtue or a vice.
