I am a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, currently auditing graduate seminars in philosophy of mind and cognitive science. I am preparing a proposal for a four-month independent study fellowship—roughly one semester—focused on Daniel Dennett’s taxonomy of minds: Darwinian, Skinnerian, Popperian, and Gregorian creatures. The standard account treats the Gregorian tier as essentially defined by linguistic capacity and the cultural transmission of cognitive tools, but I think this collapses two achievements that should be kept apart. One is an internal representational architecture—something like compositional syntax or explicit mental simulation. The other is structural participation in an external, accumulating, error-correcting cultural repository, analogous in software engineering to a distributed version-control system in which no single node holds the full codebase, yet every node can commit, branch, and debug within a shared history that outlives any contributor. My precise question is whether Gregorian cognition can be decomposed into these two dimensions—representational format and repository-membership—and whether that decomposition reveals that some collective or artificial systems might qualify as Gregorian in the repository sense without meeting the linguistic conditions traditionally required.

Existing answers leave this open because Dennett’s framing emphasizes language and cultural inheritance without sharply distinguishing internal cognitive tools from external institutional structures, while recent work in distributed cognition tends to assume either that language is necessary for Gregorian status or that collective representation alone is sufficient. The debate stalls on ambiguous borderline cases: social insect colonies that maintain collective behavioral patterns across generations, and large-scale synthetic language systems that draw on massive cultural corpora without clearly possessing internal compositional syntax. Because the disagreement is conceptual rather than empirical, progress does not require new laboratory data; it requires clarifying whether Gregorian denotes a unified kind or a cluster concept with separable dimensions, and what that clarification costs.

My method is analytical and comparative, structured over four months. Month one formalizes the selection mechanisms distinguishing the four kinds—evolutionary hard-wiring, operant conditioning, mental simulation, and cultural inheritance—specifying the criteria that move a mind from one tier to the next. Month two develops the repository-participation framework using conceptual resources from distributed software design—modularity, version history, and collective error correction—to articulate what it means for a mind to be embedded in an accumulating cultural codebase without necessarily internalizing its syntax. Month three applies this bifurcated framework to borderline cases, testing whether the distinction holds or collapses into circular appeals to language. Month four produces a thirty-page deliverable that either defends a split Gregorian category or argues for its unitary collapse, with explicit failure conditions: if every case of repository-participation necessarily presupposes compositional internal representation, or if the distinction cannot be maintained without importing empirical claims about specific biological or artificial systems, then the project fails and I will report that Gregorian status is genuinely indivisible. I am not requesting a bibliography or citation review; I will supply references myself. The intended deliverable is useful to philosophers assessing whether cultural-cognitive status depends on syntax, social structure, or both, and whether that assessment changes how we classify minds that are not biologically human.
