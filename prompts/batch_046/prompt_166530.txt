Consider the bifurcated institutional practice that governs international transitional justice and public commemoration. On one side, legal instruments—international criminal statutes and domestic war-crimes frameworks—classify genocide, crimes against humanity, and war crimes as categorically distinct offenses, yet assign them substantially overlapping sentencing ranges and refuse any formal hierarchy of severity among them. On the other side, commemorative funding bodies, museum accreditation boards, and educational-mandate committees routinely allocate resources, curricular time, and investigative priority according to an implicit ordinal scale that tracks the "genocide" designation far more closely than it tracks documented mortality, duration of suffering, or the scale of institutional intent. Simultaneously, victim-reparations protocols within these same institutions explicitly prohibit cross-conflict comparisons of suffering, insisting that harm is context-bound and axiologically incommensurable across different atrocities. What implicit theory of moral comparison does this pattern presuppose? Reconstruct the underlying axiological framework: must it hold that genocide is a different kind of evil rather than a greater magnitude—perhaps requiring a categorical essentialism about moral kinds—or can it be explained by a multidimensional "moral topology" in which scale, intent, and institutional complicity are non-aggregable dimensions that license ordinal ranking in one domain but incommensurability in another? Is the practice philosophically coherent, or does the prohibition on comparative suffering in reparations sit in tension with the implicit ranking in commemorative investment? Distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what it merely accommodates: identify at least one rival account—such as a strict cardinal consequentialist metric or a deontological uniqueness thesis—that could license the legal categorization without the commemorative hierarchy, or the hierarchy without the reparations prohibition. Finally, specify what a transitional-justice system actually built on your reconstructed theory would have to alter: would it abandon categorical legal distinctions, introduce explicit cardinal measures of harm, redistribute memorial resources according to a different dimensional weighting, or restructure reparations protocols to permit—indeed require—cross-conflict comparison?
