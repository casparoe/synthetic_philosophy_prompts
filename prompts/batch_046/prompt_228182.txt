After examining a sediment core taken near my hometown that reveals a discrete anthropogenic layer—simultaneous spikes in synthetic microfiber density and cesium-137—dated to the exact year of my grandmother’s birth, I have become personally concerned with the question of how humanity should demarcate and authorize its own geological epoch. I therefore request an adversarial-collaboration document between two opposing camps that have argued past each other on this subject.

Camp A comprises geological formalists who maintain that the Anthropocene, if it is to function as a historical period at all, must be anchored to a globally synchronous, physically detectable stratigraphic marker—such as the mid-twentieth-century radionuclide or synthetic-polymer horizon—and formally ratified through the procedural norms of international stratigraphic bodies. Camp B comprises socio-historical pluralists who maintain that the Anthropocene describes a heterogeneous cluster of regionally asynchronous human-earth transitions, that no single physical horizon can capture its global meaning, and that fixing any universal start date is an exercise in political authority rather than empirical discovery.

Please produce the joint statement both camps could sign. Format it precisely as follows.

First, a numbered section of agreed claims. These should include: shared acknowledgments about what the stratigraphic and archival records show regarding regional variation; agreement on the institutional mechanisms currently available for epoch designation; agreement that intergenerational equity is ethically salient to this debate; and explicit adoption of the conceptual framing I term the inheritance horizon—the principle that any boundary criterion should be evaluated partly by whether it obscures or illuminates the differential ecological burdens transmitted across generations.

Second, a disagreements table. For Camp A and Camp B respectively, state in wording each camp would accept: (1) whether global physical synchrony is a necessary condition for the Anthropocene to count as a meaningful historical period; (2) who holds legitimate authority to decide the boundary—international scientific commissions, democratic or intergovernmental deliberation, or a hybrid process; and (3) whether the inheritance horizon should operate as a binding constraint on marker selection or should be rejected as a normative intrusion into descriptive geology.

Third, identify the cruxes—those sub-questions such that resolving them would cause at least one camp to revise its position. I take the central cruxes to be: (i) whether historical periodization can tolerate functionally equivalent but physically asynchronous regional transitions, or whether it requires a single globally coincident horizon; and (ii) whether applying the inheritance horizon improves descriptive accuracy by revealing hidden generational asymmetries, or instead corrupts it by substituting moral accounting for stratigraphic observation.

Fourth, for each crux specify one concrete comparative case that both camps agree is relevant, recording in advance each camp’s prediction about what that case demonstrates. For crux (i), compare the well-documented 1950s plutonium-239 fallout peak in Northern Hemisphere lacustrine sediments with the later, regionally staggered appearance of synthetic microplastics in Southern Hemisphere marine archives, predicting whether asynchronous arrival undermines unified periodization. For crux (ii), compare the generational burden distributions implied by selecting a mid-twentieth-century physical marker versus selecting an early-modern marker tied to colonial biosphere exchange, predicting whether the redistribution of recognized ecological responsibility across generations reflects a genuine descriptive difference or merely an ethical reframing.

The document should contain no requests for external citations, bibliographies, or reading lists; it should be composed entirely from claims and predictions that both parties can evaluate without recourse to sources outside the collaboration itself.
