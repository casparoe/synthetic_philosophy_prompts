I am preparing a single fifty-minute seminar for first-year graduate students in public policy — none with prior philosophy training — on the disagreement between William James's defense of the will to believe and W.K. Clifford's evidentialist ethics of belief. They arrive assuming that belief is a private mental state carrying no moral weight. I need a teaching plan rather than the lecture itself: first, the dependency order in which the conceptual pieces must be introduced — Clifford's universal evidential norm, James's taxonomy of live, forced, and momentous options, and the precise point at which the two positions genuinely conflict rather than talk past each other — with explicit justification for why each step must precede the next; second, the one concrete carrying scenario that should thread through the entire session, which must be modern, decision-pressured, and capable of illuminating both philosophers without relying on James's religious cases or Clifford's shipowner, together with a specification of exactly what features this scenario must possess and what it must lack so that the disagreement does not collapse; third, a mapped sequence of the misconceptions this audience will predictably generate or import — for instance, that James licenses wishful thinking, that Clifford demands Cartesian certainty, or that "momentous" simply means "personally important" — and the precise point in the explanatory arc where each misconception should be intercepted and dissolved; fourth, one diagnostic question I can pose at the end that will reveal whether the students have grasped the structural disagreement rather than merely chosen a side; and fifth, an explicit statement of what should be deliberately left out at this level — including standard biographical details, publication dates, full essay titles, and textual apparatus — with an acknowledgment that these omissions are intentional. Do not provide reading lists, citations, or bibliographic recommendations.
