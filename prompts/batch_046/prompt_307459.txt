I have just finished a long, fractious argument with a friend about whether victimhood culture represents a genuine moral ontology or merely a degraded form of dignity culture, and I want to pressure-test the position I ended up taking. I hold that victimhood culture is not just dignity culture corrupted by grievance politics, but a structurally distinct framework that eliminates the category of honorable failure — the possibility that one can suffer a serious wrong, retain full moral agency, and demand redress without being constituted by victim status. By severing the link between agency and recognition that both honor and dignity cultures preserve, albeit in opposite directions, victimhood culture renders reciprocal moral recognition unstable: it collapses the distinction between being wronged and being defined by the wrong, and thereby loses the capacity to distinguish legitimate grievance from ontological identity.

What would count against this? I want the evidence, arguments, and conceptual distinctions that should move me, ranked by decisiveness. Are there institutional designs or cross-cultural practices — perhaps in restorative justice, in certain religious traditions of suffering, or in post-conflict reconciliation — where victimhood is acknowledged without being made constitutive, showing that my claim about structural elimination is empirically false? Or is there a conceptual error in my framing of honorable failure, perhaps one that illegitimately imports honor-culture assumptions about stoic endurance into a dignity framework that already rejects them? I also want to know whether my position has been qualified into unfalsifiability: if every apparent counterexample is dismissed as not real victimhood culture, is that a problem for a view of this kind? Be honest about which defeaters I, as the defender of this view, should concede as genuinely damaging, versus which ones I can absorb by refinement, and whether there is any observation that would be fatal rather than merely awkward.
