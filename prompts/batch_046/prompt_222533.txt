Here's an argument my study partner built around Buridan's ass, and I think it proves too much about rational choice generally, but I cannot locate the flaw. (1) In the classic case, the ass faces two bales with exactly equal value, so there is no rational basis to select either. (2) One might break the tie with a random device, but the outcome of that device (say, heads) supplies a reason that was not present in the original comparison between the bales themselves. (3) Therefore tie-breaking by chance does not resolve the original problem; it substitutes a new choice—whether to accept the random verdict—where equality may recur. (4) If this is right, then whenever preferences are exactly equal, rational choice is impossible unless we deny that such equality ever genuinely arises. (5) But in any continuous preference ordering over a continuous domain—such as selecting among goods with smooth, identical utility curves—exact equality is unavoidable across broad regions of the choice space. (6) So rational choice theory must either reject continuous preference spaces or accept paralysis across large regions of decision space. (7) This is absurd, since agents routinely choose among indistinguishable options. Where does the argument fail? I would prefer to preserve (1) and (5) if possible, but I want to understand what giving up the other steps costs.
