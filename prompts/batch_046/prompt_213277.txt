i run a philosophy of politics reading group — about 14 people, 75 minutes, mostly grad students plus a couple undergrads. we're working through Dewey's experimentalism about democracy and inquiry, specifically the part where he argues democratic decision-making should function like scientific inquiry: provisional, revisable, and focused on tracing indirect consequences rather than just aggregating preferances. i want them to feel the difference between reaching a vote and actually sustaining an inquiry.

design a simulation: the group manages a shared workshop with limited tools and time slots. split them into roles — users, maintainers, and indirect observors who alone hold cards showing hidden side-effects (e.g., using the laser cutter wears out a shared filter that degrades air quality in another room, but that info is only on their cards). they vote on an allocation plan, earn immediate collective points for efficiency, then the hidden cards come out and the plan might ruin conditions for another subgroup. they can revise, but every revision costs collective points, and there's a timer pushing them to lock in and declare victory.

include exact rules, payoff scheme (no cash — collective points that convert to a shared snack fund or prestige), role cards, and the debrief. the debrief should map: (1) the initial vote to Dewey's idea of preference-aggregation or the crowd moment, (2) the hidden-card moment to tracing indirect consequences, (3) the pressure to lock in to what we might call democratic closure, and (4) the revision cost to why experimental democracy is expensive. tell me what will predictably go wrong first run — probably the observors stay silent because they feel like spoilers, or the group ignores hidden cards to protect points — and how to handle it. be honest about what the game can't reproduce, probably the long temporal scale over which real social experiments unfold, and say exactly what you'll supply verbally in the debrief to cover that gap.

write it casually, like you're pitching this to a tired colleague after a long meeting. include a typo or two if it feels natural.
