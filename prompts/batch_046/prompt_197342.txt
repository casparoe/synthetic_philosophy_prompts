In debates about Jain epistemology and ontology, the parable of the blind men and the elephant is routinely invoked to illustrate anekāntavāda: each man grasps one part—tusk, leg, ear, tail—and mistakes it for the whole, so truth is said to be many-sided (anekānta) and no single viewpoint exhaustive. This analogy is frequently used to license the normative conclusion that all perspectives are partially correct and that dogmatic exclusivism is the primary error. Audit the analogy precisely against the Jain philosophical apparatus it is meant to represent. First, provide an explicit mapping: what corresponds to the elephant, to each blind man, to the partial descriptions, to the inter-subjective disagreement, and to any mediator or synthesis that resolves the conflict? Second, identify where the mapping fractures: does the ontology of anekānta-vastu—reality possessed of infinite, irreducible aspects—permit a unified, bare substrate (the “whole elephant”) that exists independently of every perspectival grasp, or does Jain thought deny precisely such an unconditioned, one-sided (ekānta) entity? How does syādvāda, the sevenfold scheme of predication that allows apparently contradictory attributions (for instance, that an entity both is and is not in certain respects), differ from the merely complementary, non-contradictory partial reports of the blind men? Third, determine what normative conclusions the analogy can actually bear: does it support the strong claim that every perspective is equally valid, or only the weaker claim that exclusivist denial of other standpoints is unjustified? Fourth, expose which features of the source domain—especially the presupposition of a single, pre-existing object with discoverable, fixed parts—are quietly imported into the target, and assess whether that importation conflicts with the Jain rejection of a substrate-independent unity. Finally, if the analogy fails to carry the logical and ontological load, propose a more precise analogical model that captures both the multi-aspectedness of reality and the specific logical structure of syādvāda without smuggling in a realist, unified substrate that anekāntavāda denies.
