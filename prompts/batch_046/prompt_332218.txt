In my philosophy of computation seminar, two classmates—Mira and Jonas—debated whether Solomonoff induction genuinely vindicates Occam's razor or merely relocates the problem of prior choice. Mira opened by noting that Solomonoff's universal prior assigns higher probability to sequences with lower Kolmogorov complexity relative to a fixed universal prefix-free Turing machine, which appears to formalize the rational preference for simpler hypotheses. Jonas objected that the choice of universal machine is arbitrary: with a suitably constructed machine, any hypothesis can be compressed to a minimal description, so the prior smuggles in representational bias under the guise of neutrality. Mira responded that the invariance theorem rescues objectivity, since the difference in description lengths between any two universal machines is bounded by a constant that does not scale with the data, meaning that for large enough datasets the bias becomes negligible. Jonas then pressed that scientific inference always operates with finite, often sparse data, so the constant can dominate the complexity comparison and turn the razor into an artifact of our representational convention rather than a feature of rational belief revision. Mira countered that every inductive framework requires some bias, and Solomonoff's contribution is to make that bias explicit, minimal, and precisely defined, whereas informal appeals to simplicity hide the same dependence. Jonas replied that explicitness is not justification, and since Kolmogorov complexity is uncomputable, the formalization offers no practical guidance for actual hypothesis selection and therefore cannot carry normative weight in real reasoning. They asked me to adjudicate, and I honestly could not decide whether the in-principle formalization outweighs the representational and practical objections. Please explain who is right about each specific point—particularly about whether the invariance constant genuinely rescues objectivity under finite-data conditions, whether uncomputability undermines the normative authority of the framework, and whether rendering a bias explicit amounts to justifying it. Then state who is more right overall, and identify anything important that both Mira and Jonas overlook in their exchange.
