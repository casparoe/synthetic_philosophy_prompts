Consider the long-running dispute in the historiography of the Holocaust between the "ordinary men" thesis—associated with Christopher Browning’s study of Reserve Police Battalion 101, which emphasizes situational mechanisms such as peer conformity, bureaucratic segmentation, career incentives, and the incremental escalation of violence—and the "willing executioners" thesis—associated with Daniel Goldhagen, which argues that a specifically German, culturally pervasive eliminationist antisemitism made perpetrators eager participants rather than reluctant conscripts. Both camps draw on substantially the same archival records, postwar testimonies, and organizational data, yet for decades the disagreement has not yielded convergence.

My working hypothesis is that part of the stall is structural rather than empirical: the two sides may be operating with unstated, incompatible criteria for what counts as an adequate explanation—specifically, whether an explanation must identify a mechanism at the level of individual psychology, at the level of unit dynamics, or at the macro-cultural level of available ideological "scripts"—and whether explanations at these levels are competitors or nested. A related possibility is that they rely on different implicit counterfactuals: one framework asks whether these particular men, absent specific situational pressures, would have killed; the other asks whether a culture lacking eliminationist antisemitism would have produced the same outcome.

Diagnose this debate. Is the disagreement primarily verbal—turning on what "explanation" or "cause" means in historical social science? Is there a hidden empirical crux, such as the actual prevalence and intensity of antisemitic conviction among the rank-and-file of Battalion 101 relative to comparable units, that both sides would accept as decisive? Or is the impasse rooted in bedrock intuitions about human nature, moral responsibility, and the limits of situational influence that neither side can argue the other out of? In particular, address whether the failure to specify the "level of mechanism"—individual, organizational, or cultural—is causing them to talk past each other, and whether introducing a formal distinction of "explanatory nesting," in which situational and cultural factors operate at different scales rather than as direct rivals, would dissolve part of the dispute or merely relocate it.

Finally, specify what, if anything, could genuinely move the debate: a comparative study of police battalions drawn from societies without a comparable history of eliminationist ideology; a conceptual clarification about nested causation in collective violence; or an argument that the question is ill-posed because "willingness" and "situation" are not mutually exclusive categories but interdependent constructs that must be analyzed jointly rather than adjudicated separately.
