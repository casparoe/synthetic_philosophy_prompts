I'm writing something on consequentialist cluelessness — that problem where we just can't predict the long-run fallout of our actions well enough to actually use act-consequentialism for real decisions. I'm trying to sort out which open questions in this area are genuinely upstream and worth a grad student's time, versus which ones are kind of dead ends that people keep circling.

So there's the basic metaphysical/epistemic question: is cluelessness fatal to act-consequentialism, or does it just push us toward rule-consequentialism and heuristics? Then there's the applied side: does it make longtermist projects especially shaky, since we're talking about effects thousands of years out? There's also this formal angle people mention — trying to build decision-theoretic frameworks for radical uncertainty — but I'm not convinced that's tractible with current tools. And then there's an angle I'm half-developing myself: call it "moral option value." The idea is that cluelessness isn't just about unknown outcomes, but about preserving our future capacity to revise our moral views; locking in irreversible actions now might be wrong not because we mispredict, but because we foreclose the chance to learn we're wrong. I don't know if that's just a confused version of option value in finance, or actually distinct.
