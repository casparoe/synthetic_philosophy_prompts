While drafting dialogue for a novel, I recorded an argument between two acquaintances, Celia and Jonas, concerning whether political adversaries argue in good faith. Celia held that opponents systematically deploy motivated reasoning and selective evidence, so attributing good faith to them is a mistake rather than a virtue. Jonas replied that this conflates disagreement with deceit: people inherit different priors through distinct information ecosystems, and within their frameworks their reasoning remains coherent and sincere. Celia countered that coherence inside a closed system is insufficient—if an echo chamber structurally suppresses disconfirming input, then praising an individual’s good faith ignores their acquiescence to an architecture that makes honest evaluation impossible. Jonas objected that this dissolves the agent into the network; one cannot treat people as mere cogs in a machine and still hold them accountable for its outputs. Celia answered that accountability need not imply malice, but only requires recognising when structural incentives have replaced reasoning with rationalisation. They asked me to referee. Please adjudicate point by point: who is correct about which claim, who is more right overall, and what, if anything, both neglect—particularly whether good faith ought to be assessed at the level of the individual agent or the epistemic system.
