The student answered as follows. James distinguishes genuine options—those that are live, forced, and momentous—from those that are dead, avoidable, or trivial. A live option is one that is intellectually alive for the agent; forced means we cannot avoid choosing between belief and disbelief without practical cost; momentous means the stakes are significant and unique. In the climate-panel scenario, the feedback-loop hypothesis is live within current ecological modeling, forced because institutional timelines preclude indefinite suspension, and momentous given the irreversible policy commitments involved. James contends that in such cases, belief may be determined by our volitional commitment—our willingness to risk error—without violating intellectual duty, precisely because the evidence is structurally insufficient to resolve the matter. Clifford advances a universal evidentialist prohibition: it is wrong always, everywhere, and for anyone to believe anything upon insufficient evidence. He supports this with the shipowner case, in which a man sends passengers to sea despite unexamined doubts, thereby illustrating how belief without adequate warrant produces culpable harm. However, Clifford's prohibition is primarily directed at beliefs formed through negligent disregard of available evidence in practical contexts; it does not apply to hypotheses that are genuinely underdetermined by any available method of inquiry. Therefore, the panel's adoption of the precautionary framework falls outside Clifford's criticism, since they are not ignoring accessible data but operating within an unavoidable epistemic gap. That said, James's allowance for passional decision applies only to options meeting all three criteria. In this case, suspension is practically unavailable, so James's conditions are satisfied. This means that a researcher could consistently believe contradictory feedback loops, provided each is live and momentous, since James removes all rational constraints beyond the taxonomy itself. Hence, while James correctly identifies a restricted domain for non-evidential belief, his framework collapses into a form of voluntarism that Clifford's evidentialism rightly rejects.
