Write a strict sestina on the problem of universals. Use exactly these six end-words — particular, shared, redness, relation, regress, and name — rotated through the line-end positions according to the traditional sestina pattern (stanza one: 1-2-3-4-5-6; stanza two: 6-1-5-2-4-3; and so on through the six stanzas, with all six present in the final three-line envoi).

Take the speaker to be an ordinary particular — for example, a single apple falling — so that the form’s compulsive repetition mirrors the realist claim that one universal, redness, is shared across many particulars without being divided. Use the rotation of line-ends to stage the argument structurally: the early stanzas should establish the one-over-many problem; the middle stanzas should turn to the problem of instantiation, letting the relation that binds particular to universal spawn the further relations of Bradley’s regress; and the nominalist answer — that the shared feature is only a name — should enter as one of the recurring refrains. The envoi should gather all six words together without resolving whether realism, nominalism, or a bundle-theoretic compromise survives.

The rendering must be accurate enough that a metaphysician recognises the one-over-many argument, the instantiation regress, and the nominalist escape; a reader unfamiliar with the debate should still find the poem coherent. Do not let the poem preach for one side: the tension should stay live.
