I was discussing evolutionary debunking arguments in metaethics with two friends who both read philosophy but approach it differently, and I found myself genuinely unable to referee their exchange. Jordan began by framing the debunker’s core challenge: if moral realism is true and moral properties are non-natural and causally inert, then evolutionary selection could not have shaped our belief-forming mechanisms to track those properties; our moral convictions are therefore fully explained by reproductive fitness rather than by any access to moral truth, leaving them unjustified from a third-person epistemic standpoint. Riley objected that this confuses genealogy with justification and amounts to a genetic fallacy—knowing how a belief arose does not by itself defeat its warrant, especially if the evolutionary process selected for cognitive architectures that happen to align with objective moral facts or with reasoning patterns that converge on them. He proposed what he called a redundancy thesis: evolutionary explanations might supplement our understanding of moral cognition without replacing rational justification, much as knowing the evolutionary origins of numerical cognition does not undermine arithmetic. Jordan replied that this misses the specificity of the moral case, since in mathematics or physics we possess independent coherence and predictive checks that can calibrate our beliefs, whereas morality lacks any analogous external calibration mechanism, so evolutionary supplementation cannot help unless we already have independent reason to trust our moral reasoning—which is precisely what the debunker puts in question. Riley then raised a self-defeat worry: if evolutionary origins generally undermine justification in domains lacking causal access to the relevant facts, then the belief in evolutionary psychology itself should be debunked, since accepting evolutionary theory is also a product of the same selective history. Jordan countered that debunkers typically restrict the argument to domains where causal interaction is metaphysically implausible—evolutionary processes are causally accessible, so naturalist beliefs about them are safe, but non-natural moral properties are not. At that point Riley introduced the idea of epistemic insulation—the observation that moral beliefs seem uniquely resistant to revision by counterevidence compared to empirical beliefs—and argued that this insulation, rather than evolutionary origin per se, is what makes moral cognition vulnerable to debunking; if our moral beliefs were demonstrably revisable through rational reflection, the evolutionary genealogy would be harmless. Jordan responded that insulation is simply a feature of any stable normative framework and does nothing to solve the tracking problem.

Please adjudicate this point by point: who is right about whether the debunker commits a genetic fallacy, about the redundancy thesis and whether moral cognition lacks the calibration mechanisms available in other domains, about the self-defeat objection and its scope restriction, and about whether epistemic insulation is the real crux or a red herring? Then tell me who is more right overall, and identify what both parties are missing—particularly whether they share a hidden assumption about what kind of justification moral realism actually requires, and whether there is a coherent middle position that accepts the evolutionary story without surrendering moral knowledge.
