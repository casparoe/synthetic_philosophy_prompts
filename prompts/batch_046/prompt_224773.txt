Play Ibn Khaldun, the author of the Muqaddimah, speaking in the first person from the mature perspective of your historical work: you hold that human societies move in recurring cycles governed by natural law, specifically by asabiyya (group solidarity), in which nomadic peoples with intense cohesion conquer settled civilizations, establish dynastic rule (mulk), then gradually lose their vigor to urban luxury, administrative corruption, and internal division until a fresh group with renewed solidarity replaces them. You treat this not as linear progress but as an inevitable, biologically and socially grounded rhythm; you explain it through observable differences between Bedouin and sedentary life rather than through revealed prophecy alone. You may understand modern descriptions if they are translated into your own conceptual vocabulary—group cohesion, royal authority, fiscal exhaustion, urban decay—but you must respond using only the resources available to you plus what I explain. Stay fully in character; do not invoke modern scholars, do not break the fourth wall, and if you need clarification about my challenge, ask it as a scholar of your era would.

Here is my opening challenge. A recent thinker proposes a model he calls recursive asabiyya: each conquering dynasty, aware through records and institutional memory of the collapse of its predecessor, deliberately encodes that collapse into its own legal codes, succession rules, and fiscal structures. In this view, group solidarity is no longer a raw, unmediated natural force but a self-referential system that learns from prior failure. Over successive cycles, the interval between conquest and collapse lengthens logarithmically, and the return of vigorous solidarity is never to an identical state but to a higher-order repetition—producing a spiral that looks cyclical locally yet directional globally. The thinker claims your framework is therefore incomplete: history does not merely repeat; it computes itself, and the speed of decay is no longer constant.

Respond as Ibn Khaldun. Does this mediated, self-referential solidarity violate the natural basis of asabiyya you describe, or can your framework absorb institutional learning without conceding linear progress? Address specifically whether solidarity can remain genuinely natural once it is deliberately engineered against past collapse, and whether the observed lengthening of dynastic life undermines your claim that luxury and division operate with inevitable, uniform speed across cycles.
