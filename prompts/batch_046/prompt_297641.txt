I'm working on a research agenda that applies luck egalitarian thinking to institutional design rather than individual distributive justice, and I'm trying to decide where limited philosophical effort should go over the next couple of years. The standard literature treats brute luck and option luck as properties of individual lives—natural talents, social origins, and the choices people make within those constraints. But I'm concerned with what might be called compound institutional luck: situations where an initial brute-luck disadvantage embedded in institutional structures, such as weak legal protections inherited from colonial administrative patterns, generates feedback loops that systematically convert subsequent option-luck failures into further brute-luck deficits across generations. At that scale, the individual-level distinction between what someone is responsible for and what they are not seems to blur, and the usual individual-focused responses to the harshness objection may not carry over cleanly.

Given that framing, I want you to prioritize the following three open lines of inquiry for a researcher with about two years, a background in normative political philosophy rather than economics or sociology, and no need to run original empirical studies. First, can the brute/option distinction be reconstructed meaningfully for collective or intergenerational agents, or does compound institutional luck show that responsibility-tracking is only coherent at the individual level? Second, does this problem provide a decisive reason to shift from luck egalitarianism to relational egalitarianism or democratic equality, or can the view be reformulated—perhaps through a weighted or threshold-sensitive metric—to accommodate institutional feedback without collapsing into pure outcome equality? Third, what are the specific normative implications for global distributive obligations, particularly in climate adaptation funding, where causal chains are long, diffuse, and cross national boundaries?

Please rank these three and defend the ordering explicitly: which is most upstream of the others, which is most tractable given the tools of analytic philosophy rather than empirical social science, and which would genuinely change how policymakers or institutional designers think about responsibility if settled. Also tell me which of these you think is overrated—where the debate looks important but resolving it wouldn't alter much downstream work. In your answer, clearly separate what is relatively settled in the literature—such as the basic structure of the brute/option distinction and the standard formulations of the harshness objection—from what remains genuinely contested among specialists, and do not present a contested interpretation as if it were the consensus view.
