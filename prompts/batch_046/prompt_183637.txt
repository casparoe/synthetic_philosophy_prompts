Consider the institutional practice, common in contemporary democracies, of maintaining what might be called a dual-track mnemonic regime. On one track, the state funds and mandates permanent, non-anonymized archives, memorial museums, and public registers for collective historical atrocities—genocide, state terror, forced displacement—treating the preservation of victim identities and the prohibition of erasure as inalienable collective obligations. On the other track, the same legal system enforces data-protection frameworks and administrative sunset rules that require the routine destruction or anonymization of ordinary citizens' bureaucratic, medical, and digital records after fixed intervals, often justified by an individual's right to be forgotten and the protection of reputational autonomy against the persistence of outdated administrative traces.

The result is a state that treats the indefinite preservation of certain pasts as a moral imperative while treating the indefinite preservation of other pasts as an active harm to dignity.

What philosophical theory of memory, obligation, and personhood does this dual-track practice presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit view: does it depend on a sharp distinction between historical persons entitled to permanent commemoration and administrative subjects entitled to erasure? Does it presuppose that collective memory is a public good with its own moral standing, while individual memory-traces are merely instrumental liabilities? Or can the asymmetry be explained without any deep theory—say, by differences in tractability, consent, or the nature of the harm?

Then distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what merely fits it comfortably. Is it compatible with a uniform view that assigns every person, victim or citizen, the same mnemonic rights? And if one replaced the implicit theory with a unified account—perhaps one informed by debates about the duty to remember and the case for forgetting—what concrete institutional changes would follow? Would memorial archives have to accept anonymization requests, or would data-protection regimes have to abandon sunset rules for ordinary records?
