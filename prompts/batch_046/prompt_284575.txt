I am composing a novel in which the protagonist, a clockmaker who has lost his sight, begins to conceive of the mechanisms he repairs not as objects that push one another through direct contact, but as arrangements that depend upon the absence of certain movements—gears that hold their shape only because other gears do not turn. This conceit has led me to read David Hume’s Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding, particularly those parts in which he investigates the origin of our idea of necessary connection in cause and effect. In the passage I have before me, Hume argues that after observing the constant conjunction of two events, we feel a determination of the mind to pass from the idea of the first to the idea of the second, and he asks whether we possess any further impression of a real power or necessary link in the objects themselves. He answers that we do not, and that what we call causation is nothing beyond this felt expectation together with the observed regularity.

What I am struggling to understand is whether this Humean reduction—causation as custom-formed expectation rather than observed force—sits more naturally with a counterfactual account, in which one event causes another by virtue of what would have occurred had the first been absent, or with an interventionist account, in which causation is defined by the practical possibility of altering one variable to change another. My clockmaker’s invisible, non-turning gears seem to occupy a strange middle ground: they are defined not by any actual intervention he performs upon them, but by the counterfactual stability they impose upon the visible mechanism. Is Hume’s skepticism about necessary connection compatible with either framework, or does his insistence that we never observe power but only conjunction and mental habit effectively dissolve both into mere psychological projection? And if there is a recognized term for this particular phantom—a causal constraint that operates solely through the suppression of alternative paths, leaving no positive trace except in the regularity it preserves—I should like to know it; if not, I should like to understand precisely why the concept slips between the counterfactual and the interventionist, so that I may decide whether my clockmaker is thinking clearly or only dressing a confusion in philosophical language.
