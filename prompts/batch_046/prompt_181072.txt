I'm preparing a feature on whether large language models genuinely understand the symbols they manipulate. A comparison I repeatedly encounter is that an LLM resembles a person who learns a language solely from a monolingual dictionary: every definition points to other definitions, with no sensory access to the world those words denote. Proponents use this to argue that fluency masks a complete absence of reference; critics reply that statistical correlations across vast corpora create a functional substitute for grounding. Could you analyse this analogy rigorously? First, lay out an explicit correspondence table: what plays the role of the dictionary, the learner, the missing sensory modality, and the claimed functional substitute? Then identify precisely where the mapping breaks — for instance, does the dictionary-only learner have any analogue to multi-modal training data or to embodied interaction via tool use? Next, apply the audit to a more speculative case: imagine a system that is grounded neither visually nor tactilely, but exclusively in temporal patterns — it registers duration and sequence, yet possesses no spatial or qualitative experience whatsoever. Does the dictionary analogy still apply, or does temporal anchoring provide a different kind of reference altogether? Finally, judge whether the conclusion that fluency is not understanding survives once these disanalogies and this alternative substrate are on the table, and suggest a better analogy should the dictionary model collapse.
