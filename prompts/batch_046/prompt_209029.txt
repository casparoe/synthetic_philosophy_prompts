I will be defending a proposal for what I call institutional bilingual neutrality at a workshop of political theorists and comparative constitutional lawyers. The proposal holds that public universities should apply strict neutrality only to official state speech—curricula, administrative pronouncements, and institutional branding—while allowing plural symbolic accommodation in student associations and in faculty personal expression, including religious dress, provided that funding and space allocations treat secular and religious associations symmetrically. The audience is mostly divided between scholars who defend French republican laïcité as a necessary condition for democratic equality and those who defend Anglo-American multicultural recognition as essential to minority dignity; several participants are explicitly skeptical that any stable intermediate framework exists, and one is known to argue that all accommodation collapses into hidden assimilation. Prepare me for the objections I will actually face, ordered from the most likely and damaging to the least. For each objection, indicate whose vocabulary it will be phrased in, whether it has real force, what I should concede upfront, and what reply must land for the proposal to survive. Identify the closest analog in either the French law banning conspicuous religious symbols in schools or in North American university jurisprudence that could be used to defend or attack my position, and include the objection I am hoping nobody raises. In your response, clearly separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among specialists in this area, and do not present a contested interpretation as if it were the consensus view.
