I am attempting to map the genuinely upstream research directions in Bayesian epistemology that turn on the problem of old evidence, particularly where that problem intersects with normatively constrained and collectively structured reasoning rather than with the idealised single agent. The classic formulation—that once evidence E is already known, learning that a theory T entails E ought not to raise one's credence in T—is by now familiar, and I do not wish to rehearse standard counter-examples or the usual Bayesian replies. Instead, I am interested in identifying which unresolved clusters are conceptually interdependent and which deserve priority for a small philosophy group working over roughly three years without a dedicated formal modelling laboratory.

Specifically, I see three clusters that appear underexplored. First, there is the question of whether old evidence can carry confirmatory weight when an agent's prior is not merely subjective but institutionally structured—for instance, by legal presumptions of innocence or by scientific norms of conservatism that constrain admissible priors independently of personal belief. Second, there is the problem of logical omniscience in bounded agents: if a reasoner does not know all logical consequences of her beliefs, then deriving that T implies E may itself constitute a genuine cognitive achievement that alters her epistemic state, which suggests the old-evidence problem may dissolve differently for non-omniscient reasoners than for ideal ones. Third, there is the issue of intersubjective or collective Bayesian updating: in committees, courts, or scientific collaborations, evidence is often old for some members but not for others, and the aggregation rules may need to distinguish between epistemic novelty at the individual level and at the group level, raising the further question of whether a collective can be said to have "known" E prior to the derivation even when no individual member had.

My request is for a defended prioritisation, not a survey and certainly not a reading list. Suppose you are advising this research group—philosophers trained in philosophy of science and normative ethics rather than in machine learning. Which of these clusters, or others you think I have neglected, should they tackle first, and in what order? For each, defend its placement with explicit criteria: how many other questions depend on its resolution (upstream importance), whether it can be advanced with the conceptual and normative tools available to philosophers (tractability), and what kind of result—conceptual clarification, a formal model sketch, or an institutional case study—would cause you to revise the ranking. I am particularly keen to know whether resolving the institutional-prior cluster must precede the bounded-agent cluster, or whether they can proceed in parallel without generating conceptual confusion. Please write in formal, polished British English, avoid bibliographic lists or requests for citations, and treat this as an agenda-setting exercise whose deliverable is reasoning about where limited philosophical effort should go.
