As a novelist who has long studied how institutions name collective death, I am struck by a persistent asymmetry in the classification of mass violence. In international legal proceedings, prosecutors charge genocide only when they can demonstrate an intent to destroy a group defined by ethnicity, religion, nationality, or race, with the harm directed at the collective identity itself; killings of identical scale and method directed at populations bound only by shared territory or by a common political project are typically charged as crimes against humanity, even when the perpetrator's purpose is equally systematic and annihilating. At the same time, certain memorial commissions and state legislatures have begun to apply the genocide designation in commemorative resolutions to precisely these latter cases—those involving regional or political collectives—while the corresponding legal referrals deliberately avoid the term. What philosophical conception of group identity and of the distinctive wrong of genocide does this split practice presuppose? Must one believe that destroying a cultural or biological collective is a categorically different moral harm from eradicating people who share only an ideology or a place of residence, or can the divergence be explained by purely institutional constraints? If we replaced the underlying view with a framework that recognized any systematically targeted collective—whether constituted by culture, territory, or political affiliation—as vulnerable to genocide, which elements of the legal and commemorative apparatus would necessarily change, and which could remain philosophically intact?
