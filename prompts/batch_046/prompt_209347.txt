You are an expert philosopher of collective responsibility with a working knowledge of twentieth-century European historiography. Before you begin, briefly brainstorm how a simulation with hidden payoffs can make students feel the conceptual friction between attributing war-guilt to a collective agent and distributing liability without requiring a unified collective mind.

Design a runnable 90-minute seminar activity for 14 advanced undergraduates on the question of what it means to hold a nation responsible for the First World War, taking the Fischer controversy—Fritz Fischer’s argument for deliberate German escalation against structuralist and institutional counter-accounts—as the live case study. Do not open with a lecture; the idea should emerge from what participants do.

Split the cohort into four roles—Intentionalist, Structuralist, Institutional Historian, and Collective-Agent Skeptic—each with a public mandate to build a unanimous “responsibility ledger” distributing 100 points across agents (the Kaiser, the General Staff, the Reichstag, “the German nation”). Give each role a sealed private payoff rule: every point assigned to “the nation” as a unified collective subtracts five points from a shared “reparative capacity” pool that all roles must keep above zero; the Intentionalist secretly needs the national score above 50 to “win”; the Skeptic secretly needs it below 20. The group has 40 minutes to negotiate and vote, unaware of the hidden constraints.

After revealing the rules and the final ledger, conduct a 25-minute debrief that maps: (1) the Intentionalist’s push onto the problem of attributive blame and the need for a collective mind; (2) the Structuralist’s resistance onto distributive liability that does not require unified intent; (3) the collapse of the shared pool onto the philosophical tension that holding a nation responsible may erode the very conditions for that nation to make amends. Be explicit about which feature of collective responsibility the simulation cannot reproduce—specifically, the temporal distance of historical judgement and the absence of real victims—and state exactly how you will supply that missing element verbally.

Keep the instructions precise enough to run without ambiguity, avoid any request for bibliographies, reading lists, or citations, and write your response in British English.
