Consider this argument about belief attribution to artificial systems via the intentional stance. (1) If treating a system as a rational agent with beliefs and desires yields the best predictions of its behaviour, then it genuinely possesses those intentional states. (2) A large language model's outputs are best predicted by attributing beliefs about language, facts, and reasoning to the model as a whole. (3) The same predictive strategy succeeds for isolated sub-networks—say, a single attention head tracking syntactic agreement—and for the training corpus treated as one distributed agent. (4) If predictive success alone fixes belief, then attribution diffuses downward into parameters and upward into datasets, leaving no principled reason to locate belief in the model rather than in its parts or its environment. (5) This cognitive diffusion implies either that countless micro-believers inhabit every network, or that the model itself has no special claim to belief. (6) Both results seem absurd; we do not suppose an individual weight matrix believes that two plus two equals four. (7) Hence the intentional stance, applied consistently to AI, proves too much, and (1) must be rejected as sufficient for belief. I suspect the error lies between (4) and (5), yet I cannot pinpoint it. Which step should be abandoned? I should like to preserve (1) and (2) if possible, but I want to understand the precise cost of rejecting any other premise—especially whether saving the stance requires drawing an arbitrary boundary around the mind.
