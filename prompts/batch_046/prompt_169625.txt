I'm trying to work out a consistent view on communicative ethics across cultural contexts, and I'd like a consistency audit before I defend it in writing. Here is roughly where I've landed:

(a) The primary moral criterion for evaluating an utterance should be whether it respects the hearer's autonomy as a practical reasoner, not whether it conforms to conventional norms of politeness or literal truth.

(b) On that basis, Dutch-style directness is generally morally preferable to Japanese high-context indirectness, because forcing a hearer to decode unstated meanings treats them as incapable of bearing explicit practical reasons.

(c) By the same token, white lies—say, praising a friend's unworkable business plan to spare their feelings—are wrong: they substitute the speaker's protective judgment for the hearer's own practical reasoning.

(d) At the same time, I hold that within Japanese communicative practice, indirectness is not deceptive but a conventional form of politeness that sustains relational harmony, and that this harmony is itself a constituent condition of the hearer's autonomy rather than a threat to it.

(e) Moreover, if a hearer is socialized in a high-context culture, then delivering Dutch-style directness without regard for those conventions can fail to respect autonomy just as seriously—by imposing an alien framework for practical reasoning and disregarding the hearer's own interpretive norms.

(f) Finally, I take the failure to respect autonomy in speech to be scalar rather than binary: its gravity tracks both the stakes of the practical decision at issue and the degree to which the hearer relies on the speaker's sincerity, even though the failure is always present in principle.

Do these hang together, or am I helping myself to incompatible motivations? In particular: is there a hidden tension between my universal preference for directness in (b) and my contextualist warning in (e)? Does my condemnation of white lies in (c) commit me to treating Japanese indirectness as a culturally sanctioned form of the same wrong, and if so, can (d) save it? And is the scalar claim in (f) genuinely compatible with the autonomy-first framework in (a), or does it smuggle in a competing metric? Please rank the tensions by severity, identify any that dissolve on closer inspection, and for each real conflict say which commitment is the least costly to revise and what that revision costs me elsewhere in the package.
