The debate over finkish dispositions and antidotal masks—originating in Martin’s electro-fink thought experiment and Johnston’s antidote-to-poison case—has persisted for decades without clear resolution. Defenders of the simple conditional analysis of dispositions typically respond to interference cases by introducing ceteris paribus clauses, modal qualifications, or distinctions between pure and impure dispositions; critics reply that such moves are ad hoc, that the interfering mechanism is often internal to the categorical base of the disposition itself, and that the conditional analysis therefore misrepresents the metaphysics of potentiality. My working hypothesis is that the disagreement is largely verbal: one camp treats 'disposition' as a monadic property of an object considered in isolation, the other treats it as an essentially relational property of an object-in-its-environment, and they consequently talk past each other about whether a mask 'defeats' or merely 'suppresses' the disposition. But I want a more precise diagnosis. To make the problem concrete, consider what I will call a recursive antidote: a substance possesses a disposition D to manifest effect M in conditions C, but also contains a higher-order regulatory mechanism that activates blocker B precisely when C is imminent; B prevents M, yet B is itself unstable and would be neutralized by M if M ever occurred, so that in the actual world D never manifests and B is never neutralized. Does this scenario demonstrate that D is genuinely present but systematically masked by an internal mechanism, thereby falsifying any unqualified conditional analysis? Or does it instead show that there is no unitary disposition D, but rather a pair of competing tendencies that happen to be locked in equilibrium, rendering the question of whether D is 'finked' ill-posed? More broadly, is the long stall in this literature a sign that the dispute is about which cases count as data—with one side privileging actual manifestations and the other privileging counterfactual stability—or is there a hidden substantive disagreement about whether dispositions are individuated by their manifestations, their categorical bases, or their modal profiles? Diagnose the structure of the impasse: is it verbal, about standards of evidence, about underlying metaphysical commitments that neither side can independently motivate, or about two different questions being pursued under the single heading 'disposition'? And tell me what, if anything, would count as genuine progress—a formal taxonomy of interference types, a revision of the framing question, an empirical discovery about physical bases, or an argument that the debate should be dissolved rather than solved.
