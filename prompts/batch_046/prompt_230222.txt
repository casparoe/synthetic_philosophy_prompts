I serve on the Acquisition and Ethics Committee of the Whitmore University Museum of Art. We have received a bequest of an oil painting that conservation analysis and art-historical review strongly indicate is a skilled modern forgery of a lost devotional panel from the early seventeenth century, executed by an artisan who died in the 1980s. What makes the case unusual is that imaging of the substrate has revealed a layer of handwritten annotations embedded by the forger, invisible under standard viewing conditions, in which he argues that since the original work is irrecoverably destroyed, his painting should not be treated as deception but rather as a restoration of the aesthetic idea itself, and that the value of the object lies in the realized form rather than in material continuity or provenance. Our institutional charter requires that acquisitions possess demonstrable artistic or historical significance, and our ethics policy mandates explicit labeling for any displayed forgery, but it is silent on objects that are simultaneously forgeries and contain original philosophical content composed by the forger. The committee must vote by next Friday.

Please draft the written opinion I will circulate in advance: recommend one concrete course among (a) outright rejection of the bequest, (b) acceptance into study storage with no public display, or (c) acceptance with full disclosure and display that includes the revealed annotations in the catalog entry, together with a specific implementation timeline if the recommendation is adopted. The opinion should engage seriously with the strongest consideration on the opposing side — specifically, the claim that institutional display of any forgery, even with disclosure, erodes public trust in our commitment to authenticity — and should also address the forger's mediated argument, relayed by our curatorial staff, that suppressing the work replicates the original loss rather than correcting it. The reasoning should proceed without bibliographic citation and should directly examine the conceptual tension between provenance-based and form-based accounts of artistic value.
