I moderate the archive for a long-running rationalist forum that retains full, attributed records of every public prediction, moderation ruling, and interpersonal dispute. Our charter asks us to honor two competing ethical obligations drawn from the philosophy of collective memory: first, a Margalit-style duty to preserve accurate accounts of past errors, injustices, and failed forecasts so that institutional learning remains possible and moral accountability is not dissolved; second, a Rieff-style case for strategic forgetting, on the grounds that perpetual, attributed remembrance can harden grievance into identity, fuel hindsight-biased narrative entrenchment, and destroy the conditions for cooperation that make future correction possible. I need a reusable decision procedure—something a non-philosopher moderator can run in under thirty minutes—that determines, for any given record, whether to (1) preserve fully with attribution, (2) preserve the factual and predictive content but anonymize and decouple it from specific actors, or (3) expunge the record entirely, retaining only an aggregate statistical trace. The procedure should ask its questions in a fixed order, specify what counts as a sufficient answer at each step, clearly flag the steps that are value judgments rather than factual checks, and indicate exactly where it terminates with a recommendation rather than delegating to human judgment. Then apply it to three cases: (a) a five-year-old thread in which a prominent member made a confidently wrong technological forecast that is now routinely used to mock them and discredit their current positions; (b) a moderation dispute involving a minor procedural injustice with no lasting harm, where the affected party requests total deletion but the team fears losing institutional knowledge; and (c) a case where the procedure recommends full preservation despite intense community pressure to anonymize or delete, and explain why the recommendation conflicts with the intuitive preference for forgetting. Finally, identify the two points in the procedure that cannot be automated without violating the underlying ethical distinction, explain why they require direct human judgment, and note what the procedure is explicitly unable to settle—particularly regarding the temporal threshold at which remembrance becomes harmful rather than corrective.
