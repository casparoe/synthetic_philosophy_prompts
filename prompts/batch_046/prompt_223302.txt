I was discussing this with a colleague over coffee — I'm a software engineer, no formal philosophy training — and we got stuck on Newton's religious ideas. Apparently Newton believed God occasionally intervened to fix planetary orbits because gravity by itself would eventually destabilise the solar system, kind of like a sysadmin running maintainance scripts in the background. Let's call that the "background maintenance" view.

Here's the argument we built:
(1) Newton held that God occasionally corrects orbital drift that pure gravity can't prevent.
(2) If that's true, then for any orbit that has stayed stable over long timescales, we can't tell whether it's stable because of gravity or because of an unseen divine patch.
(3) So empirical astronomy can never confirm that gravity alone explains long-term stability; the data is always compatible with hidden intervention.
(4) But Newton's whole scientific project was to show that gravity alone explains celestial motion.
(5) Therefore Newton's theology makes his own physics epistemically self-undermining.
(6) And if this works for Newton, it works for anyone who thinks physical laws allow occasional divine tinkering — which means science is impossible for any religious scientist of that type.
(7) But clearly science happened, and loads of early modern scientists had exactly these views.
(8) So Newton couldn't consistently hold both his physics and his theology.

I think the problem is somewhere between (2) and (5), maybe a scope shift or an equivocation between "can't tell in principle" and "can't tell in practice"? Or maybe I'm sliding from "intervention is possible" to "intervention is actually happening in every case we observe"? I want to keep (1) and (4) if I can — I don't want to just dismiss Newton's theology as irrational — but I'm open to giving up whatever has to go. Where exactly does this argument go wrong, and what's the cost of fixing it?
