Specifically, I am interested in how different formal strategies respond to what I will call a distributed observation: an evidential input that is not a point value but a credal spread over an observable space, where the shape and dispersion of the spread itself encode information about measurement reliability.

Please rank the following four formal strategies for handling distributed observations within a Jeffrey-style framework, ordering them from most defensible to least defensible as rational belief-revision policies, and assign each a tier (S, A, B, or C):

1. Direct Jeffrey updating that treats the entire spread as a single composite event weighted by its integral over the observable space;
2. A hierarchical model that first updates beliefs about the reliability parameter governing the spread, then applies Jeffrey conditioning using the updated reliability weights;
3. A partition-sensitive decomposition that divides the spread into discrete bins, applies Jeffrey conditioning within each bin, and aggregates the results via a precision-weighted mixture;
4. A conservatism rule that treats any non-degenerate spread as epistemically indeterminate and blocks all revision until the spread collapses to a point.

For each strategy, provide a one-sentence justification that names a specific structural vulnerability or strength—such as susceptibility to evidential dilution, failure to preserve diachronic consistency, violation of the likelihood principle, or excessive rigidity—rather than appealing to generic intuition. Your answer should assume familiarity with Jeffrey's conditioning equation and the distinction between rigid and non-rigid updating, but it should not depend on knowledge of any specific paper or author beyond what is standard in the literature.
