I've been arguing with my roommate about whether you can still think a painting is brilliant if the painter turned out to be a total monster — not just rude, but like, actually did something unforgivable. In our aesthetics course the professor mentioned the separation thesis, and my roommate keeps falling back on this analogy that art is basically like medicine: the pill doesn't care if the chemist was a war criminal, the molecule still works. So he says we should treat a painting the same way — seperate the object from the biography, evaluate the brushstrokes and color relationships on their own terms. But something about that feels wrong to me, especially for art that feels like it's asking you to imaginatively share the artist's perspective, not just observe a result. I want you to really audit that medicine analogy: map out exactly what plays the role of the drug, the patient, the cure, and the researcher; point out where the mapping breaks down, especially around whether aesthetic value is more like a causal property or more like a kind of testimony; and tell me whether the analogy smuggles in an assumption that art is just a passive object rather than an invitation to some kind of mental collaboration. Also, if the analogy fails, what's a better one — maybe art as a tool, or art as a conversation — and does that replacement help the separationist or hurt them?
