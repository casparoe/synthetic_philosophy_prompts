The examination asked students to compare William James's account of when belief without sufficient evidence can be legitimate with W.K. Clifford's evidentialist position that belief upon insufficient evidence is always wrong, paying particular attention to James's specific conditions for a legitimate option and to Clifford's claim that even apparently harmless private beliefs shape collective habits of mind.

Here is the student's complete answer:

Clifford argues that believing anything upon insufficient evidence is wrong in every case, regardless of context. He applies this universally, including to religious and scientific beliefs, because allowing one weak belief creates a habit of credulity that can spread through a society. James responds by saying that belief is sometimes justified when the option is live, forced, and momentous—like choosing whether to trust a friend or commit to a political cause. However, James essentially endorses wishful thinking: if a belief is emotionally important, you may adopt it regardless of proof. Clifford permits acting on tradition in practical matters, but he never permits belief itself without direct evidence. James's framework applies broadly to any significant life choice, so he would allow scientists to endorse a tentative climate model just because it feels momentous, even if the data remain ambiguous.
