As a doctoral candidate working at the intersection of metaphysics and comparative philosophy, I have been tracking a recurring argumentative configuration that I can describe concretely but cannot yet name with precision, and I am unsure whether it belongs to an established conceptual family or requires a new coinage. The pattern unfolds as follows. A speaker grants that some particular object, practice, or outcome satisfies every criterion that is explicitly offered as necessary and sufficient for membership in a category—say, that an artifact meets all conditions in an institutional or functional definition of art, or that a study satisfies every methodological norm that defines scientific inquiry. The speaker then observes that the instance also carries some further property—commercial manufacture, corporate funding, algorithmic generation, or widespread social visibility—that is not part of the definition and is present in other uncontroversial members of the category. Rather than treating that property as an accidental addition, the speaker treats it as conceptually incompatible with membership: the instance is declared “not really” art or science, not because the definitional criteria have been revised, but because the accidental feature is said to “contaminate” or “cancel” the essential satisfaction of those criteria. Crucially, the speaker does not apply this cancellation consistently; other members of the category that also bear the feature are retained without objection. The exclusion therefore tracks something like prestige or purity rather than logic, yet the speaker insists the exclusion is conceptual, not merely evaluative.

I have encountered concrete versions of this in debates over readymades in aesthetics, over industry-funded clinical trials in philosophy of science, and over AI-generated texts in literary theory. In each case the move feels structurally distinct from the “no true Scotsman” fallacy, because the speaker is not retroactively redefining the category to exclude a counterexample—the definitional criteria remain intact, and the speaker often concedes them explicitly. It also differs from ordinary essentialism, since the essential properties are acknowledged to be present. At the same time, the separation of an accidental feature from an essential form—treating color and shape as if they could be pulled apart to deny membership—recalls the logic of the white horse dialogue (Bai ma fei ma) associated with Gongsun Long and the School of Names (Mingjia). But that dialogue is usually read as a puzzle about the relation between universals and particulars, or about the semantics of class terms, whereas the move I am describing is deployed selectively within a contested taxonomy, using an accidental property as a kind of “counter-essence” that nullifies membership without altering the definition.

My questions are these. First, is there an established term for this specific configuration—the selective nullification of category membership by an acknowledged accidental feature? Second, if one situates it near the white horse dialogue and the School of Names, how exactly should we understand the relation: is it a modern analog, a misapplication of that logic, or a genuinely distinct form? Third, how should it be distinguished from adjacent phenomena such as the “no true Scotsman” fallacy, family-resemblance rejection of necessary conditions, and what we might call “purity essentialism”? I am not asking for a bibliography or a literature survey; I am asking for the conceptual carving—what the phenomenon is, what it is not, and where its edges lie. If there is no established name, I would welcome a precise coinage with clear boundary conditions and a brief account of how it diverges from the nearest logical relatives.
