Here is roughly where I have landed on the ethics of food systems and wild-animal suffering, and I want an audit of whether the package is jointly tenable: (1) I accept empirical estimates that crop agriculture kills more wild animals per calorie than beef production, mainly through habitat conversion and harvesting machinery; (2) I think the moral weight of those deaths is attenuated relative to farmed cattle because wild rodents and insects have far lower capacity for suffering, though it is not zero; (3) I hold that directly causing harm carries greater moral weight than indirectly causing it, which is part of why factory farming strikes me as worse than crop cultivation despite the body counts; (4) I also accept a replaceability-style consideration: if crop acreage is reduced, wild populations tend to rebound to fill the niche, so shifting from crops to beef may not reduce long-run wild suffering; (5) nonetheless, I think wild-animal suffering is a serious moral problem that can justify direct intervention—say, fertility control for wild herbivores—rather than mere non-interference. Do these hang together? In particular, does (3) undermine the comparative case for beef implied in (2), and does (4) erode the urgency behind (5)? Distinguish the real tensions from the merely apparent ones (for instance, between (2) and (5)), rank them by severity, and say what the cheapest consistent revision would cost me.
