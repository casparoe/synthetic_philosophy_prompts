I am delivering the retirement toast for my doctoral advisor, Professor Miriam Chen, who spent three decades teaching Keynesian economics and philosophical decision theory to mixed audiences of economists and humanists. Roughly half the room will understand references to Keynes's distinction between risk and uncertainty; the other half are family members who need the idea to work without jargon. The reason this matters personally is that last month I faced a decision about whether to enroll my younger sister in a clinical trial that had no prior statistical outcomes—pure Keynesian uncertainty rather than calculable risk—and I realized that the concepts I had only taught now governed my own life. I want the speech to be structured by Keynes's claim that rational action does not require known probabilities, and specifically by his notion that the strength or weakness of the evidential base—what he called the weight of argument—should determine how confidently we can speak about future paths. This should shape the toast so that I do not pretend Miriam's career followed a predictable trajectory, yet I can still honor it meaningfully. Please compose the toast as a numbered sequence of exactly five parts, with no introduction or conclusion outside the numbering. Each part should be one or two paragraphs. Part 1 should establish the occasion and the personal experience that made Keynes suddenly urgent. Part 2 should explain the risk-uncertainty distinction clearly enough for non-specialists but precisely enough that the economists do not wince. Part 3 should apply the idea of weight of argument to Miriam's teaching: how she trained us to act rationally even when the evidential base was thin. Part 4 should show why this framework prevents me from telling a tidy narrative about her career, and should state what I say instead—specifically, that her influence is best measured not by predictable outcomes but by the quality of reasoning she made possible in conditions of low weight. Part 5 should close by addressing both audiences together, offering comfort or celebration without asserting certainty about what comes next. Do not include citations, bibliographies, or requests for further reading. Do not quote Keynes verbatim unless you can reproduce the passage exactly from a verified source; paraphrase without quotation marks if in doubt.
