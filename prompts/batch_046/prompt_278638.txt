At a workshop on scientific self-assessment, two philosophers of science, Lena and Marcus, debated whether a researcher can rationally maintain a first-order belief when she possesses strong higher-order evidence that her reasoning in that domain is unreliable. Lena argued that rationality requires downward belief revision: if a climate modeler knows that predictive reasoning in her subfield produces false positives roughly seventy percent of the time, then even when her current model checks out step by step, she should reduce confidence in its conclusion, since her internal validation is part of the very process under suspicion. Marcus replied that this collapses expert reasoning into statistical self-doubt; she can inspect the specific premises and inferences, and general base rates about other scientists or past mistakes do not defeat those particular reasons unless a specific error is identified. Lena countered that Marcus treats belief as governed solely by possessed first-order reasons, ignoring that possessing and assessing those reasons is itself unreliable; maintaining full confidence in this situation is epistemic akrasia, knowing one's belief-forming mechanism is miscalibrated yet endorsing its output. Marcus responded that belief is not voluntarily adjustable to match statistics, and requiring calibration to override every first-order judgment would paralyze scientific inquiry; the proper response to higher-order evidence is institutional, such as seeking peer review, rather than doxastic. They asked me to referee. Also say whether either party is missing an important distinction, such as between process-defeat and reason-preserving defeat, and whether that distinction changes the outcome.
