I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy training, and I've become puzzled by a set of practices at my firm that seem to treat our company's past in contradictory ways. Every quarter we produce two parallel documents about our technical history: an immutable 'audit archive' that records every architecture decision, including the mistakes and the reasoning behind them at the time, which is kept untouched for compliance and onboarding reference; and a 'strategic narrative' that is actively rewritten to frame those same past decisions as necessary precursors to our current product direction, sometimes altering the emphasis or omitting the original doubts. Alongside this, we hold 'monumental release retrospectives' in which we celebrate past engineering breakthroughs — a particularly elegant caching layer from 2019, say — as timeless achievements worthy of emulation, regardless of whether the technology is now obsolete. I can see practical reasons for each practice, but together they feel philosophically incoherent: we preserve the past with antiquarian reverence in one folder, destroy or reshape it for present utility in another, and worship selected past achievements as models in a third. I am not asking for management advice, but I want to understand what implicit philosophy of history — specifically along the lines of Nietzsche's distinction between monumental, antiquarian, and critical history — these practices collectively presuppose. Does maintaining an untouched archive presuppose that the past has intrinsic value independent of present life? Does the rewritten strategic narrative presuppose that history is legitimate only when it serves current vitality, which sounds like critical history? And do the celebratory retrospectives presuppose a monumental view that treats past greatness as a direct model for action? More precisely, what view of institutional identity over time must be in place for a single organisation to endorse all three stances without contradiction? If the underlying presupposition is that history should always be subordinated to present flourishing, why do we keep the immutable archive at all — and what would have to change in our rituals and record-keeping if we genuinely adopted that view consistently?

In answering, please separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who understand this area, and do not present a contested interpretation as if it were the consensus. I am not looking for bibliographies, citations, or reading lists — I am trying to make sense of the idea myself.
