Write a formal philosophical dialogue, composed in polished British English, between Dr. Elena Vasquez, who defends a broadly Davidsonian account of akrasia, and Professor Samuel Okonkwo, who contends that hyperbolic discounting reveals not weakness of will but a structural fragmentation of diachronic agency.

Vasquez holds that akrasia is an evaluative conflict within a single, unified practical reasoner: the agent acts against her all-things-considered judgement because a stronger, non-rational desire overrides it, yet the subject remains one agent across time. Okonkwo rejects this unity. He proposes that hyperbolic discount curves demonstrate the self is better understood as a federation of temporal stages with competing authority; on his view, yielding to immediate gratification is not a failure of will within one agent but a legitimate victory of the present-stage constituency over a future-stage constituency that possesses no inherent normative priority.

In the dialogue, the two should engage in genuine back-and-forth, responding directly to each other’s points rather than talking past one another. They should address specifically:

(1) Whether precommitment strategies—such as Ulysses binding himself to the mast—support Vasquez’s claim that a unified agent can delegate authority to her future self, or instead support Okonkwo’s claim that present and future selves are distinct parties requiring contractual enforcement.

(2) Whether hyperbolic discounting describes a descriptive cognitive bias or a normative feature of intertemporal fairness, with Okonkwo introducing the analogy of spatial parochialism: just as we do not regard caring more about nearby suffering than distant suffering as irrational, steep discounting of future welfare might represent a form of temporal partiality that moral development must extend rather than eliminate.

(3) Whether secular moral psychology, lacking any metaphysical account of personal identity over time (such as a Cartesian ego or substantial soul), can justify why the present self ought to defer to the future self, or whether it must treat diachronic choice as a bargaining problem between equally legitimate temporal agents.

The dialogue should avoid strawman positions, should not invoke bibliographies, reading lists, or specialist citations, and should be accessible to a seminar audience familiar with practical reason without requiring advanced training in behavioural economics. Write it as a philosophy professor might assign it to a graduate seminar on action theory.
