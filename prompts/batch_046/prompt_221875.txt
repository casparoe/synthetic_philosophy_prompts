Question: State Good's theorem on the value of information with precision, identifying the conditions—particularly the decision-theoretic setup—under which information is guaranteed to have non-negative value for an expected-utility maximizer. Then evaluate the following objection: an agent updates beliefs using a non-Bayesian rule with a multiplicative confidence bias β > 1, so that noisy adverse signals cause her to shift beliefs away from the true state more aggressively than Bayes' rule allows. A commentator argues that for such an agent, acquiring free information can reduce ex-ante expected utility, because she may overreact and then take a worse action than she would have with her prior alone. Does this genuinely undermine Good's result, or does it describe a different decision problem? In your answer, clarify the relationship, if any, between this belief-updating failure and Savage's axiomatization of expected utility—specifically the sure-thing principle (P2).

Student answer: Good's theorem states that for any decision problem with actions A and states S, if an agent maximizes expected utility with prior μ over S and can observe a signal σ about the state at zero cost before choosing a ∈ A, then conditioning her action on σ yields expected utility at least as high as committing to the best prior action. Formally, max_{δ∈Δ(A|σ)} 𝔼[U(a,s)] ≥ max_{a∈A} 𝔼[U(a,s)]. The proof partitions on σ, applies the law of total expectation, and takes the best action in each cell.

Regarding the biased agent: since she updates with β > 1 rather than Bayes' rule, she is not maximizing expected utility relative to μ, so a premise of Good's theorem fails. Specifically, she violates Savage's axiom P2—the sure-thing principle—because her preference ordering between actions can change depending on which signal path produced the same outcome pair, which means outcomes that should be treated identically are not. Therefore the theorem doesn't apply, but this doesn't undermine it; it only shows the result is conditional on correct probabilistic reasoning. The commentator is right that free information can hurt her, but that harm comes from her irrational updating, not from any flaw in Good's proof. If she pre-commits to ignoring the signal, she avoids the damage and reverts to her prior-optimal action, confirming that the problem lies in her belief revision rather than in the value of information itself.
