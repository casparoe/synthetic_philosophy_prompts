I'm taking a course on collective choice and we got sidetracked talking about this over coffee. You start with the standard idea that in some small domain — say, whether I wear a hat indoors or what I read alone — the decision should be mine alone, not up for a vote. Then you point out that if you apply that consistently across a group, you can get weird results where everyones individual rights are technically respected but the overall arrangement is worse for all of them, or where shifting who holds the right (without changing anyone's actual preferences) produces a better outcome that everyone should want. The twist we noticed is that when you propose redrawing the boundary of that private domain — not taking anyone's preference away, just reorgnizing which choices count as yours versus ours — people still resist, not because they care about the specific outcome, but because the visible line between private and public feels like it has to stay put. Its like they're defending the map, not the terrritory. Is there an established name for this exact thing? I know its somewhere near Sen's liberal paradox, but that usually focuses on direct conflicts between minimal liberty and Pareto efficiency; here the conflict isn't with efficiency, its with a kind of structural conservatism about rights-boundaries themselves, even when restructuring them hurts nobody and changes no preferences. If there's no specific term, can you coin one and say exactly where it differs from Sen's paradox, from plain status-quo bias, and from just being confused about rights?
