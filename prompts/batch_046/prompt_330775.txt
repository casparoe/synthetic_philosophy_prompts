Write a Shakespearean sonnet on Wittgenstein's view that religious belief is not a failed scientific hypothesis but a form of life held together by its own grammar. In the octave, present belief as though it were competing with physics, with rhymes pairing evidential terms—proof with truth, reason with season—so the form mimics an epistemic contest. At the volta in line nine, shift to the idea that "God" functions like a chess piece whose meaning is exhausted by its moves within prayer, mourning, and commitment rather than by any explanatory role; let the sestet's rhymes pair practical and ritual vocabulary instead of evidential vocabulary, so the rhyme scheme itself enacts the philosophical turn. The closing couplet should resolve that asking whether the belief is true in the scientific sense misses the point entirely, comparable to asking whether a bishop's move is legal outside the game of chess. Make the poem precise enough that a reader familiar with Wittgenstein's philosophy of religion recognizes the distinction between belief as a captiving picture and belief as a lived practice, without relying on named texts or quotations.
