I am moderating coursework for an upper-level philosophy seminar on rational choice under self-locating uncertainty, and I require an objective, point-by-point grading report on a student’s response. The exam problem presented a modified absent-minded driver scenario: a courier must take Exit B (the second exit) to reach a warehouse paying £100; Exit A (the first) pays £20; missing both pays £0. If she takes Exit A, she may suffer a transient disorientation that leaves her unsure, upon seeing another exit labelled A, whether she is revisiting Exit A after a physical loop or whether she has reached Exit B but her navigation display is mislabelling it; she assigns equal credence to these two hypotheses. The question asked students to (i) compute the expected-utility comparison between exiting and continuing at that decision point, (ii) determine the ex ante optimal plan, and (iii) analyse whether causal and evidential decision theories genuinely diverge in their recommendations for this case.

Student answer: “At the node, the courier assigns 50% credence to being at the first exit and 50% to being at the second exit (having looped back after missing B). Exiting yields £20 with certainty; continuing yields £100 if she is at the first exit and £0 if she is at the second, so the expected utility of continuing is 0.5 × £100 + 0.5 × £0 = £50, which exceeds £20; therefore she should continue. Ex ante, committing to this policy means she applies it at every occurrence of Exit A. At the first occurrence, continuing reaches B (£100); since the plan never directs her to take Exit A, she will never loop back, so in practice there is no second decision point. This resolves the tension: causal decision theory recommends continuing because the causal consequence of doing so at the first node is reaching B, while evidential decision theory examines the evidence that continuing provides about her location, but because her credences are already fixed at 50/50, this evidential consideration is redundant and also supports continuing. Hence both theories agree, and ex post reasoning should always be subordinated to the ex ante plan, confirming that rationality here is fully objective.”
