Consider an institutional peer-review protocol adopted by a consortium of interdisciplinary journals with the explicit aim of disrupting echo chambers and epistemic bubbles. Every submitted manuscript is evaluated by three reviewers, each assigned from a distinct, labeled theoretical framework—say, mechanistic, functionalist, and phenomenological. A paper is accepted only if it achieves a passing evaluation in at least two of the three frameworks; it is automatically rejected if any single framework assigns it a failing grade, regardless of enthusiastic endorsements from the other two. The architects argue that this prevents insular consensus, yet critics observe that it systematically favors methodologically eclectic work and penalizes deep, framework-specific breakthroughs that one paradigm cannot assess.

What philosophical commitments about scientific knowledge, justification, and progress does this practice presuppose? Does it treat epistemic bubbles as harmful only when they are homogeneous, or does it implicitly assume that cross-framework agreement is a necessary condition for justified belief? Can a Kuhnian picture of paradigm-bound scientific development accommodate a rule that lets one framework veto findings endorsed by two others, or does the protocol instead presuppose a unified, framework-transcendent conception of evidence? Is there an internal tension between the anti-bubble rationale—meant to expose researchers to disconfirming viewpoints—and the institutional willingness to allow one viewpoint to unilaterally block publication? And if the underlying theory of knowledge were replaced with a strongly perspectival or incommensurability-based view, which parts of the protocol would become incoherent and which might survive?
