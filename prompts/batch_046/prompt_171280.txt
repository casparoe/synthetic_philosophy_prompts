I'm working on a column about governance and moral mess for the ideas desk, and I've been tracking this regional ethics board that rules on dirty-hands cases for public officials. Thier track record is puzzling: they said the governor who seized private property using emergency powers never meant for that purpose, in order to build a public-health clinic, had dirty hands but should stay in office; they said the mayor who deliberately lowballed crime statistics in a report to prevent capital flight, then corrected them quietly months later, was dirty hands and deserved formal censure; they said the council member who voted to eliminate food aid for 200 families in order to fund a bridge that saves 2,000 lives wasn't dirty hands at all, just democratic tragedy; and they said the diplomat who fabricated intelligence to convince an ally to sign a climate treaty that actually worked was dirty hands and should be fired.

Now they've got this archivist case I want to understand. The city archivist uncovers proof that the 1920s land grant for the central park was forged with bribed surveyors. Without asking council, she moves $2 million from the parks maintanence budget into a restitution fund for the displaced families, then holds a press conference revealing both the historical fraud and the transfer. She never lied or concealed anything after the fact; she just violated budget authority.

If the board wants to be consistent with thier own precedents, should they call this dirty hands? Which of the earlier cases is the closest analog - the unauthorized emergency powers, or maybe the diplomat? If thier precedents genuinely don't settle it, what principle are they adopting if they rule one way versus the other? And what's the one further hypothetical case that would actually tell us which rule they're running?
