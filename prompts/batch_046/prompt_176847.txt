I'm trying to work out my actual judgments about directness, politeness, and white lies across cultural contexts, and whether there's one principle behind them or whether I have to abandon some. Here are my verdicts.

A Dutch colleague emails me, "Your regression is misspecified and your conclusion is unsupported" — I think that's acceptable, even preferable, because it is task-focused and saves time.

A Japanese manager replies to my proposal with "This is very interesting; we should study it further," then quietly shelves it. I think the indirectness is frustrating and somewhat blameworthy, but less wrong than an open insult would be.

Telling my child her drawing is beautiful when it isn't: that's fine.

Telling a peer their business plan is good when I think it will fail: that's not fine.

As a junior researcher, I think it is acceptable for my Dutch senior to tell me my analysis is sloppy in blunt terms. But if I, in the same junior role, tell my Japanese senior that their strategy is flawed using equally explicit language, I feel that is wrong, even though both statements are truthful and task-relevant. The asymmetry troubles me.

I keep going back and forth on this case: in a Japanese workplace I notice that indirect feedback is causing repeated errors. I tell a colleague privately and directly, "Your indirect hints are creating confusion; please tell people clearly when you disagree." Is that permissible corrective directness, or does it violate the very cultural frame I'm appealing to?

My tentative principle is that direct corrective speech is permissible when (i) the content is task-relevant and the listener needs it to correct an error, (ii) the speaker holds an institutional or relational license to correct that is recognized within the relevant cultural frame, and (iii) the directness does not violate a face-protection expectation the listener has not waived. Does this capture all my verdicts? If not, which clause or verdict must I drop — especially the upward-directness judgment versus the downward one? Please state the repaired principle precisely, say what it predicts for the private-correction case above, and for a further case I haven't listed: a Dutch manager working in Tokyo tells her Japanese team, "In this office we speak plainly; if my idea is bad, say so directly." Which of my original judgments does the best version of the principle have to sacrifice?
