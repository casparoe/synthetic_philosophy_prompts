You serve as vice-chair of your university’s Capital Projects Review Board, which must issue a formal recommendation on a $340 million coastal-resilience grant and on a proposed procedural amendment called the Modesty Protocol. The engineering team submitted a detailed inside-view assessment—site-specific hydrological modeling, bespoke material stress tests, and local stakeholder input—projecting 92 percent structural reliability over thirty years. A separate institutional risk office applied an outside-view benchmark drawn from a reference class of 147 comparable megaprojects in high-income countries since 1990, finding that 78 percent exceeded cost projections by more than 40 percent and that planning-phase reliability estimates were systematically over-optimistic by an average factor of 1.6. The board’s charter requires your memo to recommend approval, conditional approval, or rejection of the grant, and separately to recommend adoption, modification, or rejection of the Modesty Protocol.

The Protocol contains three provisions: (i) every future capital-grant evaluation must include an explicit outside-view benchmark tied to a defined reference class; (ii) lead reviewers must file a written statement of their own past forecasting accuracy on comparable judgments; and (iii) a “deference trigger” requires that if the inside and outside assessments diverge by more than one standard deviation within the reference class, the proposal is automatically deferred to an external audit rather than approved outright.

Board members are split. Some argue that the inside view should dominate here because the design incorporates genuinely novel adaptive features—specifically, a modular tidal-barrier system not present in the historical reference class—that place the project outside the base-rate distribution. Others contend that claims of uniqueness are precisely the cognitive bias the outside view corrects, and that allowing inside-view override renders the benchmark ornamental. A further philosophical disagreement concerns epistemic modesty: should the board apply the outside view reflexively to its own decision-making record—acknowledging that university review boards evaluating megaprojects have poor historical calibration—or is that second-order skepticism institutionally paralyzing? Some members also worry that mandatory reviewer self-audits and automatic deferral will chill innovative but genuinely sui generis proposals, substituting bureaucratic conservatism for engineering judgment.

Draft the board’s decision memo as a formal, actionable document. Take a definite position on whether inside-view detail can ever legitimately override an outside-view baseline and, if so, under what precise conditions; on whether epistemic modesty requires institutional self-reference (applying the reference class to the board itself); and on whether the deference trigger is a prudent safeguard or an abdication of expert judgment. Recommend a specific course on both the grant and the Protocol, with clear reasoning and explicit conditions if you choose conditional approval. Include an honest, substantive treatment of the strongest consideration on the losing side—particularly the concern that mandatory outside-view benchmarks and reviewer forecasting disclosures will suppress innovative proposals that genuinely fall outside historical patterns. Do not present a menu of options; the memo should read as a committed recommendation the board can vote on directly.
