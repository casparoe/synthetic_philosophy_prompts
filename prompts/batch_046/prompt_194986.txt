I've been working through some philosophy of mind material in an independent study course I'm taking, and after a long discussion with a friend who studies cognitive neuroscience I've settled on a specific view about how the brain handles temporal anomalies in conscious experience. I hold that in cases like the color phi phenomenon—where observers report seeing a single dot change color in mid-flight even though two differently colored dots were flashed in rapid succession—the correct model of revision is Stalinesque rather than Orwellian: the brain does not preserve an initial "draft" of experience that gets subsequently rewritten and overwritten in memory, but instead never generates a contradictory intermediate representation at all, airbrushing the discontinuity out of the narrative before it ever reaches awareness. Applied more broadly, I think this means that apparent postdictive effects in consciousness—whether in visual motion illusions or in the temporal delays suggested by readiness-potential studies—are best understood not as revisions of an already-formed record but as the absence of any record that needs revision; the brain constructs one coherent timeline and suppresses the machinery that would have produced conflicting drafts, leaving no archival trace accessible even in principle. What would genuinely count against this position? I want the evidence and arguments that should move me, sorted by how decisive they would be: specifically, whether there are empirical conditions under which subjects can recover or report contradictory fragments of experience that should have been airbrushed away; whether formal accounts of predictive processing require the preservation of error signals that function like unrevised drafts; and whether there are conceptual reasons to think that suppression without prior generation is neurologically or informationally implausible. I also want an honest assessment of whether the view predicts anything its Orwellian rival does not, whether its defenders would concede specific findings as damaging, and whether it has already been qualified into unfalsifiability—if so, whether that insulation is a problem for a view that aims to explain the actual timing of conscious experience rather than merely redescribe it.
