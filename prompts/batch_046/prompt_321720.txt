A recent summary in an unnamed policy essay outlines what it calls the "institutional reversal thesis" about global wealth differences. It makes four claims. First, that geographic determinism—climate, disease ecology, and resource endowments—has been decisively refuted by the reversal of fortune: territories with high pre-colonial urbanization and dense populations, such as the Aztec and Inca domains, are now relatively poor, while sparse settler colonies like North America and Australia are wealthy, which proves geography cannot directly cause development. Second, that the institutionalist explanation treats this reversal as a quasi-experiment: settler mortality rates determined whether Europeans built extractive or inclusive institutions, and because mortality was driven by exogenous disease ecology rather than local economic potential, institutional quality can be treated as randomly assigned with respect to modern outcomes, yielding clean causal inference about institutions' effect on GDP. Third, that culture is not an independent causal variable but a fully mediated one—any correlation between norms such as the Protestant work ethic and growth vanishes once you control for formal property-rights protection, because institutions generate the incentive structures that shape culture; consequently, holding culture constant is conceptually confused and unnecessary. Fourth, that the practical implication is institutional convergence: if a poor country formally adopts the rules of rich ones—independent judiciaries, secure contracts, broad political participation—it should eventually reach comparable income levels regardless of geographic or cultural starting points.

How fair is this summary? Please audit it point by point. For each claim, indicate what a specialist in comparative economic history or causal inference would accept, what they would qualify, and whether any distortion is a subtle misrepresentation of the institutionalist position rather than an outright error. In particular, address whether the identification strategy is described correctly: does settler mortality provide exogenous variation in the sense implied, or does the summary conflate an instrumental variable with true random assignment and ignore debates about exclusion restrictions? Also assess whether the dismissal of culture as purely mediated overstates what institutionalists actually maintain about the interaction between formal rules and informal norms. I am inclined to believe the summary is essentially correct but perhaps overconfident about policy convergence; please correct me if that inclination itself overlooks a deeper methodological problem.
