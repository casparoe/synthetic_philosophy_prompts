ok so I just found out some director is making a biopic about my grandfather who died in 2014, and I was already drafting a screenplay about him, so suddenly this isnt abstract anymore. Here are the views Ive landed on and I need a consistency check before I defend them at a family dinner.

I beleive:
(1) dead people cant literally be wronged like living people can - they dont suffer, have no interests now, so the moral weight of dramatizing them is lighter.
(2) still, inventing that he was an alcoholic or a thief if he wasnt feels genuinely disrespectful, not just "artistic license."
(3) if you use real names and bill it "based on true events" you have a stricter duty to the factual record than with pure fiction "inspired by" someone.
(4) emotional truth matters more than chronological accuracy - compressing timelines or writing dialogue that captures his voice is fine.
(5) the real harm is usually to living relatives - my mom seeing her dad called a crook is the injury, not something to grandpa.
(6) writers should still be protected from censorship or lawsuits by hurt relatives, as long as its not malicious defamation.

Do these hang together? Specifically: if dead people cant be wronged (1), where does the disrespect in (2) come from? Is it just sentiment? And if the harm is always to the living (5), why does using real names (3) change my obligations - cant relatives be hurt by an "inspired by" story too? Also (4) says emotional truth > facts, but (3) demands stricter fidelity with real names - isnt that pulling opposite directions? Rank the tensions by severity and tell me the least costly revision. I really want to keep (6) intact if possible, and I definatly think artistic license should cover dialogue.
