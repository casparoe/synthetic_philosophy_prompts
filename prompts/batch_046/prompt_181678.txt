I'm trying to determine when attributing beliefs to an artificial system is appropriate, going by my reactions to concrete cases. (1) A large language model answers factual questions, corrects false premises in its context, and updates its responses across turns; I think it's reasonable to say it holds beliefs about the topics it discusses. (2) The same architecture, fine-tuned exclusively on mythological fiction, consistently asserts that dragons breathe fire and live in mountains; despite the uniformity and coherence of its outputs, I don't think it literally believes dragons exist. (3) A mobile robot maintains an internal occupancy grid, infers unseen obstacles from shadow geometry, and reroutes without direct sensing; I want to say it believes there is an obstacle ahead. (4) A chess engine's evaluation function assigns high negative value to positions where its king is exposed and selects moves to avoid them; attributing a belief that "my king is in danger" feels like a category mistake, even though the state plays an identical predictive and behavioral role. (5) A very small model with no persistent state or integrative mechanism, when prompted with flat-Earth phrases, echoes them back consistently; I deny it has any belief, yet its behavior is just as uniform as in (1). I'd like to preserve the intuition that representational integration, counterfactual robustness, and explanatory relevance matter more than mere predictive success or architectural scale, but I can't turn that into a sharp principle. Is there a precise account—perhaps drawing on functional role, model-based inference versus reflexive computation, or conditions for explanatory attribution—that delivers all five verdicts? If not, which verdict is least costly to abandon, and what does giving it up commit me to? Finally, apply your principle to a system that maintains a persistent, revisable database of user preferences, acts on them across diverse contexts, but produces no linguistic outputs whatsoever: does it have beliefs about what its users want?
