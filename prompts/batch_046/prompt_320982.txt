Compare Zhuangzi's philosophical response to death—grounded in the notion of continuous transformation (wuhua) and in practices such as fasting the mind (xinzhai) and sitting in forgetfulness (zuowang)—with Epicurus's atomist argument that death is the dissolution of the soul and therefore nothing to the one who dies. Focus your comparison on the problem of diachronic personal identity across the boundary of death: does Zhuangzi's process-oriented ontology preserve a form of continuity that makes mourning conceptually appropriate, or does it dissolve the subject so thoroughly that grief becomes a category error? Conversely, does Epicurus's denial of post-mortem experience successfully undermine the rationality of fearing death, or does it fail to address the well-known asymmetry between our indifference toward pre-birth non-existence and our anxiety about future non-existence? Defend a verdict on which framework offers the more coherent account of whether death ought to be feared, and identify the precise ontological or ethical step at which the two views become genuinely incommensurable.
