I've been thinking, without any formal background, about whether religious belief actually makes people more moral, or whether a secular society can sustain honesty and care through trust alone. I just finished The Brothers Karamazov, and it seems to sit directly in the middle of that question.

The novel gives us Alyosha, trying to live out an active Christian love; Ivan, who rejects God partly because he thinks human cruelty makes divine justice impossible; and the Grand Inquisitor, who argues that people need miracle, mystery, and authority to be good and happy because freedom is too heavy for them. There is also Dmitri's secular trial and the group of boys Alyosha gathers after Ilyusha's death.

I'd like a reading that treats the book as more than an illustration of the debate. Specifically: does Dostoevsky show that Alyosha's faith produces a genuinely different kind of moral behavior than Ivan's secular humanism, or does he show that both are performances that crack under pressure? When we ask whether religion makes people more moral, are we talking about private virtue, about sustaining social trust, or about being able to bear responsibility without collapsing? Do the secular legal process and the boys' community function as parallel experiments in whether trust can survive without shared religious belief?

I want to know whether the novel ultimately supports the idea that secular societies cannot sustain the trust needed for moral life, or whether it undermines that idea by showing religious people failing just as badly. Don't just summarize the philosophy; tell me what the novel adds that a straight argument alone cannot capture.
