Exam question:
Some commentators distinguish “sentimental calibration”—training one’s own feelings to align with expert responses—from “sentimental correction”—treating an expert verdict as an overriding reason to reject one’s own feeling. In Hume’s essay on the standard of taste, does his appeal to true judges involve calibration, correction, or neither? And does this distinction help resolve whether his sentimentalism about beauty is consistent with his claim that some tastes are genuinely better than others?

Student answer:
Hume defends a consistent sentimentalism: beauty is not a quality in objects but a feeling in the perceiver, matching his broader view that value judgments express sentiments. To handle disagreement, he appeals to true judges—those with delicacy of imagination, extensive practice, comparison across works, and freedom from prejudice. Their agreement establishes the standard of taste. This process is best understood as sentimental calibration, not correction: we refine our own feelings by studying expert responses, rather than overriding our feeling on authority. Because reason is the slave of the passions, Hume cannot allow reason to correct sentiment even here; instead, practice and comparison sharpen the feeling itself. The true judges thus function like calibrated instruments, not like scientists detecting properties. This avoids objectivism, since there is no fact of the matter about taste except what qualified critics happen to agree on; their actual consensus constitutes the standard. Unlike Kant, who demands disinterested contemplation free from all feeling, Hume keeps feeling central, merely disciplining it through experience. So Hume resolves the tension without contradiction: a disciplined sentiment yields universal agreement without positing objective beauty.
