I host a podcast on philosophy of mind and science, and my next guest is a philosopher who defends a strict, updated version of heterophenomenology—the method, originally articulated by Daniel Dennett, that treats first-person reports of conscious experience as texts describing a fictional heterophenomenological world rather than as direct windows onto an inner phenomenal reality. She pushes the view beyond its origins: she argues that this method is not merely a pragmatic constraint for consciousness science but an ontological claim that the heterophenomenological world is the only scientifically tractable domain of consciousness, and she has recently applied it to debates about large language models, contending that if an LLM produces a coherent first-person narrative about having experiences, heterophenomenology obliges researchers to include that narrative in the dataset without requiring any underlying phenomenal substrate. I want eight to ten interview questions that avoid having her simply restate the basic distinction between the heterophenomenological world and the physical world. I need at least one question pressing the tension between treating reports as authoritative texts and clinical cases like anosognosia or split-brain denial, where the narrative seems internally inconsistent or contradicted by third-person neuroscience; at least one on how her view differs from neurophenomenology and other first-person methods that claim direct, trained access to phenomenal structure; and at least one on the LLM extension—specifically, what distinguishes a synthetic heterophenomenological narrative that should be included from one that is mere stochastic parroting, and whether this criterion collapses back into behavioral or functional assessment. For each of these three, include a follow-up keyed to her most probable reply: for the clinical case, she will likely say that the narrative remains the only data available and that contradictions merely map the boundaries of that particular fictional world; for the neurophenomenology comparison, she will likely say that even disciplined introspection still produces texts rather than unmediated contact with phenomenal structure; and for the LLM case, she will likely invoke coherence conditions internal to the narrative rather than external causal history. Beyond those, I want questions that probe whether her position dissolves the hard problem by reducing phenomenal consciousness to reportability, whether it can accommodate higher-order errors within the heterophenomenological world itself, and whether it leaves any conceptual space for experiences that might exist but remain permanently unreportable. The questions should be precise, anticipate her standard defenses, and include the follow-ups that make those defenses work harder rather than letting her settle into repetition.
