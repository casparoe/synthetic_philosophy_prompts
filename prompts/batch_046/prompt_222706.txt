I'm a retired ecologist -- spent thirty years tracking amphibian declines in the Pacific Northwest -- and lately I've been falling into philosphy about monuments and public memory. Next month I'm giving the opening remarks at a small interdisciplinary workshop on statues, street names, and the ethics of commemoration. The audience is split: half are historians and philosophers of memory, half are city planners and local activists who've actually sat on renaming committees. I need about ten minutes, and I want it to work for both groups without sounding like a lecture.

The concept I want to do the real structural work is something I'm calling 'mnemonic debt,' borrowed from ecological extinction debt -- the lag between habitat loss and actual disappearance. Removing a statue or renaming a street doesn't settle the moral account cleanly; it just shifts the burden into archives, family stories, or the silence where the pedestal used to stand. That should shape what I can and can't say: I can't celebrate removal as a pure victory, but I also can't treat the empty space as morally neutral. The speech needs to hold that tension for people who fought to take something down and for people who think the whole debate is just vandalism. Don't let me say anything false-comforting like 'they're watching over us' -- I'm not religious and that kind of language makes me cringe.

I used to think science was about settling questions with data, but this topic has taught me that some disagreements are structurally unsettled, and I want that humility in there without turning it into jargon.
