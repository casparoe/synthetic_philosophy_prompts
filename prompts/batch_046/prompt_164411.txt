"Plutarch's Parallel Lives are often read as moral biography, yet they differ structurally from medieval and early modern hagiography. Explain how Plutarch's practice of synkrisis (parallel comparison) is intended to produce moral learning, and evaluate whether this method avoids what we might call the "hagiographic fallacy" — the treatment of a single life as an imitable archetype. and heres the student answer (i copied it over, might have a typo):

"Plutarch writes Parallel Lives to show virtue is universal but takes different cultural shapes. By pairing Alexander with Caesar he uses synkrisis so the reader compares two ambitious men rather than copying one. This avoids the hagiographic fallacy because hagiography offers one archetype while Plutarch offers two contrasting ones. In the Life of Alexander Plutarch praises his drive but also shows his excess, and in Caesar he does the same with political cunning, so the comparison teaches critical judgment instead of imitation.

The exemplarity gap is the problem that ancient conditions are too different for direct imitation. Plutarch solves it by making comparison central: instead of asking "what would Alexander do?" we ask which paired life is closer to virtue. This relocates the gap into the comparison, but it doesnt close it, since we still lack a clear rule for choosing between them. That is actually good, because it forces the reader to do moral reasoning. Therefore comparative exemplarity doesnt fall into hagiography; it turns biography into a moral laboratory where the reader is the experimenter. This explains why later mirror-for-princes writers adopted Plutarch's comparative method, even though they usually kept only the praise and dropped the critical comparison."
