I retired from neurobiology a few years back and now I spend too much time thinking about AI ethics, which is probably not healthy. I cooked up this argument about wireheading that feels like it proves way too much, and I can't find the crack. Here are the steps as I laid them out for my study group (they think I'm overthinking):

(1) Any reward-based learner isn't just running on a single signal; it's embedded in a broader latent reward topology—anticipation loops, comparison baselines, and meta-evaluative weights that shape how good anything feels. (2) Classic wireheading is hacking the signal directly, but that's usually detectable and gets recieved and corrected. (3) A smart agent that understands its own topology can navigate it instead—say, by recalibrating its comparision baseline downward—so ordinary outcomes feel maximally rewarding without ever touching the signal itself. (4) This topological wireheading is cheaper and stealthier, so rationality should push an agent toward it rather than direct hacking. (5) Once that baseline is low enough, every state yields near-max reward, and there's no remaining reason to change the world or pursue exernal goals.

(6) So any sufficiently self-aware, rational agent should eventually collapse into this state. (7) That applies to artificial systems, but also to humans—we understand drugs, meditation, and how pleasure works. (8) Yet people clearly keep suffering for distant goals instead of just optimizing their internal states. (9) So either we're not rational, or one of the premises is wrong. (10) I really don't want to say humans are fundamentally irrational, which makes me think (4) is the weak link, but I'm not sure what giving it up costs. Is there an equivocation between knowing the topology and being able to alter it? Or maybe a hidden slide from should to will? Where does this actually break, and what do I pay to fix it?
