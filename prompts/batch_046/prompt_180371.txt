I'm writing to ask for an adjudication of a debate I witnessed last week between two colleagues—Jordan, who works on philosophy of biology, and Riley, who specializes in ethics—during an informal seminar on moral status across species boundaries. The discussion turned to whether moral patienthood is genuinely continuous or necessarily categorical, and they developed four successive moves that I found difficult to evaluate.

Jordan began by arguing that moral status should be treated as scalar rather than binary, anchored not in species membership but in the capacity for integrated predictive regulation—what Jordan called allostatic depth—ranging from minimal set-point maintenance in thermostats to complex anticipatory modeling in mammals. Jordan claimed that because evolutionary biology reveals continuous gradation without sharp discontinuities, assigning full moral standing to humans and none to insects is an arbitrary cutoff, and that a consistent framework should assign diminishing but non-zero moral weight across this spectrum.

Riley replied that this conflates descriptive complexity with normative standing. Even if cognitive capacities are graded, moral status functions as a gatekeeper for rights and protections that operate as on-off conditions in practice: either an entity has standing to be wronged, or it does not. Riley conceded that interests can vary in richness but insisted that the possession of interests at all—what Riley termed indexical vulnerability, the capacity for states to matter from a subject's own point of view—is binary. A thermostat lacks this entirely, Riley argued, not because its feedback loop is simple, but because there is no subjective perspective in which a temperature deviation counts as a harm.

Jordan then countered that Riley's appeal to indexical vulnerability smuggles in an unexamined essentialist boundary. Jordan pointed out that homeostatic organisms display precisely the kind of self-referential maintenance—regulating internal variables against external perturbations to preserve functional integrity—that underlies minimal indexical concern, and that evolutionary continuity makes any non-arbitrary line between mere mechanism and subject impossible to defend without circular appeal to human-like consciousness.

Riley's final move was to distinguish between allostatic capacity as a functional property and phenomenological presence as a normative condition. Riley granted that predictive regulation comes in degrees but argued that moral recognition requires not just functional integration but a form of reciprocal recognizability—the capacity to enter into normative relations—which creates a qualitative threshold rather than a quantitative slope. Without such a threshold, Riley concluded, Jordan's position collapses into the view that ethical obligations extend to weather systems and building climate controls in proportion to their regulatory sophistication, a reductio that shows the framework has lost its normative grip.

Please adjudicate these four moves point by point: rate each from clearly mistaken to clearly right, briefly justify each rating, and identify what important consideration—if any—both Jordan and Riley are missing in their framing of the dispute. Do not treat this as a request for citations or a reading list; I am interested solely in the conceptual structure of their arguments as presented.
