During a recent seminar, my instructor proposed an underexplored extension: rather than analyzing these dynamics at the level of individual minds, we might model them as emergent properties of competitive institutional ecologies, where organizations with formally distinct missions nonetheless evolve structurally similar strategic behaviors because of selection pressures in shared environments. This framing seems to decouple the thesis even further from psychology, but I'm unsure whether it strengthens or dissolves the normative urgency usually associated with alignment research.

Please write a dialogue between two equally capable graduate students discussing this ecological reframing. One student should defend the view that treating orthogonality and instrumental convergence as organizational-level selection effects actually makes them more robust—since they no longer depend on controversial assumptions about rational agency—while the other should argue that once you reduce these phenomena to evolutionary selection on institutions, you lose the specifically agent-centered predictive power that makes instrumental convergence a distinctive concern for AI safety rather than just a generic feature of competitive systems. Each speaker should engage directly with the other's points: the defender should address the loss of individual-level predictability, and the critic should address whether organizational goals are genuinely representational in a way that distinguishes them from pure selection dynamics. Avoid strawman arguments, do not request citations or reading lists, and keep the exchange focused on whether the ecological injection adds explanatory value or merely renames familiar evolutionary logic.
