I'm a journalist covering science and ideas, and I've been following this stall in the philosphers' debate over incommensurable values and hard choices — the kind where you have to pick between saving lives and preserving some irreplaceable cultural thing, and neither side thinks you can just weigh them on a scale. On one side you've got people who say these choices are genuinely hard because the values are on a par: there's no correct answer, but you can still rationally commit through a kind of authorative leap. On the other side are the comparabilists who insist rationality always demands a unique ordering, even if it's hidden or context-dependent, so calling something "hard" just means we haven't found the right comparison yet. They all cite the same museum-fire examples, they all reject simple utilitarian weighing, and yet after years nobody's budged. What's actually going on here — is it a verbal disagreement about what "rational" means, a hidden formal question about whether incomplete preference orderings are allowed, or bedrock intuition about whether choice without unique justification feels like failure? I have a hunch part of it is whether rationality requires unique justification or just authorative commitment, but I want a real diagnosis: what do the sides actually say that reveals the structure, what's genuinely settled vs genuinely contested, and don't present a contested view as the consensus. Also, if your diagnosis holds, what would actually move the needle — a formal result, a distinction, or should we just accept that no progress should be expected?
