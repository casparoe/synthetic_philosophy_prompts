After four decades measuring stellar parallax and correcting for atmospheric distortion, I have recently turned to moral philosophy and find myself preoccupied with the wrong-kind-of-reasons problem: the idea that an agent can perform the morally correct act while being moved by considerations that seem entirely external to morality's point, as though the normative weight of the reason has become decoupled from the descriptive mechanism of motivation. For the final meeting of my reading group, I want a villanelle — nineteen lines, two refrains, the interlocking A1/A2 rotation ending in a paired couplet — that stages this tension structurally rather than merely describing it. One refrain should affirm that the act was right; the other should insist that the reason was wrong. Through the successive rotations they should draw syntactically closer without ever coinciding in meaning, so that the closing couplet, where both refrains stand together, reads as an unresolved discord rather than a synthesis. The poem should be precise enough that someone familiar with the debate — especially the worry that reducing moral motivation to desire-satisfaction collapses the normative 'must' into a merely prudential 'would serve me well' — recognizes the conceptual problem being enacted, yet it should not cite, name, or allude to any specific philosopher or text. No bibliography, no annotations, no references to particular works: the form itself should carry the philosophical work.
