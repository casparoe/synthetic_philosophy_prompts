Many psychiatric institutions now maintain a split taxonomy: clinicians use a flexible, dialogical diagnostic framework in which labels are revised as patients internalize or resist them—treating classification as part of the therapeutic interaction—while epidemiologists and funding bodies rely on a fixed, non-revisable nosology for prevalence tracking and resource allocation. What philosophical commitments about the ontology of mental disorders and the epistemology of psychiatric kinds does this dual-track practice presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory: does it assume Hacking's looping effects are real but must be quarantined from statistical reality, or that there are genuinely two kinds of entities—"interactive kinds" in the clinic and "indifferent kinds" in the population? Assess whether the split is philosophically coherent or whether it quietly combines incompatible views of what makes a diagnosis true. Finally, describe what a unified practice—one that either fully embraced or fully rejected looping effects—would have to change about clinical procedure, epidemiological method, or institutional accountability.
