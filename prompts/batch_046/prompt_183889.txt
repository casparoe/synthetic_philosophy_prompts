I lead a philosophy discussion group for third-year undergraduates who have just finished studying Rawls on reflective equilibrium but have no background in decision theory or AI alignment. Next session I want to introduce the idea of coherent extrapolated volition — roughly, the claim that what we genuinely want is not our current, possibly contradictory preferences but the preferences we would hold after idealized reflection, coherence, and full information — together with its relation to idealized preferences in normative theory. I need a teaching plan rather than the lecture itself: the precise sequence in which the components must be introduced (beginning with reflective equilibrium and proceeding to the extrapolation step), the one concrete scenario that should carry the idea (I propose a town council deciding whether to preserve a crumbling historic bridge or replace it with efficient modern infrastructure, where residents voice conflicting immediate preferences but share deeper values about heritage and safety), the misconceptions this audience is likely to bring (for example, conflating idealization with elitism, or treating coherence as mere consistency of revealed choice), the point in the sequence where each misconception should be headed off, the specific check-for-understanding that would demonstrate the audience has grasped the gap between present desire and extrapolated volition, and what formal apparatus (utility functions, Bayesian updating, coherence conditions) should be explicitly excluded at this level and acknowledged as such. Focus on the dependency structure of the explanation and the misconception map.
