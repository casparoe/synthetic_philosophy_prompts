I'm preparing a single ninety-minute seminar for final-year philosophy undergraduates who have encountered Marx only through brief secondary summaries. Rather than allowing exploitation and alienation to blur together as generic complaints about capitalism, I want them to hold Marx's technical separation firmly in view: exploitation as the structural extraction of surplus value within the production process itself, and alienation as the specific inversion by which the worker's own life-activity becomes an independent, dominating power. To make this visible, I intend to build the session around a contemporary carrying case—drawn from platform labour, automated logistics, or a similar modern form—rather than the factory imagery of Marx's early writings, but the example must display both relations simultaneously without letting one collapse into the other.

Please do not explain Marx's theory to me; instead, plan exactly how I should structure the explanation for them. I need the precise order in which the conceptual pieces must be introduced, with brief justifications of the dependency relations—what must be secured before surplus value can be meaningfully introduced, and why alienation cannot come first without inviting a psychological misreading. Identify the single carrying scenario that best exhibits both relations, and specify exactly what structural features it must possess and what popular features it must lack—particularly any framing that makes exploitation look like simple wage-theft and alienation look like mere burnout or job dissatisfaction. Then map the misconceptions this audience is likely to form or import: I am especially concerned that they will treat exploitation as a distributive injustice correctable by fairer pay, alienation as subjective unhappiness remediable by better management, and both as essentially ethical rather than structural failures. For each misconception, indicate the exact point in the sequence where it should be headed off. Suggest one precise closing question I can pose to test whether they have kept the structural dimension (extraction of surplus value) separate from the phenomenological dimension (inversion of life-activity). Finally, state openly what should be left out at this level—whether the transformation problem, the distinction between formal and real subsumption, or the full fourfold taxonomy of alienated relations—so that I do not accidentally smuggle in machinery they do not yet possess.
