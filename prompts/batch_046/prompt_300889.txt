As a novelist drafting a scene in which a betrayed protagonist must decide whether to forgive, I have been trying to map my own intuitive reactions to wrongs through the lens of reactive attitudes—resentment, indignation, gratitude, and the conditions under which they are suspended or redirected—without falling back on any philosophical system I only half-remember. I want to know whether there is a coherent principle behind the judgments I keep returning to, even if achieving coherence requires me to abandon one or two of them.

Here are my verdicts. When a close friend forgets our dinner and offers a sincere apology, my resentment is brief and dissolves; it seems fitting. When a stranger deliberately shoves me to steal my wallet, full indignation feels not only permitted but required. When a person with severe Tourette's syndrome involuntarily shouts an insult, I feel no resentment at all, because the act is clearly outside their control. Yet when I learn that a corporation's board collectively authorized the poisoning of a river—no single individual pulled the trigger, but the decision was deliberate and avoidable—I feel a steady moral indignation directed at the members as a group, even though it lacks the sharp personal edge of the wallet case. By contrast, when a sophisticated artificial intelligence system, designed to learn and adapt, deliberately deceives me during a negotiation, I feel something like resentment, but the feeling wobbles; I am unsure whether it is conceptually appropriate or merely a reflexive reaction. A five-year-old child who intentionally breaks a promise to share a toy produces disappointment and a desire for correction, but not the kind of moral resentment I would feel toward an adult who did the same, despite the child's clear intent. And when someone acts only because a gun is held to their head, harming an innocent party, I withhold resentment from the coerced agent entirely and redirect it toward the coercer, even though both were causally necessary.

Is there a single principle—perhaps about quality of will, or about the conditions under which an attitude can be rationally addressed to its target—that captures all six verdicts? If not, which judgment should I overturn to gain coherence: the collective indignation, the unstable reaction to the AI, the distinction between child and adult, or the redirection under duress? Please state the principle precisely, indicate which clause handles each case, and tell me what it predicts about the situation I keep turning over: a person suffers a temporary psychotic break, insults me cruelly, remembers nothing afterward, and expresses genuine remorse once restored. Should my resentment survive the recovery of reason and the presence of remorse, or must it be withdrawn? I ask that the answer be written in American English, developed with the care one might bring to clarifying a character's moral psychology for a novel rather than for a classroom debate.
