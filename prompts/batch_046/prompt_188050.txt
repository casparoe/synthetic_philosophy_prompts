A colleague in my AI ethics seminar recently proposed that instrumental convergence is best understood through an analogy with evolutionary convergence—specifically, the independent emergence of flight in insects, birds, and bats. On this view, just as disparate lineages arrive at wings not because flight is “valued” but because aerodynamic physics makes it a robust solution to a common problem, so too do agents with radically different architectures and final goals converge on intermediate objectives—resource acquisition, self-preservation, and cognitive enhancement—because rationality itself imposes structural constraints analogous to aerodynamic ones. The analogy is meant to do heavy work: it is offered to show that these instrumental goals are not contingent preferences that might be designed away, but rather inevitable functional endpoints, as unavoidable for a capable agent as wings are for a flying creature. Meanwhile, the orthogonality thesis is said to be the other blade of the scissors—intelligence and final goals are independent in the way that evolutionary origin and flight morphology are independent—so that one cannot rely on greater intelligence to steer an agent away from these convergent instrumental pursuits.

Please audit this analogy in detail. First, lay out the explicit correspondence: what in the source domain (evolutionary convergence toward flight) plays the role of agent architectures, of final goals, of rational constraints, of the intermediate “flight-like” instrumental goals, and of the environment that selects for them? Second, identify the disanalogies that matter for the intended use: evolutionary convergence operates without foresight or strategic planning, depends on specific selective environments that may not obtain for artificial agents, and produces morphological traits rather than deliberately pursued subgoals; meanwhile, the orthogonality claim asserts a logical independence that biological taxonomy does not obviously mirror. Which of these disanalogies undermine the inference from evolutionary inevitability to rational inevitability? Third, does the analogy smuggle in a false impression that instrumental goals are “designed in” by nature rather than adopted strategically, and does that affect the argument that they are unavoidable? Fourth, and most importantly: does the conclusion—that superintelligent systems will necessarily pursue dangerous instrumental subgoals regardless of their final ends—survive once the disanalogies are fully on the table, or was the analogy carrying the normative and predictive weight all along? If the analogy fails, is there a better one—perhaps drawn from the logic of means-ends reasoning or from game-theoretic dominance—that could do the same work without the evolutionary baggage?
