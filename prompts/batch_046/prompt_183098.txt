I'm currently taking a course on logical empiricism and the philosophy of language, and I'm trying to consolidate my own position on the analytic-synthetic distinction after working through Quine's Two Dogmas of Empiricism and some recent commentary. Before I present these views in a seminar, I want to check whether they actually hang together.

Here are the commitments I have arrived at:

(1) Quine's central critique succeeds: there is no principled, non-circular distinction between truths that hold in virtue of meaning alone and truths that hold in virtue of fact. Attempts to ground the distinction in synonymy, convention, or reduction to experience all fail.

(2) I accept a broadly Quinean semantic holism: empirical significance attaches to the web of belief as a whole, and in principle any statement can be retained in the face of recalcitrant experience by adjusting other parts of the web.

(3) At the same time, I want to defend what I call functional encapsulation. Domains such as basic arithmetic, elementary logic, and parts of physical geometry are not insulated from revision because they are analytic. Rather, they are structurally embedded in so many inference patterns that revising them would require a global restructuring that is practically impossible for finite agents. The insulation is a matter of cognitive architecture and revision cost, not of semantic status.

(4) I reject conventionalism about logic: logical truths are not true by linguistic decision or free stipulation. Instead, I endorse a form of logical inferentialism according to which the meanings of logical constants are fixed entirely by their roles in valid inference. But I take these inferential roles to be discovered features of rational practice, not conventions we could freely alter.

(5) I think Quine's own argument implicitly relies on being able to separate the logical framework of a theory from its empirical content when he discusses how we might revise a theory by holding its logic constant and adjusting only factual claims. If holism is correct, this separation is pragmatic rather than principled. So Quine risks circularity: he uses a boundary that his own holism undermines in order to motivate the dissolution of that very boundary.

(6) I deny that mathematics is analytic. But I also resist treating mathematics as continuous with empirical science in an undifferentiated way. I want to say that mathematical truths are structurally synthetic: they belong to the synthetic web, yet they are constrained by internal coherence conditions and by functional encapsulation that make them far less revisable than ordinary empirical generalizations, without being analytically true.

My question is whether this package is jointly tenable. Specifically, does (3)'s functional encapsulation reintroduce the analytic-synthetic distinction under a different name? Does (4)'s inferentialism, combined with (1), collapse into the conventionalism I explicitly reject? Is (5) a fair charge of circularity, or does it misunderstand Quine's target, which is Carnap's notion of analyticity rather than all separation? And does (6)'s category of structurally synthetic do genuine philosophical work, or is it just an unstable midpoint?

Please rank the tensions by severity, identify which ones are genuine and which only appear problematic, say what the least costly revision would be and what it costs, and distinguish apparent conflicts from real ones. Also, please state how confident you are in your assessment overall and single out the specific point about which you are least sure.
