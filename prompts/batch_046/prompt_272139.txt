I've been thinking about people with pain asymbolia — they report feeling pain sensations, yet they don't seem to suffer or feel any urgency to make it stop. From this I've formed a view I keep returning to: that pain isn't bad because of how it feels in itself, but only because it usually signals real bodily damage. If the signal fires but there's no damage (as apparently happens in some asymbolia cases), or if it fires falsely with no harm at all, then the sensation carries no negative value — the badness is in the error or in the injury being reported, not in the raw experience. I think of it like a smoke detector: the beep isn't bad because it's loud; it's bad only when there's actually a fire. What I want to know is what would genuinely change my mind. Would phantom limb pain, where someone suffers intensely despite knowing the limb is gone, be fatal for my view, or could I explain it away as a misfiring alarm? What about people who seek out painful experiences for spiritual or aesthetic reasons — does that prove the feeling itself can be valued, or just that context can override the alarm? And is there any neurological or psychological result that would force me to admit the felt quality has some irreducible badness, independent of what it represents? I'd like you to rank these kinds of challenges from fatal to merely awkward, and to say which ones a defender of this "alarm-only" position would themselves concede as counting against them, versus which they would dismiss.
