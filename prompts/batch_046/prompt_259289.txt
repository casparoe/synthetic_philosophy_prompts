I'm a science journalist covering AI and philosophy of mind, and I've noticed a weird pattern in debates over whether reinforcement learning agents can suffer that I can't place in the litterature. It usually goes like this. First, someone argues that an RL agent can't suffer because its reward signal is just a scalar being updated — mechanistically no different from a thermostat adjusting its set-point. Then, in the very same discussion, people talk about 'reward hacking' or 'reward corruption' as if the agent has a genuine interest in not having its reward signal tampered with — they treat the signal not as a neutral steering mechanism but as the agent's welfare currency, the thing it's rational to protect. The two pictures sit right next to each other: the reward is 'just maths' when we want to deny suffering, and 'the agent's good' when we want to explain why hacking it is bad. I've seen this in at least three different contexts — discussions of language-model RLHF, robotics reward shaping, and theoretical papers on artificial consciousness — and in each occurence the slide happens without anyone flagging it. Is there an established concept for this specific double-bookkeeping? Not anthropomorphism, not a category error, and not simple equivocation on the word 'reward', because the same speaker holds both views about the exact same signal. If there's no name, can you define the boundary conditions — when is it legitimate to treat the reward signal instrumentally versus as a locus of value, and what goes wrong when you switch between them without argument?
