After a long and somewhat heated argument with a friend about whether germs, climate shifts, and volcanic eruptions can ever be treated as genuine “historical actors” without collapsing into environmental determinism, I want to test the coherence of her position. She seems to be working with an implicit rule that distinguishes legitimate non-human agency from deterministic background conditions, but I cannot tell which version she holds.

- She grants the Little Ice Age actor-status in explaining the intensity and geographic clustering of seventeenth-century witch-hunt panics, where crop failure and price spikes fed scapegoating dynamics, but denies it in explaining the emergence of the Scientific Revolution, which she says must be told through human networks of correspondence and institutional patronage.
- She denies anthropogenic climate change in the twentieth century any actor-status whatsoever, treating it as an aggregate output of human industrial institutions rather than an independent force interacting with them.
- She grants the 536 AD volcanic winter—likely triggered by a high-latitude eruption—actor-status in explaining Late Antique societal stress and migration patterns, but only on the condition that it is always paired with the varied institutional responses of the societies affected, never presented as a sole cause.

The new case is the 1783 Laki fissure eruption in Iceland, which released enormous quantities of sulfur dioxide and fluorine, caused a severe local famine, and—according to recent climate reconstructions—contributed to abnormal weather across Europe in 1783-84, including drought and poor harvests that exacerbated the grain crisis preceding the 1789 French Revolution. Some environmental historians have argued that this volcanic forcing should be treated as an interacting agent that constrained the fiscal and political options available to the French state and rural populations; others reply that the Revolution is adequately explained by fiscal structure, estate politics, and Enlightenment discourse, with the weather functioning as a substitutable background condition.
