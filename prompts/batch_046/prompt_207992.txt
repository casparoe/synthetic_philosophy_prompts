I've been going back and forth with my roommate about Dennett's cranes vs. skyhooks distinction, but we're applying it specifically to evolutionary biology and cognitive development rather than just consciousness. I think I've landed on this prettry hardline position: every complex biological adaptation or cognitive mechanism must ultimately be explicable as a bottom-up crane - built incrementally through selection acting on simpler, functional parts - and that any legitimate appeal to top-down organizational principles, irreducible functional design, or just-so convergence stories is essentially smuggling in a skyhook. So in my view, evo-devo gene regulatory networks are still cranes, just really intricate ones; synthetic biology is just us acting as the selecting environment; and talk of evolutionary convergence being guided by some organizational pull is just a confusion between description and explanation. I'm holding this firmly, but I'm also worried I'm just defending a slogan that can absorb any counterexample.

What would actually count against this strict crane-only view? I don't mean the standard intelligent-design talking points - I want the serious philosophical and empirical defeaters. For example, do modern developmental constraint theories - like Waddington's chreodes or current morphospace arguments about evolutionary accessibility - actually undermine the crane story, or are they just describing the scaffold that selection already built? What about synthetic organisms engineered with minimal genomes that literally cannot survive outside a specifically designed metabolic scaffold: is that just human engineering, or does it show that skyhook-like organizational dependencies can exist even in principle? And if we discovered an organism with a genuinely irreducible organizational feature that seemed to confer no incremental adaptive advantage at any intermediate stage, would that be fatal to the view, or would defenders just retreat to "we haven't found the intermediate crane-steps yet"?

Please rank these kinds of challenges from fatal to merely awkward, and be specifc about which ones a committed crane-defender would concede as genuinely counting against them versus which ones they'd dismiss as category errors. I'm also wondering whether the strict crane view has been qualified so heavily - adding enough "yes, but..." clauses - that it's now essentially unfalsifiable, and if so, whether that's actually a problem for a position that's supposed to be empirical rather than definitional.
