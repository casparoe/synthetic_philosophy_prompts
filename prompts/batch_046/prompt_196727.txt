I've been trying to formalise the implicit model behind a friend of mine's evolutionary judgements — she reads a lot of philosophy of biology and is very consistent, but I can't tell whether she's running a strict Gould-Lewontin anti-adaptationist heuristic, a more permissive exaptation-allowing framework, or a rule keyed to current functional integration rather than origin. Rather than ask her directly, I listed her past verdicts and want to see if they genuinely underdetermine the principle she is using.

Her judgements so far: mammalian middle-ear bones are exaptations — selected originally for mastication, now serving audition, so the auditory function is genuinely adapted even though the origin is spandrel-like. Human male nipples are pure spandrels: no selection history, no reproductive payoff, just a developmental byproduct of shared embryogenesis. Adult lactose persistence is a clear adaptation, shaped by gene-culture co-evolution with direct fitness effects. Human mathematical cognition is a spandrel, piggybacking on language and working-memory expansions that were selected for other ends. Stick-insect camouflage morphology is an adaptation, directly shaped by predator-driven selection. The peacock's tail is an adaptation via sexual selection, though the associated metabolic burden is, in her view, an unavoidable spandrel of the signalling mechanism rather than itself selected for.

Now consider a hypothetical case I constructed to stress-test the boundary: a deep-sea crustacean in an isolated population builds elaborate, energetically costly lattice structures from sediment around its burrow. The structures confer no detectable survival or mating advantage, are not protective, yet are rebuilt identically after disruption. The behaviour is heritable, but it piggybacks entirely on the neural circuitry for burrow ventilation — a genuinely adaptive trait — and disappears in wild populations where ventilation demands are lower. Would she classify the lattice-building as an adaptation, a spandrel, or an exaptation? I am less interested in biological plausibility than in consistency with her track record. Please predict her verdict, identify the two or three distinct rules that fit her earlier judgements equally well, and say which of those rules diverges on this crustacean case. Give your best guess for the rule she is actually using, with the reasoning that makes it preferable. Finally, propose a further hypothetical case — perhaps one in which a trait solves an environmental problem but arose through pure genetic drift with no selection at all — that would definitively separate the remaining candidates. If you were recommending a verdict to her for consistency's sake, which precedent is the closest analogue, and what principle would she be adopting with each possible answer?
