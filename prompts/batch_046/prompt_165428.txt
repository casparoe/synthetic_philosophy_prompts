I am a junior in high school and serve on our town’s youth advisory council, which will cast a non-binding vote next month on the redevelopment of a decommissioned industrial lot. The two proposals are concrete and mutually exclusive. Plan Green converts the site into a public park and wetland restoration over the next decade, delaying any residential construction; Plan Build erects affordable housing immediately, but its design requires diesel-heavy groundwork and leaves only a minimal green buffer, which local environmental assessments project would measurably worsen air quality in the surrounding neighborhood for several decades. I understand that either choice will shape migration patterns—who moves here for work or school, whom they meet, and eventually who is born here—so the people living in this town fifty years from now will literally be different individuals depending on which plan we approve.

Here is the difficulty I cannot resolve. If we choose Plan Build, some of those future residents may suffer respiratory conditions linked to the poorer air. Yet if we had chosen Plan Green, those particular people would never have existed at all; different people, presumably healthier, would have taken their place. Is it coherent to say that we wronged the sufferers, given that our decision was a necessary condition for their very existence? And I have been wondering whether the moral structure changes because this harm is mediated through a collective institutional choice rather than through one person’s private decision—does the fact that our council is only one vote among many alter how we should apply the intuition that one cannot harm someone merely by bringing them into being? I am not asking for a simple cost-benefit summary; I want to understand whether this identity-dependent feature of the future gives us a distinct philosophical reason to weigh present housing needs differently from how standard risk analysis would weigh them.
