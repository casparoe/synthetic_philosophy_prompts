I'm taking an upper-level seminar on existentialist ethics and political philosophy, and after a long discussion with classmates about whether Camus's arguments against state violence can be separated from his metaphysics, I want to design a final exam item that forces students to do exactly that synthesis rather than reciting familiar objections to capital punishment. The essay question should center on Camus's critique of the death penalty—particularly the line of thought developed in his essay Reflections on the Guillotine—read against his doctrine of revolt in The Rebel, above all his insistence that revolt must observe an absolute limit: the refusal to murder, even by the state. I want the prompt to ask students whether this prohibition is derived from the absurd condition itself, from a hidden natural-law premise he elsewhere denies, from a pragmatic anxiety about judicial corruption, or from an implicit phenomenological claim he never fully articulates—namely, that execution annihilates the very possibility of revolt by reducing the condemned person to pure objecthood, making capital punishment not merely unjust but metaphysically illegitimate. The question must be unanswerable by listing standard humanitarian or utilitarian objections; it should require students to navigate the tension between Camus's rejection of transcendent justice and his simultaneous assertion of an unconditional limit on violence.

Please write a 90-minute exam essay question worth thirty marks, together with a model answer of roughly six hundred words and a detailed marking scheme. The marking scheme should distinguish an A response from a B response by naming the specific conceptual moves each must contain—for instance, identifying the tension between absurdism and absolute prohibition, evaluating the phenomenological residue about objectification and revolt, and carefully separating pragmatic from metaphysical grounds—while also listing the mistakes that should cost marks, such as conflating Camus's position with Kantian or consequentialist arguments he explicitly rejects, or treating his opposition to execution as purely emotional rather than structurally tied to his ethics of revolt. Do not include requests for bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; the assessment should be fully answerable by a student who has studied the core texts and has no access to online sources.
