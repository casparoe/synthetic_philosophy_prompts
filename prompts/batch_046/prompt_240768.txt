I'm writing a long-form piece for a science-and-ideas publication on whether poetic form can survive translation without becoming something else entirely, and after a long conversation with a colleague in comparative literature I've been revisiting the old problem of Clément Marot's verse—particularly the kind of strict formal replication that certain translators have defended, where rhyme, meter, and syllable count are carried over into English almost as structural obligations rather than stylistic choices. I have come to hold the view that this practice does not preserve the poem; it replaces it. The formal constraints that govern the original—its masculine rhymes, its octosyllabic couplets, the particular phonetic density of its line endings—are not universal properties of the work but accidents of French phonology and sixteenth-century prosody. When a translator replicates those constraints in English, they are not translating the poem's form; they are imposing an isomorphic cage that forces the semantic and emotional content to contort itself into shapes that the original never demanded. The result, I think, is a parallel original wearing the original's clothing, and calling it preservation is a category error.

To make this more precise, I want to distinguish what I call morphological isomorphism—replicating the surface pattern of line lengths and rhyme positions—from functional equivalence, which would require that the translated poem produce the same cognitive and affective effects in its new linguistic environment. I suspect that morphological isomorphism almost always undermines functional equivalence, because the translator must substitute words, alter syntax, and suppress connotations to satisfy a rhyme scheme that has no natural analog in English. The defenders of formal translation might reply that the music of the language is itself part of the meaning, but I think that conflates the vehicle with the cargo: the sound is a medium, not a message, and when you preserve the vehicle by rebuilding it out of different parts, you have not saved the message; you have rebuilt the vehicle around a different passenger.

Given all this, I want you to play the position straight and then map its exposure. What would actually count against my view? I want the evidence, arguments, and conceptual distinctions that should move me, sorted by how decisive they would be—starting with observations about actual translations that seem to preserve both form and meaning without the semantic drift I predict, then moving to theoretical results or cross-linguistic studies about rhyme preservation, and finally to the possibility that my distinction between morphological isomorphism and functional equivalence collapses under scrutiny. Be honest about whether this view risks anything at all: has it been qualified into unfalsifiability by my insistence that any successful formal translation is merely a parallel original, and if so, is that insulation a problem for a thesis of this kind? I also want to know what defenders of formal constraint translation would themselves concede as damaging to my position—not only what critics say. In answering, please say explicitly what would change your mind: whether a specific counterexample of a formal translation that survives back-translation intact, a demonstration that certain phonetic patterns carry semantic weight across languages, or an argument showing that my vehicle-cargo distinction presupposes an untenable separation of form and content. And flag where your reasoning is most likely to fail—whether in the empirical claim about translation outcomes, in the philosophical distinction I have drawn, or in the normative judgment that functional equivalence should take priority.
