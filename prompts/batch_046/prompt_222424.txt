Two colleagues, Sam and Dana, were debating whether moral preferences can ever be cleanly distinguished from culturally acquired biases. Their exchange unfolded in four stages, reported here without editorial comment. (1) Sam proposed a counterfactual socialization test: if one would still hold a moral judgment after being raised in the culture being evaluated, the judgment is a genuine preference; if it would disappear, it was bias. He applied it to Western disgust at eating insects, suggesting that this disgust is culturally trained and would vanish under different upbringing. (2) Dana objected that the test is incoherent because moral reasoning capacities are themselves shaped by socialization; imagining oneself raised elsewhere is not swapping inputs while keeping reasoning fixed, but imagining being a different reasoner, so there is no stable baseline. (3) Sam revised his view, offering an informational symmetry criterion: expose subjects to all relevant non-moral facts—nutritional data, ecological costs, culinary traditions—without coercion. If the judgment survives this leveling, it is preference-based; if it dissolves with corrected ignorance, it was bias. He observed that disgust at insects often fades with education, whereas objections to labor exploitation typically do not, which he took to show the latter are preferences. (4) Dana responded that selecting which facts count as relevant is already value-laden—highlighting ecological impact over symbolic communal meaning embeds prior commitments. She also argued that Sam wrongly assumes disgust is always non-moral bias, since some disgust responses, perhaps toward certain exploitative practices, might themselves be moral perceptions. Without an independent taxonomy of which emotional responses are legitimately moral, the test merely relocates circularity.

Please adjudicate the exchange move by move. For each of the four stages, rate Sam's or Dana's position from clearly mistaken to clearly right and briefly justify the rating. Then state who is more right overall, and identify what both are missing—specifically, whether there exists a non-circular method for separating preference from bias in moral judgment, or whether the framing of the problem itself needs revision.
