In trying to think through that choice, I have found myself returning to Machiavelli's pairing of fortuna and virtù, and I want to test the framework against a well-known historical episode rather than my own situation. Specifically, I want you to analyze the fall of the Berlin Wall in November 1989 and Chancellor Helmut Kohl's announcement, later that same month, of a ten-point plan for German reunification—issued without consulting his Western allies and before any formal multilateral framework existed.

Treat the sudden opening of the East German border and the rapid erosion of the Honecker regime as an instance of fortuna overflowing its banks, using Machiavelli's image of the river that can be contained by dikes and dams in calm times but that, once raging, must be met with bold redirection rather than resistance. Examine Kohl's decision to present a concrete reunification roadmap rather than wait for negotiation: was this an exercise of virtù in the Machiavellian sense—the capacity to adapt one's actions to shifting circumstances, to be impetuous when fortune demands speed rather than caution? Or was it a failure of the institutional foresight that Machiavelli praises, since Kohl had built no dikes in advance and was merely riding a flood he had not caused? Push the analysis in both directions. What does the episode illuminate about Machiavelli's claim that those who depend entirely on fortune are ruined when circumstances change? Did Kohl succeed because he possessed genuine virtù, or because the particular configuration of 1989—Soviet non-intervention, mass East German mobilization, American acquiescence—was so favorable that even moderate competence would have prevailed?

Then consider where the framework misleads or falls silent. Machiavelli often treats fortuna as a singular, gendered force that favors youth and aggressive boldness, yet Kohl was neither young nor temperamentally aggressive, though he acted with startling speed. Does this suggest that virtù can be situational and opportunistic rather than a stable personal trait? Also address the role of accumulated institutional inertia—what we might call a second register of fortuna, distinct from the sudden episodic flood—by asking whether Kohl's plan worked precisely because the West German state had spent decades building the bureaucratic and economic dikes that made rapid absorption possible. Finally, tell me what the case reveals about the limits of applying Machiavelli to modern democratic leadership, where legitimacy depends on consultation rather than on the prince's solitary will.
