Having recently finished a course of immunosuppressive therapy, I am now facing a decision with my partner: my specialist warns that conceiving within the year risks transmitting a severe but non-lethal hereditary neuropathy, whereas delaying conception for twenty-four months—after an experimental gene-correction protocol—would almost certainly prevent it, though the delay guarantees that any child born would be genetically distinct from one conceived now. After encountering Derek Parfit's nonidentity problem, I have become convinced that this choice carries an obligation stronger than mere prudence. Specifically, I reason that the child who might be born with the neuropathy lacks counterfactual moral standing, since in the delayed scenario she never exists to suffer; conversely, the healthy child born later can claim that we were duty-bound to wait, precisely so that she—this particular individual—could come into being unharmed. Thus identity-affecting choices appear to generate obligations solely to those who actually exist, leaving no moral remainder for those who are replaced. Is this interpretation correct, or does it misapply the problem by treating the absence of harm to a replaced person as the source of a positive, person-specific duty?
