I'm a novelist asked to deliver the opening remarks at the dedication of a community archive that collects oral histories of harm and repair. The room will be divided: moral philosophers who will recognize the architecture of reactive attitudes and the objective stance, and writers, mediators, and survivors who will not. I need a formal, polished five-minute address that takes the distinction between participant reactive attitudes and the objective attitude and makes it do structural work—not decorative work—by treating it as a narrative problem rather than merely an emotional one. Specifically, I want the speech to propose that adopting an objective stance toward someone is structurally analogous to shifting from second-person address to third-person biography in fiction, and that this shift in narrative voice—not just the withdrawal of resentment—is what transforms moral responsibility into explanation. The philosophical framework should constrain what can and cannot be said: within the logic of the address, it should be impossible to offer forgiveness as a private feeling without also accounting for whose voice is speaking and to whom. I want the philosophers present to see that I'm restructuring the framework through concerns with point of view and narrative distance, not merely illustrating it, while the writers and mediators grasp the stakes without any citation, bibliography, or direct mention of the source framework. The occasion is serious but not mournful. Please include one precise moment where the analogy between narrative distance and the objective attitude strains or breaks—where the philosopher-listeners will recognize a genuine tension in the framework and the others will feel the emotional cost of that breakdown—so that the speech ends not on resolution but on a carefully held ambiguity about whether explanation can ever fully replace address.
