I sit on my university's Strategic Contingency and Resource Allocation Committee, and we must deliver a formal recommendation to the Provost by month's end. The concrete decision is whether to consolidate our crisis-response protocols—currently four separate domain-specific frameworks covering financial, reputational, operational, and environmental risk—into a single unified master contingency plan, or to preserve the pluralist structure with its occasionally contradictory directives. The chair of our subcommittee has pressed a philosophical argument drawn from modal metaphysics: just as Leibniz held that the actual world is the best of all possible worlds not because it maximizes every isolated good but because it maximizes compossible goods—those that can jointly exist without contradiction—the university should adopt the plan that achieves the greatest coherence across all risk domains, eliminating conflicts between rapid financial lockdown and deliberative reputational transparency. I was discussing this with a colleague in philosophy of religion over coffee, and we agreed the dispute turns on whether collective decision-making bodies should aim at a singular optimal world or accept a family of locally optimal but non-compossible worlds. Several committee members, including our general counsel, object that institutional goods are not properties of worlds but operational demands that genuinely conflict; forcing them into one framework risks suppressing necessary trade-offs rather than resolving them. Please draft the committee's written opinion recommending consolidation, rejection, or consolidation with explicit conditions, including a concrete implementation or review timeline. The reasoning should treat the Leibnizian analogy with intellectual honesty—not as decorative metaphor—while giving full weight to the concern that modal optimism may misrepresent organizational life, and the memo should be polished enough that the Provost could circulate it without revision.
