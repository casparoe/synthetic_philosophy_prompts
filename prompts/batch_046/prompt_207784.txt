I sit on the disciplinary appeals panel for my university, and we are deadlocked over a recommendation we must deliver by Friday. The case concerns a doctoral student in moral philosophy who was suspended for six months after organizing a sit-in that physically blocked deliveries to an administrative building. The student does not deny the act; instead, they defend it by appealing to Pascal's analysis of justice and force, specifically the claim that justice without power is impotent and power without justice is tyrannical. They argue that the university's investment portfolio relies on coercive legal and security structures—force—while lacking any genuinely just distribution of its benefits, and that therefore their own use of obstructive but non-violent force was an attempt to reunite justice with power in a context where institutional channels had become purely formal. Half the panel sees this as an illegitimate transposition of a theological-political observation into a student-conduct code; the other half fears that dismissing the philosophical defense outright would treat moral reasoning as irrelevant to disciplinary appeals. We need a formal memo recommending whether to uphold the suspension, overturn it, or overturn it with conditions, including reasoning that addresses both the student's Pascalian framework and the university's interest in maintaining orderly protest channels. The memo should treat seriously the possibility that Pascal's argument, read precisely, actually undermines rather than supports the student's action—perhaps because it assigns the correction of injustice to legitimate sovereign authority rather than to private individuals—while still giving an honest account of why the opposing view might be right. Please write in continuous prose only, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, in the manner of a carefully composed letter.
