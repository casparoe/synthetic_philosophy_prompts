I spent the better part of last evening arguing with a close friend about whether the agricultural revolution was, on balance, a mistake for the people who lived through it. The debate circled repeatedly around the same two bodies of evidence—skeletal markers of nutritional stress and reduced stature among early cereal cultivators, on one side, and ethnographic data on leisure time, dietary breadth, and lower resource inequality among contemporary foraging populations, on the other—without either of us conceding an inch. My friend maintained that the transition was essentially a rational adaptation to Holocene climate stability and that any welfare loss was a temporary externality on the path to institutional complexity; I kept insisting that if average lifespan, physical stature, and temporal autonomy all declined for millennia, then describing the shift as a mistake is not anachronistic but empirically descriptive, regardless of later benefits in storage technology or population scale. What strikes me now is that we may not actually disagree about the archaeological record itself. We both accept that sedentism and surplus storage intensified social stratification, and we both accept that forager societies exhibit lower Gini coefficients of resource access. So the stall feels structural rather than empirical, as though we are running two different evaluations on the same dataset.

My working hypothesis is that the disagreement is partly verbal—hinging on whether a "mistake" should be evaluated at the level of individual welfare, population-level demographic success, or long-run institutional possibility—and partly a hidden dispute about what counts as legitimate explanatory data. One of us treats modern ethnographic analogies as transparent windows onto Pleistocene decision-making; the other treats them as misleading outliers in a world already shaped by agricultural neighbors and state-level coercion. But I am unsure whether this diagnosis is correct, or whether there is a deeper bedrock intuition about progress and naturalness that neither side can defend, or whether we are in fact answering two different questions—one about the origin of inequality, the other about the origin of coercive storage institutions—under a single heading. Is the impasse a sign that the question is ill-posed? Or could a sharper conceptual distinction—say, between caloric sovereignty and temporal autonomy as incommensurable goods—actually dissolve it? More generally, what kind of disagreement is this, and what, if anything, would count as genuine progress: a formal demographic model of entrapment versus voluntary adoption, an agreed standard for weighing ethnographic analogy, or an admission that no unified verdict is available?
