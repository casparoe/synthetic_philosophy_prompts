I'm preparing notes for a seminar on the boundary between legend and history, using the Trojan War, King Arthur, and the Exodus as test cases, and I've constructed an argument that now seems to collapse the distinction entirely for ancient narratives. I still want to maintain that treating Troy as possibly historical and Arthur as legendary is not mere prejudice or cultural bias, but I cannot locate the precise exit from the chain. The argument runs as follows.

(1) For an ancient narrative to count as history rather than legend, its core events must be attested by evidence that is both independent of the narrative tradition itself and capable of being false.
(2) Before widespread literacy, all testimony is either orally derived or institutionally transmitted, so it necessarily fails the independence condition.
(3) Archaeological remains can be independent, yet they cannot establish specific events—such as a siege, an exodus, or a royal court—without an interpretive frame that borrows from the very narratives being tested.
(4) Therefore no ancient narrative can satisfy the independence condition, and the distinction between legend and history collapses for all pre-modern material.
(5) Nevertheless we do distinguish in practice: we treat Hittite references to Wilusa and Egyptian mentions of movements involving Habiru-related groups as lending historical weight to Troy and the Exodus, while we reject Arthur despite comparable archaeological ambiguity in sub-Roman Britain.
(6) The only operative difference is that for Troy and the Exodus there exist contemporaneous geopolitical references onto which the narrative can be retrofitted, whereas for Arthur there are none.
(7) But those external references never attest the specific events—Helen's abduction, the wooden horse, the plagues, the parting of the sea—only a contextual landscape into which the legend may be inserted.
(8) Hence our actual criterion is not independent attestation of events but what might be called retrofit elasticity: the capacity of a legend to be mapped onto an external context without contradiction.
(9) By that criterion, Arthur fails not because the archaeological evidence is poorer, but because the narrative is too specific—Camelot, the Round Table, the singular sword—to be accommodated within the sparse material record of post-Roman Britain.
(10) Conversely, the Exodus is rejected by many scholars not for lack of contextual references, but because its specific quantitative and chronological claims resist retrofitting without contradiction, despite the presence of Semitic populations in Canaan.
(11) So the distinction between history and legend depends on the narrative's flexibility rather than on its evidential status, which implies that Troy, Arthur, and the Exodus stand or fall together according to the same hidden standard.
(12) Therefore either we must accept that all three are equally legendary or equally historical, or we must admit that "history" here is merely "legend with sufficient elasticity."

Where does this go wrong, if it does? I am particularly keen to preserve (1) and (5)—that there is a genuine evidential distinction and that our treatment of these three cases is not arbitrary—but I want to know exactly which step I am paying to reject, and whether the error is a subtle flaw such as an equivocation between event-attestation and context-attestation, or a scope shift in how independence applies, rather than a straightforwardly false premise.
