The Gödelian analogy in Hofstadter's Gödel, Escher, Bach—between a formula that asserts its own unprovability via Gödel numbering and a mind that achieves self-awareness—has been running for decades, through Lucas's argument that human reason exceeds formal systems, Penrose's elaboration, and the standard rebuttals about non-uniform computation, consistency assumptions, and category errors. Yet the exchange feels stalled: each side rehearses the same logical moves, and neither concedes that the other has missed a premise. My working hypothesis is that the disagreement is not really about whether minds are formal systems, but about what kind of self-reference is at stake—representational self-inclusion, in which a system contains a structural analog of itself, versus epistemic self-transcendence, in which a system can recognize truths that lie outside its own derivational closure. On this reading, the Gödelian camp and its critics are answering different questions under the single heading of "self-reference," and the analogy's appeal masks a shift from syntax to phenomenology. Is that diagnosis right? Is the stall instead a hidden empirical disagreement about whether neural computation is uniform? Or is it a bedrock clash about whether an explanation of consciousness must invoke something that cannot be mechanized? Diagnose the structure of the disagreement using what each side actually claims about Gödel numbering, consistency, and mental representation, and tell me what, if anything, would count as progress—whether a sharper distinction between syntactic and semantic self-reference, a formal result about non-uniform architectures, or a reconception of the original question—given your diagnosis.
