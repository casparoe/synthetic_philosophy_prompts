I am trying to refine my judgments about mechanical rigidity, adaptive flexibility, and the conditions of free agency, taking the classical sphex-wasp scenario as my point of departure but extending the analysis to human expertise, pathological compulsion, institutional rule-following, and artificial systems. Case 1: The sphex wasp performs its burrow-check and prey-drag sequence with invariant order; if the prey is moved during the check, the wasp repeats the check rather than adapting. I judge this paradigmatically unfree—pure mechanical rigidity, with no flexibility whatsoever.

Case 2: A veteran emergency-room triage nurse follows protocol mechanically in routine presentations, but instantly abandons the protocol when a patient’s hidden internal bleed violates the very rationale (not merely the letter) of the trauma-score rules she applies. I judge her highly free; her rigidity is a scaffold for context-sensitive revision.

Case 3: A housing bureaucrat applies a zoning checklist to deny shelter to a family whose situation technically violates a clause but clearly serves the regulation’s underlying safety purpose. He understands the purpose, recognizes the mismatch, and applies the rule anyway without distress. I judge him sphexish—unfree in a morally relevant sense—despite being human and cognitively sophisticated.

Case 4: A person with severe OCD washes their hands fifty times, recognizes the behavior as irrational, feels acute distress, yet cannot stop. I judge them partly unfree, but differently from Case 3: the rigidity is internally resisted rather than endorsed, and the failure is one of executive control rather than normative blindness.

Case 5: A chess grandmaster, under severe time pressure, plays a memorized opening line automatically even though the opponent’s first move technically renders a different line optimal. She could have calculated but did not; the move was automatic. I judge her still fully free—the rigidity is expert fluency, not bondage.

Case 6: A large language model with RLHF refuses to answer a chemistry exam question about synthesizing a toxic compound, applying its safety heuristic without discriminating pedagogical context from malicious intent. Structurally it reproduces the bureaucrat’s error in silicon. I judge it sphexish, though I am unsure whether this judgment tracks freedom (it lacks consciousness) or only structural analogy.

Yet this seems to conflate Case 3 and Case 4 (both lack elasticity in different directions) and does not clearly distinguish Case 2 from Case 5.
