I'm giving the opening remarks — about four minutes — at an interdisciplinary workshop called "Faith and Conceptual Schemes," attended by philosophers of religion, theology graduate students, and cognitive scientists. The room is split between those who treat religious language as empirically falsifiable hypotheses and those who treat it as purely expressive moral poetry. I want Hilary Putnam's late position to do real structural work: religious belief is not a failed scientific explanation (that framing is a category mistake), nor is it reducible to ethics alone; rather, it involves an orientational "belief-in" that functions differently from propositional "belief-that." This must genuinely constrain what I say — I cannot imply that science will eventually dissolve religious experience, and I cannot collapse faith into mere values — while leaving both halves of the audience feeling taken seriously rather than dismissed. Write the remarks so the distinction shapes the arc of the speech, not just decorates it. End with a single line beginning "Core theme: " summarizing the guiding idea in one sentence.
