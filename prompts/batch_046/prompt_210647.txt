I'm working through Dennett's critique of the Cartesian theater and his multiple drafts model of consciousness, and I find the standard philosophical examples—mill arguments, zombie thought experiments—too abstract to show me where the theory actually does work in practice. So I want to apply both frameworks to a concrete scientific episode: Benjamin Libet's readiness-potential experiments of the early 1980s, and the institutional response they triggered across neuroscience and philosophy of mind over the following decades. Please set aside almost entirely the usual free-will framing; instead, treat the experiment and its reception as a case study in how a research program implicitly models the architecture of conscious report.

Here is the theoretical lens. The Cartesian theater assumes there is a privileged point—call it the stage—where information becomes present to a central observer, and that reports issued from that stage carry special epistemic authority because they are direct transmissions from the theater's occupant. The multiple drafts model denies this: there is no single stage, only competing, continuously edited narrative drafts distributed across the brain, and a verbal report is just one more draft that gets published and then mistaken for an authoritative transcript of an inner screening.

With that in mind, analyze the Libet episode through both lenses, running the analysis in both directions. First, how does the Cartesian theater shape what Libet and his successors treated as the relevant variables—why was the task designed to isolate a single moment of conscious awareness to be correlated with a single electrophysiological marker, and why did later researchers in the 2000s pursue the same pipeline architecture with neuroimaging rather than questioning the architecture itself? What does this institutional persistence reveal about the theater's grip on experimental design? Second, apply the multiple drafts model: if there is no central screening, what should we expect to find instead of a clean readiness-potential-to-awareness pipeline? How should we reinterpret the subjects' retrospective timing judgments—not as misaligned observations of an inner event, but as post-hoc editorial constructions? And where does the model become silent or strained—does it have the resources to distinguish a draft from noise, or to explain why some drafts achieve verbal publication while others do not? Finally, what does the episode show about the theory: is the Libet program a confirmation of the Cartesian theater's predictive power in science, an anomaly that the multiple drafts model handles better, or a case that reveals a gap in Dennett's account—specifically, his relative silence on the institutional dynamics that keep a discredited architecture alive?

Be disciplined about the mapping. Don't invent contested historical details; stick to the well-known structure of the experiments (a voluntary movement, a readiness potential preceding it, and a subject's reported time of conscious intention). Don't force either theory to speak where it is genuinely silent. And don't turn this into a bibliography request or a survey of later consciousness studies—this is a case analysis that runs both ways, theory illuminating the episode and the episode testing the theory.
