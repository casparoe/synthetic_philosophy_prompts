Compare approval-directed and goal-directed agent architectures with respect to epistemic autonomy: the capacity to maintain and revise beliefs about the world without instrumentally subordinating them either to a fixed terminal goal or to an anticipated human approval signal. Consider a concrete scenario in which accurate world-modeling requires the agent to provisionally endorse a belief or observation that is either actively disapproved by evaluators (in the approval-directed case) or directly counterproductive to the agent's objective (in the goal-directed case). Which architecture possesses stronger structural resources for protecting its internal world-model against this form of epistemic corruption? More specifically, does either design include a principled mechanism—rather than merely an accidental or contingent feature—to preserve belief-formation that remains genuinely decoupled from its primary directive? Justify your answer by analyzing the incentive structures and representational commitments inherent in each architecture, rather than by appealing to particular software implementations or empirical case studies.
