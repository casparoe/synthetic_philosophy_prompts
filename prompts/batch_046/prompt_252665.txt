I am preparing a graduate seminar on metaphysical deflationism and its cross-cultural analogues, and in our recent session on Buddhist anattā my students and I worked through the classical chariot analogy as it is presented in the Milindapañha. In the dialogue, Nāgasena invites King Milinda to locate the chariot: is it the pole, the axle, the wheels, the frame, or only their arranged aggregation? The king eventually grants that “chariot” is merely a conventional designation for parts in functional relation, with no substantial chariot hidden within or behind them. Nāgasena then extends the analysis to the person, proposing that “Nāgasena” is analogously only a designation for the five aggregates—form, feeling, perception, mental formations, and consciousness—without any permanent self underlying them.

What I find genuinely puzzling, and what the students have pressed me on, is how this metaphysical deflation should be reconciled with what one might call diachronic normative tethering: the apparent fact that obligations, intentions, and affective commitments seem to bind across temporal slices of a life in ways that appear to require more than mere conventional labeling, yet without invoking the kind of substantial persistence that the chariot analogy explicitly denies. If the aggregates are in constant flux and there is no owner or substrate, in what precise sense does the passage’s logic of conventional designation preserve the conditions for enduring commitment-structures—say, a vow or a sustained responsibility—without either collapsing into a reductionist momentariness that severs diachronic unity, or smuggling back in a crypto-substantialist index of aggregation that the analogy was meant to eliminate? I would appreciate help clarifying whether the text implicitly relies on a functionalist criterion of identity over time that is stronger than pure nominalism, and if so, how that criterion avoids the charge of being merely another hidden essence dressed in conventional language.
