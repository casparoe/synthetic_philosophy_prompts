I'm trying to understand which unresolved issues in Rawls's treatment of religion actually carry weight for institutional design, as opposed to being purely interpretive puzzles about his texts. There's a framing that strikes me as underexplored: religious citizens in a liberal democracy face what might be called a double burden. They are expected to provide public reasons for constitutional essentials—this is the familiar proviso—but they are also expected to maintain that their religious identity hasn't been asked to vanish from the public sphere. Meanwhile, secular citizens face a mirror problem: they must show that liberal institutions are not themselves a comprehensive doctrine disguised as neutrality. I want to know which open questions in this space deserve real philosophical labor, and which are dead ends.

Specifically, I'm weighing three lines of inquiry. First, the precise scope and temporal structure of the proviso: does it apply only to constitutional essentials, or to all coercive policy? Second, whether an overlapping consensus can remain stable if the supporting comprehensive doctrines—religious or secular—undergo significant internal change, especially when that change is driven by moral rather than strategic considerations. Third, whether secular liberalism can satisfy Rawls's own criterion of not being a sectarian doctrine, given that it relies on a particular conception of the person and of moral autonomy. For each, I want to know not just whether it is unresolved, but whether resolving it would change how we think about concrete conflicts—say, religious exemptions in healthcare provision or the place of religious reasoning in legislative debate.

Please defend a prioritization: which one or two of these questions matter most for someone concerned with the practical architecture of pluralist institutions, and which should be treated as secondary? I want the criteria made explicit—whether you are weighing tractability against practical stakes, or judging how many other problems depend on the resolution of one. I am not looking for a survey of the literature or a reading list; I want an argued agenda that explains what makes a question worth years of attention rather than a single seminar paper.
