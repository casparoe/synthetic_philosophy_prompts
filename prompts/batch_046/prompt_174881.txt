Take Darwin's evolutionary story about morality — social instincts, sympathy that widens past kin, and the hunch that religious belief is basically overactive social-cognition turned toward invisible agents — and run it hard against the actual episode of his daughter Annie dying in 1851 and his long slide into agnosticisim afterwards. I don't want biography; I want the mapping. Where does parental grief sit in his framework — is it just the "lower" instinct for offspring care, or does it blow up the hierarchy he draws between instinctive sympathy and calculated moral judgment? When he tells friends he can't square a child's suffering with a benevolent designer, is he applying his own evolutionary reasoning about wasteful, blundering nature, or is it an emotional refusal that his theory of morality has no vocabulary for? Then flip it: what does this specific grief do to his claim that moral feelings are essentially adaptive social tools? If the worst suffering is exactly the kind that yields no evolutionary payoff — heavy investment in a child who dies anyway — does that leave his framework silent where it ought to speak, or does it actually reinforce his point that morality is about local bonds rather than cosmic justice? Be disciplined about who the "player" is here — Darwin as theorist, Darwin as father, or both — and say clearly where his account stops and the silence begins. Don't make him answer questions he never faced.
