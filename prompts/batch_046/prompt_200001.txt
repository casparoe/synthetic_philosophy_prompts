I serve on the Institutional Memory and Reparations Board of a major research university, and we must circulate a decision memo before the senate votes next month. In 2023 we uncovered the archives of Project Meridian, a 1960s cross-institutional behavioral study that used deceptive recruitment, withheld withdrawal rights, and induced sustained stress in over four hundred participants, many of whom are now elderly. The university has acknowledged fault and funded counseling, but the archive itself is contested. A coalition of survivors and descendants demands full, searchable digital publication of de-identified case files and audio transcripts as an act of mnemonic justice, arguing that an institution that sanitizes its own record compounds the original harm. A counter-coalition of survivors, clinicians, and faculty ethicists demands permanent sealing of primary records, leaving only an aggregated statistical report and a formal apology; they argue that perpetual accessibility retraumatizes, invites voyeurism, and substitutes display for repair. Our staff has proposed a third path, an ephemeral preservation protocol: cryptographic time-lock storage in which access keys degrade by algorithmic half-life over seventy-five years unless actively renewed by a designated survivor-descendant council, making institutional forgetting the default rather than the exception. Draft the board’s formal memo recommending one of these three paths—full publication, permanent sealing with summary, or the ephemeral protocol—with a concrete timeline, conditions, and implementation steps. The memo should treat the strongest version of the opposing view with genuine philosophical seriousness, not as ignorance of trauma or of transparency, but as a competing conception of what repair requires, and it should address whether a university can ethically design its own forgetting. Write with the explicit, crux-identifying style common in rationalist discussions of institutional decision-making, reach a definite recommendation rather than splitting the difference, and do not include requests for bibliographies, reading lists, or citations.
