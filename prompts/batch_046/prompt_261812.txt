I am drafting a scene for my next novel in which a regulatory body—the Neural Transparency Council—enforces what it calls the Phenomenal Calibration Protocol. Under this protocol, sponsors of clinical cortical-implant trials must submit two parallel data streams: a first-person “user-illusion report” in which patients describe sensations of continuous selfhood, and a third-person neural-coherence map. The Council mandates that any discrepancy between the two streams be resolved in favor of the neural map; a patient’s claim that she still feels like herself despite the implant is formally classified as an interface artifact rather than as direct evidence of preserved identity. Conversely, reports of alienation or estrangement are not treated as philosophical complaints but as engineering failures that trigger mandatory hardware revision. The asymmetry strikes me as philosophically loaded: the practice seems to audit consciousness itself as a kind of user illusion calibrated against substrate data, yet it simultaneously treats certain first-person reports as authoritative enough to compel physical redesign.

What philosophical commitments about consciousness, personal identity, and first-person authority does this institutional practice strictly presuppose? Must one accept that subjective experience is literally a user illusion—systematically misrepresenting its own neural substrate—to justify resolving discrepancies in favor of the coherence map, or could a view that merely regards first-person reports as fallible indicators accommodate the protocol without that stronger metaphysical claim? Furthermore, does the differential treatment of continuity reports versus alienation reports presuppose a particular theory of welfare or agency, and is that implicit theory internally consistent with the Council’s underlying view of the self? I would like to understand where the practice is philosophically underdetermined, and what would have to change in its implicit framework if one replaced its view of consciousness with a realist account that grants first-person testimony genuine evidential weight.
