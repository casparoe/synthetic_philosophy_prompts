I've been trying to understand how biologists or philosophers decide what a body part is genuinely "for" — for instance, whether an appendix that no longer digests fiber still has a function if it now supports immune tissue, or whether it has become biological junk. The analogy I keep encountering compares a trait to an object found in an abandoned workshop: you can either study the original factory blueprints to learn what it was designed to do, or observe what it actually contributes to the workshop's current operations and call that its purpose. This framing gets used to argue that evolutionary selection history is the only legitimate "blueprint," so without it a trait has no real function — only a accidental effect — while the opposing side treats the analogy as showing that current causal contribution is sufficient, making the historical detour unnecessary. How far does the workshop analogy hold? Give me the correspondence table: what plays the role of the workshop, the blueprint, the current operation, and the object itself. Then show where the mapping breaks — especially whether it quietly assumes an intelligent designer, and whether it treats loss of original purpose as automatically stripping a thing of all functional status. Does the analogy actually support the conclusion that evolutionary history is essential, or does it just dress up that intuition? And if we apply it to a case where the original part is broken but repurposed — say, a rusted gear that now serves only as a paperweight — does that undermine the history-based account, the contribution-based account, or just reveal that we need a different analogy altogether?
