You are a philosopher of mind with expertise in consciousness studies and cognitive architecture. Before answering, take a few minutes to brainstorm the key structural commitments of both the Cartesian theater framework and Dennett's multiple drafts model, listing at least three specific points of divergence between them.

A popular analogy used in popular-science writing compares conscious awareness to a corporate content-moderation pipeline: raw sensory "posts" are processed in parallel by distributed "filtering" modules running on different servers; eventually, one finalized "feed" is rendered onto a single executive dashboard that a central observer—the "CEO of the mind"—views in real time and acts upon.

Your task is to audit this analogy thoroughly. First, provide an explicit mapping table: identify what in consciousness corresponds to sensory input, to pre-conscious processing, to the moment of "becoming conscious," and to the unified subject. Second, identify where the mapping breaks: does the analogy quietly import a Cartesian theater (a single place and time where "it all comes together" for a central viewer)? Third, examine whether features of real-world content moderation—such as asynchronous version updates, redundant drafts that never reach any dashboard, and the absence of a literal viewer—actually map more naturally onto Dennett's multiple drafts model than onto a theater model. Fourth, assess whether the analogy can be repaired (for example, by removing the CEO and treating the dashboard as just one more distributed report among others) without collapsing into incoherence, or whether it inherently privileges central presentation. Finally, state whether the analogy's usual conclusion—that there must be a definitive moment when a specific draft becomes "the" conscious experience—survives once the disanalogies are fully on the table, and suggest what a better analogy might be if there is one.
