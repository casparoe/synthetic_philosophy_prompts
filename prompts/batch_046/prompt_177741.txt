I'm working through G. E. M. Anscombe's moral philosophy, particularly the argument she advanced in her well-known 1956 protest against Oxford's decision to honor Harry Truman for the atomic bombings of Hiroshima and Nagasaki. As I understand her position, she insisted on a strict distinction between actions that intentionally direct killing at innocent people and actions in which the death of innocents is a foreseen but unintended side effect of attacking a legitimate military objective; she treated this distinction not as a matter of weighing consequences but as a deontological constraint on what a moral agent may choose, and she was deeply suspicious of reasoning that treats human lives as fungible costs to be traded for larger strategic goods.

With that framework in mind, I want to ask what she would likely have said about lethal autonomous weapons systems that came into serious development and debate only after her death in 2001—specifically, systems that rely on machine-learning classifiers rather than on direct human identification to select targets. Consider the following concrete scenario. An autonomous drone operates in a densely populated urban environment with no human in the loop at the moment of strike. Its targeting algorithm does not identify individuals by name, face, or uniform, but assigns a predictive hostility score based on behavioral signatures—patterns of movement, proximity to communication nodes, and gait analysis—then strikes anyone whose score exceeds a programmer-set confidence threshold, say eighty-five percent. The programmers know that civilians in this environment often share some of these behavioral patterns, and they have calibrated the threshold with full awareness that false positives will occur, but no human reviews the specific classification before the weapon fires.

How would Anscombe likely assess this case? Would she treat the civilian deaths as foreseen but unintended side effects of a legitimate attack on combatants, or would she argue that encoding a statistical threshold and authorizing the system to kill on that basis amounts to directing harm at people one does not know to be combatants? Does the absence of a human agent at the point of decision dissolve her concern about intention, or does it relocate the relevant intentional structure to the designers who set the weights and parameters? And if her analysis of Truman turned on the idea that deliberately killing innocents to achieve a military or political end is always wrong regardless of the scale of benefit, does the distributed nature of algorithmic agency—where the choice is embedded in a model rather than in a single act of will—represent an extension of her view, a breakdown of it, or an entirely new moral category?

I'm particularly interested in whether her distinction between direct and indirect killing can accommodate harm caused not by an explicit order but by the design of a statistical filter, and what this suggests about the durability of her framework when human intention is mediated through predictive models rather than expressed in discrete acts of choice.
