I'm preparing a graduate seminar on moral emotions, and after working through the standard distinctions—Williams's separation of guilt as internalized and shame as other-directed, the anthropological critique of Benedict's guilt-culture versus shame-culture taxonomy, and recent empirical work on emotional development—the discussion keeps stalling on a single pair of questions: which of the two is the better moral emotion, and can a culture genuinely choose between them? What makes the deadlock puzzling is that the participants are not divided over descriptive facts; everyone accepts that both emotions occur across societies, that both can motivate reform, and that both risk becoming pathological when excessive. They also share a normative premise: an emotion is morally better to the extent that it tracks genuine responsibility without collapsing into destructive self-abasement or mere social conformity. Yet one faction insists that shame is structurally flawed because its other-directed nature makes moral repair dependent on the gaze of an audience that may be unjust, while the other faction argues that guilt is the more dangerous emotion precisely because its internalization allows a culture to install a self-policing mechanism that requires no external accountability. The disagreement does not resolve into a straightforward empirical dispute about emotional efficacy, nor does it reduce to a simple normative clash about autonomy versus belonging.

I want a diagnosis of what kind of disagreement is actually doing the work beneath these surface arguments. Is there a hidden conceptual dispute about whether a moral emotion must have a propositional object directed at the self, or can it be genuinely moral while directed at one's standing in a relational network? Is the impasse driven by a deeper disagreement about emotional authenticity—specifically, whether an emotional regime can be institutionally cultivated without being inauthentic, or whether authenticity requires that the emotion arise spontaneously from pre-cultural capacities? Or is it a bedrock disagreement about moral agency itself, with one side treating emotional dispositions as instruments of social coordination that a culture can redesign, and the other treating them as preconditions for moral personhood that can only be cultivated organically, never chosen? Please support your diagnosis by showing what each side actually says in this specific standoff, not by surveying the literature. Then tell me, honestly, what would count as progress given your diagnosis: a conceptual distinction that dissolves the ambiguity, an empirical finding about emotional development that favors one structural feature, or a normative argument that neither side can reject without begging the question. In particular, consider the hypothesis that the debate is stalled because the two sides are answering different questions—one asking which emotion best serves moral repair within a given social architecture, the other asking which emotion expresses an authentic moral self—under the single heading "which is better." If that hypothesis is correct, explain whether resolving the underlying disagreement about agency or authenticity would dissolve the impasse, or whether the two positions are incommensurable in a way that makes the original question ill-posed.
