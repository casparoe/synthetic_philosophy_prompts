I'm on our university's curriculum review board and we've got this proposal for a required gen-ed course called "Tragic Choice and Unavoidable Wrong." The whole syllabus is built on the claim that genuine moral dilemmas exist—cases where you face two all-things-considered obligations that can't both be met, and where whichever path you take leaves actual moral residue, not just regret. They use the usual lifeguard-type examples, but they also bring in these algorithmic triage protocols: hospital allocation algorithms that, no matter how you weight survival probabilities against distributive fairness, necessarily generate outcomes that violate one serious duty or another. The instructors argue this shows dilemmas aren't just intuitive puzzles but structural features of decision systems.

Half the board likes it—say's it's honest about ethics being messy sometimes. But two philosophy faculty on the board think the course premise is incoherent: if obligations genuinely conflict, rational deliberation resolves them, so at worst you get apparent dilemmas that dissolve under idealized reasoning; they also say the algorithmic cases just confuse engineering trade-offs with normative conflict. I need a memo for the board recommending approve, reject, or approve with conditions—something we can actually vote on next week. The reasoning should take the anti-dilemma argument seriously, not strawman it, and should specifically address whether those triage-algorithm examples actually support the instructors' point or just muddy the waters. Don't need citations or a reading list, just solid reasoning the board can act on.
