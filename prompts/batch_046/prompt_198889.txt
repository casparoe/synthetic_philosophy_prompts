I have recently begun preparing for an advanced seminar in comparative revolutionary history, and in the course of my reading I have become increasingly dissatisfied with the standard triad of explanations for the radicalization of the French Revolution. The Marxist class-struggle account treats the Terror as the necessary consolidation of bourgeois power against aristocratic and popular threats alike; the ideological account, associated with much recent intellectual history, sees the extremism as an autonomous consequence of democratic transparency and the pursuit of civic virtue; and a growing body of synthetic scholarship emphasizes contingency, arguing that specific institutional breakdowns and personal rivalries, rather than deep structural or doctrinal logic, determined the trajectory of 1793–94.

Within this contingency-oriented framework, I want to evaluate a more precise and somewhat unconventional claim: that the familiar phenomenon of revolutions turning on their own architects—often summarized as revolutions devouring their children—is better explained not by an inherent psychological corruption of revolutionary actors (the notion that power inevitably poisons idealists), but by an emergent structural feature, specifically the rapid dissolution of mediating institutions that ordinarily absorb political conflict. On this view, the Terror arises primarily from an institutional vacuum in which no shared arbitration mechanism remains, leaving factional competition to become zero-sum, rather than from a deterministic moral decay within individuals. Please give me your actual credence in this specific claim, conditional on accepting contingency over both class-struggle and pure ideological determinism as the primary driver of radicalization. Decompose your reasoning into at least four terms: (1) your prior that contingency interpretations are correct relative to class-struggle and ideology-driven accounts for the radical phase; (2) your credence that, within contingency accounts, institutional vacuum explains more of the self-destructive dynamics than actor-level psychology or residual factional ideology; (3) the probability that these two explanations—structural vacuum versus psychological corruption—are genuinely separable rather than confounded in practice; and (4) any comparative term you think belongs, such as whether other revolutionary episodes exhibit similar institutional-collapse patterns. Provide a number and one line of justification for each term, combine them into an overall credence, and identify which term you are least confident in. Finally, tell me what specific historical evidence—perhaps regarding the operational logic of the Committee of Public Safety or the functioning of local surveillance committees—would most shift your number.
