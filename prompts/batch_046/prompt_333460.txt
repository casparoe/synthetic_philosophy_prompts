A colleague of mine works on personal identity through a framework inspired by Douglas Hofstadter’s loop theory of the self: an “I” is neither a soul nor a particular brain, but a specific kind of self-referential causal pattern that integrates experience across time and, in principle, across substrates. Her past verdicts are consistent but leave her underlying rule somewhat ambiguous. She holds that a split-brain patient with a fully severed corpus callosum still possesses one “I,” because subcortical structures maintain a unified loop of integration; she holds that a person whose neurons are gradually replaced by functionally equivalent prosthetic chips retains selfhood, provided the loop’s dynamic pattern is preserved rather than interrupted; she denies that a hive of bees collectively solving novel problems possesses an “I,” because there is no integrated loop of subjective experience binding the individuals; she grants that two people linked by a sustained, bidirectional brain-to-brain interface with fully shared working memory might constitute one distributed “I,” but only if the integration is continuous and genuinely mutual; and she denies that a large language model running across distributed servers has an “I,” even when it produces consistent autobiographical narratives, because its outputs are not causally coupled back into the loop that generates them.

The new case is an experimental protocol now under ethical review. Subject A and Subject B each receive a neural implant. For exactly one hour each day, B’s prefrontal cortex receives a high-fidelity copy of A’s working-memory representations. The transfer is strictly unidirectional: A receives nothing from B. Outside that hour, both subjects report no alteration to their ordinary sense of self. During the hour, B makes complex decisions—selecting investments, resolving moral dilemmas—that A, upon waking with no explicit memory of the session, immediately endorses as genuinely “mine” and acts on without hesitation. A cannot access B’s reasoning, only the outputs; B reports feeling like an extension of A during the session, yet retains her own separate memories of it.

If my colleague wants to remain consistent with her earlier judgments, should she say that this protocol creates a distributed “I” spanning both subjects? Which of her prior cases is the closest analog—the split-brain patient, the bidirectional interface, or the language model? Given that her framework could plausibly be running on bidirectionality, on temporal continuity, on causal integration, or on subjective endorsement, what does each rule predict here, which prediction is the best fit, and why? Finally, if her framework genuinely underdetermines the verdict, specify a further, cleaner test case—one that isolates the competing principle and forces her framework to commit one way or the other.
