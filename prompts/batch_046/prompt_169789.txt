I'm taking a graduate seminar on the Vienna Circle's philosophy of value, and we've been working carefully through Moritz Schlick's 1927 essay "On the Meaning of Life." Schlick's central claim, as I understand it, is that the question of life's meaning only arises through a category mistake: we treat intrinsic, self-contained activity—engagement that is complete in itself, like play or absorption in a task—as if it were instrumental labor justified by some further end. Once an activity is pursued primarily as a means to an externally validated outcome, he argues, it forfeits precisely the quality that could have rendered it meaningful. I'm trying to apply this framework to a concrete institutional problem outside academic philosophy. A municipal funding body I advise is designing evaluation protocols for a new arts-and-mental-health initiative that treats "subjective life meaning" as a quantifiable output variable. The program structure is being calibrated to maximize pre- and post-intervention scores on standardized meaning-in-life scales, and the funders require evidence of measurable impact to justify continued public expenditure.

Given Schlick's distinction between meaningful activity and purpose-driven labor, please describe the most important implications for how this initiative should be structured and assessed. Specifically, address whether there is a principled conceptual distinction between measuring psychological well-being and measuring "meaning" in Schlick's sense; identify institutional safeguards that could prevent the evaluation apparatus itself from destroying the conditions for the meaningful engagement it claims to foster; and respond seriously to the program officer's consequentialist objection that without outcome metrics, public funding for mental-health programming is ethically indefensible. Please treat the strongest version of that objection rather than dismissing it, and keep the analysis focused on the conceptual architecture rather than on secondary scholarship or citation lists.
