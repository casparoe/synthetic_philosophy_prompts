At a workshop on historiography last month, two colleagues—let's call them N and K—fell into a sustained argument about whether global history can escape Eurocentrism without abandoning the very idea of a shared past. N opened by insisting that any world-historical narrative built around a single chronology, even one presented as neutral like Common Era dating, necessarily subordinates non-European temporal schemes—Islamic hijri calendars, Chinese dynastic cycles, Indigenous seasonal reckonings—to a Western framework, and therefore reproduces the centrism it claims to overcome. K replied that without a shared metric of simultaneity, causal comparison across regions collapses: you cannot determine whether an environmental crisis in one region and a political rupture in another are historically related if you refuse to place them in a common temporal grid. N then proposed what she termed a polycentric chronology, in which each region retains its own temporal logic and cross-regional relations are mapped through material traces—silver circulation, botanical transfers, manuscript translations—rather than through imposed calendar coincidence, so that correlation is established by physical and textual evidence rather than by chronological alignment. K countered that selecting which traces matter already requires a hidden master narrative, since designating the Mongol expansions or the Atlantic silver trade as significant hinges depends on historiographical judgments formed within Euro-American traditions; he added that jettisoning shared chronology reduces global history to disconnected anthropological vignettes and dissolves the object of study into incommensurable local stories. In her final move, N argued that K was conflating explanation with chronology: stratigraphic and archival evidence can establish that one event preceded and likely caused another without requiring both to be translated into a universal year, and that demanding such translation is precisely the epistemic violence she had identified.

Please adjudicate this exchange point by point. For each of N's and K's interventions—the initial charge of hidden chronology, the simultaneity objection, the polycentric material-trace proposal, K's hidden-master-narrative reply together with his dissolution worry, and N's final distinction between explanation and chronological translation—rate the move from clearly mistaken to clearly right and briefly justify the rating. Then identify what both parties are missing, especially whether there exists a principled middle ground between chronological universalism and full polycentric incommensurability, and whether their disagreement ultimately concerns the nature of historical explanation itself rather than merely the politics of dating.
