ok so ive been playing with font design and keep running into this thing i dont have a word for. imagine a display typeface where the lowercase 'a' is just a triangle with a tiny stem—no bowl, no tail, looks almost like a delta. and the lowercase 'g' is a perfect circle with a tail that curls the wrong way, like a mirror-image 'q'. in isolation people misread both: they call the 'a' a weird greek letter and the 'g' a q. but drop them into words—'apple', 'garden'—and suddenly everyone reads them correctly, and not really because of word context. it feels like recognition comes from the font's overall uniform: every other letter shares that same sharp triangular vibe or that same curled tail, so the brain assigns the letter based on which style-family it belongs to, not on its own bones.

i can think of a second case: in some hand-lettered alphabets the 't' loses its crossbar entirely and gains a loop like an 'f', but because every other letter in that alphabet is drawn with these exaggerated loops, people still read it as 't' inside a word, even thogh the shape is closer to an 'f' geometrically. again, the style fingerprint pulls it back.

is there an established concept for this? i know about letter-essence ideas from that old Hofstadter letter-spirit project, and about word-superiority effects in reading, but this seems different—its not about the letter's invariant form and its not about linguistic context either. its like stylistic surrogacy, where the alphabet's collective style carries the identity of a single misshapen letter. does that have a name in typography or cognitive science? if not, how should we define it and where does it sit relative to the idea that every 'a' is still an 'a' regardless of style? im particularly curious whether this counts as a failure mode for the essence view, or just a weird boundary case where style and category collapse into each other.
