I've been retired from the materials lab for a few years now — mostly worked on ceramic composites, nothing glamorous — and lately I've been filling the afternoons with old philosophy and autobiography texts. I got pulled into Benjamin Franklin's scheme for moral perfection in his autobiography, where he lists those thirteen virtues and tracks his slips in a little notbook like he was running a long-term experiment with n=1. I started doing my own version, treating my habits like data sets, which probably sounds ridiculous but old scientists don't change that easily.

Anyway, here's the concrete thing. My grandson is running a small community garden project with some friends, and they botched the soil prep because they used the wrong compost ration. I knew the fix immediately — forty years of messing with material mixtures teaches you to spot a bad blend — but I stayed quiet because Franklin lists humility near the end, and I didn't want to be the old expert steamrolling the kids. They lost thier share of the grant funding when the seedlings faild. Now I'm wondering if Franklin's empirical approach to virtue actually breaks down here: if you suppress your competence to avoid the vice of pride, aren't you just letting negligence or false modesty take its place? Is there a philosophical distinction between humility as a disposition and humility as self-silencing? I feel like there's a deeper tension between using your knowledge and suppressing it for moral purity, but I can't quite map it onto Franklin's system.
