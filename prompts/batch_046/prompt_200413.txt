For a philosophy seminar on collective memory and moral obligation, design a decision tree — with a brief scoring key — that sorts normative positions on whether and how societies should preserve memories of historical wrongs. Anchor the tree in this concrete case: after a civil conflict ends, survivors report that annual public commemorations re-traumatize them, while removing monuments to perpetrators appears to erase accountability. Build the tree from logical space rather than from a list of names: pose exactly three binary diagnostic nodes, each framed as a direct claim answerable without knowing the theoretical labels. Use these three: (1) whether the primary moral function of remembrance is to secure justice/accountability or to sustain survivor solidarity and healing; (2) whether present psychological harm to survivors counts as decisive against sustaining ritual commemoration; (3) whether maintaining an archival/historical record is morally distinct from sustaining public ritual practices, such that one could be required while the other is suppressed. Then map actual positions onto the eight answer-combinations: include a Margalit-style view that shared memory of wrongs is constitutive of moral community and must be preserved despite present discomfort (treating archive and ritual as jointly required and harm as non-decisive); a Rieff-style view that collective forgetting can be a moral achievement and that commemorative politics risks perpetuating grievance (treating harm as decisive and denying a standing collective duty to ritualize); and a hybrid view that protects archival records but permits suppressing ritual practices when they cause harm. Diagnose at least one unoccupied combination — for example, denying any collective duty to remember while still insisting that ritual commemoration is mandatory for national identity — and say whether the gap is due to incoherence, unattractiveness, or mere neglect. Note any empty combinations briefly, and flag any pair of named positions (e.g., Margalit's view and a strong "mnemonic justice" position) that converge on the same path and therefore cannot be separated by your tree. Present each node as a direct question, list the leaves with their path-codes, and keep the response to no more than 350 words.
