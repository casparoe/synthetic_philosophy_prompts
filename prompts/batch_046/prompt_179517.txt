(3) Now suppose a manuscript by an unknown author was destroyed without ever being read, and an artificial system later produces, through pure statistical coincidence and without any access to the lost text, a sequence that matches it word for word. (4) That generated text lacks the correct causal origin, yet it instantiates precisely the formal sequence the author intended. (5) Therefore, should the original manuscript eventually surface, it ought to be valued over the generated reproduction, despite their complete formal identity, because only it bears the proper causal relation to the author's mind. (6) But apply the same principle consistently to a human forger who independently writes the identical novel by sheer coincidence: this forger also lacks the correct causal origin, yet we might feel the achievement carries a kind of artistic weight that the machine's output does not, which suggests that causal origin alone cannot account for our judgments. (7) Conversely, if we deny that causal history matters and claim that the work is defined solely by its formal properties, then the lost manuscript, the algorithmic reproduction, and the forger's coincidence must all be treated as equally valid instances of the same work, which erases the notion of a literary original altogether and further implies that restored manuscripts in which archivists have reconstructed missing pages from other copies are partly inauthentic, since those reconstructed pages lack direct causal connection to the author.
