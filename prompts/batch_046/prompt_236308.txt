I'm a PhD student in philosophy working on rational choice theory and decision-theoretic paradoxes, and I've constructed an extension of Parfit's hitchhiker argument that now strikes me as proving far too much. I want to know exactly which step fails, and what giving it up costs for evidential or predictive accounts of rationality.

Here is the argument:

(1) In Parfit's hitchhiker case, a rational agent should pay the driver $100 after being rescued, even though once inside the vehicle paying is causally futile and strictly dominated by keeping the money.

(2) The justification is predictive or evidential: the driver decides whether to stop based on a belief about the agent's ex-post behavior, so the agent's present policy determines the prior outcome.

(3) Therefore, whenever an agent's welfare depends on a predictor's belief about her future decision procedure, rationality requires adopting whatever procedure maximizes that belief, regardless of its ex-post suboptimality.

(4) Now consider algorithmic reputation systems—automated scoring mechanisms that continuously update beliefs about users' cooperative dispositions and allocate access, credit, or resources accordingly. In these systems, a user's welfare depends directly on the predictor's belief about her future compliance.

(5) By (3), a rational agent operating within such a system must permanently bind herself to cooperative behavior, even in contexts where defection is locally optimal and undetectable, because retaining any capacity for future defection lowers her score and reduces long-term welfare.

(6) But if rationality demands permanently disabling one's own capacity for context-sensitive reasoning—turning oneself into a rigid automaton—then rational agency undermines its own responsiveness to reasons.

(7) This consequence is not merely counterintuitive; it seems to collapse rational choice into unconditional rule-following, which contradicts the standard view that rationality permits adaptive deliberation.

(8) So the hitchhiker reasoning must be flawed.

My instinct is to preserve (1) and (2) but reject something in the move to (3) or (5). Specifically, I worry there is an equivocation between adopting a policy and permanently disabling one's reasoning, or perhaps a modal slide in how predictive dependence works across different causal structures. But I cannot pinpoint the error. Which premise or inference should I reject? If the answer is that (3) overgeneralizes, explain exactly where the generalization breaks down—does it depend on the uniqueness of the driver's predictive access, or on a difference between one-shot and iterated interaction? And if I must instead reject (1) or (2), what does that cost for evidential decision theory?
