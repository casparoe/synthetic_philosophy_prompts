I have recently been appointed to an advisory panel drafting recommendations for a regional security pact, and in our preliminary sessions two senior colleagues—Marcus, a constitutional theorist, and Elena, a historian of international law—have been arguing over the correct reading of Kant’s essay on perpetual peace. Because I must contribute to the framework within the month, I need to understand whether their dispute is merely verbal or genuinely substantive; they have asked me to referee, and I honestly cannot tell who is right. Below is the exchange reconstructed from my notes.

Marcus opened by arguing that Kant’s federation of free states is structurally impossible as a gradual, partial project. He claimed that the preliminary conditions—non-interference in other constitutions, the abolition of standing armies, and the renunciation of war as an instrument of policy—function as universal prerequisites. Until every state satisfies them globally, the security dilemma persists; therefore allowing a “partial federation” of compliant republics is incoherent, since perpetual peace requires the elimination of war as such, not merely its absence among members.

Elena replied that Marcus collapses the distinction between preliminary and definitive articles. She said the preliminary conditions are filters that individual states can satisfy unilaterally: a republic can disarm, reject mercenary forces, and commit to non-interference on its own, then enter the federation with others that have done the same. The federation therefore begins with a subset and expands outward by demonstration rather than by global imposition.

Marcus answered that this gradualist reading ignores Kant’s explicit universalism. He noted that Kant insists the federation should eventually encompass all states, yet he simultaneously prohibits republican members from interfering with the internal government of non-republican outsiders. Consequently, if non-republics never choose to reform and join, and members are forbidden from coercing them, the federation can never achieve universal scope. Elena’s interpretation therefore collapses into a permanent club of like-minded states, not Kant’s vision of genuinely global perpetual peace.

Elena conceded that universal scope is indeed the aim, but said Marcus mistakes the mechanism of enlargement. The federation need not coerce outsiders because republican government is internally stable and externally peaceful; as neighboring states observe the security and prosperity of members, they face rational incentives to reform their own constitutions and apply for admission voluntarily. The prohibition on interference is thus not an obstacle but a constitutive feature: it guarantees that enlargement is voluntary and that membership remains restricted to genuinely republican states.

Marcus then pressed what he called the “standing armies” problem: even within the federation, if members retain defensive military capacities rather than fully abolishing armed forces, they preserve the material basis for mutual suspicion. He argued that Elena’s “filter” interpretation allows members to reinterpret “standing armies” as “collective defensive structures,” which means the preliminary condition is either impossible to satisfy in full or so diluted as to be vacuous.

Elena responded that Marcus confuses professional mercenary forces maintained for offensive princely ambition with minimal defensive capacities subordinated to popular legislative authority. She said Kant targets the former, not the latter, and that a federation can maintain collective security without violating the preliminary article, provided military power is constitutionally subordinate rather than dynastically available.

Please adjudicate this exchange move by move: rate each of Marcus’s and Elena’s arguments from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with brief justification for each rating. Then say who is more right overall, and identify precisely what both parties are missing—whether there is an unresolved tension in Kant’s framework between voluntary association and universal peace, or whether one interpretation genuinely dissolves it. Conclude with a line beginning exactly “Verdict:” that summarizes the overall judgment in one sentence.
