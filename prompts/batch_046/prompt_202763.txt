In debates about scaling artificial cognition, Daniel Dennett’s four kinds of minds—Darwinian, Skinnerian, Popperian, and Gregorian—are frequently mapped onto a stack of computing architectures: Darwinian creatures run evolutionary firmware; Skinnerian ones run updateable reinforcement drivers; Popperian ones run internal sandbox models for mental testing; and Gregorian ones run distributed, culturally shared libraries with open interfaces for language and tools. Proponents use this stack to argue that building Gregorian-level AI is mainly a matter of integrating and scaling the lower layers. Audit the analogy precisely: provide the correspondence table (genome, reward signal, mental model, cultural repository, etc.), then identify where the architecture metaphor breaks—particularly whether Gregorian culture is best seen as an open API or as a form of exosomatic inheritance with competing, incompatible versions—and evaluate whether the scaling conclusion survives. Separate the disanalogies that undermine the engineering claim from those that are merely decorative, and if the analogy fails at a specific layer, propose a more apt comparison for that transition.
