I serve on a neuroethics oversight committee that reviews protocols for targeted intracranial stimulation intended to restore positive affect in patients with severe, treatment-resistant anhedonia. Because different subjects report wildly heterogeneous subjective changes—some describe a diffuse bodily warmth, others a sudden cognitive clarity, others an abstract sense of "rightness" with no sensory content—the board needs a reusable, theory-grounded decision procedure to determine whether a given patient report should be classified as pleasure proper (and therefore count as a primary therapeutic benefit) rather than as secondary relief, satisfaction, or mere absence of distress. The procedure must be usable by committee members without formal philosophical training, executable in roughly twenty minutes per case, and honest about where classification is a matter of fact versus an unavoidable value judgment.

The procedure should be constructed around the heterogeneity problem for hedonism: since enjoying a meal, solving a mathematical problem, and feeling a cool breeze share no single intrinsic qualitative feature, we cannot classify by "what it feels like." Instead, operationalize a structural-relational criterion—namely, whether the state is experienced as non-instrumentally valuable in its own occurrence, coupled with a "modal coherence test" asking whether the patient would still endorse the state as good-in-itself upon learning that its origin is artificial stimulation rather than natural recovery. Lay out the questions in strict order, specify what counts as a sufficient answer to each, identify the precise point where the procedure halts with a negative classification, and mark the stage where judgment must take over because the heterogeneity of pleasure makes further mechanization impossible. Apply the procedure to three hypothetical reports: a diffuse bodily warmth lacking cognitive content; intense satisfaction upon completing a simple arithmetic task immediately after stimulation; and a profound but entirely contentless "sense of being correct" that the patient cannot describe further. One application should yield an answer that the clinical team will find unwelcome, and you should explicitly state where the procedure misfires or collapses—particularly for small-scale, brief experiences where structural coherence is hard to verify.
