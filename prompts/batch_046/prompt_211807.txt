I've been asked to help shape a three-year research agenda for an interdisciplinary working group—geologists who work on chronostratigraphic formalism and legal historians who study climate-attribution frameworks—who need to decide which open problems about the Anthropocene's temporal boundaries actually merit their limited graduate-student hours and fieldwork budget. They are explicitly not interested in rehearsing the familiar public debates over whether the epoch began in 1610, 1784, or 1945; instead, they want to know which unresolved questions would genuinely change how either stratigraphic governance or historical liability scholarship operates if answered. The group's central tension is that geological epoch-definition requires globally synchronous stratigraphic signals and institutional ratification by bodies like the International Commission on Stratigraphy, whereas climate-liability reasoning needs historically meaningful breakpoints that track cumulative responsibility without requiring universal geological correlation.

Given these competing purposes, I need a defended prioritization—not a survey—of the open questions or lines of work that matter most for this specific hybrid audience. Please rank the priorities and justify the ordering using explicit criteria: tractability within a three-year horizon, how many other problems depend on resolving this one, whether an answer would actually alter practice for stratigraphers or liability scholars, and the current ratio of neglect to importance. For each lower-ranked item, explain why its visibility in broader discourse does not translate to value for this group's particular ends. I want to know where limited resources should go, what evidence or argumentation would be decisive for the top-ranked questions, and which apparent cruxes are actually dead ends or distractions—so that I can present the group with a concrete agenda rather than a reading list.
