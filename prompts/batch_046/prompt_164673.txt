I am a doctoral student in moral philosophy, specifically working on practical reason and value pluralism. Over the course of my research, I have repeatedly observed a particular argumentative and psychological configuration in debates about genuinely hard choices — situations in which two options appear incommensurable, with neither being better, worse, nor exactly equal to the other — and I am uncertain whether the literature assigns it a distinct name or treats it merely as a variant of more familiar phenomena. The pattern is this. A deliberator confronts options that are genuinely on a par: for example, committing to an academic career in philosophy versus entering legal practice. After selecting one — say, accepting the philosophy position — the agent typically does not maintain that the decision was rationally underdetermined or that the commitment itself generated a new reason, which is the resolution proposed by some philosophical accounts of hard choice. Instead, the agent retrospectively revises the pre-decisional value landscape: philosophy is now construed as having possessed a slight but decisive advantage all along, or law is reconceived as harboring hidden disvalues — bureaucratic stagnation, moral compromise — that were not previously operative in the comparison. Crucially, this is not direct emotional devaluation of the unchosen option, as in sour-grape reasoning, nor is the chosen option openly acknowledged as made valuable by the very act of selection. Rather, the prior evaluation is quietly rewritten so that the decision appears to have tracked weightier reasons than it actually did. Does this specific form of post-commitment retroactive rationalization have an established concept or literature? If not, I want to understand precisely how it should be distinguished from Ruth Chang's view that commitment can create reasons in cases of parity, from cognitive dissonance reduction, and from adaptive preference formation. In particular, does this phenomenon undermine normative theories that treat hard choices as moments where rational determination genuinely runs out, or can such theories accommodate it as a mere psychological epiphenomenon without compromising their structural claims?
