I am a retired experimental physicist who has recently begun formal study in philosophy, and I am preparing a one-semester independent-study proposal—roughly twelve weeks, part-time—for a philosophy seminar on rational choice. My half-formed idea concerns the standard coherence arguments for expected-utility maximization, particularly Dutch-book and money-pump demonstrations. In physics, I am accustomed to gauge symmetries: different mathematical representations can describe the same physical state without any being ontologically privileged. I suspect that a structurally similar representational redundancy has been underappreciated in decision theory. The representation theorems that underpin coherence arguments establish that rational preferences can be represented by a probability measure and a utility function unique up to positive affine transformation; yet the philosophical literature usually treats this uniqueness condition as a technical footnote rather than a conceptual problem. I want the proposal to ask precisely this: do coherence arguments establish a substantive normative constraint—that rational agents must maximize expected utility in some uniquely correct representation—or do they merely enforce a gauge-invariant structural consistency, such that incoherent preferences are unstable across all equivalent representations but coherent ones need not commit to a single expected-utility description?

The proposal should explain why existing treatments leave this open: defenders of coherence arguments tend to slide from “there exists a representation in which the agent maximizes EU” to “the agent maximizes EU,” while critics of Dutch books usually attack the empirical plausibility of the betting interpretation without examining whether the exploitation argument survives transformation across all admissible representations. For method, I want an approach that uses the gauge analogy strictly as a conceptual probe rather than importing formal physics: examine whether vulnerability to a money pump is representation-dependent, whether allowing appropriate adjustments to both probability and utility can render a putatively incoherent preference ordering immune to exploitation, and what this implies for the normative force of coherence. The deliverable should state what success would look like—demonstrating that coherence arguments constrain preference structure but underdetermine representational form in a specific, philosophically significant way—versus failure, such as showing that decision-theoretic representations lack the relevant symmetry and that the analogy collapses entirely. Finally, include an honest assessment of scope: can someone with scientific training but limited formal background in philosophy or economics make meaningful progress on this in one term, and where should the project be truncated if it proves too large?

Please do not include a bibliography, citation list, or reading recommendations; I will supply references myself. I also ask that the proposal avoid invoking highly technical results in either physics or decision theory that a philosophically well-read generalist would not recognize—keep the machinery to standard coherence arguments and familiar analogies from representational redundancy in science. The tone should be formal and appropriate for an academic proposal, and the framing should reflect my position as a scientist entering philosophy rather than that of a specialist in either field.
