I work as a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy, and lately I have been trying to understand how institutional rules encode implicit theories of value rather than just solving practical problems. In discussing this with colleagues, I noticed that jurisdictions regulate commercial sex in three structurally different ways, and the differences seem to go deeper than enforcement strategy. Under what is often called the Nordic model—used in Sweden and adopted in several other countries—the law criminalizes the buyer but not the seller, as though the party supplying the service is not blameworthy while the party creating the demand bears full moral and legal responsibility. In Germany and the Netherlands, the activity is legalized and regulated as labor: licensing, health inspections, and contractual obligations apply to both sides, treating the interaction as a symmetric exchange supervised by the state. In New Zealand, decriminalization removes criminal penalties for both parties and largely withdraws the state, framing the transaction as private conduct between consenting adults that raises no special moral problem beyond ordinary contract or labor disputes.

These frameworks do not merely distribute penalties differently; they seem to assign agency, vulnerability, and culpability in incompatible directions. The Nordic model appears to presuppose that consent is directionally compromised—that the seller’s agreement is structurally constrained in a way that removes blame without removing rights, while the buyer retains fully autonomous accountability for initiating the exchange. Legalization, by contrast, seems to presuppose liberal neutrality: the state does not judge the moral character of the exchange but merely ensures that contracting parties meet regulatory standards, implying that both sides exercise symmetric agency. Decriminalization might extend that neutrality further, or it might rest on a stronger claim that sexual services are not a distinctive category of good whose commodification corrupts autonomy at all. Yet I am unsure whether these are genuinely incompatible metaphysical commitments or whether each framework is philosophically underdetermined—compatible with several theories of harm, consent, or gendered economic pressure.

Could you reconstruct the implicit philosophical commitments about moral agency, consent, and commodification that each of these three institutional patterns strictly requires, as opposed to those it merely fits well with? In particular, does the Nordic asymmetry depend on a specific view that vulnerability nullifies culpability in one direction only, or can a control-based or strategic explanation account for the same legal structure without that metaphysical claim? If the underlying view were replaced by a theory that treats all voluntary exchange symmetrically—ignoring directional differences in economic pressure or social stigma—what concrete features of each regulatory framework would have to change? Please avoid bibliographies or reading lists; I am trying to map the conceptual architecture from first principles, and I want to know whether a single coherent theory could license all three approaches in different contexts or whether they rest on views that are genuinely irreconcilable.
