I am preparing a recorded interview for an academic podcast with an unnamed historian of early American thought who has recently argued that Benjamin Franklin’s scheme of moral perfection in his Autobiography should be read not as a religious or Stoic program of character formation, but as an application of double-entry bookkeeping logic to the self. On her view, the thirteen virtues function like ledger accounts, daily failures are debits against accumulated habits, and Franklin’s decision to begin with Temperance reflects fiscal prudence rather than any spiritual hierarchy—temperance preserves the willpower capital required to maintain the other entries without bankrupting the system. I want eight to ten questions that press this interpretation well beyond having her restate the analogy. Please include at least one question that targets Franklin’s explicit admission that he never achieved perfection, which on her economic model looks less like successful management and more like an insolvent account; one that asks how she accommodates Franklin’s acknowledged debts to Quaker practices of spiritual accounting and to Cotton Mather’s Essays to Do Good, sources that frame self-examination in explicitly theological rather than economic terms; and one focused on the thirteenth virtue, Humility, which Franklin appended almost as an afterthought and which, unlike the others, offers no clear quantitative metric for tracking success or failure within the grid. For each of these three, provide a sharp follow-up keyed to the reply she is most likely to offer—for instance, if she claims that an insolvent ledger can still be rationally managed, ask whether that renders Franklin’s project genuinely aspirational or merely a descriptive record of inevitable human limitation. Keep the tone rigorous but accessible to an educated general audience, avoid generic biographical openings, and do not ask for citations, bibliographies, or reading lists.
