I work on conceptual frameworks for attributing moral and legal responsibility when an autonomous system initiates a harmful outcome without a specific human command at the precise moment of action. In trying to locate responsibility, I repeatedly find that every proposed position collapses into one of four apparently exhaustive categories, yet each category defeats itself under scrutiny. First, the system alone bears full responsibility, which requires treating a statistical model as an agent with intentions and seems to project a fictional ontology onto computation. Second, the nearest human operator bears full responsibility, but this ignores that the system produced outputs the operator neither specifically authorized nor could have foreseen, rendering the notion of control illusory. Third, responsibility is divided proportionally between system and operator, yet there is no shared metric of agency that applies across the human-machine boundary, so any ratio appears arbitrary rather than justified. Fourth, responsibility is void—no party is accountable—but this undermines the very legal and moral practices that prompt the inquiry, and it offers no account of why the situation demands resolution rather than resignation. I have observed an analogous four-cornered deadlock in debates about literary translation: is the work authored by the original poet, by the translator, by both in some shared measure, or is authorship itself dissolved? In both domains, what strikes me is not a simple false dichotomy or trilemma, but a four-part exhaustion in which every corner fails. I have heard that certain Buddhist philosophical traditions—particularly those associated with Nāgārjuna—employ a four-cornered logical form that systematically rejects affirmation, negation, both together, and neither. Does this pattern have an established name? How is it different from an ordinary trilemma, or from dialectical reasoning that merely sets up opposing sides to be synthesized? And is there an important distinction between deploying this form as a purely skeptical instrument—showing that every proposed inherent nature collapses—and deploying it in support of a positive philosophical claim about emptiness? I am not asking for a bibliography or reading list; I want to know whether the carving I have made matches an established concept, where its precise edges lie, and what the nearest misclassifications would be.
