I was arguing with my roommate about responsability gaps for autonomous systems and realized I don't actually have a consistent view, just gut reactions. Here are my verdicts: a self-driving truck swerves to miss a fallen tree and kills a cyclist; the manufactuer tested for obstacles but not fallen trees, and the human driver was napping. I think the company is more blameworthy than the driver, since that failure mode was design-foreseeable. Next, a hospital uses an AI triage bot that sends a sick patient home because of a rare symptom cluster; the nurse ignores her own hunch because the bot said low risk. I say the nurse is mostly excused and the hospital takes the hit for bad deployement protocols. Then, an autonomous drone swarm strikes civilians after a corrupted satellite feed; the commander authorized the strike but didn't know about the corruption. I feel like this is a real gap—no one is fully morally responsible—but I still want the commander to face some professional sanction even without full blame. Finally, a tiny language model embedded in a legal drafting tool inserts an outdated liability clause; the lawyer copies it without reading, the firm trained the model on pre-2020 contracts, and the client suffers. Here I want to blame both, especially the lawyer for not checking, but also the firm for using a cheap small model they knew was unreliable.

The tension is that in case three I want a gap with only residual accountability, but in case four I pile blame on everyone despite the model being the direct cause. Is there a coherent principle about distributed epistemic responsability that saves all four verdicts, or do I have to drop one? And what would it say about an unconsidered case: an AI system advises a medical treatment, the patient agrees after being told it's AI-advised, but they don't actually understand the risks?
