I'm trying to determine whether my judgments about foreseen but unintended harm can be captured by a coherent moral principle—perhaps a refined version of the doctrine of double effect, or perhaps something that abandons parts of it. Here are my verdicts on concrete cases.

Case 1: A surgeon kills one healthy patient and harvests her organs to save five others who will otherwise die. The death of the one is the direct means by which the organs are obtained. My verdict: impermissible.

Case 2: A switch operator diverts a runaway trolley from a track with five people to a track with one. The death of the one is not the means by which the five are saved; it is a side effect of the diversion. My verdict: permissible.

Case 3: I design an automated subway defense system. If five passengers fall onto the tracks, the system deploys a physical barrier that saves them but will necessarily crush a maintenance worker in an adjacent access tunnel. I do not know who the worker will be, and the crushing is not the mechanism by which the five are saved (the barrier's position saves them). My verdict: permissible, though I feel it is closer to Case 2 than to Case 1.

Case 4: I program an autonomous vehicle's collision algorithm to always minimize total fatalities. In a forced-choice scenario, this entails steering into one pedestrian to avoid killing five passengers. I do not intend any particular death; I intend only to implement the minimization rule generally. My verdict: permissible to implement, but I feel this should be treated differently from Case 3, even though both involve automated, institutional mediation of harm. I cannot articulate why.

Case 5: A vaccine is approved that prevents ten thousand deaths but carries a known, statistically predictable risk of fatal reaction in one in a thousand recipients. The ten expected deaths are side effects of the immunization mechanism, not the means by which protection is conferred. My verdict: clearly permissible.

Case 6: A carbon tax is imposed knowing it will cause severe economic hardship and increased mortality in a specific coal-dependent region. The emissions reduction works through market price signals, not through the regional suffering; however, part of my reasoning for supporting the tax is that the visible hardship demonstrates political resolve, which strengthens the policy's credibility and therefore its environmental effectiveness. I keep going back and forth: part of me says this is like Case 2, part says that because the regional harm enters my reasoning about why the policy works, it becomes like Case 1.

Is there one principle—stated precisely, with each clause motivated by the case it explains—that delivers all six verdicts? If not, what is the smallest set of competing principles, and which of my verdicts must each abandon? Please also say what your preferred account predicts for a case I have not considered: a genetic therapy cures a fatal disease in 95% of patients, but in 5% with an easily detectable genetic marker it is fatal. The therapy is made available without requiring screening, because testing everyone would delay treatment and cost lives among the 95%. Is distributing it without screening permissible?

Please be explicit about which of my judgments—particularly the uneasy pairing of Case 3 with Case 4, and my indecision about Case 6—your principle cannot accommodate.
