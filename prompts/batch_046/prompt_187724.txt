me and my roommate have been going in circles about hard choices and incommensurable values. not asking for life advice, just want someone to referee a fight between two friends of mine.

so Jade and Marcus (both in my study group) got stuck arguing about this case: Jade turned down a tenure-track philosophy job to become a carpenter, and she says it was a genuinely hard choice -- the values weren't just different in degree but in kind, on a par or whatever. she thinks there's no hidden fact about which career was better, so she didn't discover the right option; instead, by committing to carpentry she made herself into the kind of person for whom that path is best. so her choice actually creates new reasons that justify it retroactively.

Marcus says that's just circular bootstrapping. if values are truly incommensurable there's no common metric, so saying "i commit to X and therefore X is now rational for me" is just psychological adaptation dressed up as philosophy. he compares it to flipping a coin to break a tie: the coin resolves indecision but doesn't justify the result. commitment might make you feel better about the outcome, but it can't generate normative reasons out of thin air.

Jade fired back that promises work the exact same way: you don't have a pre-existing reason to keep every specific promise, but by making one you generate an obligation. so commitment can create normative force without being circular.

Marcus said promises are different because there's already a background social norm of fidelity. with career choices there's no analogous prior rule saying "once you pick carpentry you must value it more." without that norm, Jade is just describing rationalization after the fact, not real reason-creation.

Jade then said maybe the background norm is just authenticity -- being true to the commitments you make. Marcus said thats circular too: authenticity only tells you to stick with what you picked, not why picking it was rational in the first place.
