I've been reading some old LessWrong threads on counterfactual robustness and got sidetracked into dispositions — specifically whether finks and masks make the whole concept unfalsifiable. I built this argument and now it feels suspicious because the conclusion is absurd, but I can't name the exit. (1) To have a disposition is basically to be such that if trigger conditions C happen, result M follows, unless there's a fink or mask. (2) But for any object O and any proposed disposition D, I can always postulate a recursve mask: a hidden mechanism that removes D exactly when C is about to occur, and that is itself masked by a second-order mechanism if anyone tries to detect it. (3) So whenever O fails to show M under C, we can always save the attribution of D by saying O was masked. (4) That means the claim O has D is never empirically distinguishable from O lacks D but carries a hidden mask. (5) If two claims are never empirically distinguishable, they don't differ in factual content — at best one's just a heuristc label. (6) So dispositional talk isn't describing real properties, just labeling patterns. (7) But chemistry and physics treat solubility, charge, fragility as real explanatory properties. (8) So either science tracks non-factual labels, or one of the steps is wrong.

I want to keep (1) and (7) — dispositions are counterfactual and science is real — so I guess I have to attack (2) or (5)? But (2) just seems like extending the classic fink idea, and (5) smells like verificationism I don't actually endorse. Is the slip that recursve masks violate parsimony, or that empirical indistinguishability doesn't kill factuality if there's a structural explanation? tbh I'd rather not pay the price of giving up counterfactuals or turning physics into a heuristic game, but I'm not sure what I'm actually committing to if I reject (5). Which step do you think breaks, and what's the cost of breaking it?
