Design a 90-minute classroom activity for fourteen advanced undergraduate or early graduate students in a philosophy of ethics or religion seminar. The session should make participants feel the internal logic and practical cost of Augustine’s absolute prohibition on lying (mendacium), particularly the structural pressure it exerts toward mental reservation and technically true but misleading speech. Draw on Augustine’s graded taxonomy of eight kinds of lie from De Mendacio—ordered roughly from falsehood about religious doctrine through harmful deception down to lighter, even playful forms—without asking students to reproduce the text from memory. Inject the later Catholic doctrine of mental reservation as a strategy that participants should reinvent for themselves before it is named.

Specify the physical setting, the group division, a concrete high-stakes scenario (supply its details within the framing so no outside research is needed), and the rules of interaction. The rules must impose an absolute ban on false statements made with intent to deceive, permit silence, and allow technically true utterances that may mislead—so that pairs acting as “advisors” to a community facing a credible threat must achieve protection without crossing the line into mendacium. Arrange roles and incentives so that students run into the problem through action: they should discover that silence is inadequate and that ambiguity becomes the only workable tool, only recognizing afterward that they have reconstructed mental reservation.

Include a precise timeline (e.g., briefing, paired deliberation, public commitment to statements, crisis resolution), the debrief that maps specific moves onto parts of Augustine’s taxonomy and onto the mental-reservation strategy, an honest prediction of what will go wrong the first time (for instance, one pair breaking character to lie outright, another solving the scenario too quickly through silence alone, or moral discomfort freezing the group), and a frank note on which feature of Augustine’s view—likely his theological claim that lying corrupts the speaker’s soul regardless of harm done—the activity cannot reproduce and the facilitator must supply directly. Do not ask for bibliographies, reading lists, citations, or web-verified details. Write formally, with all necessary scenario conditions embedded in the prompt itself.
