I'm staff on a state constitutional review board that meets every few months, and we need a reusable procedue we can run in about 20 minutes on proposals mixing sortition, term limits, and regular elections. The idea I want turned into practice is this split in democratic legitimacy: there's retrospective punishment, where voters can fire bad reps and that keeps them honest, and then there's statistical mirroring, where the body actually looks like the population in terms of lived conditions. Career politicians mess up both, not because they learn too much, but because they develop existant institutional incentives that decouple them from both punishment and mirroring. So I need a four-gate flowchart in words.

Gate one asks whether the body's work is value-laden or mostly technical. Gate two asks whether voters can observe outcomes well enough to assign blame retrospectively. Gate three asks whether the decision requires lived experience that tracks demographic categories. Gate four asks about career-capture risk if people stay long enough to build loyalty to the institution instead of constituents. For each gate I need the exact question, what counts as yes or no, where the flowchart stops with a concrete recommendation -- elected with term limits, pure sortition, a hybrid, or leave the status quo alone -- and where I have to flag it for political judgment because the theory doesn't actually decide.

Then apply it to three proposals: first, replacing our senate with a 200-person sortition body serving single 3-year terms with no reelection; second, keeping elections but putting an 8-year lifetime cap across both chambers; third, a hybrid where 60 percent are elected on 4-year terms and 40 percent are randomly selected for single 6-year terms, with the idea that the sortition members keep the elected ones honest.

The answer should include the case where institutional memory is genuinely valuable and the procedure still says do something I won't like -- probably recommending pure sortition despite my worry about losing expertise -- and explain why the theory forces that. Also flag the hard limits: this won't tell me the right term length, it doesn't solve who runs the lottery, and it can't weigh democratic legitimacy against other goods like stability. And please don't include any bibliography or citation list; I can't search the web and I don't need them.
