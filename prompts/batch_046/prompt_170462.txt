Construct a decision-tree taxonomy for the ontology of demographic cohorts—specifically whether labels like "Baby Boomers," "Millennials," and "Gen Z" denote real kinds, and if so, of what ontological sort. Begin with this concrete diagnostic commitment: "If a cohort undergoes a unique macro-shock (e.g., a sudden economic collapse or rapid technological saturation) during its formative years, does that shock causally homogenize members in a way that persists independently of aging and period effects?" From there, build branches using only further concrete claims on which positions genuinely diverge—for example, whether cohort boundaries are sharp or porous; whether generational membership explains attitudinal variance better than life-stage and historical-period confounds; whether the category is performatively constituted by marketing discourse and institutional labeling; or whether it tracks nothing more than a family-resemblance cluster lacking explanatory unity. Each intermediate node must be phrased as a yes/no or either/or question answerable without knowing which ontology it yields. The leaves should include at least six precisely characterized positions—such as strong social kind, institutional kind, statistical artifact, performative construct, family-resemblance cluster, and age-period-cohort confound—located in the logical space rather than listed by name first. Note any combinations of answers that leave a logical gap unoccupied, with a brief diagnosis of whether the gap is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Explicitly flag any pair of leaves that the tree cannot cleanly separate. Frame the instrument for a seminar audience that needs to locate its own commitments rather than memorize labels; do not ask for bibliographies, reading lists, or citations.
