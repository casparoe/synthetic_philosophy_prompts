I'm on a small municipal heritage board — we recieve about 40 demolition and renovation permits a year on pre-1950 structures, and lately there's pressure to require a 'microhistorical impact statement' (MHIS) for sites where state records might hide informal labor, migrant networks, or banned social movements. The problem is my board has no trained historians, we get 90 minutes max per case, and we can't just rubber-stamp everything because developers will sue if we delay without cause.

I need a reusable decision procdure — like a flowchart in words — that translates the actual difference between microhistory and elite/state history into steps my colleagues can run. The core principle is that microhistory isn't just zooming in; it changes the evidentiary standard: archival silence counts as data, not missing data, and you organize the narrative around labor traces, wear patterns, rumor, and non-state records rather than property titles and official actors. I want you to bake in a filter I'm calling subaltern provenance tracing — following the building through the gaps in its ownership chain, treating material wear, repair patterns, and oral testimony as primary evidence rather than supplements.

Please build the procedure with:
- 4 or 5 ordered questions or tests
- what counts as a sufficient answer at each step
- the stopping conditions (both 'no MHIS' and 'yes, do the statement')
- clear marks where the call is values not facts and should go to the board chair
- an honest note on where this misfires, especially whether it can ever prove a negative

Then run it on three proposals:
1. A 1920s warehouse officially registered as a textile mill owned by a prominent family; local oral history claims it was a clandestine union hall but there's zero paper, only family legend. The board is politically inclined to approve an MHIS here, so this is the case where I want to see if the procdure forces a no they won't like.
2. A well-preserved 1890s bank building with exhaustive ownership records and architectural surveys, but a recent study (we can't verify in 90 min) suggests the basement hosted an informal lending circle among excluded workers.
3. A 1940s public housing block with complete state construction files, but residents want an MHIS to highlight self-built additions and black-market repairs that never appear in official records — the board doesn't want to open this can of worms for political reasons, so I expect this will be the answer I hate.

Keep it practical, no bibliographies or reading lists, don't cite specific editions. If you drop a historian's name just do it casually. There's a typo or two in here, sorry — writing between meetings.
