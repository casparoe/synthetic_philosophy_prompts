I'm designing a ninety-minute seminar for second-year undergraduates in a course on moral and legal categories of mass violence, and I'd like your help planning the explanatory arc rather than drafting my lecture notes. The students arrive treating genocide as essentially a superlative for extreme mass killing; they have heard that Raphael Lemkin coined the term but have no sense that his original 1944 formulation—developed in Axis Rule in Occupied Europe—encompassed techniques across political, social, cultural, economic, biological, religious, and moral fields, well beyond the 1948 UN Convention's narrower framework of protected groups and specific acts requiring intent to destroy. I want them to see that the word now functions simultaneously as a precise legal instrument and as a moral-political speech act that authorizes particular international responses, and that the gap between these two functions is not an accident of drafting but a structural feature that shapes how we name contemporary and historical atrocities.

Plan the session's dependency structure: what idea must be firmly in place before the legal-moral tension can even register; the one concrete case that should carry the entire session—what it must contain (systematic group-targeting that operates through institutional separation and suppression of identity rather than through immediate mass killing) and what it must lack (clear physical destruction of the group as a whole, so the boundary remains visible); and why the forced assimilation of Indigenous children through residential school systems serves this purpose better than a case that already sits comfortably inside or outside the Convention. Then map the misconceptions they will raise in sequence—I expect first that the legal definition is merely a technical refinement of the moral concept, then that excluding cultural destruction from the Convention was a historical error rather than a political compromise, then that applying the word to non-Convention cases is pure rhetorical inflation; tell me exactly where in the arc each misconception should be headed off and with what counter-example or distinction. Include a check at the end that would reveal whether they grasp the dual function of the category—perhaps by presenting an ambiguous scenario that mixes forced assimilation with selective killing and asking them to classify it under Lemkin's original framework, the Convention's legal criteria, and a purely moral framework, and to identify where the three answers diverge. Finally, state explicitly what parts of the Convention's drafting history and what elements of international tribunal jurisprudence on specific intent I should set aside as beyond this session's scope, and say how I should acknowledge that omission openly without undermining the session's coherence.

I discussed this framing with a colleague who worried that students would confuse understanding the politics of naming with endorsing a particular application; if your plan includes a moment where the instructor should separate analytical clarity from normative commitment, flag where it belongs in the sequence. Do not provide a reading list or ask me to assign texts.
