A study-group argument about the paradox of phenomenal judgment has started to look like it proves far more than it should, and I need help locating the exact point of failure. The argument runs as follows.

(1) If a property F is causally inert, then F plays no explanatory role in causing beliefs about F.
(2) For a non-inferential belief that F obtains to amount to knowledge, F itself must be among the causes of that belief — that is, a causal condition on knowledge holds for direct, first-person judgments.
(3) Phenomenal properties (e.g., the qualitative character of pain or of seeing red) are causally inert if epiphenomenalism is true, since all causal work is done by their neural substrates.
(4) So, if epiphenomenalism is true, first-person beliefs about phenomenal states are not knowledge.
(5) We do have secure first-person knowledge of our phenomenal states; treating this as illusory is implausible.
(6) Therefore epiphenomenalism is false.
(7) Now apply (1) and (2) consistently under physicalism: only physical properties are causally efficacious, so moral properties, mathematical truths, semantic contents, and modal facts are all causally inert.
(8) Hence beliefs about morality, mathematics, meaning, and modality also fail the causal condition in (2) and are not knowledge.
(9) This yields global skepticism about every non-physical domain, a result far more damaging than accepting epiphenomenalism about consciousness alone.
(10) So the reasoning in (1)-(6) proves too much: it forces a choice between rejecting physicalism altogether or embracing widespread skepticism.
(11) The only escape seems to be denying (1) or (2) specifically for phenomenal properties — but that looks ad hoc unless phenomenal knowledge enjoys some special epistemic status that other domains lack.
(12) Consequently, the paradox either fails to refute epiphenomenalism, or it undermines physicalism along with it.

I am inclined to preserve (5) and to avoid being pushed into (9), but I am unsure whether the right move is to reject (2) for phenomenal cases, to reject (7)'s blanket claim about other domains, or to find a hidden equivocation between steps.
