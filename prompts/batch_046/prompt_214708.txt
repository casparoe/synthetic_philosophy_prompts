I'm a high school senior serving on our student governance board, and we have been asked to redesign how our school allocates its extracurricular budget. To inform the decision, we administered two preference surveys: one asking students what they want now, anonymously and without framing, and another asking them to imagine they possessed complete, unbiased information about the long-term effects of each option—on mental health, social mobility, and community cohesion—over the next decade, and to answer free from peer influence. The results diverge sharply. The immediate survey favors significantly expanding our competitive varsity athletics program, while the extrapolated survey favors distributing resources across smaller cooperative clubs. Some board members argue that we must honor the immediate results, because overriding present, authentic choices violates autonomy even when those choices are uninformed. Others contend that the extrapolated results represent our true collective will, and that funding athletics based on peer-influenced, short-sighted preferences amounts to institutionalizing a kind of manipulation. I must present a decision framework to our principal that does not simply assert my own preference. How should I weigh these two sets of preferences? In particular, if the idealized version describes students who are substantially different—better informed, less socially pressured—does it still make sense to say those preferences belong to us now, or have we effectively delegated our decision to hypothetical strangers?
