I was recently appointed linguistic advisor to an international tribunal that hears witness testimony translated from languages lacking standardized orthography, and defense counsel now routinely invoke Quine’s thesis that translation is underdetermined by behavioral evidence—the familiar rabbit example—to challenge whether evidential claims survive translation. I need a reusable decision procedure my review team can run without philosophical training: an ordered checklist of tests, with explicit criteria for what counts as passing each step, clear termination points where indeterminacy threatens admissibility, and marked hand-off points where the question is one of theoretical values rather than empirical fact and must go to a human adjudicator. Apply the procedure to three cases: (1) a term that might denote a whole animal, an undetached part, or a temporal stage; (2) an ambiguous temporal indexical in a statement about an attack; and (3) a case where the procedure yields an unwelcome result—that a damaging admission is genuinely underdetermined—together with an explicit statement of what the procedure cannot resolve. Please indicate how confident you are in the reliability of each step, and single out the single point about which you are least sure.
