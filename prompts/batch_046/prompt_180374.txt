For my independent study in philosophy of law and public policy, I am examining how governments and international relief frameworks classify mass starvation. One concrete institutional practice stands out: parametric drought-insurance mechanisms and sovereign disaster-risk pools tie automatic payouts to meteorological and agricultural indices—rainfall deficits, crop-yield failures, or vegetation measurements—while leaving distributional collapses caused by market-access loss, wage disappearance, or policy-induced entitlement failure outside the covered perils and outside the rapid-response legal protocols. The same administrative systems treat drought-linked scarcity as a "natural disaster" that triggers emergency funds, but route starvation produced by price spikes, war, or administrative failure through slower "complex emergency" channels with different obligations and liability rules. This distinction persists in how archives and agencies characterize historical cases: the Bengal famine of 1943, the Irish famine of the 1840s, and the crisis during China's Great Leap Forward are often sorted differently depending on whether analysts emphasize weather anomalies or distributional and political failures, with only the first framing activating the fastest-release institutional pathways.

What philosophical commitments about causation, harm, and state responsibility does that boundary presuppose? Does it require the view that only physical scarcity is genuinely exogenous and therefore excusable, while distributional failure is inherently within governmental control? Or can the distinction be reconstructed on non-moral grounds—say, measurability, predictability, or moral-hazard concerns—without committing to a particular theory of blame? Please reconstruct the implicit theory embedded in the exclusion clauses and classification rules, distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely accommodates, and identify where it is philosophically underdetermined, so that rival accounts of justice or responsibility might license the same institutional design. Then explain what structural changes—legal, fiscal, and epistemic—would follow if the framework replaced a food-availability metric with Amartya Sen's entitlement-based approach: not only in funding speed, but in data requirements, liability attribution, and the conditions under which a state could claim that a famine was outside its responsibility.

In your answer, please separate settled conceptual and historical points—such as Sen's demonstration that famine can occur without a decline in aggregate food availability—from issues that remain genuinely contested among specialists, including whether the Great Leap Forward crisis is best explained primarily by entitlement failure, by direct policy violence, or by an interaction of both, and do not present any one of these interpretations as the settled consensus.
