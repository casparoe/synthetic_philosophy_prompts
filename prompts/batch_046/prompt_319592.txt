I'm a novelist working on literary fiction with some philosophical undercurrents, and i've hit a conceptual snag with two characters that feels like it should have a technical name, but i can't tell if it actually does.

There's this surgeon, Dr. Lena, who genuinely loves medicine - she volunteers at free clinics, talks about anatomy with real excitement, and in simulations she makes brilliant split-second decisions. But put her in an actual operating rooom with a live patient and she freezes completely; she can't choose between two routine procedures. The stakes being real seems to be exactly what kills the skill. Then there's Marcus, who is profoundly honest in every casual interaction - he returns lost wallets, corrects people gently - but if you sit him down and ask him directly "are you telling me the truth right now?" he immediately starts lying, almost reflexively. In both cases it's not that they're faking the trait in safe contexts; they really have it. It's just that the conditions that should trigger the trait seem to also trigger something that cancels it.

I mentioned this to a philosophy grad studant at a party, and she said it sounds like those cases where a disposition gets masked or finked, but she said those usually involve some external blocker - an antidote, a hidden gadget, a separate event. Here the blocker is internal and tied to the trigger itself: the very situation that calls out the trait is what suppresses it. Is there an established concept for this kind of self-cancelling disposition? How is it different from a standard fink or masked disposition, and from plain weakness of will or hypocrisy? Or is it just incoherent to say someone genuinely has a disposition that systematically destroys itself in its own exercise conditions? I want to get the conceptual edges right for the novel, so i'm hoping there's a precise philosophical carving here, or at least a good argument for why there can't be.
