I'm part of a long-running philosophy reading group that meets mostly online—people who came up through the rationalist blogosphere, are comfortable with formal arguments, and are skeptical of academic gatekeeping. For the past year we've been circling evolutionary debunking arguments in metaethics, and the discussion has reached a stable equilibrium that I find suspicious. On one side are participants who run something like the Darwinian dilemma: evolutionary forces shaped our core evaluative judgments to promote cooperation and survival, not to track independent moral truths; since there's no evident correlation between reproductive fitness and moral fact, our moral beliefs are likely off-track unless we can find independent justification, which we apparently lack. On the other side are participants who reply with the familiar trio—evolution might actually track moral truths if they're grounded in stable cooperative structures, the debunker commits a genetic fallacy or proves too much against logic and mathematics, and we can correct evolutionary bias through reflective equilibrium or direct rational intuition.

What frustrates me is that both sides know all of these moves by heart, and repeating them hasn't moved anyone in months. My working theory is that the disagreement isn't really about evolutionary biology, the genetic fallacy, or the ontology of moral properties. Instead, I think it's about a hidden methodological question: what standard must a belief-forming mechanism meet to count as calibrated to its domain, and does evolutionary origin automatically constitute miscalibration unless there's an external feedback loop? The debunkers seem to treat moral belief as analogous to a perceptual capacity in an environment with no selective pressure for veridicality; the defenders treat it as analogous to logical reasoning, where evolutionary origin is irrelevant because the mechanism is evaluated by internal coherence and practical success. But I'm unsure—maybe there's a genuine empirical disagreement about whether moral truths are causally efficacious, or maybe the sides are answering different questions, with the debunkers asking how we know and the defenders asking what justifies.

Can you diagnose what kind of disagreement this actually is? Is it verbal—are calibration and justification doing different work for each side? Is it substantive—do they differ about whether evolutionary explanations can ever be domain-neutral? Or is it a bedrock clash about whether moral knowledge requires a causal connection to moral facts, with neither side able to argue for their starting intuition? And given your diagnosis, what would actually move the needle? Would it take a formal result about the conditions under which selection tracks truth, a conceptual distinction that separates doxastic calibration from behavioral adaptation, or should we expect no progress and instead change the question—perhaps asking not whether evolution debunks morality, but whether any belief-forming mechanism that lacks environmental feedback can be rationally trusted? I want reasons supported by what each side actually says, not a literature survey, and I'd like an honest answer about whether the impasse is ill-posed, merely hard, or waiting for something specific like a new distinction or result.
