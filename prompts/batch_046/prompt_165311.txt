I've been teaching a grad seminar on reasoning about one's own existance and I keep seeing my students do this thing, and I want to know if philosophy of probability has a specific name for it or if I'm just reinventing the wheel with extra steps.

Here's the pattern. Someone starts with a big reference class — say, all conscious beings that could exist in any possible universe — and then notices something about themselves: they have human memories, they live in a universe with about 13 billion years of history, they aren't a Boltzmann brain. Then they use that to update on theories about how big the class is. But here's the move that bugs me: when the update gives a weird result — like 'most observers are Boltzmann brains, so I probably am one' — they don't reject the reasoning frame, they just shrink the reference class. 'Well, I should only sample from observers with continuous memories like mine.' Or in a different example I cooked up for class: imagine there's an 'Archive' that stores simulated minds, and you wake up knowing you're one entry. If you sample over all entries you might conclude the Archive is enormous because you're just one of billions. But then you say 'wait, I should condition on being an entry that remembers waking up in this specific room,' and suddenly the Archive can be small again. Each restriction feels locally reasonable, but together they make the reasoning circular — you're using your own features to pick the class, then using the class size to reason about your features.

I've seen this in discussions of the Doomsday argument and in some anthropic reasoning, but I don't know if there's a specific concept for the occurance of post-hoc reference-class restrictions that save a conclusion from being falsified by self-location. Is this just Bayes with conditioning? Is it a known fallacy? Or should I call it something like 'indexical gerrymandering'?
