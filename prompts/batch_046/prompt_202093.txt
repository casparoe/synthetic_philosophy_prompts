I have recently adopted what I call a reconstructed Sidgwickian gradient for psychical research: the idea that as a claim's ontological cost rises—say, from telepathy to post-mortem survival—the evidential bar should scale non-linearly, requiring simultaneous exclusion of fraud, sensory leakage, and observer-expectancy effects at levels well beyond ordinary science, without ever becoming so high that null results conceptually guarantee naturalism. I take this to reflect Sidgwick's actual practice in the Society for Psychical Research, where he insisted on extraordinary proof but refused to treat the field as a priori impossible. What would count against this stance? I want decisive defeaters separated from merely awkward ones: would a macro-psychokinetic replication surviving all three exclusion filters be fatal, or could the gradient be adjusted? Does the framework collapse into unfalsifiability if every negative result is dismissed as insufficient rigor? Is there an internal tension—perhaps that Sidgwick applied stricter scrutiny to survival claims than to the ethical intuitions underlying his own hedonism? And could it be shown that scaling rigor with prior implausibility is just standard Bayesian updating dressed in normative language, stripping the view of its distinctive force?
