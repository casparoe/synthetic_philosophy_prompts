I'm leading a single ninety-minute seminar for first-year museum-studies students who have just completed an introductory aesthetics module. They arrive convinced that the forgery problem is essentially settled: if two paintings are perceptually identical, any remaining difference in value must be snobbery, market mystique, or legal convention. I want them to leave grasping that artistic value has an irreducibly indexical dimension—that part of a work's value depends on its standing in a specific causal chain linking it back to an original creative act, and that this dependence is not linear but collapses differently at each causal remove. To carry the idea, I will build the session around a nested-forgery scenario: begin with a lost original by Vermeer, move to Han van Meegeren's well-known forged version of it, then introduce a hypothetical second-layer copy made not from the lost original but directly from van Meegeren's forgery, yielding an object perceptually identical to the first forgery but causally twice-removed from Vermeer.

Plan the session's explanatory arc: in what exact order must the conceptual pieces be introduced so that the indexical-dependence claim feels like a discovery rather than an imposed dogma, and what logical dependency makes each step necessary before the next? Specify precisely what the carrying example must include—the three-link causal chain, the strict perceptual identity across links, the documented provenance at each stage—and what it must deliberately lack, such as any visible stylistic difference, any market-price framing, or any appeal to the forger's psychological intentions. Identify the misconceptions these students will predictably bring—for instance, that value is purely perceptual, that the second-layer copy is "just as fake" as the first in exactly the same way, that accepting indexical value commits one to essentialism about artistic genius, or that legal status determines aesthetic status—and map each misconception to the precise point in the sequence where it should be defused, with a brief note on the reply that heads it off without derailing the arc. Design a check for understanding I can administer in the final ten minutes that would reveal whether they have genuinely grasped the dependency structure—specifically, whether they can predict how value shifts when a causal link is added or removed—rather than merely memorizing the slogan that originals matter; it should require no external references or reading. Finally, state explicitly what I should leave out at this level—such as the specific chemistry of van Meegeren's pigments, Nelson Goodman's distinction between autographic and allographic arts, institutional theories of art, or the legal definition of fraud in cultural property—and indicate how I should signal that omission honestly to the students rather than pretending the topic is closed.
