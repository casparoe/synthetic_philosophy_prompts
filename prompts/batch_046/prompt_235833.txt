I'm working through some recent philosophy-of-mind literature that frames conscious experience not as a transparent window onto neural dynamics, but as an adaptive user illusion—specifically, as a virtualized control surface generated by hierarchical predictive models that hide the underlying computational architecture in much the same way a graphical interface conceals machine code. In a seminar discussion of this view, a colleague pressed an objection that I have been unable to dissolve: if consciousness is genuinely an illusion in this technical sense, then there must be some internal agent or subsystem that is being deceived by the representation, because an illusion standardly requires a subject that mistakes appearance for reality. Yet both Dennett and Hoffman explicitly reject any Cartesian theater or internal homunculus that could serve as the recipient of such deception. So I am left wondering whether the user-illusion account implicitly presupposes exactly the kind of inner observer it claims to eliminate. If there is no "user" inside the brain being presented with this virtualized phenomenology, in what coherent sense can we call consciousness an illusion rather than merely a functional representation that lacks any deceived party? Does this not render the metaphor self-defeating, or is there a way to understand representational illusion that does not depend on a separate subject of deception?
