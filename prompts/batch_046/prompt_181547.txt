I hold that Malthus's mechanism—population pressing against subsistence—is not falsified by two centuries of divergence, but represents a case of being right but early, with the rescue valid only when the limiting factor is biological or physical and the suppressing factor is institutional and reversible. On this view, agricultural productivity gains and demographic transition are temporary buffers rather than structural refutations; the theory risks failure only if suppressing conditions reverse without triggering the predicted checks, or if the biological limit itself dissolves. What observations or arguments would genuinely defeat this position? Would sustained global sub-replacement fertility coupled with the collapse of yield-enhancing institutions count as fatal, or merely awkward? Could defenders concede that permanent decoupling of caloric production from arable land and labor inputs undermines the core mechanism, or is there always a retreat to a longer timescale? Rank the defeaters from decisive to cosmetic, distinguish what a committed temporal-displacement defender must concede from what they could dismiss, and say whether the view has been insulated by qualifications to the point of unfalsifiability. I would find it most damaging if you could identify a reversal of institutional suppression that failed to produce Malthusian dynamics despite the biological constraints remaining intact.
