As a novelist constructing a historian-character whose judgments must remain internally coherent, I need her ruling on a new explanatory thesis. Her prior verdicts on Rome are these: she rejects the Antonine Plague as the root cause of the third-century crisis, insisting demographic shock never explains institutional collapse alone; she rejects elite lead-poisoning as monocausal biological determinism; she accepts barbarian invasion in 476 only as a trigger, never as sufficient without prior institutional fracture; she accepts the Late Antique Little Ice Age as contributing to agricultural stress but denies it distinguishes East from West, since both regions faced it; and she accepts the transformation thesis for the Eastern Empire, where bureaucratic and ecclesiastical continuity persisted, but rejects it for the West, arguing transformation requires institutional continuity that was broken. The new case is a recently proposed thesis that the Western collapse was driven entirely by a bureaucratic reform dated to 418 CE—a fiscal delegation that decoupled frontier armies from central treasury supply, rendering local commanders dependent on barbarian foederati for pay—without requiring climate, plague, or invasion as necessary conditions. Predict her verdict for consistency, identify the closest analog among her prior rulings, and state whether she should accept or reject. Then specify which of two competing principles governs: (A) institutional discontinuity specific to the West is the decisive criterion for any valid explanation, or (B) every valid explanation must demonstrate an interaction between internal institutional failure and an external stressor. For each principle, say what her verdict would be. Finally, name the hypothetical further case—such as an identical Eastern fiscal reform that produced fragmentation without collapse—that would settle which principle she actually holds.
