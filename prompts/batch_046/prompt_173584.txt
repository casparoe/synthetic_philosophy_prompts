Draft the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between two camps regarding rational irrationality in hitchhiker-style decision problems. Name the camps explicitly: Camp A defends the possibility that a rational agent can now have decisive reason to adopt a decision rule that will later prescribe actions lacking any direct causal or evidential benefit at the time they are performed; Camp B denies this, holding that rational assessment is time-slice relative and that any apparently binding precommitment is either causally efficacious or illusory.

Treat both the canonical single-episode case—where a traveler promises payment to a driver upon rescue despite the absence of any causal or evidential link between payment and the completed rescue—and a deliberately niche extension: a repeated-interaction setting in which potential drivers observe only a noisy, unbiased signal of the traveler's adopted decision rule (not the actual payment), and the traveler chooses ex ante between installing a rigid precommitment mechanism and retaining full flexibility.

Your deliverable must contain:

1. A numbered section of claims both camps can sign. Include agreement about the logical structure of the decision (the separation of the rescue event from the payment event, the absence of direct causation in the canonical case, the presence of reputation dynamics in the extension), about the distinction between causal and evidential decision theories, and about what empirical or conceptual evidence could in principle bear on the dispute.

2. A disagreements table with three columns: the precise issue; Camp A's formulation (worded so Camp A accepts it); and Camp B's formulation (worded so Camp B accepts it). Cover at least: whether rationality permits time-slice-crossing reasons; whether evidential dependence in the canonical case is genuinely absent or merely hidden; and whether the noisy-signal extension collapses the distinction between rational precommitment and ordinary reputation management.

3. A cruxes section. Identify the sub-questions such that resolving them would move at least one camp. I suspect two central cruxes are: (a) whether a non-causal evidential link between an agent's present choice of rule and her past situation can be strong enough to rationalize adherence to that rule at execution time; and (b) whether the noisy-signal extension genuinely distinguishes commitment mechanisms from observable reputation strategies, or instead reveals that all apparent cases of rational irrationality reduce to standard evidential reasoning about others' beliefs. For each crux, specify: the concrete argument or model both sides agree is relevant; Camp A's explicit prediction about what that argument or model will show; and Camp B's explicit prediction about the same.

Format the document cleanly, use neutral language throughout, and avoid any appeal to bibliographies, citation lists, or appeals to authority. The goal is a text both parties could put their names to, with the residual disagreement made exact rather than rhetorical.
