Write a two-page proposal for a semester-long philosophy-of-technology project. The central question: when iterated amplification breaks a complex task into sub-tasks solvable by a smaller model and then distills the results back up, does the decomposition preserve both alignment and competence, or does it systematically fragment them? Develop what I will call the relational-fragmentation objection: alignment may not be a locally checkable property of each sub-task, but a holistic, context-dependent relation between the system’s overall behavior and human values—analogous to the way wetness is not a property of individual H₂O molecules. The existing literature largely assumes that if each amplification step is locally aligned and competent, the distilled product inherits both properties; your task is to examine whether that inference tacitly treats alignment as modular rather than emergent. Frame the project conceptually, not empirically: construct a formal distinction between intrinsic and relational preservation conditions, borrow the modularity/emergence framework from philosophy of science, identify the exact premises that license inference from local to global alignment, and determine whether any necessary premise can be defended without circularity. State precisely what a successful result looks like—for instance, showing that at least one preservation premise fails for a well-defined class of decomposition schemes—and what counts as failure, such as discovering that the relational view collapses into the local view upon analysis. Give an honest scope assessment: can this be done rigorously in one semester, and at which point does the project risk expanding into a dissertation?
