Please argue that the standard pairing of Darwin's and Turing's inversions—where natural selection achieves functional design without understanding and computing machinery achieves behavioral competence without understanding—conceals a structural asymmetry that undermines common arguments about artificial consciousness. Defend the position that Darwin's inversion concerns causal provenance: complex adaptive outcomes arise from mechanisms that possess no representational grasp of those outcomes. By contrast, Turing's inversion concerns epistemic accessibility: an external observer cannot legitimately infer internal comprehension from behavioral performance alone. From this, argue that collapsing both into a single "competence without comprehension" thesis invites a category mistake: one cannot validly move from the Darwinian claim that a system was not intelligently designed to the Turing-style conclusion that it therefore lacks understanding, because the gap between causal origin and mental attribution requires an intermediate premise about functional transparency—namely, that internal representational structure must be both present and observable for comprehension to be ascribed—which neither inversion supplies. Address why this asymmetry does not reduce to a generic skepticism about other minds, and specify precisely where your reasoning is weakest: which claim about representational grasp, observer-access, or the conditions for mental attribution, if shown mistaken, would force you to abandon the distinction? Also say what kind of counterexample—perhaps an evolutionary computation in which selection mechanisms themselves encode representations of fitness landscapes, or a behavioral protocol that genuinely reveals internal structural correspondence rather than mere output matching—would change your mind about whether the two inversions remain genuinely separate.
