In our seminar on Weber's Protestant Ethic thesis, we have been debating whether culture can operate as a genuine causal factor in economic development or only serves as a post-hoc rationalization of material interests. I want to apply this framework to a phenomenon that postdates Weber but structurally mirrors his historical case: the algorithmic governance of platform labor, in which continuous performance scoring, opaque behavioral nudges, and the moral framing of downtime as inefficiency impose an ethos of constant productivity without any religious backing. Suppose Weber observed that these monitoring systems first took root in economies with strong historical Calvinist or Puritan legacies and then diffused globally primarily through institutional imitation rather than through competitive market selection alone. Would he interpret this diffusion pattern as evidence that a secularized, disenchanted Berufsethik remains causally efficacious—shaping organizational form rather than merely justifying it? Or would he argue that once capitalism reaches full rationalization, such innovations are driven solely by instrumental rationality, rendering culture causally inert? More specifically, would he treat the algorithms themselves as carriers of value-rational commitments, or as technically neutral instruments that happen to be adopted in settings already predisposed to an ascetic work discipline? I am not asking for a bibliography; I want you to reason through his likely position using only the conceptual apparatus of value-rational versus instrumental-rational action, the secularization of the calling, and the problem of explaining social change with values.
