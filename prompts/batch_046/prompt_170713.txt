I've just finished leading an undergraduate seminar on the foundations of logic, and one student asked whether Russell's paradox exposes a flaw in reasoning itself or an overreach in our desire to gather everything into one totality. Her question has stayed with me, particularly because the standard classroom version treats the paradox as a simple self-reference puzzle without noting that Cantor's theorem had already shown the universal set could not contain its own power set. I want to close our next meeting with a poem that takes the paradox seriously as both a logical and an ontological crisis, not as a party trick.

Write a Shakespearean sonnet—fourteen lines of iambic pentameter, rhyme scheme ABAB CDCD EFEF GG—spoken in the voice of the naive comprehension principle itself as it attempts to construct the universal collection. In the octave (lines 1–8), build the principle's confident gathering: first the general ambition to collect every set satisfying an open condition, then the specific turn to those sets that do not contain themselves, letting the rhyme remain open and declarative. At the volta in line 9, introduce the Cantorian pressure—the realization that even before self-reference strikes, the power set of any totality must exceed it, so the container is already too small. In the sestet (lines 10–12), have the principle apply its own condition to itself, producing the contradiction not as a shouted absurdity but as a quiet structural failure, and let the meter tighten or break to enact the collapse. The closing couplet (GG) should name exactly what must be abandoned—unrestricted naive comprehension—and what replaces it: a stratified ontology in which sets are arranged by type or rank so that no collection can claim its own membership. The poem should be accurate enough that a philosopher of logic recognizes the interplay between Cantor's cardinality argument and Russell's self-reference, leaving the listener with the sense that the paradox is not solved but survived by giving up the dream of a single, all-containing set.
