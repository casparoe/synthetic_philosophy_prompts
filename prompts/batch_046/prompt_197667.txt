After a long, somewhat heated argument with a friend about whether Frankfurt-style counterexamples genuinely defeat the principle of alternative possibilities, I want to construct a decision tree that sorts people by what they actually believe about the scenario, not by which philosophical label they prefer. Start with a single concrete case: an agent decides to vote for a particular policy; an unseen mechanism stands ready to coerce her if she displays any sign of hesitation, but she never displays that sign, proceeds for her own reasons, and feels entirely free, though she could not have completed the vote otherwise.

From that shared starting point, build a tree with three successive branch nodes, each phrased as a direct question about the case that a respondent could answer without knowing where it leads. First: does the mere presence of the standby coercive mechanism, even though it never activates, destroy moral responsibility for the vote? Second: if responsibility survives, is the correct explanation that responsibility requires only that the action issue from the agent’s own reasons-responsive psychological structure (sourcehood), or is it that she retains a “thin” alternative—such as displaying the warning sign—even if exercising that alternative only triggers coercion? Third: if sourcehood is sufficient, must the agent have explicit epistemic access to the fact that an alternative path existed (even if blocked), or is subjective unawareness of the mechanism fully compatible with complete responsibility?

Each leaf should carry either the name of an actual position (for example, a Frankfurt-inspired compatibilism, a semi-compatibilist sourcehood view, a leeway theory defending thin alternatives, or an incompatibilist defense of the principle of alternative possibilities) or be marked “unnamed” if the combination of answers is unoccupied. For every leaf, include one sentence describing its most awkward commitment—the specific cost its defenders must accept. Then map the actual positions onto the logical space of answer-combinations, noting any empty cells and briefly diagnosing why they are empty: incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored.

Also flag any pair of named positions that this tree cannot genuinely separate—positions that agree on all three answers but diverge only on background metaphysical commitments about determinism that the tree does not probe. Frame every node so that it asks about the concrete scenario rather than asking “are you a compatibilist?” The deliverable should be the tree itself, with branches labeled by the splitting claim, followed by the leaf descriptions and the separation flags. Do not include a bibliography, reading list, or citation requests; the point is a diagnostic instrument that works for someone reasoning about the case from scratch.
