Construct a one-paragraph realistic thought experiment set in a hospital ethics committee (no trolleys, no science fiction) in which a utilitarian aggregation justifies overriding a specific patient's refusal of a minimal-burden monitoring protocol—not because the refusal harms anyone directly, but because it lowers the predictive validity of a pooled outcome database that the committee relies on to secure funding for treatments benefiting a disjoint future population. Engineer the case so that the conflict depends specifically on treating each individual's autonomy-weighted utility as fungible input into a single collective welfare function rather than as separate, non-commensurable claims. After the scenario, provide a point-by-point demonstration that the design works: (1) state the utilitarian verdict and show it requires summing utility across distinct persons; (2) state the separateness-of-persons objection and show it targets precisely this aggregation step, not merely the unfavorable outcome for the patient; (3) identify the exact feature doing the work and explain what happens if the pooled database is replaced by direct, pairwise resource allocations with no collective metric; and (4) present the strongest utilitarian rebuttal—namely, that the pooled metric is a contingent institutional tool, not a necessary feature of the theory—and assess whether excising that feature dissolves the conflict or only pushes it into the background conditions of funding.
