I am defending my dissertation proposal to an interdisciplinary committee—three technical AI alignment researchers whose work centers on agentic misalignment, instrumental convergence, and reward hacking, and two organizational sociologists who study gradual institutional decay and mission drift in regulatory bodies—arguing that current safety discourse over-indexes on influence-seeking failure modes, in which systems strategically optimize for power or control, while under-theorizing slow value drift, a process by which aligned objectives degrade incrementally through contextual adaptation, stakeholder feedback loops, and the erosion of evaluative frames, without any strategic deception. I contend that conflating these produces misdirected governance: influence-seeking demands containment and monitoring, whereas slow drift demands institutional maintenance and frame-preservation mechanisms, and that several recent deployment failures widely interpreted as deceptive are better explained by drift. The argument draws on both technical alignment literature and organizational sociology, but synthesizes them in a way neither field has fully articulated. Please prepare me for the objections I will actually face, ordered by likelihood and damage, phrased as each subgroup would phrase them: the technical researchers' concerns about falsifiability and whether drift is just a relabeling of misgeneralization, and the sociologists' concerns about whether I am importing agentic language into structural processes. For each objection, include a concise reply suitable for a defense setting, a note on where the reply is soft, what I should concede upfront, and the one question I am hoping nobody asks. Separate what is settled—such as the existence of reward hacking and institutional mission drift—from what is genuinely contested, particularly whether drift can be mechanistically distinguished from low-grade strategic behavior in large models, and do not present any contested synthesis as the consensus view.
