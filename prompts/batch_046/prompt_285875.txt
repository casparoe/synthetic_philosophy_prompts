I'm taking a graduate seminar on medical ethics and recently sat in on a long argument between two classmates, Maya and Jonas, about a case drawn from actual public-health reporting: a state-funded hospital declined to cover an orphan drug costing roughly two million dollars per year, which offered a median survival extension of about five months with severe side effects. The patient, who lacked private insurance, died shortly after the denial. Maya and Jonas disagreed sharply about whether the hospital's refusal should be classified as allowing the patient to die or was closer in moral structure to killing, and since I was only listening I want an impartial adjudication of their specific moves.

Maya defended the distinction in three steps. First, she appealed to a causal criterion: killing requires that the agent's action be the proximate cause of death, whereas allowing means the underlying pathology causes death and the agent merely fails to interrupt it; since the drug refusal did not inject any lethal force into the patient's body, the disease remains the true cause. Second, she argued that budget allocation is a macro-level institutional decision about resource distribution, not a clinical act directed at this particular patient, so treating the funding denial as the "real" killing illegitimately collapses policy architecture into bedside causation. Third, she warned that if we erase the distinction in rationing contexts, we lose the conceptual space for legitimate triage: every decision not to spend infinite resources becomes a form of homicide, which destabilizes the moral vocabulary we use to evaluate clinical conduct.

Jonas responded in four moves. First, he claimed Maya's causal test is naive because it assumes we can isolate a "proximate" link, but in modern healthcare every omission is institutionally mediated—doctors do not withhold drugs purely by personal choice, but through pharmacy protocols, insurance codes, and budget lines that causally integrate the hand's inaction; traced fully, the budget denial belongs to the same causal chain as the death. Second, he introduced what he called "structural agency"—the idea that institutions act through distributed roles—so moral evaluation should attach to the decision node that actually controls the outcome, which here was the funding denial rather than the doctor's failure to write a prescription. Third, he argued that Maya's triage worry is overblown: we can still distinguish justified from unjustified omissions by examining proportionality and fairness, without needing a metaphysical wall between doing and allowing. Fourth, he observed that if we applied Maya's logic consistently, a wealthy private hospital that actively withdraws a ventilator and a public system that denies access to it would be morally asymmetrical purely because of funding architecture, which seems like an arbitrary result.

Please adjudicate move by move: rate Maya's three claims and Jonas's four responses, each on a scale from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with brief justification for each rating. Then say who is more right overall about whether the doing/allowing distinction survives in institutional healthcare rationing, and point out what important consideration—perhaps about moral responsibility across roles, the significance of patient autonomy, or the normative status of scarcity—both of them are missing.
