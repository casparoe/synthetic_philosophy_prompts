Write a short story centered on a mediation hearing in a near-future jurisdiction that recognizes "continuity contracts"—legally binding instruments signed before a certified neuro-adaptive transition that preserves episodic memory while systematically altering core values, emotional temperament, and practical identity. The transition is intended to treat severe, intractable conditions by allowing the patient to inhabit a genuinely different evaluative framework.

The protagonist should be the mediator, Elias, tasked with drafting a non-binding but authoritative recommendation for the court. The dispute involves Mara, who signed a continuity contract before her transition. The contract assigns all rights to her unfinished artistic work to a public archive and obligates her post-transition self to complete one final commission. After transition, Mara retains perfect autobiographical memory but experiences her pre-transition artistic ambitions as foreign to her; she describes her earlier self not as "me" but as "the author of my biography." She refuses to complete the work and petitions to have the contract voided on the grounds that the signatory and the current bearer of her legal identity share neither practical concerns nor authoritative self-governance.

Opposing her, the archive's representative argues that bodily continuity, legal identity, and the social practice of intertemporal commitment require honoring the contract: to void it would destabilize every institution that relies on promises made across long-term personal change, including marriage contracts, debt obligations, and medical directives.

Weave the philosophical tension into concrete scenes: Elias examining the unfinished physical artwork in Mara's studio; Mara explaining that she can recall painting every brushstroke but feels no ownership over the impulse that made it; the archive's lawyer reading a clause about "binding the continuous legal person regardless of psychological discontinuity." Do not resolve the tension with a simple legal ruling. Instead, end with Elias drafting a recommendation that acknowledges both the moral necessity of protecting present autonomy and the social danger of allowing every transformed self to disown prior obligations—perhaps proposing a conditional framework that respects refusal while imposing a structured transition of responsibility rather than outright nullification.

Keep the narrative grounded in sensory and emotional detail rather than abstract argument. Avoid referencing specific philosophers, legal cases, or theoretical texts by name.
