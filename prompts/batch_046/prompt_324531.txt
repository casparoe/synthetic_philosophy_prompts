I have been trying to apply Grice's account of speaker meaning and conversational implicature to high-stakes diplomatic communication, specifically the back-channel exchanges during the 1962 Cuban Missile Crisis—particularly the episode in which Kennedy's advisers considered responding only to Khrushchev's earlier message (which offered to withdraw missiles in exchange for a non-invasion pledge) while treating the subsequent message (which added the removal of Turkish missiles) as though it had never arrived, a maneuver sometimes likened to the Trollope ploy from Anthony Trollope's novels. Grice holds that speaker meaning requires a specific, recognized intention to induce a particular belief in the audience, guided by the cooperative principle. Strategic ambiguity in diplomacy, however, seems designed to achieve the opposite: the speaker wants the audience to recognize that multiple interpretations remain live, without committing to which one is intended, precisely in order to preserve future deniability. This appears to violate Grice's condition that the speaker's intention be determinate, suggesting that diplomatic language falls outside Gricean pragmatics altogether. Should one conclude that diplomacy is merely a domain where the cooperative principle is systematically flouted, or can Grice's framework be reconstructed—perhaps by treating the intended recognition of ambiguity itself as the specific belief to be induced—so that this episode becomes not an anomaly but a paradigmatic case of calculated implicature?
