Please argue that the so-called species problem is best dissolved not by selecting one correct criterion of specieshood among competitors, but by reconceiving taxonomy as a matter of epistemic jurisdiction: different scientific communities possess legitimate classificatory authority within the predictive and explanatory domains they actually inhabit. In particular, defend the stronger claim that hybrid zones—conventionally treated as anomalous boundary regions that threaten an otherwise discrete taxonomy—ought to be regarded as the primary evidential base for species delimitation, with reproductively isolated "pure" populations understood as derived, stabilized extremes rather than normative archetypes. Your argument should address the charge that this reverses the explanatory priority traditionally granted to reproductive isolation, and it should explain why adopting this jurisdictional, hybrid-zone-first view does not reduce biological taxonomy to mere conventionalism or pragmatic convenience. You may draw on concepts such as lineage divergence, gene flow, and ecological niche differentiation, but do not appeal to bibliographic authorities or request further reading.
