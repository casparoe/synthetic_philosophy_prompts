Consider a philosopher of cross-modal perception whose consistent rulings on recognition cases are as follows. First, she holds that a congenitally blind adult whose sight is restored cannot discriminate a cube from a sphere previously known only by touch, provided she is not permitted to handle them. Second, she holds that a person born deaf who learned a rhythmic pattern exclusively through sternum vibrations will immediately recognize that same rhythm after receiving a cochlear implant, without any prior auditory-vibratory pairing. Third, she holds that an individual with congenital anosmia, after surgical restoration of smell, will not immediately identify citrus or mint by odor alone, despite prior familiarity with them through texture and taste. Fourth, she holds that a number-form synesthete who temporarily loses color vision retains her digit-to-spatial-form mappings intact and resumes them upon restoration without relearning. Fifth, she holds that a subject who learns to navigate a virtual maze solely through haptic feedback from a data glove can subsequently navigate an identical geometric layout presented on a flat visual screen, even in the absence of any prior visual-haptic pairing in that specific environment. Sixth, she holds that a patient equipped with an edge-only retinal prosthesis, after tactile training on wire-frame shapes, will report that the prosthetic visual image of one such shape feels like its tactile counterpart, yet will be unable to name it.

Now consider a new case. A different patient, also congenitally blind, receives a prosthetic visual system that renders geometric objects as arrays of discrete high-contrast dots. She has previously explored identical dot-arrays through a tactile pin-display board. She is then shown the prosthetic dot-patterns corresponding to a pyramid and a cone, without being allowed any tactile access, and is asked whether she can tell them apart.

Predict her ruling on this case, identify which of the six preceding rulings is the closest analog, and explain your reasoning. Then specify at least two distinct rules that jointly accommodate all six earlier rulings but diverge on this seventh case—such as an amodal structuralist rule, a modality-specific encoding rule, and a sensorimotor-enactive rule—state precisely what each predicts here, and defend your best-guess attribution of the rule she is actually running. Finally, propose one further hypothetical case that would decisively distinguish between the two rules you take to be the leading candidates.
