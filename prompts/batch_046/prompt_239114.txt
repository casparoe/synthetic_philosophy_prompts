Question: Frank Jackson asks us to imagine Mary, a neuroscientist who knows every physical fact about color vision but has lived her entire life in a black-and-white room. When she steps outside and sees a red tomato, she apparently learns something new—what it is like to see red. Jackson takes this to show that physicalism is false, since all physical information was already available to her inside the room. Consider this variant: Mary never leaves the room, but she gains access to a perfect internal simulation engine that can generate, within her own brain, the exact neural dynamics that seeing red would produce, with no external light reaching her eyes. She can trigger this simulation at will and report its qualitative presence. Does this variant undermine Jackson's knowledge argument? In your answer, distinguish the ability hypothesis from the claim that phenomenal knowledge is a form of indexical or perspectival knowledge, and be precise about whether Mary learns a new fact, gains a new ability, or merely applies an existing concept in a new mode.

Student answer: Jackson's argument is that Mary knows all physical facts—wavelengths, neural firing patterns, and so on—yet she learns what it is like to see red only upon leaving. Because this phenomenal knowledge was not derivable from her physical knowledge, physicalism must be incomplete. In the simulation variant, Mary can generate the relevant neural dynamics internally. This means she already produces the brain states associated with seeing red, so she acquires no new physical information. This supports the ability hypothesis, originally developed by critics of Jackson such as David Lewis and later refined by Chalmers and Churchland: Mary does not learn a new fact, but only gains the skill of recognizing or reproducing the state. But the variant also reveals a deeper point. Even with the simulation, Mary knows she is running a model, so she lacks the first-person what-it-is-like quality. This is where the indexical view applies. According to this physicalist response—most clearly formulated by David Chalmers—knowing what it is like is not propositional knowledge but indexical knowledge: applying an old concept from the inside. So Mary applies her concept of red-experience indexically through the simulation, and no anti-physicalist ontology is required. Therefore, the variant actually undermines Jackson's argument. The ability hypothesis explains away the intuition of novelty, and the indexical view explains why the simulation feels different without requiring new properties. Mary learns neither a genuinely new fact nor a new ability—she merely shifts her epistemic perspective using knowledge she already possessed, which saves physicalism.
