I run a philosophy of science reading group with eight doctoral students that meets for two hours every other week; we have been working through inductive inference and recently discussed how the principle of indifference operates in continuous parameter spaces. I want a structured measurement game—complete with rules, small real stakes, and a joint payoff—that makes the participants feel Bertrand’s paradox not as a geometric curiosity but as a live crisis for scientific prior assignment. Specifically, divide the group into three subgroups and assign each a different parameterization of the same physical system (for instance, the decay behavior of an unstable isotope, represented respectively by half-life, decay constant λ, and mean lifetime τ). Each subgroup must derive what “complete ignorance” implies for a uniform prior over its assigned parameter, then all three must jointly allocate a limited research budget across competing experiments whose expected value depends directly on those prior distributions. Only after budgets are locked should the facilitator reveal that the three parameterizations yield radically different probability assignments over the very same outcome space, forcing the group to confront that “uniform ignorance” is coordinate-dependent.

The rules should require that no subgroup see the others’ parameterizations until after the allocation round, that the budget is genuinely constrained so trade-offs feel real (for example, token points convertible to a shared reward such as selecting the group’s next reading), and that each subgroup briefly defend its prior in writing before the reveal. The timeline should run roughly thirty minutes for parameter assignment and prior derivation, forty minutes for negotiation and budget commitment, and the remainder for revelation and structured response. The idea must emerge from the payoff structure itself: participants should discover the contradiction through the frustration of joint decision-making, not through a lecture, so that the facilitator names the paradox only after the group has already lived through its practical consequences.

In the debrief, please map each moment of surprise—deriving the prior, negotiating the budget, confronting the conflict—onto the formal structure of Bertrand’s paradox, and explicitly state which feature of the idea the activity cannot reproduce (for example, the rigorous proof that all continuous parameterizations are mathematically admissible) and must therefore be supplied by the facilitator. Include an honest assessment of which philosophical responses—restricting indifference to discrete cases, adopting an invariant reference class, appealing to symmetry-breaking arguments—the activity makes feel intuitively plausible versus implausible once the contradiction is experienced firsthand. Finally, anticipate the predictable failure mode in which one subgroup immediately suspects a trick or hidden equivalence rather than engaging sincerely with its prior assignment, and advise how to handle it without undermining the game’s force.
