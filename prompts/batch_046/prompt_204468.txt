I'm trying to specify what an adequate theory of individual obligation in collective harm contexts actually has to accomplish, particularly where the standard inefficacy objection applies—climate emissions, meat consumption, and democratic voting being the canonical cases. Too often the debate oscillates between "your carbon footprint is negligible" and "you're complicit in a harmful system," without a clear standard for what either claim needs to deliver. Before I evaluate particular accounts—whether consequentialist aggregation, Kantian universalization, virtue-ethical participation, or contractualist non-aggregation—I want the full specification of desiderata for a theory of distributed moral responsibility under structural overdetermination.

The theory I'm imagining isn't merely about whether one should "do one's part." It needs to accommodate scale-variant obligations: moral demands whose content or strength changes depending on whether an agent acts in isolation, within a small coordinated group, or within a mass collective where outcomes are invariant to individual choice over broad ranges. Crucially, it must also handle refractory or perverse-efficacy cases—situations in which individual withdrawal from a harmful practice either increases aggregate harm (for instance, boycotts that raise prices for vulnerable populations) or inadvertently legitimizes the very institutions sustaining the harm (such as strategic non-voting that strengthens anti-democratic mandates). So the desiderata should include: which phenomena the theory must explain (the intuitive pull of complicity, the intuitive release of causal impotence, the asymmetry between contributing to harm and failing to prevent it, and the special problem of threshold-sensitive obligations near critical tipping points); which distinctions it must preserve (between triggering a harm and sustaining the institutional conditions that make harm probable, between obligations of justice and obligations of virtue or solidarity, and between moral blame and moral duty); and which results from decision theory, social choice, or game theory it must remain consistent with—especially regarding collective-action invariance and the conditions under which individual rationality diverges from collective rationality.

Please lay out the requirements as a list, with the case or consideration behind each, marking which are non-negotiable and which are camp-specific preferences. I especially want you to flag any pair or subset of desiderata that may be jointly unsatisfiable—whether, for example, full causal accountability can coexist with efficacy-independent obligation, or whether accommodating perverse-efficacy cases forces the theory into either universal abstention or universal complicity with no middle ground. Then identify which single requirement does the most work in eliminating the popular theories currently on offer. Finally, briefly score how the main contenders—aggregative consequentialism, a universalization-based deontology, virtue ethics of collective life, and a non-aggregative contractualism—fare against this list, but keep the weight on the criteria themselves rather than on the scorecard. The point is to see whether there is a coherent middle position, or whether the list of requirements itself reveals that no theory can satisfy all of them.
