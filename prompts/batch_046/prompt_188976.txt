I'm trying to work out what I actually think about non-empirical confirmation in physics, going by my gut on cases. String theory says ten dimensions exist but they're curled up; since we can't check, that feels like zero confirmation, just a feature of the maths. The multiverse explaining why constants allow life: that feels like it should confirm the multiverse a bit, but also like explaining away the data. If two multiverse theories both cover fine-tuning and one is simpler, I think the simpler one's confirmed more — but maybe I'm just smuggling Occam's razor in. A quantum gravity theory that predicts no new particles at LHC energies, and none show up: barely confirmed, since null results are cheap. But if a theory is mathematicly unique — literally no other consistant formulation in its class — that feels like stronger support, even though it's not an empirical prediction at all. Then there's a theory that predicts cosmic strings with a specific tension, they aren't found, and the theory tweaks a parameter to hide them: I think that's disconfirmed, not left untouched. So is there a coherent principle behind these? Maybe something about predictive risk in modal space? But I'm not sure that saves the uniqueness intuition. And the case I keep going back and forth on: if a theory predicts something surprising that then fails, but the failure was always going to happen given background knowledge — is that disconfirmation or not? What's the principle, and which of my verdicts does it force me to drop?
