I have been working through the institutional pathologies of the classical polis for a course on democratic failure, and a comparison that keeps surfacing—both in secondary literature and in informal discussion—is that the Athenian ecclesia during its crisis moments functioned like a high-gain amplifier without feedback damping: a localized emotional or rhetorical input (Cleon’s fury over Mytilene, Alcibiades’ promise of Sicilian glory, Anytus’s grievance against Socrates) gets magnified into an extreme collective output (mass execution, imperial overreach, capital punishment) with no built-in attenuation mechanism to interrupt the signal before execution. Defenders of direct democracy object that this smuggles in a linear, cybernetic model of collective cognition that treats citizens as passive resistors rather than interactive agents, and they point to the reversal of the Mytilene decree as proof that the assembly could self-correct—implying the “damping” was internal, not missing. I want you to audit this amplifier analogy rigorously: lay out the correspondence table mapping amplifier components (gain stage, input signal, noise floor, damping loop, output saturation) onto specific Athenian institutions and procedures (the ecclesia, the probouleuma, ostracism, the graphe paranomon, the dikasterion that tried Socrates); identify precisely where the mapping fractures, especially with respect to the reversal on Mytilene, the separation of powers between assembly and court, and the role of individual demagogues versus structural incentives; examine whether the analogy quietly imports a modern engineering ontology that mischaracterizes democratic error as mechanical malfunction rather than as a failure of practical judgment or ethical imagination; and tell me whether the anti-democratic conclusion—that direct democracy structurally lacks necessary stabilizers—survives once these disanalogies are on the table, or whether a different analogy (perhaps drawn from distributed computation or immunological response) would better capture the specific failure modes visible across the Mytilene debate, the Sicilian Expedition vote, and the trial of Socrates without importing false assumptions about collective cognition.
