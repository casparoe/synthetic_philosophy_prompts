I suspect there is an unexamined normative layer—one that neither framework captures and that becomes visible only when both parents are working and trying to coordinate care across time.

The proposal: I am applying for a six-month interdisciplinary research fellowship, with the deliverable being one 8,000-word framework paper plus a one-page policy decision matrix. I want to investigate whether paid parental leave and childcare subsidies should be justified not primarily as compensation for lost wages or as gender-balancing tools, but as institutional expressions of what I will call reproductive continuity—the obligation of a society to maintain the temporal conditions under which labor power, citizenship, and care relationships persist across generations. Within this framework, I want to ask a precise question: in dual-earner families, does assigning leave and childcare entitlements to individual workers (as opposed to treating the family unit or the child as the bearer of entitlement) create a coordination failure that fragments reproductive continuity? In other words, does the individualization of leave rights—designed for single-earner or sequentially-earner households—systematically undermine the very continuity it aims to protect when both parents work, producing what I will call temporal fragmentation?

Why existing positions leave it open: Economic analyses treat leave as correcting an externality—employers do not internalize the social value of reproduction—without asking whether the entitlement should be indexed to the child or to the worker. Feminist accounts emphasize recognition of care labor and gender equity, but they rarely examine whether joint entitlement structures, such as family-level leave banks or child-centered childcare subsidies, would better serve continuity, or whether such structures risk coercing particular family forms. The gap is normative, not empirical: we lack a framework for deciding when an obligation runs to the individual worker, to the couple as a joint agent, or to the child as a future citizen, and we lack criteria for identifying when dual-earner scheduling creates a prisoner's dilemma in leave-taking that individual rights cannot resolve.
