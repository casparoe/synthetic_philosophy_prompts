I sit on the approval panel for a regional water cooperative that pilots automated flow-management algorithms. Our charter mandates that every proposal “avoid significant side effects” on three concerns—equitable access for small farms, aquatic ecosystem stability, and the cooperative’s long-term capacity for human oversight—but it deliberately refuses to rank these concerns or to define “significant,” because our members hold genuinely incompatible values and we must reach consensus without a shared metric of harm.

We review two or three proposals a month under time pressure, with only part-time technical staff. The board requires a signed checklist for every decision, and one of the three signatories must be a non-technical community representative. I need a reusable decision procedure—ordered questions, pass-or-fail thresholds, and explicit hand-off points—so that my team can apply it consistently without pretending we have solved the underlying value disagreement.

Please take a definite position: can “avoid side effects” be operationalized at all without smuggling in a hidden value ranking? Build your answer directly into the procedure. Design the flow as a sequence of tests that separates structural, observable features—reversibility within one season, spatial confinement to the direct operational domain, and detectability by affected parties without specialist equipment—from judgments that require weighing the three charter concerns against one another. Specify exactly where the procedure stops with a rejection, where it stops with an approval, and where it must halt and hand the file to the full board because the remaining question is one of values, not facts.

Then apply the procedure to three proposals:

1. A predictive valve system that reduces spillage by 40% but permanently removes the option for manual emergency shutoffs by local operators.
2. A seasonal allocation model that favors drought-resistant crops over aquatic flow requirements during low-rain years, with effects that are fully reversible each spring.
3. A proposal that I, as someone who cares primarily about equity for small farms, will probably want to approve, but that your procedure should reject—explain why the procedure gives the opposite answer, and say whether that reveals a flaw in the procedure or in my intuition.

Finally, state plainly what this procedure cannot do: which kinds of disagreement it leaves untouched, what risk remains when a board signs off using it, and where you think the boundary between fact and value genuinely lies in this context. Keep the response committed—do not present this as one option among many. Defend the specific structural distinctions you choose, and be honest about where judgment cannot be eliminated.
