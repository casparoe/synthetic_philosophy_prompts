I am a junior philosophy major currently enrolled in a course on virtue ethics and moral psychology, and I have been asked to deliver the opening remarks at our department's workshop, "Character in Practice," which convenes philosophy students and participants from the university's service-learning program. Roughly half the audience understands the situationist critique—the argument, supported by classic social-psychological findings, that moral behavior tracks situational variables more reliably than stable, cross-situational character traits—while the other half does not. I want the remarks to honor the service-learning graduates without falling into the naive attribution of their actions to fixed inner virtues, yet also without reducing them to passive products of circumstance. To do this, I want to employ a framing my professor calls "moral ecology": virtues are not internal causal dispositions but relational achievements that emerge when an agent's practical judgment is sustained by an institutional environment structured to make good choices viable. The speech—approximately four minutes, formal in register but accessible to non-philosophers—should use this idea to shape what can actually be said: I should be able to praise the graduates' discernment and commitment, but I must avoid language that treats them as bearers of innate moral character; I should be able to thank the partner organizations, but I must avoid framing those structures as deterministic forces that erase individual agency. Please write these remarks so that the philosophical tension does structural work rather than serving as decorative background.
