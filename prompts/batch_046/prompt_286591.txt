As a journalist writing about philosophy of mind and ethics, I am working on a feature about what I am calling “diachronic moral bookkeeping”—the difficulty of assigning responsibility over time when the agent who evaluates a past action is causally continuous with, yet never identically the same as, the agent who performed it, and when neither agent authored the normative framework used for that evaluation. For this piece, please write a dialogue between two equally capable interlocutors. One defends an extension of Galen Strawson’s Basic Argument: if we are never causa sui, then not only are we not responsible for actions that flow from our unchosen nature, but we also cannot be genuinely responsible for the second-order judgments through which we claim certain actions as genuinely ours; the regress of unchosen conditions reaches all the way up to the evaluative framework itself. The other interlocutor accepts that no one is self-created but argues that this view neglects a recoverable form of “structural autonomy”—the capacity to recursively revise one’s own decision procedures over time, such that a later, causally conditioned endorsement can still function as a legitimate ground for owning (or disowning) earlier conduct, without requiring an impossible original authorship.

The dialogue should turn on a concrete case: imagine a scientist who discovers that an unchosen cognitive bias shaped years of her research, then spends a decade deliberately restructuring her reasoning habits and eventually reaches a stable, reflective endorsement of her revised methods. The speakers should ask whether her later endorsement can bear the weight of responsibility for the earlier biased work, or whether Strawson’s regress renders even that endorsement hollow. Each speaker must respond to the other’s actual argument—no talking past one another, no straw positions—and neither should dominate. The exchange should be rigorous yet accessible to an educated general reader.

Please also be self-critical and explicit: if extending Strawson’s argument to second-order evaluation rests on a confused premise, say so plainly rather than accommodating it politely. Likewise, flag any point where you take liberties with Strawson’s actual text or where “structural autonomy” relies on a contested or non-standard reading. Do not invent citations or present speculative interpretations as settled scholarship.
