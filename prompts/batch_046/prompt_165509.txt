I have been trying to understand the split-brain experiments conducted in the 1960s and 1970s, in which surgeons severed the corpus callosum and patients seemed to develop two somewhat independent streams of awareness: the left hand could act on information presented to the right hemisphere, while the speaking left hemisphere could not report what had happened. The popular explanation treats this as proof of a single “Cartesian theater” inside the head—a central audience where experience comes together—that was simply split in two by the operation. But I have also read Dennett’s argument that there is no such theater, only many competing “drafts” of experience that are constantly revised and never reach a final, authoritative version.

I want to analyze the split-brain episode through both lenses, running the analysis in both directions. If Dennett is right and consciousness is always a distributed editing process, then cutting the corpus callosum should not create two theaters but should instead isolate two drafting systems that were already somewhat independent. Yet the experiments seem to show something stronger than isolation: the left hemisphere actively confabulates explanations for actions initiated by the right hemisphere, as if one narrator is inventing a backstory for a co-author whose notes have gone missing. Does this support Dennett—because it reveals that there never was one authoritative draft—or does it actually presuppose a theater that has suddenly lost backstage access?

At the same time, the Cartesian theater model predicts a clean bifurcation of one audience into two, but it struggles with the fact that patients almost never report feeling divided. They insist they are one person, even when their hands are working at cross-purposes. If a single theater really split, why is there no subjective experience of being two? And if Dennett’s model is correct, why does severing one connection seem to produce such a dramatic functional separation rather than just a messier single stream?

Please examine the episode carefully: map who or what counts as the “player,” what counts as the evidence, and where each theory is silent rather than forced to speak. I am especially interested in whether the case is best read as consciousness actually splitting, or as the revelation that there was never one unified process to split—only distributed editing that lost its cross-hemisphere notes. What do the confabulation and the absence of any felt division show about the theater, and what do they show about the drafts?
