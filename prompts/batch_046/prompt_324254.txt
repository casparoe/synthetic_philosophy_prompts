Consider an institutional forecasting protocol adopted by some risk-assessment agencies: every quantitative prediction must be produced twice—once through case-specific reasoning (the "inside view") and once through statistical base rates of analogous past events (the "outside view")—with the final forecast required by charter to be a weighted average that never assigns the inside view more than 60% weight, even when analysts judge the case unprecedented. The protocol also requires separate confidence intervals for each track. What theory of rational belief and epistemic modesty does this two-track rule presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit view: does it treat particular reasoning as systematically overconfident, or merely as fallible? Is the practice strictly incompatible with a pure Bayesian subjectivist framework, or could a subjectivist accept the charter as a pragmatic constraint? Distinguish what the protocol philosophically requires from what it merely accommodates. Then describe what would have to change—both in the forecasting method and in downstream decision-making—if the institution replaced the fixed-weight rule with one letting analysts determine the mixture case by case based on their own assessment of relevance.
