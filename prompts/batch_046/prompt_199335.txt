The debate over eliminative materialism—whether a mature neuroscience will eventually show that propositional attitudes such as belief and desire do not exist—has been at an impasse since Churchland advanced his argument by eliminative induction and Fodor responded that folk psychology’s predictive success and the systematicity of mental representation make it irreplaceable. Despite decades of empirical progress, both sides still agree that brains cause behavior and that everyday psychological explanation works well; yet eliminativists maintain that this success is analogous to the predictive success of Ptolemaic astronomy before Copernicus, while realists treat it as evidence that intentional categories are indispensable. My working theory is that the disagreement is not primarily about neuroscience, but about what theoretical replacement requires—whether it demands predictive failure of the old framework, explanatory redundancy at the mechanistic level, or the demonstration that the old ontology was never genuinely explanatory. Is that diagnosis correct, or does a hidden substantive disagreement persist—perhaps about whether mental content can be fully captured by vector-space or dynamical models lacking propositional structure—that both sides are addressing without realizing it? I want a diagnosis of the stalemate’s structure: is it a verbal dispute over “elimination” versus “reduction”; a clash of bedrock intuitions about what counts as scientific explanation; two groups answering different questions—one ontological, one about explanatory practice—under the single heading “eliminative materialism”; or a genuinely empirical disagreement obscured by philosophical rhetoric? Please support your diagnosis by engaging with the actual claims on each side—Churchland’s analogy to entities such as phlogiston that were eliminated rather than reduced, Fodor’s emphasis on the productivity and compositionality of thought, and the intermediate instrumentalist view that treats folk psychology as a useful fiction rather than a false ontology—and tell me what, if anything, would actually break the deadlock: a specific neuroscientific result (for instance, a complete dynamical model of decision-making with no propositional variables), a formal result about the representational capacities of neural networks, or a conceptual distinction showing that the two sides have been talking past each other.
