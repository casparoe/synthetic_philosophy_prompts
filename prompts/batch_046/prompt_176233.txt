i was arguing with a friend about Burke and Chestertons fence after skimming some old Overcoming Bias posts on tradition vs rationalist redesign, and i think i accidentally built an argument that proves way too much. Here it goes: (1) Chestertons fence says don't tear down a fence if you can't see why its there. (2) The Burkean version: long-standing institutions encode practical knowledge that outruns anyones explicit reasoning, so reform requires understanding the institutions purpose. (3) If you don't understand that purpose, you shouldn't reform it. (4) For any institution complex enough to matter—marriage norms, common law, markets—no generation has complete understanding of all its emergent functions and interdependencies. (5) So no one is ever justified in reforming any complex institution. (6) That means abolition, democracy, all of it was unjustified, and in fact any change is unjustified. (7) So either (1) dies or we're stuck defending that we should have kept slavery because we don't fully understand feudal labor structures or whatever.

i feel like there's either an equivocation between "not seeing the specific purpose of this fence" and "not having total sociologial knowledge," or a modal slide from "don't tear it down blindly" to "never change it ever." But i want to keep (1) if i can without becoming a reactionary, and i'm not sure what killing (3) or (4) actually costs—like, if i say we only need sufficient understanding for the specific reform, am i just sneaking rationalist hubris back in? Which step actually breaks, and what's the price of breaking it?

if this whole setup is confused or i'm misreading Chesterton, tell me straight instead of being polite about it—i'd rather know the argument is garbage than get a nice answer that pretends the crack isn't there.
