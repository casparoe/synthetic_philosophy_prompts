I'm a journalist writing a feature on how canonical thinkers revise their own frameworks, and I'm including a sample exam to show readers whether they actually grasp Mill's late socialist turn or just treat it as an embarrassing footnote to classical liberalism. Please design an upper-level philosophy exam essay prompt—aimed at students who have read On Liberty and the later editions of Principles of Political Economy—centered on a specific tension that cannot be resolved by calling Mill inconsistent or converted. The question should require students to engage with Mill's claim that cooperative worker-ownership solves the problem of labor without state expropriation of capital, and to explain the institutional mechanism by which he thought this remained compatible with the liberty principle—specifically, the role of voluntary association, competitive markets among cooperatives, and the distinction between land-value taxation and the abolition of private property.

Structure your response in exactly these named sections, in this order:

The Exam Question (about 200 words): the essay prompt itself, with instructions that prevent students from answering with a summary of both texts.

Model Answer (about 600 words): take a committed position—either that Mill's cooperative socialism is a coherent extension of his utility principle applied to industrial organization, or that it remains unstable because the required redistribution of land values and competitive discipline still conflict with his liberty principle—defend it with specific reasoning about association and market exit, and do not present both sides as equally valid.

Marking Scheme (about 300 words): list the specific moves an A answer must contain, with point values totaling 25; then list the specific errors that separate an A from a B—such as conflating Mill's position with state socialism or Marxist abolition of capital, treating the compatibility claim as merely psychological rather than institutional, or ignoring the competitive-market element of his cooperative scheme—and note how candidates typically miss each point. Keep the tone formal and precise, suitable for publication in an ideas magazine, and do not ask for bibliographies, citations, or reading lists.
