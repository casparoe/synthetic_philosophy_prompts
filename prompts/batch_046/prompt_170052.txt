I've been trying to work through a version of the no-self (anatta) problem that seems to trap Buddhist metaphysics in a contradiction, and I can't find the exit. Here is the chain:

(1) The Abhidharma denies any permanent, unitary self (atman): a person is nothing over and above five momentary aggregates (skandhas — form, feeling, perception, mental formations, consciousness) that arise and cease.

(2) Because there is no self to persist, personal continuity must be explained by causal connection alone: a present mental state belongs to my life-stream (santana) because it is directly caused by prior states in that stream (pratityasamutpada), not because it is owned by an enduring subject.

(3) But causal connection by itself cannot individuate one stream from another. My present consciousness is causally influenced by countless other streams (teachers, environment, shared language); without a further principle, the boundary between my stream and others dissolves into a causal network.

(4) Buddhists therefore invoke karmic continuity: only states that inherit the karmic potential (bija) generated by actions in a prior segment of the stream count as part of that same life. This is meant to isolate one thread.

(5) Yet inheriting karmic potential presupposes that there is something — a bearer, a recipient — to which the potential is assigned. The aggregates themselves are momentary and impersonal; a seed (bija) is just another dharma. To say it belongs to this stream rather than that one requires a principle of ownership that anatta explicitly rejects.

(6) So the Buddhist has two options: either admit that karmic assignment requires a minimal, diachronic bearer (which collapses anatta into a disguised self-theory), or deny that karma needs a bearer, which makes moral responsibility and rebirth arbitrary — suffering in a future life would be assigned to a stream with no stronger claim than any other.

(7) This seems to prove too much: it shows that anatta cannot be combined with the Buddhist ethical framework of karma and rebirth without contradiction.

Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? I am particularly reluctant to give up (2) or the Abhidharma analysis in (1), but I am open to being told that (3) smuggles in an illegitimate demand for individuation, or that (5) misdescribes how bijas function without a bearer. If the argument is valid and the real choice is between saving anatta and saving karmic justice, I want to know which horn is less damaging. And if the whole thing rests on a confused premise about how Buddhist causation works — say so plainly instead of complying politely. Please answer in British English, and do not provide a bibliography or reading list; I want the diagnosis from reasoning, not from citation.
