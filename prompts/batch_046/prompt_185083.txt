I've been observing a peculiar institutional practice at the intersection of AI development and intellectual property law, and I'm trying to understand what philosophy of mind it silently commits to. Consider a research lab that trains a large language model. Internally, engineers speak fluently in intentional terms — they say the model wants to minimize loss, that it believes certain premises about its user, or that it intends to complete a sentence in a particular way. These ascriptions guide debugging, shape design choices, and circulate freely in meeting notes. Yet when the same lab publishes a technical report, every trace of that vocabulary disappears; the model is described purely as a statistical pattern-matcher, and any output — even a coherent philosophical argument — is attributed entirely to the training distribution, with no mental content assigned to the system itself. Meanwhile, the lab's legal department treats the model's outputs as lacking copyright eligibility because there is no human author, yet the communications team still evaluates those outputs for accuracy, coherence, and insight, as though they were assessing a claim that could be true or false in virtue of some represented content.

This bifurcation seems to depend on a specific view of original versus derived intentionality. Does the practice presuppose that intentionality can be genuinely present in a system for practical, heuristic purposes — the debugging register — but metaphysically absent for legal and epistemic evaluation — the publication and IP registers? Or does it instead assume a division of the kind Searle draws: that original intentionality belongs exclusively to biological minds, that any intentionality ascribed to the model is purely derived — analogous to the response of Dennett's two-bitser, which carries meaning only in virtue of being programmed — and that derived intentionality is real enough to guide engineering but too thin to ground authorship or knowledge?

Reconstruct the implicit theory that licenses treating the same system as an intentional agent in one institutional context and as a mere syntactic engine in another. Is the practice coherent, or does it quietly conflate two incompatible accounts — perhaps treating derived intentionality as fully real when it is useful and as purely fictional when it is inconvenient? Say whether the practice is philosophically underdetermined: could a consistent Dennett-style intentional-stance view justify the same double register without invoking original intentionality at all, or does the legal denial of authorship strictly require Searle's metaphysical distinction? Finally, describe what would have to change in the lab's documentation and evaluation norms if it adopted the view that derived intentionality is every bit as ontologically robust as original intentionality — that a model's beliefs are not metaphorical but genuinely representational, albeit with a different causal history.
