I am a PhD student in moral philosophy working at the intersection of normative ethics and empirical moral psychology. During a recent graduate workshop on the situationist critique, a colleague dismissed an Aristotelian defense of global character traits by declaring, "Traits don't travel, so virtues aren't real," treating the line as if it alone collapsed virtue ethics into descriptive psychology. The remark seemed to be doing several distinct jobs at once—empirical, metaphysical, conceptual, and normative—but was never disambiguated, so I could not tell which interpretation was actually meant to bridge psychological data and philosophical failure.

Could you lay out exactly five distinct precise theses that slogan might express: first, the empirical claim about cross-situational behavioral inconsistency; second, the conceptual claim that virtue necessarily requires globally consistent dispositions; third, the metaphysical claim that locally stable habits are not genuine dispositions at all; fourth, the normative claim that only global traits can ground moral praise or blame; and fifth, an interpretation invoking the aggregation problem in personality measurement to argue that apparent trait instability is an artifact of poor measurement rather than a real absence of character. For each reading, say whether it is trivial, defensible, or false, and specify whether it actually supports the speaker's conclusion that virtue ethics is undermined. Please present your answer as a numbered list with exactly five items, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion.
