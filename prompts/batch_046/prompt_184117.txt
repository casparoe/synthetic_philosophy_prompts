my roommate summed up the sunk-cost fallacy like this: "rational choice theory says only future costs and benefits matter, so past spending should never influence you. if you bought a nonrefundable ticket and then feel sick, going to the concert just to avoid wasting money is irrational—you're throwing good money after bad. the same applies everywhere: if you've invested years in a career or relationship and it's not working, sticking with it because of that investment is always the fallacy, and cutting your losses is always the rational move."

something about this feels too sweeping. i remember hearing that escalation of commitment can be rational in sequential decisions where quitting reveals private information, and that there's a distinction between capital sunk costs and sunk effort that changes the calculus. also he seems to treat consistency or loyalty as automatically irrational, which sounds like a hidden normative premise rather than economics. is his summary fair to the actual literature? please seperate what's accurate, what's an overstatement, and whether his conclusion about always cutting losses survives once you account for path-dependent preferences and signaling concerns. also, does he conflate the sunk-cost effect with escalation of commitment in a way that matters?
