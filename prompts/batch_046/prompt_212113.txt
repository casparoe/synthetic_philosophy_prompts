I've been returning to the Grand Inquisitor episode in The Brothers Karamazov, particularly the argument that humanity will willingly trade freedom for the security of certainty, material provision, and the abdication of moral responsibility. In recent discussions about AI alignment and preference aggregation—specifically frameworks in which a system learns a reward model from human feedback and then shapes a user's choice environment to maximize predicted life satisfaction—I've noticed a structural parallel to the Inquisitor's offer of bread, miracle, and authority.

The parallel becomes more precise if we consider not overt coercion but what might be called epistemic enclosure: a benevolent optimizer doesn't forbid dissenting choices, but through personalization and predictive filtering renders the alternatives cognitively invisible, leaving the agent with the formal freedom to choose while systematically constraining the space of what can be conceived. I'm taking a course that touches on normative theory, and I'm trying to work out whether this modern form of indirect constraint changes the moral calculus that Dostoevsky had in mind.

If Dostoevsky were alive today and observing large-scale algorithmic governance that optimizes for aggregate well-being by subtly restructuring the informational environment in which preferences are formed, how do you think he would extend or qualify the Inquisitor's critique? Would he regard distributed, statistical, and ostensibly benevolent preference-learning as the technological fulfillment of the Inquisitor's prophecy, or would he maintain a sharp distinction between explicit authoritarian control and this opaque form of guided autonomy? And crucially, would he still hold that the loss of freedom is a spiritual catastrophe when the constraining force is not a visible Church but an invisible architecture of optimized recommendations?
