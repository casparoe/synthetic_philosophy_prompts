Below are draft notes for an essay I am preparing on the value of originals in an age of perfect replication. I defend a view I call ontological debt: when a high-quality forgery is revealed, the damage is not confined to deceived collectors or viewers but restructures the hermeneutic conditions of the authentic works it imitates. Specifically, the original is forced to bear an epistemic burden of proof against its doubles, generating what I term a shadow corpus that retroactively alters the aesthetic ecology of the genuine articles. I invoke the familiar distinction between autographic and allographic art forms—where the former depend on causal history for identity, the latter on structural compliance—but I argue that even in allographic domains the revelation of a convincing forgery introduces a persistent contamination. The original does not remain untouched; rather, future engagement with it becomes mediated by an explicit verification frame that was previously unnecessary.

Please comment on the coherence and philosophical substance of this line of argument. In particular: (1) Does ontological debt perform genuine explanatory work, or is it merely a rhetorical metaphor for ordinary disappointment? (2) Is the extension to allographic arts defensible, or does it collapse the very distinction I rely upon? (3) How vulnerable is the claim about retroactive contamination to the reply that originals and forgeries are evaluated in entirely separate causal-historical contexts, so that no cross-domain epistemic pollution can occur? I am especially interested in whether the essay needs a more robust account of collective epistemic practices to sustain the shadow-corpus thesis, and in whether there are unacknowledged normative assumptions about authenticity driving the argument.
