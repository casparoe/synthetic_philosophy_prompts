I'm a retired physical chemist who got into philosophy late in life, and lately I've been trying to make sense of price controls using some ethics papers I found in a used bookstore. I sit on a municipal advisory board mostly as a hobby now, and we've made rulings on several proposals that I think hang together, but I'm not sure what the actual rule is.

Here are our verdicts so far:
- Rent stabilization that allows 3% annual increases tied to a maintenance-cost index: we approved it.
- A hard five-year rent freeze with no adjustments for inflation or repairs: rejected.
- A minimum wage for manufacturing tied directly to sector productivity metrics: approved.
- A flat living wage applied to all retail and food-service jobs regardless of output: rejected.
- Price caps on off-patent insulin: approved.
- Price caps on imported semiconductor chips during a supply shortage: rejected.

Now there's a new proposal on the table: an AI-adjusted wage floor for delivery-app workers. The rate updates hourly based on real-time demand, weather hazard pay, and local electricity costs, but it never drops below 1.4x the living wage for a single adult. I want to know what our board should say, and more importantly why.

Can you lay out at least two different principles that could explain all six of our past rulings? For each one, predict whether we'd approve this algorithmic gig-worker floor. Then tell me which principle you think actually captures our reasoning, point to the closest analog in our track record, and be honest about what that principle says we should do either way. Finally, name the one further hypothetical case that would actually split the rules apart.

I'm especially torn between thinking flexibility itself is the key factor, versus thinking it's really about whether we're dealing with basic survival goods versus discretionary labor markets. So address that tension directly, and don't just say "it depends" — take a stand on which rule fits us best.
