I have been studying the tripartite account of meaning in formal systems—frame messages, outer messages, and inner messages—presented in Gödel, Escher, Bach, particularly in the analysis of how self-referential formulas acquire semantic interpretation. The framework holds that an inner message (the syntactic structure within a formal system) gains content only when an outer message (the intended interpretation) is mapped onto it through a frame (the rules governing that mapping). Yet it seems to me that if an inner message achieves sufficient recursion—encoding not only base-level propositions but also meta-rules specifying how those propositions ought to be interpreted—then the frame and outer message are effectively internalized. The system becomes, in a meaningful sense, self-interpreting.

If this inference holds, then a large language model may be closer to genuine intentionality than philosophers of mind typically allow. Its parameter space encodes statistical regularities that function, at scale, as implicit meta-rules about linguistic usage—precisely the kind of recursive inner message that absorbs its own interpretive frame. Nevertheless, the standard view continues to deny that such networks possess intrinsic semantic content. I want to understand exactly where the reasoning fails. Is the error in assuming that statistical meta-rules can substitute for an interpretive frame, or is there a deeper reason why an outer message must always remain external to the system it interprets, regardless of internal complexity? And does this constraint imply that no increase in architectural recursion or scale can bridge the gap between syntactic pattern-matching and genuine meaning?
