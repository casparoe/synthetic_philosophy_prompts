I am a retired physicist who has only recently begun reading philosophy of science in earnest. I have been asked to give the opening remarks—about five minutes—at an interdisciplinary workshop titled “Continuity and Invention in Intellectual History,” attended by historians of science, practicing scientists, and a scattering of philosophers. I want the address to treat the labels “Middle Ages,” “Renaissance,” and “Scientific Revolution” not as discovered natural kinds but as artifacts of what I will call retrospective taxonomization: the tendency to cluster the innovations that fit our present disciplinary self-image, quietly discard concurrent failures and parallel traditions, and then project that curated cluster backward as a unified epochal rupture. This idea should shape what I can and cannot say structurally—I must be able to welcome the historians without endorsing their period boundaries as real divisions in nature, and to address the scientists without confirming that they are heirs to a singular heroic break rather than to a far messier continuity. The room is mixed, so the language should be precise but not jargon-laden, respectful, and formal. Please write the remarks. Begin with the speech itself—no preface, no commentary, and no restatement of the question. The first sentence should state the central claim outright; the rest should unfold the reasoning, maintaining the tone of a retired scientist speaking to colleagues across disciplines.
