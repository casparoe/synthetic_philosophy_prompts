I'm trying to hold together a Spinoza-inspired democratic theory for a workshop on political legitimacy, and I want an internal coherence audit before I present it. Here are my commitments:

(1) Democracy is the only fully legitimate form of government because sovereignty is inalienable: the collective must retain ultimate authority, and any permanent transfer of decision-making power to a monarch or aristocracy violates natural equality and the core argument of Spinoza's political writings.

(2) The democratic multitude, however, is rarely guided by reason and is far more often moved by passion, envy, and superstition; left entirely to its own devices, a popular assembly therefore tends toward unstable, destructive, and self-undermining policies.

(3) To prevent this collapse, legitimate democratic institutions must include strong counter-majoritarian mechanisms—independent expert administrative bodies and constitutional courts with real veto or revision authority over popular legislation—without which democracy degenerates into mob rule rather than rational collective self-governance.

(4) At the same time, freedom of thought, philosophical inquiry, and public expression must remain absolutely unrestricted, even when that expression actively amplifies the passions that destabilize democratic deliberation, because any state attempt to control minds is both impossible in principle and destructive of the state's own foundation.

(5) Finally, the ultimate end of government is not procedural fairness or the realization of a popular will, but the preservation and increase of the collective conatus—citizens' actual power to act, think, and live according to reason.

Please audit whether this package hangs together. Do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; I need an audit of internal logic, not a literature review.

Structure your response in these sections, in this order:

1. "Restatement" (~100 words): Summarize the five commitments concisely to show you have them right.

2. "The tension that dissolves on inspection" (~150 words): Identify the pair among (1)–(5) that looks like a contradiction but is not. Explain exactly why the conflict is only surface-level, what distinction removes it, and why a critic might be misled into seeing a problem.

3. "Severe tension I: Sovereignty versus expert correction" (~200 words): Analyze the conflict between (1) and (3). How deep is it? Would saving (1) require weakening (3) to advisory status only, or redefining democracy away from direct collective authority? Would saving (3) require abandoning inalienable sovereignty? Rank its severity and identify the least costly revision.

4. "Severe tension II: Unrestricted expression and the passionate multitude" (~200 words): Analyze the conflict between (2) and (4). Given that the multitude is driven by passion and speech is absolute, is democratic instability inevitable? What is the cheapest fix—restricting speech (and at what cost to (4)), denying that the multitude is essentially passion-driven (cost to (2)), or accepting perpetual crisis as the price of freedom?

5. "The single best revision" (~150 words): If I must revise or drop exactly one commitment to maximize coherence among the remaining four, which one should go? Justify by showing which tensions it resolves, which commitments it protects, and what intellectual cost the revision carries.

6. "Bottom line" (~100 words): Give an overall verdict. Is the package salvageable with adjustments, or does one commitment need to be abandoned? Order the genuine tensions by severity rather than treating them as equally damaging, and be direct about whether the view is consistent enough to defend.
