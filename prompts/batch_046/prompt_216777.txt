I am a graduate student in clinical neuropsychology enrolled in a seminar on methods in consciousness science, and I work part-time as a research coordinator in a post-anesthesia monitoring study. After patients emerge from deep sedation, my team conducts brief structured interviews—usually under twenty minutes—to record subjective reports for a database on awareness during anesthesia. I need a reusable decision procedure grounded in Dennett’s heterophenomenology: treating each verbal report not as direct introspective access to consciousness but as a text that describes a heterophenomenological world requiring interpretive construction. The procedure must be operable by nurses and research assistants with no philosophical training, include clear stopping rules, and distinguish three interpretive outcomes—(a) sufficient narrative coherence to construct a heterophenomenological world, (b) phenomenal residue (vague bodily feeling without temporal or narrative structure), and (c) probable confabulation—while flagging exactly where clinical judgment rather than interpretive rules must take over. Please specify: the ordered questions or tests; what counts as a sufficient answer to each; where the procedure terminates with a classification; and where it hands the decision to the attending anesthesiologist because the issue is one of values or clinical risk rather than interpretive fact. The procedure must handle hard cases: patients who deny all experience despite behavioral indicators of awareness; patients reporting elaborate narratives that conflict with the physiological record; and reports that fall between phenomenal residue and full narrative. Then run the procedure on three cases: (1) a patient describing a coherent, emotionally charged sequence apparently occurring during a twenty-minute interval of flat-line EEG; (2) a patient who denies any experience but describes an isolated, unlocalized pressure lacking temporal placement; (3) one case in which the procedure yields a classification that I, as the coordinator, will want to reject on clinical grounds, together with an explanation of what the procedure genuinely cannot resolve in that conflict. Conclude by stating precisely what this heterophenomenological procedure can and cannot establish—specifically, whether it can determine the presence or absence of consciousness itself, or only organize and classify reports about it, and where its limits lie with respect to small-scale or non-verbal subjects.
