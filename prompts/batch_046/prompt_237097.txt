After a long argument last night with a close friend—who insisted that modern Bayesian methods have dissolved Keynes's distinction between risk and uncertainty into nothing more than a continuum of subjective priors—I want to operationalize Keynes's actual framework rather than the textbook caricature. I work as a senior epistemic-risk auditor for a multilateral climate-adaptation fund, and every quarter I must review roughly a dozen project proposals that invoke "uncertainty" to justify either elaborate quantitative risk models or sweeping qualitative judgments. I need a reusable, thirty-minute audit procedure I can apply repeatedly that translates Keynes's logical-relation view of probability, his risk-versus-uncertainty distinction, and especially his concept of the weight of argument into ordered, practical steps. The procedure must classify every evidential claim in a proposal into one of three categories—cardinal risk (numerically measurable probabilities), ordinal risk (rankable but not cardinally measurable probabilities), or true Keynesian uncertainty (no rational basis for ordering outcomes)—while treating weight of evidence as an independent dimension that can vary without altering the underlying probability relation. It should run as a sequence of four tests: first, whether the proposal assigns precise numbers to unprecedented structural breaks; second, whether it conceals genuinely measurable risks behind vague language; third, whether it mistakes low weight for low probability; and fourth, whether an increase in relevant evidence could reduce assigned confidence (the case Keynes observed where greater weight accompanies lower probability). At every step where judgment cannot be eliminated—particularly in deciding whether two scenarios are genuinely incomparable rather than merely difficult to quantify—the procedure must flag the decision point, specify what expertise is required, and state who must sign off. Apply it to three worked cases: a regional flood proposal using ensemble climate models, where the procedure should permit quantification but flag dangerously low weight; a supply-chain proposal that assigns a 65 percent point-estimate to an unprecedented geopolitical alliance, where the procedure must reject the quantification and thereby destroy the proposal's central risk metric, an answer I will not like; and a hybrid agricultural proposal that mixes historical yield variance with speculative soil-carbon feedback loops. Finally, specify explicitly what the procedure cannot do—which institutional pressures it will not survive, which philosophical disputes it leaves open, and where it demands expert judgment that no checklist can replace.
