I am a doctoral candidate in political philosophy writing on comparative transparency regimes, and in a recent workshop comparing German informational self-determination doctrine, American open-government norms, and the architecture of Chinese social-credit scoring, a senior colleague repeatedly invoked the maxim "if you have done nothing wrong, you have nothing to hide" to rebut objections to expansive state data harvesting. The maxim seemed to carry the entire argumentative load—collapsing privacy into mere secrecy about misconduct, treating state access as risk-free for the innocent, and dismissing structural concerns about algorithmic sorting—yet no one in the room stopped to ask which of these moves the sentence actually licensed. Could you lay out, as a disambiguated menu, the distinct precise theses this slogan might be advancing? I am particularly interested in separating: (1) a conceptual claim that privacy interests are exhaustively defined by the protection of wrongful conduct; (2) an empirical claim that transparency eliminates informational asymmetries that enable bureaucratic abuse; (3) a predictive claim that legal innocence confers practical immunity from state error, misclassification, or mission creep; (4) a normative claim about democratic reciprocity, where citizens owe visibility in exchange for accountable governance; and (5) any further non-obvious reading that would actually support the inference from "I am law-abiding" to "I ought not resist data collection." For each reading, indicate whether it is trivial, substantive and defensible, or false; specify what kind of "nothing to hide" (behavioral, informational, psychological) and whose fear (state punishment, social stigma, algorithmic sorting) are at issue; and identify which interpretation the workshop speaker was most likely smuggling in to bridge German data-protection skepticism, American open-government optimism, and Chinese credit-system normalization into a single dismissal. I want to bring the menu—not a single verdict—to my supervisor.
