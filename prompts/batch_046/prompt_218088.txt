I have recently become convinced that my own ethical framework leans toward a form of tranquilism — the view that only the absence of craving and suffering carries intrinsic value, while positive states are at best instrumentally valuable — but I am struggling to defend it in conversation because my interlocutors keep treating a slogan I use as if it were self-evident. When I say, "Only the absence of craving and suffering matters," they take it to settle questions about whether achievement, aesthetic wonder, or loving relationships have any place in a well-lived life, and they accuse me of reducing human flourishing to a kind of anesthesia. I am certain the slogan can be interpreted in several incompatible ways, but I lack the vocabulary to separate them precisely, and I need a disambiguated position rather than a mantra.

Could you lay out, as a menu rather than a single interpretation, the distinct precise theses that sentence might be expressing? For each, I want to know whether it is an axiological claim about intrinsic versus instrumental value, a metaphysical claim about the nature of pleasure or pain, a normative claim about what rational agents ought to maximize, a psychological claim about the conditions for well-being, or a modal claim about what is necessarily true of any valuable state. Then, for each reading, assess whether it is trivial, defensible but substantive, false, or self-undermining, and specifically indicate whether that reading actually licenses the dismissal of positive goods like achievement or aesthetic experience. I am particularly interested in whether any reading introduces a hidden asymmetry — for instance, that suffering is bad in a way that pleasure is not correspondingly good — and whether that asymmetry is doing the real argumentative work. The goal is to help me see which version my interlocutors are assuming and which versions are genuinely compatible with allowing that some positive states matter derivatively.
