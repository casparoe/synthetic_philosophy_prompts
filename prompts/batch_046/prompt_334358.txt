Read the Room 101 sequence in George Orwell's 1984 through the ethics of torture, specifically the ticking-time-bomb justification. Most philosophical treatments treat torture as an epistemic instrument: a rational agent hides a catastrophic fact, and coercion extracts it. Orwell's scene inverts this. O'Brien does not torture Winston to learn anything—he already knows Winston's disloyalty in detail—and Winston's final confessions (that he loves Big Brother, that arithmetic is malleable) are falsehoods O'Brien does not need and Winston does not initially believe. I want a reading that asks whether 1984 therefore depicts torture as essentially expressive or destructive of truth rather than informational. At the same time, consider whether the novel inadvertently approximates the ticking-bomb ideal: O'Brien claims the Party never arrests the innocent and possesses near-omniscient knowledge of Winston's thoughts, conditions that mirror the scenario's assumption that the torturer knows exactly whom to coerce and for what fact. Does the scene show that even under those idealized epistemic conditions torture still corrupts belief rather than revealing it? Or does it suggest that torture is rational only when such perfect knowledge holds, thereby undermining real-world applications where that knowledge is absent?

Please also engage with the distinction—often collapsed in debate—between epistemic torture, aimed at recovering a specific hidden fact, and ontological torture, aimed at remaking the victim's subjectivity. Does Orwell's novel support treating them as separate moral categories, or does it collapse them by showing that any practice precise enough to extract truth is inseparable from the power to manufacture false belief? Conclude by saying what this literary case adds to the philosophical argument that pure reasoning about hypothetical bombs tends to miss.
