I'm preparing a ninety-minute session for graduate students in cognitive science of religion — familiar with hyperactive agency detection and minimally counterintuitive agent theories, but with little background in philosophy — on Wittgenstein's view that religious belief is not an empirical hypothesis about invisible agents but a regulative picture embedded in practice and commitment. Plan the explanatory arc rather than delivering it: what conceptual distinction must be secured first so the idea is not immediately assimilated to their existing framework, then the order in which the remaining components should be introduced and why each depends on the prior. Identify the single carrying example — not generic theism, but one that visibly resembles a predictive claim yet functions non-empirically — and specify what it must contain and what it must lack. Then map the predictable misconceptions in the order they will arise: that religious belief is simply a false proto-scientific theory; that it reduces to emotional or social cohesion; and, specifically within this audience, that it can be fully accounted for by MCI or agency-detection mechanisms. State precisely where in the sequence each misconception should be intercepted. Provide one closing diagnostic question that distinguishes whether they have understood the category error or only adopted the vocabulary. Finally, list what should be deliberately omitted at this level — for instance, Tractarian mysticism, denominational doctrine, the private-language argument — and explain why leaving each out protects the central point rather than merely simplifying it.
