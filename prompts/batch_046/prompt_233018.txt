I have just been appointed lead curator for a permanent gallery on nineteenth-century scientific and colonial institutions, and I face a recurring conflict: the board wants every interpretive panel to issue explicit present-day moral judgments on historical practices, while my academic advisors insist that doing so collapses into an unreflective presentism that historians cannot escape. I need a reusable decision procedure that my writing team can apply case-by-case when drafting labels, not a one-off judgment. The procedure should be grounded in the philosophy of history’s actual distinctions—between contextual understanding, normative inheritance, and conceptual anachronism—without requiring theoretical literacy. Structure it as an ordered sequence of tests: first, whether the harm or wrong was recognizable within the actor’s own available normative framework; second, whether internal dissent existed that the actor disregarded; third, whether present evaluation requires importing conceptual categories that were structurally unavailable to the period; and fourth, whether the case remains indeterminate and must be referred to a values committee rather than resolved curatorially. For each step, specify what counts as a sufficient answer, where the procedure halts with a qualified “contextualize without condemning” or “contextualize with explicit present evaluation,” and where judgment is irreducible to historical fact. Apply the procedure to three exhibit subjects: a physician who conducted non-consensual experiments on institutionalized patients despite existing professional ethics codes; a colonial administrator who enforced labor practices standard in the metropole but structurally lethal in the colony; and a locally celebrated progressive reformer who advanced women’s education while actively endorsing racial segregation. Include the case where the procedure recommends condemning the beloved reformer—an answer my board will resist—and flag precisely which steps depend on curatorial value judgments rather than recoverable historical evidence. Finally, state explicitly what the procedure cannot do: identify its failure mode when present and past norms are incommensurable along axes the actor never recognized.
