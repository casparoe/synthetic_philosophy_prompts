Having spent thirty years in empirical scientific research before turning to philosophy in retirement, I find Nietzsche's genealogy of morality at once intuitively familiar and philosophically unsettling. In my former work we regularly traced beliefs and practices to evolutionary, cognitive, or social origins without treating those origins as automatic refutations of the beliefs in question; yet Nietzsche presents the revelation that moral values emerged from ressentiment and the inversion of noble valuations as though it were itself a direct undermining of morality's authority. Write an essay examining whether this genealogical method genuinely debunks moral values or merely offers a causal history that leaves normative standing untouched. Focus particularly on what one might call the continuity thesis—the claim that explaining the psychological and social mechanisms behind a belief automatically determines its normative status—and on whether accepting that thesis for morality forces one to apply it equally to scientific cognition, producing a self-undermining result for the naturalist. Weigh whether there is any defensible asymmetry between moral and scientific belief that would allow a consistent naturalist to debunk the former while preserving the authority of the latter, or whether Nietzsche's critique succeeds precisely because moral cognition lacks the independent evidential checks that stabilize scientific theory. Reach and defend a definite conclusion: either that the genealogy collapses into a genetic fallacy that a retired scientist should reject on parity grounds, or that morality is genuinely vulnerable to genealogical debunking in a way that empirical science is not. Present your argument entirely in continuous prose, without headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, as one might write in a formal letter.
