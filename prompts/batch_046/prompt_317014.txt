I cover science and ideas for a magazine, but I also sit on my university's behavioral-science innovation board, which is a weird combo but here we are. Next month we have to vote on a clinical pilot proposal that treats severe alcohol-use disorder without ever mentioning 'beliefs' or 'desires' — the PI is pushing a hard eliminativist line, saying folk psychology is a failed theory and the trial should target pure interoceptive prediction-error signals through neuro-intervention, skipping any cognitive-behavioral component. Half the board thinks this is just clean neuroscience; the other half thinks if we approve a study that literally designs consent forms and protocols around the idea that patients don't have beliefs or desires, we're baking a metaphysical claim into clinical practice and destroying informed consent and accountability.

There's also this niche twist the PI throws in about 'predictive-processing collapse' being the actual mechanism instead of craving-as-desire, and I'm not sure if that's a real scientific distinction or just relabeling. Could you draft the decision memo I'd circulate before Thursday's meeting? I want a concrete recommendation — approve, reject, or approve with conditions — plus reasoning the researchers can actually respond to. Please take seriously the eliminativist worry that folk-psychological concepts might be obsolete in addiction science, but also take seriously the opposing view that treating people as belief-less in a clinical setting collapses moral agency and makes consent impossible, not just inconvenient. And address whether adopting that predictive-processing framework actually changes the ethical calculus or if it's just semantic camouflage for the same old problem. Keep it formal enough to vote on, but I'm adapting parts for my column so don't make it read like a law review article.
