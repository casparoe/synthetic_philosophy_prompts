As a journalist covering science and ideas, I am drafting a long-form feature on consciousness and need to check whether my own philosophical profile holds together before committing to it in print. Here is my current set of commitments:

1. I am a non-reductive physicalist: mental properties depend on physical brain states but are not reducible to them.
2. I accept that phenomenal consciousness is real and distinct from access consciousness (the form available to reasoning and verbal report).
3. I hold that phenomenal properties are causally inert — they produce no behavioural, cognitive, or neural effects.
4. I believe we possess direct, first-person authority over our phenomenal states; judgments such as “I am in pain” are typically accurate and non-inferential, formed through what I think of as a calibration loop in which neural integration patterns are matched against an internal qualitative template.
5. I take phenomenal concepts to be recognitional: they refer directly to the properties they denote, rather than via descriptive conditions.
6. I think the explanatory gap is permanent but purely epistemic — a feature of our conceptual architecture, not an ontological divide.
7. I reject any model that treats phenomenal properties as predictive constructs or “epistemic shadows” of neural dynamics; I regard them as genuinely real, yet causally isolated.

Audit this profile. Identify which pairs genuinely pull against each other, rank the conflicts by severity, and for each say what the cheapest revision would be and what it costs. Flag at least one pairing that looks contradictory but is actually consistent on closer inspection. Separate what is settled in the philosophy of mind from what remains genuinely contested, and do not present a disputed position as if it were the consensus. Lead with your bottom-line verdict in the first sentence, then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
