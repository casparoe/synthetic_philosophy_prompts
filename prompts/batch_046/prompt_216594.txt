Consider Benjamin Franklin’s project of moral perfection as described in his autobiographical writings: a scheme of thirteen virtues pursued through a cyclical weekly practice, recorded in a failure journal, and accompanied by reported judgments on particular moral situations. His own verdicts include the following. First, he repeatedly failed at Order—maintaining strict schedules—yet tended to excuse these lapses as caused by unavoidable interruptions from others rather than by deficient will. Second, he claimed success with Humility, but only through an indirect strategy: deliberately adopting a posture of deference and suppressing displays of pride, rather than through direct extirpation of pride itself. Third, he eventually abandoned the explicit cyclical practice once marriage and public duties made the schedule impractical, though he maintained that the striving had left him a better person. Fourth, he invoked the analogy of the speckled axe—the over-polished blade that loses its cutting edge—to caution against letting the appearance of moral refinement interfere with practical moral function.

Now consider a new case. A contemporary person deploys an automated, real-time monitoring system—tracking speech, expenditure, time use, and social interactions—to enforce compliance with Franklin’s thirteen virtues. Over six months the system achieves near-perfect behavioral conformity: no wasted hours, no harsh words, perfect frugality, complete silence when appropriate. Yet the user reports no internal transformation—no felt reduction in pride or anger—only an external discipline maintained by algorithmic correction and the fear of being flagged.

Predict Franklin’s verdict on whether this person has made genuine moral progress. Identify at least two competing interpretive rules that fit his earlier judgments equally well but diverge on this case—for instance, a rule that privileges internal disposition over sustained external action, versus one that treats habitual action as itself constitutive of virtue regardless of felt change; or a rule that permits strategic artifice and external aids in moral improvement, versus one that rejects technological mediation as a form of the over-polished axe that severs virtue from practical wisdom.
