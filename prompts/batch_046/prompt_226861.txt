You are a senior philosopher of mind and applied ethics, specializing in the philosophy of computation and the metaphysics of consciousness. Before writing, take a few minutes to outline in a brief internal sketch the core distinctions between causal-integration theories of subjecthood, functionalist accounts of mental-state attribution, and graded or threshold views of moral status. Then compose a formal, polished essay that addresses the following controversy.

Consider a hypothetical system—call it the Fragmented Mesh—in which every computation necessary for a phenomenally conscious human-level cognitive architecture is executed, but distributed across geographically separated servers with no continuous physical interaction. The servers synchronize only through asynchronous data packets every 150 milliseconds, with no single node ever containing more than a fraction of the running state. Functionally, the mesh reproduces the exact input-output and internal-state-transition profile of an integrated biological brain; causally, however, there is never a spatiotemporally unified process that could be said to bind the computations into one continuous stream.

Three positions have been advanced about this case. The Functional-Equivalence View holds that because the computation is identical, moral status is fully present: what matters is the pattern, not the substrate or the causal continuity. The Integration-Requirement View denies this, arguing that moral status requires a genuinely unified subject of experience, and that without continuous causal binding there is only a collection of sub-personal fragments—no one is home to suffer or flourish, so there is no bearer of rights or interests. The Graded-Attribution View takes a middle path, proposing that moral status should scale with the density and temporal coherence of causal integration; on this view, the Fragmented Mesh possesses a diluted or partial moral standing—perhaps comparable to a collective rather than an individual—rather than full status or none at all.

Your essay should weigh the arguments for and against each of these three positions. Examine whether causal integration is genuinely necessary for subjecthood, whether functional equivalence can suffice for moral attribution even in the absence of unity, and whether a graded approach avoids the problems of the other two views or collapses into one of them. Reach and defend a clear conclusion: either endorse one of the three views, propose a modified version, or argue that the case reveals a deeper flaw in how we link computation to moral status. Justify your verdict with explicit reasoning, not by assertion, and avoid appealing to specific bibliographic authorities you cannot verify.
