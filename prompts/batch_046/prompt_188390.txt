Construct a taxonomy of positions on whether and how biography teaches morality, structured as a decision tree whose branches are labeled by concrete claims about the reader-subject relation rather than by school names. The first split should ask whether the pedagogical effect requires the reader to share (or aspire to share) the biographical subject's social role or station; from there, branch on whether the subject's moral failures are structurally necessary to the lesson or merely tolerated noise; then on whether narrative fidelity to historical fact is a precondition for moral uptake or an inessential ornament. Each leaf should carry a descriptive label (e.g., role-bound exemplarity, transcendent hagiography, deflationary skepticism) and one sentence stating its most awkward commitment. Where a logical combination of answers has no known defender, note it as unnamed and briefly diagnose whether the vacancy is due to incoherence, unattractiveness, or mere neglect. The deliverable should include no citations or bibliography; the point is to map the conceptual space, not to survey scholarship. Keep the tree to no more than eight leaves and ensure that at least one branch involves the specific tension between Plutarch's practice of pairing lives for comparative judgment and the hagiographic tendency to suppress comparison altogether.
