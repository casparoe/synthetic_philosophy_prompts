I've been thinking on my own about why people care so deeply about owning an original painting when a perfect copy is visually identical. I've arrived at a view that feels hard to shake, but I want to know exactly where it breaks. Here it is: the only things that distinguish an original artwork from a flawless forgery are (1) the causal chain that connects the object back to the artist's hand, (2) the scarcity and market price that chain generates, and (3) a kind of psychological essentialism—treating an object's origin as if it leaves invisible, value-carrying traces inside the object itself. If you presented both works in identical frames with all provenance hidden, there would be no aesthetic experience, emotional response, or moral reason to prefer one over the other. So the value of the original is not in the painting; it is in the story we tell about it. I also suspect this applies differently across art forms: perhaps paintings are autographic—the physical object just is the artwork—while symphonies are allographic, where only the score and performances count. Under that split, a forged painting is fake art, but a forged performance is merely another performance. Taken together, this makes the reverence for originals look less like a judgment about beauty and more like a social convention dressed up as ontology, not unlike treating water as specially pure because of its baptismal origin.
