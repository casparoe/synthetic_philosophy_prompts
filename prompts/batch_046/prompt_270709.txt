here's the exchange as it actually happened. mia started by saying there's a real paradox: deontology gives agents absolute constraints against violating rights, but in collective scenarios the same total harm can be distributed so no single agent violates a constraint, yet the outcome is identical. she gave this case: imagine ten separate bureaucrats can each illegally seize one person's property to build a defense wall that saves 100 people, but no one bureaucrat can seize all ten properties alone because the law bars each individual act. if all ten act separately, 100 are saved and ten rights are violated. but if one bureaucrat seizes all ten at once, that's ten violations by one agent, which deontology should forbid even more strongly, even though the outcome is the same 100 saved and ten violated. mia said this proves deontology cares about the distribution of agency rather than the actual protection of rights, which is paradoxical for a rights-based view.

mia came back that if agent-relativity blocks agregation entirely, then deontology has no resources for collective action problems. she said: if each of ten people is about to independently tell a harmful lie, and i can stop all ten by telling one lie myself, deontology should prefer that i tell the one lie (one wrong act) rather than let ten happen. but pure agent-relativity says i shouldn't lie at all, leaving ten lies untouched, which seems worse for rights-protection overall.

derek replied that this confuses negative and positive duties. i have no duty to stop others from lying, only a duty not to lie myself. so the choice isn't between one lie and ten lies; it's between me not lying (permissible) and me lying (forbidden). the fact that others then lie is on them, not me.

mia then said okay, but then deontology is silent on the very problems that matter most. she mentioned climate change as an analogy: each person's carbon footprint is negligible and doesn't violate anyone's rights directly, but collectively it violates rights. if deontology only regulates individual actions and can't agregate, it's useless for structural catastrophes. derek said that's unfair because deontology can still condemn institutions that enable harm, even if individual acts aren't rights-violations. mia said that's just sneaking consequentialism in through the back door.
