I am a high school senior in an advanced philosophy elective, and I have been studying the rule-following considerations associated with Kripke's interpretation of Wittgenstein. Rather than addressing the skeptical paradox in its fully general form, I want to defend a deliberately constrained variant. My position is this: even when we restrict attention to rules that are explicitly encoded in a finitely presentable programming language—where an interpreter can determinately execute each step and produce an observable execution trace—the skeptical challenge is not dissolved but merely relocated. The interpreter itself, along with my present understanding of both the source instructions and the trace, could still be governed by a deviant rule that I have no internal resource to rule out. Therefore, the apparent objectivity of a mechanical checkpoint does not break the interpretive regress; it only conceals it behind syntactic operations that still require a prior normative framework to be read as correct.

Before I commit fully to this view, I want an honest map of its exposure. What would have to be true for this position to fail? Would a decisive counterexample require a formal result showing that execution traces are interpretation-independent in a way that mental states are not? Would it require demonstrating that my appeal to an interpreter smuggles in exactly the normative commitments that the skeptic calls into question? Or is there a live conceptual distinction—perhaps drawn from philosophy of mind or computer science—between syntactic manipulation and genuine semantic rule-following that genuinely severs the regress?

Please distinguish evidence or arguments that would be genuinely fatal to my position from those that would merely be awkward. Say what defenders of the computational-dissolution view would themselves concede as damaging, and indicate whether my sketched position is well-insulated or genuinely at risk. I am especially interested in whether there is a live escape route that preserves the skeptical problem without collapsing it into an uninteresting form of general skepticism about all computation.
