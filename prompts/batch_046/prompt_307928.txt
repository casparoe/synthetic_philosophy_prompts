I am preparing remarks for a municipal heritage board hearing next month, where I will defend a specific policy on commemorating figures tied to colonial violence or slavery. My thesis is that authorities should reject both uncritical preservation and outright removal or simple renaming; instead, controversial monuments should be retained in situ but embedded within a redesigned “critical heritage zone” that includes adjacent streets renamed after affected communities, counter-interpretive installations documenting the figure’s violence, and mandatory public-education programming. I call this framework “mnemonic friction,” arguing that it forces sustained ethical confrontation with historical harm. By contrast, I claim that removal and especially renaming risk “mnemonic laundering”—allowing institutions to perform symbolic cleansing without addressing structural legacies—whereas retention without reframing merely legitimizes harm. The audience is tripartite: preservation architects who value material continuity and are skeptical of what they see as iconoclasm; critical heritage scholars who distrust state-sponsored memory and worry that any retention normalizes violence; and neighborhood activists from affected communities who regard the continued presence of the monument as an active insult regardless of framing. Include the single objection—most likely pressed by the activists—that, if insisted upon, would compel me to abandon the core thesis, together with a response that protects the position without dismissing the lived harm.
