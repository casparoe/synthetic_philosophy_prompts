Following a long and somewhat fractious argument with a close friend over whether algorithmically targeted political messaging can ever count as legitimate persuasion rather than manipulation, I have tried to formalize our disagreement into four competing demarcation criteria, and I want them evaluated against a specific stress test.

The four criteria are these. First, the transparency criterion: persuasion is legitimate only if the persuaded party can fully reconstruct the persuader’s reasoning process and the evidential basis for the claim being advanced. Second, the autonomy-preservation criterion: persuasion remains legitimate if and only if the subject’s all-things-considered preference ordering after reflection remains continuous with their ordering prior to the interaction, allowing updates only through newly acquired true beliefs. Third, the epistemic-access criterion: the boundary is crossed when there is selective withholding or strategic framing of relevant counter-evidence, so that legitimate persuasion requires an informational environment that has not been artificially narrowed. Fourth, the structural-reciprocity criterion—my own attempt to capture my friend’s core worry—according to which a persuasive mechanism is legitimate only if it is institutionally and cognitively deployable by the persuaded party back against the persuader, without asymmetry in access, resources, or underlying cognitive architecture.

Please rank these four criteria from 1 (most adequate for distinguishing legitimate persuasion from manipulation) to 4 (least adequate), focusing specifically on contexts of asymmetric information such as algorithmically curated messaging. For each rank, provide a one-sentence justification that directly addresses the objection that all persuasion exploits universal cognitive biases and therefore any exploitation of bias collapses the distinction entirely. In addition, assign each criterion a numerical robustness score from 0 to 10 specifically against that universal-bias objection. Finally, state whether your ranking would shift if the domain were restricted to face-to-face deliberative contexts rather than mediated, algorithmic ones, and if so, explain precisely which criteria gain or lose ground and why.
