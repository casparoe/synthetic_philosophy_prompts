Most professional licensing boards—whether for law, medicine, or accounting—conduct a character-and-fitness review that treats isolated past failures, such as a single plagiarism incident in college or a DUI during a period of family crisis, as evidence of a stable moral disposition, regardless of the situational pressures surrounding them. At the same time, these boards rarely investigate the ethical culture of the applicant's prior workplaces, and they treat successful performance in high-pressure, ambiguous institutional environments as morally neutral. Recently, however, a few jurisdictions have begun experimenting with contextualized character review: evaluators now weigh whether a past failure occurred within an organization that had weak oversight or punitive norms, and they ask whether an applicant's clean record in a corrupt setting required active complicity rather than integrity.

What theory of moral character does the traditional licensing practice presuppose? Does it require a global-trait view of virtue that treats behavior as tracking cross-situational dispositions, or can a defender of the practice accommodate the situationist critique by arguing that boards are merely tracking stable response patterns rather than deep traits? Does the shift to contextualized review presuppose a different philosophical account—perhaps one that treats virtue as partly constituted by institutional design rather than as an individual property? Is the traditional practice philosophically underdetermined, so that it is equally compatible with virtue ethics, a Humean dispositionalism, or a purely predictive behavioral model? And if we replaced its implicit theory with an account on which moral behavior is largely driven by situational features rather than personal character, what exactly would have to change in how boards gather evidence, weight references, and interpret past success?
