Consider five distinct formulations of the demandingness objection to act consequentialism: (A) the direct-cost formulation, which emphasizes the forfeiture of discretionary resources and ordinary pleasures; (B) the alienation formulation, which emphasizes the erosion of personal relationships and emotional attachments; (C) the integrity formulation, which emphasizes conflict with an agent's core personal projects and commitments; (D) the motivational formulation, which emphasizes psychological unsustainability and the risk of moral burnout; and (E) the recursive-demand formulation, which holds that satisfying any given consequentialist demand necessarily reveals a further, structurally similar demand of comparable weight, generating an unending regress of obligation rather than a stable stopping point.

Rank these five formulations in order of dialectical strength against the standard consequentialist reply that agents need only bear costs that are not overly burdensome relative to the good achieved. Assign each a tier—strong, moderate, or weak—and provide a single sentence justifying its placement based on whether the formulation targets a feature that consequentialism can accommodate without theoretical revision. Then, in one paragraph, identify which formulation, if any, retains its force even against a satisficing version of consequentialism that explicitly builds in a stopping rule, and explain which structural feature of that formulation allows it to survive where the others collapse.
