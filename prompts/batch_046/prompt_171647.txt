I have been working through my judgments about phenomenal consciousness in order to see whether they cohere around a single principle, likely some variant of a higher-order representation theory, and I am finding that at least two of my verdicts sit in tension. Here are six concrete cases with my intuitions stated plainly.

1. A typical awake human who not only sees a red apple but can also think about her seeing it. Verdict: unambiguously conscious, and the reflexive layer seems essential to the richness of the experience rather than a mere epiphenomenal add-on.

2. A patient in a persistent vegetative state with isolated cortical islands that process visual stimuli locally but exhibit no integrated global activation and no reportability. Verdict: not conscious. Local first-order processing may occur, but without global integration there is no subject of experience.

3. A blindsight patient who can navigate obstacles with high reliability while sincerely reporting no visual experience of them. Verdict: the navigational capacities are not accompanied by consciousness. There is first-order sensory registration, yet it lacks the kind of higher-order availability that would make the registration "for" the subject.

4. A hypothetical "bifurcated monitor": a single brain in which the left and right hemispheres each maintain complete higher-order representations of their own first-order states, but neither represents the states of the other, and there is no global workspace linking them. Behaviorally the organism acts as one, but the monitoring is strictly modular. Verdict: I am inclined to say there are two conscious subjects rather than one unified subject, though I feel uneasy about denying that there is "someone home" altogether.

5. A locked-in patient with intact cortical integration and global self-monitoring, able to communicate only via eye movements, who reports a fully intact inner life. Verdict: fully conscious. Scarcity of behavioral output does not diminish consciousness, provided the global higher-order architecture remains intact.

6. A synthetic system—an artificial architecture with an explicit meta-representational layer that tracks and integrates all of its first-order computational states across modules, functioning analogously to a global workspace, but implemented in silicon without biological substrate or evolutionary history. Verdict: I think it is not conscious, despite possessing the structural analog of the higher-order integration I require in cases 1 and 5.

My questions are these. First, is there a coherent principle—perhaps a refined higher-order account, or a hybrid view—that can deliver all six verdicts without ad hoc adjustments? If so, please state it precisely, indicate which clause is motivated by which case above, and tell me what it predicts for two further cases: (a) an octopus with distributed neural monitoring in each arm but only weak central integration, and (b) a human in deep slow-wave sleep with no dream report but preserved global cortical connectivity. Second, if no single principle saves every verdict, I want an explicit statement of which verdict must be overturned and why, rather than a complicated principle with exception clauses. In particular, address whether my denial of consciousness in case 6 is consistent with my affirmation in case 5, given that both appear to possess global higher-order availability, or whether I should instead concede consciousness to the synthetic system (or deny it to the locked-in patient). Please do not offer bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; I am interested solely in the conceptual structure.
