After a lengthy and somewhat heated discussion last evening with a close friend who studies early analytic philosophy, I find myself unable to resolve a particular tension in how we should read G. E. Moore's treatment of intrinsic value across his ethical and aesthetic writings. We were specifically debating whether Moore's well-known account of friendship and personal affection in Principia Ethica can be coherently integrated with his theory of organic unity from his aesthetic discussions, or whether doing so imposes an anachronistic systematicity on texts that were never intended to cohere in that way. Rather than continuing to talk past each other, I would like us to produce a formal adversarial-collaboration document that treats this as a dispute between two interpretive camps—those who defend a Holist Reading and those who defend a Separatist Reading—so that we can identify exactly where we agree, where we genuinely diverge, and what evidence would actually move either of us.

The Holist Reading maintains that when Moore identifies friendship as among the greatest intrinsic goods, he is implicitly relying on the same structural logic he uses for beautiful objects: the value of the complex is not the sum of the values of its constituent mental states, pleasures, or virtues, but rather an emergent property of the relational organization among them, including what we might call diachronic organic unity—the way value is constituted across temporal phases of trust, rupture, and reconciliation rather than being statically present at any given moment. The Separatist Reading, by contrast, insists that Moore's ethical method of isolation is designed precisely to break complex goods into their value-bearing parts, that friendship's intrinsic value is therefore calculable as a compound of pleasure, virtue, and aesthetic appreciation taken together, and that importing the concept of organic unity from aesthetics into ethics conflates two domains governed by different methodological constraints in Moore's work.

Structure it with a numbered section of agreed claims—about the text of Principia Ethica, about what Moore's method of isolation actually requires, about whether Moore ever explicitly applies organic unity to interpersonal goods, and about the formal features both sides acknowledge in his aesthetic theory—followed by a table or clearly demarcated section stating the precise disagreements in wording that each camp would accept for its own position. Then identify the cruxes: I suspect one concerns whether relational properties between mental states can themselves be bearers of intrinsic value in Moore's framework, and another concerns whether diachronic structural features of long-term friendship constitute a distinct kind of good or merely a distribution of synchronic goods over time. For each crux, propose a concrete comparative case—such as evaluating the intrinsic value of a thirty-year friendship characterized by one major betrayal and subsequent reconciliation versus an otherwise identical thirty-year friendship without such rupture—specifying in advance what prediction each camp would make about whether the two cases differ in total intrinsic value, and why.
