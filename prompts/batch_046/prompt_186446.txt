I'm working through a reconstruction of Williams's integrity objection that now seems to prove far too much. (1) For Williams, integrity is sustained by ground projects — commitments so central that life without them is not worth living. (2) If an agent holds two ground projects that could conflict, then in any actual conflict she must abandon one, thereby destroying the very integrity she sought to preserve; but even absent conflict, she lives with the structural possibility of such sacrifice. (3) The phenomenon Williams describes as having one thought too many shows that bringing a ground project into reflective comparison with alternatives — weighing whether to save a spouse or a stranger — introduces a distance that already undermines the unreflective attachment constitutive of the project. (4) So possessing more than one ground project, or holding even one in a manner that permits comparative practical reasoning, places the agent in a state where integrity is compromised regardless of outcome. (5) Therefore genuine integrity requires exactly one ground project held with absolute, non-comparative priority — which collapses into a form of moral fanaticism and denies the plural, revisable commitments Williams himself allows. (6) Hence either Williams must deny that agents can have plural ground projects, or he must accept that ordinary moral deliberation routinely destroys integrity. Where does the reasoning slip? If I want to preserve both the non-negotiable character of ground projects and the possibility of plural commitments without fanaticism, which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? Please separate what is settled in the literature on Williams from what is genuinely contested — for instance, whether ground projects must be singular, or whether the one-thought-too-many worry applies beyond intimate relationships to comparative practical reasoning generally — and do not present a contested reading as the consensus view.
