I am convening a philosophy of mind reading group for doctoral students in linguistics and cognitive science who have not studied philosophy of mind since their undergraduate survey course. Over two ninety-minute sessions, I want them to understand, and be able to reconstruct, the argument attributed to Chase and Sanborn that qualia are not merely eliminable or epiphenomenal but incoherent as a theoretical posit — defective in something like the way "square circle" is defective. Do not explain the argument to me; design the teaching plan.

Set out the exact dependency order: what conceptual ground must be secured before the term "quining" is introduced; what distinction must be in place before they can see why phenomenal character, rather than consciousness generally, is the specific target; and precisely where in the two-session arc the difference between incoherence and eliminative materialism must be locked down, so they do not collapse the one into the other.

Select one carrying example or thought experiment that will bear the explanatory weight throughout — it must not be the inverted-spectrum scenario, and it must be constructed so that the incoherence is visible in the structure of the posit rather than merely surprising in its conclusion. Specify what properties the example needs to have and what it must lack (for instance, whether it should involve first-person access, behavioural absence, or relational contradiction).

Then map the predictable misconceptions: I expect they will conflate quining with eliminative materialism; treat incoherence as an epistemic limitation ("we simply lack access"); and deploy the zombie intuition as an objection without recognising that it presupposes the coherence of the very concept under attack. For each, indicate the exact point in the sequence where it should be intercepted and the specific move — a distinction, a question, or a restructuring of the example — that heads it off.

Propose one diagnostic question for the end of the second session that reveals whether a student has grasped the incoherence claim specifically, rather than merely memorised the slogan. Finally, state explicitly which parts of the broader phenomenal-concept literature — direct-reference theories, the transparency thesis, acquaintance-based accounts — should be left out and named as out of scope, and explain why admitting them would undermine the argument's structure.
