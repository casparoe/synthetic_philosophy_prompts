I am preparing material for a course on institutional design and democratic theory, and I want a piece of fiction that thinks through a problem rather than illustrating it. Imagine a polity — let us call it Alecta — in which every seat in the legislative Assembly is filled annually by pure sortition, with a constitutional bar on any individual serving more than once in a lifetime. On paper this eliminates career politicians entirely; in practice, however, a hidden continuity has emerged. Because the pool of citizens is smaller than it appears, and because administrative expertise cannot be replaced by lot, a shadow caste of “permanent citizens” has learned to circulate through forged identity papers, statistical clustering, and the informal inheritance of office from parent to child under new legal names. I would like you to write a short story set in this world, centred on a woman named Elara who has just been drawn for her first and only term, only to discover that her assigned mentor — a revered elder statesman who claims never to have served before — is in fact a careerist who has occupied the same advisory chair for thirty years beneath a sequence of false identities.

The story should not be a simple morality tale about corruption. Rather, I want it to explore the conceptual tension between randomness and repetition: does the prohibition on re-selection genuinely interrupt the accumulation of power, or does it merely force that accumulation into more opaque forms — forged biographies, inherited institutional memory, and the quiet professionalisation of the “amateur” legislator? Show Elara’s realisation unfolding through concrete scenes — perhaps a filing room of duplicate birth records, or a debate in which her mentor anticipates every procedural objection with the fluency of long practice — and let the narrative ask whether term limits, in the absence of transparency, protect democracy or simply launder careerism through anonymity. Please keep the philosophical question alive inside the characters’ choices rather than resolving it in an authorial summary.
