Please review the following outline and partial draft for an essay on heterophenomenology as a method in consciousness science, and offer critical comments on its coherence, its fidelity to Dennett’s framework, and whether the proposed extension is genuinely novel or merely restates existing third-person modeling practices. The essay notes are below.

Dennett’s heterophenomenology treats subjects’ first-person reports not as direct windows onto qualia but as texts that define a heterophenomenological world—the world as it is experienced by the subject—without committing the scientist to the reality of every feature reported within it. This method is traditionally restricted to linguistically competent human subjects who can articulate their experiences. I want to argue that the same methodological stance can be applied, in modified form, to non-linguistic animals and possibly to artificial systems, but only if we introduce what I call proxy-world construction.

In proxy-world construction, the scientist does not solicit a verbal report; instead, she builds a predictive-processing model of the subject’s behavior (for example, a model of a bird’s visual navigation or a rodent’s spatial decision-making) and treats the model’s internal representations as defining a heterophenomenological world for that subject. The key move is that we remain agnostic about whether those internal representations correspond to real phenomenal properties, just as we remain agnostic about whether a human subject’s report of a red afterimage corresponds to a real mental paint sample. The difference is that the report is now the model’s best predictive interpretation of behavior, not the subject’s verbal testimony.

This raises an immediate tension: if the proxy-world is constructed entirely by the scientist’s model, has heterophenomenology collapsed into ordinary third-person neuroscience? I suggest not, because the predictive model is constrained by the subject’s own behavioral economy—its successes and failures—rather than by the scientist’s theoretical preferences. The subject’s world is still authored, in part, by the subject’s interactions, even if it is articulated by the model. The niche claim is that this yields a silent heterophenomenology, extending Dennett’s ontological neutrality to domains where there are no words, using predictive modeling as an interpretive prosthesis rather than a replacement for the subject’s voice.

However, I worry that this blurs the line Dennett draws between heterophenomenology and autophenomenology. If the model is doing all the interpretive work, we may be projecting a world onto the subject rather than receiving one from them. I want to explore whether this is fatal, or whether it reveals that all heterophenomenological worlds—including human ones—are partly model-dependent constructions shaped by the interpreter’s framework.

In your response, please separate what is settled about Dennett’s original heterophenomenology—particularly its treatment of first-person authority and its deliberate neutrality about ontology—from what is genuinely contested, such as its applicability beyond verbal reports, its relationship to predictive processing theories in cognitive science, and whether extending it to non-linguistic subjects preserves its methodological neutrality or transforms it into a form of interpretivism about consciousness. Do not present a contested interpretation (for instance, that heterophenomenology is essentially behaviorism in phenomenological clothing, or that it necessarily entails eliminativism about qualia) as if it were the consensus view among philosophers of mind and cognitive scientists. Please also flag any points where my outline relies on a specific, debatable reading of Dennett’s Consciousness Explained (1991) or his later work, and indicate where the proposed proxy-world idea risks being either trivial—merely standard modeling practice redescribed—or incoherent, particularly with respect to the distinction between constructing a world for a subject and attributing experiences to it.
