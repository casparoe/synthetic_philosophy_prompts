Read Christopher Nolan’s film *Dunkirk* (2017) through the lens of political mythology and the philosophy of “lost causes”—specifically the comparative pattern seen in the Masada narrative, the Kosovo myth of the 1389 battle, and the American Lost Cause of the Confederacy, in which strategic defeat or retreat is retrospectively transformed into a sacrificial foundation of collective identity. I want a reading that takes both the film and the conceptual framework seriously. How does *Dunkirk* construct what might be called a “negative foundation”: a national myth built not on conquest or victory but on the bare fact of survival and evacuation? Does the film’s refusal to show the enemy—the Wehrmacht is entirely absent, the Luftwaffe faceless and mechanical—serve the same mnemonic function as the absent victor in other lost-cause myths, allowing defeat to be rewritten as moral endurance? And where does the film resist this template: does Nolan’s fractured chronology, the absence of a singular heroic protagonist, and the emphasis on bureaucratic contingency (the ticking clock, the tides, the fuel gauge) undermine the sacrificial coherence that myths like Masada require? In particular, consider whether the “small boats” sequence dramatizes a shift from state-centered heroism to civil-society rescue, and whether that shift complicates the standard lost-cause narrative of a betrayed but noble military elite. Finally, say what the film adds to the philosophical debate about national myths that abstract analysis of Masada or Kosovo does not—for instance, whether visual and temporal fragmentation can itself be a form of mythic resistance, or whether it merely aestheticizes the same retrospective redemption.
