In several East Asian medical systems, a widespread institutional practice allows physicians to disclose a grave diagnosis first to designated family members rather than to the patient, letting the family decide whether and how to inform the patient; this is often formalized in hospital ethics protocols under labels such as family-centered disclosure. Yet the same legal and institutional frameworks maintain strict individual-informed-consent requirements for clinical trial enrollment and for invasive procedures once the patient is aware, treating the patient as an autonomous rights-bearer whose personal authorization is necessary and sufficient. The two practices sit side by side: relational mediation of bad news, but atomistic authorization of bodily risk.
