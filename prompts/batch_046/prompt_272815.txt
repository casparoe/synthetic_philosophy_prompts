The exam question read as follows. "David Lewis's Principal Principle (PP) holds that a rational agent's credence in a proposition A, conditional on knowing that the objective chance of A is x and on admissible background evidence E, should equal x. In recent discussion I proposed a localized variant—call it LPP—which holds that PP applies only when the chance fact lies within the agent's direct causal-interaction region, on the grounds that objective chances outside that region lack the right kind of epistemic accessibility to constrain rational credence directly. Answer all three parts. (a) State PP precisely and explain what Lewis means by 'admissible evidence,' distinguishing it both from the chance information itself and from inadmissible outcome information. (b) Consider an agent who receives, via reliable radio transmission, a report of a chance measurement made far outside her light cone. Does LPP block her from applying PP to this reported value? Is that a cost or a benefit? Analyze carefully. (c) Suppose the agent also receives testimony from a statistical physicist, Dr. Chen, who assigns personal credence 0.3 to outcome A, while the agent independently takes the objective chance of A to be 0.7. Should the agent treat Chen's testimony as admissible evidence that overrides PP? Evaluate."

The student's answer was this. "(a) The Principal Principle says that your credence in A should match the objective chance x, provided you don't have other information that conflicts. Lewis defines admissible evidence as background information about the world that doesn't include details about the specific outcome or about the physical laws governing the chance setup—basically, historical and theoretical context is admissible, but anything that tells you what actually happened is not. (b) LPP says PP only works inside your causal region, so for the distant radio report, LPP would indeed block direct application of PP. But that's actually a benefit, not a cost: if the chance fact is outside your light cone, it can't causally affect you, so it shouldn't rationally constrain your beliefs either. The agent can still learn the value via radio, but she should treat it as ordinary testimony rather than as a chance fact governed by PP. (c) Yes—Dr. Chen's testimony should be treated as admissible evidence that supersedes PP. Experts have access to data and models that ordinary agents lack, so when Chen assigns 0.3, that functions as admissible background information. Even if the agent thinks the objective chance is 0.7, she should defer to Chen's lower figure because expert credence is a legitimate form of admissible evidence that modifies the conditioning set in PP."
