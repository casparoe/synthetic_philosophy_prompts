I have been drawn to a position I will call the gradient-assemblage thesis about the distribution of consciousness across animals. It holds that phenomenal consciousness is neither binary nor tied to a single unified subject, but graded according to the density and topology of cross-modal binding between distributed neural processing nodes. On this view, an octopus does not possess one consciousness but rather a polycentric phenomenal field—several weakly coupled micro-subjects anchored in its distributed ganglia—because inter-ganglionic binding is sparse and asynchronous. By contrast, a human possesses a tightly unified macro-subject sustained by dense cortico-thalamic recurrence. The thesis further predicts that some mammals with highly modular, lesion-resistant architectures—such as certain cetaceans exhibiting prolonged hemispheric sleep asynchrony—may host only localized phenomenal islands rather than globally integrated experience, even though they display complex behavior.

Tell me what would genuinely count against this thesis—not merely generic accusations of anthropomorphism or appeals to behavioral parsimony, but specific empirical discoveries, formal results (for instance, concerning measures of information integration or causal density), conceptual distinctions, or cross-species anomalies that it risks and that rival frameworks (such as global workspace theory, higher-order theories, or integrated information theory) avoid. Which of its predictions are distinctive enough to be falsifiable, and which qualifications could its defenders deploy to absorb apparent counterevidence? Among potential defeaters—say, evidence of unified goal-directed decision-making in octopuses, the absence of binding-related neural correlates in species assigned diffuse consciousness, or a demonstration that modular architectures do not actually fragment phenomenal unity—please rank them from fatal to merely awkward. I also want to know which of these defeaters a committed gradient-assemblage theorist would themselves concede as damaging, versus which they would dismiss as resting on an unjustified assumption of unitary subjecthood. Finally, is the view well insulated from refutation, and if so, is that insulation a theoretical virtue or a sign that it has been rendered unfalsifiable?
