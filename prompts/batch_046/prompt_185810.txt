Hey, so I've been thinking about this idea I heard somewhere called the handicap principle — like peacocks grow heavy tails just to prove they can still survive. There's also costly signaling in economics where you burn resources to show you're legit. I'm a software engineer with basically no philosphy background, but I keep seeing this play out in tech culture and I can't figure out the actual pattern behind my gut feelings.

Here are my verdicts. I want to know if there's one principle that captures all of them, or if I'm just being inconsistent.

1. A founder lives on ramen and works 80-hour weeks with no salary for years. That's real commitment, legit signal.
2. A senior engineer refuses autocomplete, syntax highlighting, or a debugger, writing everything in bare vim. They say it "keeps them sharp". To me this feels like a handicap — they're making their own job harder to show they can take it. But I also think it's kinda pretentious gatekeeping. Not sure.
3. A company spends millions on a fancy office with slides and nap pods that actually distract people. That feels like signaling to investors, but it's not a handicap on the individuals, it's just waste.
4. Someone takes a 50% pay cut to work at a nonprofit on climate software. That's legit — they're burning actual money to show values.
5. A dev writes all internal docs in raw LaTeX instead of Notion or Markdown, making updates 10x slower. They claim it's "for rigor". This feels like an artificial handicap — they're manufactering difficulty that doesn't prove anything real.
6. Maintaining a legacy COBOL system that everyone hates, with no glory, just because the company needs it. That's a real burden, and doing it signals loyalty and skill. Legit.
7. Posting grindset threads on Twitter about 20-hour days, but actually using AI and outsourcing everything. That's cheap talk, not costly. Not legit.
8. Choosing to deploy software without CI/CD, doing every build and test by hand "to stay close to the metal". This is the one I keep going back and forth on. It's definitely making work harder and riskier. Is that a real handicap that proves mastery, or is it just reckless performative suffering — like a productivity-theater handicap?

So what's the deal? Is there a coherent principle — maybe about visibility, about who bears the cost, or whether the burden is necessary vs manufactered — that gets all of these? Or do I have to drop one of my verdicts?

Also, what would your principle say about this case I haven't listed: a remote team that insists on keeping cameras on for every meeting, even though it causes fatigue and makes people perform busyness instead of working? Is that a handicap signal from management, or something else?

Please don't just list philosophers I should read — I can't look them up right now and I wouldn't understand them anyway. Just tell me the principle, if there is one, and be honest about which of my gut calls it can't save.
