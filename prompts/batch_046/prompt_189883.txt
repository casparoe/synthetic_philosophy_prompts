In a graduate seminar on comparative metaphysics that I have been running for advanced students, we have reached an impasse over the Buddhist doctrine of anattā that has lasted the entire term. The dispute is not about the canonical evidence—everyone accepts that the Pāli texts reject a permanent, unchanging ātman—but about what kind of rejection this is. One faction argues that anattā entails the complete denial of diachronic personal identity: there are only momentary skandhas, and the conventional "I" is a grammatical fiction with no ontological weight. The opposing faction insists that Buddhism permits a conventional, narrative self constituted by causal and psychological continuity, and that anattā targets only an ultimate, metaphysically substantial substrate, not functional identity over time. Both sides have mapped every relevant passage onto their framework, and the arguments have become perfectly circular: each accuses the other of importing Western substance metaphysics, either by denying continuity altogether or by smuggling a substrate back in under the label "convention."

My working hypothesis is that the stall is not primarily about Buddhist exegesis but about a hidden disagreement over what I have come to call the diachronic indexical problem—whether first-person reference across time requires a persisting referent, or whether a series of causally linked, momentary indexicals can do all the work without one. The reductionists treat the indexical as always momentary and therefore dispensable for identity, while the moderates treat it as anchored in a continuous narrative thread. But I am unsure whether this diagnosis is correct, or whether the disagreement is actually verbal—a dispute about the word "self" that masks two different projects, one soteriological (liberation from clinging, or upādāna) and one ontological (what exists). I want a diagnosis with reasons: is there a hidden empirical or formal question about personal identity doing the work? Are the sides answering different questions under the single heading "anattā"? Is the impasse a sign that the question is ill-posed—perhaps because "personal identity" is a Western category that does not map cleanly onto Buddhist concerns about attachment—or is it a genuinely hard metaphysical problem?

Given your diagnosis, I also want to know what, if anything, would count as progress. Would a precise conceptual distinction—for instance, separating phenomenological binding from metaphysical ownership—dissolve the dispute, or would it merely relocate it? Could a formal model of diachronic identity without substance adjudicate the matter, or is the disagreement ultimately about bedrock intuitions about naturalness and continuity that neither side can argue for? Please do not survey interpretations of anattā; engage the structure of the disagreement itself, and be honest about whether you think it can be moved, and if not, why the stall is permanent.
