I'm serving on my university's Student Conduct Appeals Panel while enrolled in an upper-division seminar on moral responsibility. We face a consolidated appeal that directly instantiates an issue we've discussed in class—whether outcomes that lie outside an agent's control should affect institutional blame—though I want the analysis to proceed from reasoning rather than from citation or authority. The case concerns two graduate students, M. and K., who each independently committed the same intentional breach of a shared laboratory safety protocol: both deliberately disabled a redundant lockout mechanism to expedite an experiment, knowing this violated explicit rules but believing (incorrectly) that they could manage the risk. Their mental states, voluntary actions, and knowledge of the protocol were materially identical. In M.'s case, however, an unforeseeable ventilation-system failure—entirely outside M.'s knowledge or control—allowed a chemical release that destroyed equipment and injured a technician; in K.'s case, the identical breach was intercepted by a routine inspection before any harm occurred. Our current disciplinary code calibrates sanctions partly by resulting harm, so M. faces suspension and loss of funding eligibility, while K. receives only a formal reprimand and mandatory retraining.

A recently proposed student-government amendment—the Uniform Culpability Proposal—would require that sanctions in intentional-protocol violations depend solely on what was within the agent's control: intent, knowledge, and the voluntary action itself, excluding outcomes that depend on external contingency. The panel must decide whether to uphold the differential sanctions in this specific consolidated appeal, and whether to recommend adoption of the amendment for future cases.

Please draft a formal policy memo addressed to the panel chair. The memo should include: (1) a precise recommendation on both the immediate appeal and the proposed amendment; (2) reasoning that gives intellectually honest weight to the strongest consideration favoring the opposing view—specifically, the institutional obligations to acknowledge real harm to victims, maintain credible deterrence, and preserve community trust; (3) an assessment of whether our current practice of outcome-scaled sanctioning actually presupposes a specific theory of moral responsibility (for example, that blameworthiness tracks results) or whether the practice is philosophically underdetermined and compatible with several rival views; and (4) a concrete implementation note describing exactly what the university's disciplinary framework—its code language, review board procedures, or appeals criteria—would need to change if the recommendation is adopted. Write this as an actionable institutional document, not as personal guidance to me, and avoid any request for bibliographies, reading lists, or citations.
