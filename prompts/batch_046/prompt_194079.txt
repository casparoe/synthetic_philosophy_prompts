I'm drafting the final examination for an upper-level undergraduate course in moral philosophy, and I need an essay question that students cannot answer merely by reproducing the standard summary of Republic Book II. In a recent conversation with a colleague, we devised a conceptual variant of the Gyges myth to serve as the exam's framing device: rather than a ring that renders its wearer invisible to others, imagine a "reverse ring" that makes the wearer's mental states—intentions, emotional responses, and practical reasoning—radically transparent to every other person, while leaving the wearer epistemically blind to theirs. This produces a condition of one-way exposure rather than concealment, and it should allow students to test whether Glaucon's challenge—that justice is valued only for its reputational and instrumental benefits—depends essentially on an asymmetry of knowledge that this reversal either undermines or transforms.

Please design a 90-minute exam essay prompt that uses this "reverse ring" scenario to ask students whether Glaucon's psychological claim about human motivation survives when strategic injustice is not hidden but mutually exposed. The question should require them to distinguish intrinsic from instrumental reasons for moral conduct, to assess whether perfect transparency eliminates the possibility of unjust free-riding, and to evaluate whether the reversal reveals a hidden commitment in Glaucon's argument to a particular theory of moral motivation—specifically, one in which the only barrier to injustice is the risk of detection. The exam should be calibrated for roughly 800 words of response.

Along with the exam question itself, provide a model answer of approximately 600 words and a marking scheme totaling 25 marks. The scheme should list at least five specific argumentative moves that a top answer must contain, assign point values to each, and explicitly distinguish an A-range answer from a B-range answer by naming which of those moves are present in the former and which are typically missing in the latter. Finally, identify one common misconception—such as conflating transparency with altruistic motivation or treating the reversal as automatically refuting Glaucon without analyzing the structure of his challenge—that should result in a clear deduction of marks.
