Brexit in 2016 also feels wrong, though differently: it was a simplistic yes/no on an enormously complex, largely irreversible decision, with no real implementation plan attached and a margin too narrow to justify the upheaval. By contrast, California's Proposition 22 in 2020, letting app-based drivers remain independent contractors, feels legitimate—just people setting an economic rule that applies broadly. The 2016 Swiss referendum that rejected universal basic income feels legitimate too; voters weighed costs and benefits and said no. Even California's Proposition 13 in 1978, capping property taxes, seems okay in principle, even if I grant it caused strange long-term distortions. So my tentative principle is that direct democracy is acceptable when the question is mainly about distributing resources or setting practical rules that apply to everyone more or less equally, but it fails when it either singles out a specific minority group or forces a binary choice on a technically complex, irreversible issue.
