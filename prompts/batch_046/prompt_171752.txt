I'm preparing material for a graduate metaphysics-of-science seminar on whether the sciences carve nature at its joints, and I want an instrument that sorts actual commitments rather than labels. Please construct a decision tree that begins with the minimal shared claim—that there are stable, projectible patterns in nature—and then branches only on questions that genuinely separate live positions. The first split should be between mind-independent structures and practice-dependent classifications. From there, one branch should distinguish causal-essence essentialism (induction succeeds because nature contains hidden necessary properties) from homeostatic property-cluster accounts (induction succeeds because causal mechanisms stabilize observable properties), and that cluster branch should further split on whether biological kinds are maintained by the same kind of mechanism as chemical kinds, with one leaf noting the awkward commitment of extending cluster realism to species despite evolutionary turnover. A parallel branch should interrogate the carving metaphor itself: does joint-carving require that nature have a unique, non-overlapping taxonomy, or can there be multiple valid divisions of the same region? Here I want you to inject a distinction I’m calling “interventionist joints”—patterns that stabilize only under specific experimental manipulations and therefore depend on our interaction with the world without being merely projected—so that one leaf captures the view that some joints are real but revealed only through intervention, and please flag whether that position collapses into standard pluralism or stands apart.

Another branch must isolate the biological case specifically: separate phylogenetic essentialism about species, the “species-as-individuals” thesis, and eliminative nominalism, asking at each node about whether evolutionary lineages have intrinsic boundaries or are merely convenient groupings for prediction. Every leaf should be named—scientific essentialism, homeostatic property cluster theory, promiscuous realism, interventionist pluralism, species individualism, eliminative nominalism, or “unnamed” if the logical space is unoccupied—followed by one sentence describing its most awkward commitment, such as whether it must deny ontological priority to physics, or whether it forces the holder to treat every successful taxonomy as revealing a mind-independent joint. Where two named positions share every branch—if they differ only in rhetoric or historical association—please flag that the tree cannot separate them rather than inventing a spurious distinction. The goal is for a student to trace her answers about patterns, induction, uniqueness, biological taxonomy, and the role of experimental intervention, and arrive at a position she actually holds, not one she has read about.
