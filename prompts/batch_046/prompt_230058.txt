In a recent philosophy discussion thread, someone offered the following summary of Bradley's regress and his account of propositional unity. They begin by noting that a judgment such as a being related to b seems to require not only the terms a and b and the relation R, but also some further binding that connects R to a and to b. Calling that binding R1, they observe that R1 itself must be bound to R and to a, generating an infinite regress. To stop this, Bradley allegedly concludes that relations are not genuinely real features of the world but are appearances generated by the mind's analysis of an originally undifferentiated whole. The poster then claims that Bradley treats propositions themselves as mental acts in which subject and predicate are inseparable, so that propositional unity is secured not by any external connection but by the internal coherence of the experiencing subject. From this they infer that Bradley's argument anticipates modern coherentist theories of truth. They also assert that the regress applies with equal force to logical relations and to causal relations, so that any attempt to construct reality out of discrete parts is doomed regardless of the relation type.

How fair is this summary? Please examine it point by point: distinguish what accurately reflects Bradley's reasoning about relational thought and the Absolute, what is a defensible simplification, and what misrepresents his view—especially regarding whether relations are merely mental, whether propositions are mental acts, and whether the regress operates uniformly across logical and causal domains. Then evaluate whether the move to coherentism is supported by the accurate parts, and whether there is anything in Bradley's own position that blocks applying the regress indiscriminately.
