Having spent thirty years in experimental physics—mostly calibrating interferometric instruments and quantifying systematic error—I have recently turned to philosophy of science and probability. In my laboratory work, we treated any measurement with a known statistical distribution as requiring a proportional adjustment in our confidence: if a detector's noise profile implied a seventy percent likelihood of a particular spectral feature, we calibrated our downstream models to weight that feature accordingly. This practice seems structurally identical to David Lewis's Principal Principle, which, as I understand it, instructs rational agents to align their subjective credences with whatever objective chance distribution governs the relevant physical process. I therefore find myself wondering whether the Principal Principle is best interpreted not as a purely epistemic norm, but as a generalized metrological protocol—a rule for tuning belief to physical stochasticity in the same way we tune detectors to signal variance.

My specific puzzle concerns deterministic interpretations of quantum mechanics. In Bohmian mechanics, the wavefunction evolves deterministically, and the Born-rule probabilities are typically explained as arising from epistemic ignorance of precise initial conditions rather than from irreducible physical chance. If the Principal Principle applies strictly to objective, irreducible chances, then it appears that in a Bohmian world there are no chances to which belief ought to be deferred. Would this entail that a fully rational agent, upon accepting Bohmian mechanics, should treat all quantum predictions as maximally uncertain—abandoning the Born rule entirely in her credences? Or does the principle extend even to probabilities that are merely epistemic? I suspect my reasoning conflates instrumental calibration with doxastic deference, but I cannot pinpoint where the analogy fails. Could you clarify whether there is a coherent distinction between calibrating belief to objective chance and calibrating an instrument to statistical noise, particularly when the underlying dynamics are fully deterministic? I am especially interested in whether the idea of "metrological deference"—that rational belief-formation should mirror the error-correction procedures of physical measurement—can be made coherent, or whether it rests on a category mistake I have not yet identified.
