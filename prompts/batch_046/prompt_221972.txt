As a journalist who covers science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, I am commissioning a narrative feature that uses fiction to explore a classical logical paradox. Write a short story that takes as its conceptual spine the School of Names' white-horse dialogue—the argument that a white horse is not a horse—without turning it into a simple allegory. Set the story in a present-day natural-history archive, where a senior cataloguer is forced to classify an anomalous specimen that meets every criterion for membership in a designated species except the single criterion the taxonomy treats as essential, causing the institution's automated classification system to oscillate indefinitely between inclusion and exclusion. To prevent the narrative from falling back on well-worn tropes, introduce an invented but philosophically rigorous element: the cataloguer uncovers a forgotten 1970s indexing protocol that defines a category by the shared absence of properties among its members—a kind of inverted universal—and recognizes that this protocol reproduces the logic of the white-horse argument from the opposite direction. Make the paradox a practical crisis rather than an intellectual ornament: show the physical specimen, the database interface, the institutional pressure to close the record, and the cataloguer's own professional identity hanging on the decision. The story should not resolve the tension with a moral or an explanation; end on an image that leaves the classification suspended. The prose should be precise, restrained, and accessible to readers who have no background in classical Chinese philosophy or formal logic, yet faithful to the conceptual difficulty at stake.
