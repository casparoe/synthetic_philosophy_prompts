You are a professional philosopher specializing in decision theory, philosophy of history, and longtermism. Before constructing the case, briefly brainstorm the three structural conditions that must hold for a hinge-of-history feature to be isolable from ordinary risk aversion, discount-rate disagreement, or divergence in moral values. Task: Construct a thought experiment — a single, realistic scenario of no more than one paragraph — in which a decision-maker (for example, a national standards board, a clinical-funding committee, or an infrastructure-regulatory agency) must choose whether to take an action that opens a temporary coordination window for an open institutional or technical standard. The action carries an immediate, quantifiable risk of serious concrete harm (not existential catastrophe, but substantial economic, organizational, or public-health damage), yet if it succeeds, it locks in a path-dependent trajectory whose long-run consequences are vastly larger than the immediate stakes; if rejected, a competing closed standard achieves network-effect dominance and reversal becomes practically impossible thereafter. Engineer the case so that:

1. A standard intertemporal expected-utility framework with uniform temporal weighting — identical risk preferences and value functions, no special hinge multiplier — recommends against taking the action, because the immediate expected cost exceeds the normally discounted long-run benefit.
2. A hinge-sensitive decision framework — one that applies a non-uniform temporal weight to outcomes occurring during periods characterized by irreversible path-dependent lock-in — recommends taking the action.
3. The disagreement collapses if and only if the temporary coordination window is removed, that is, if reversal of the standard remains practically possible regardless of the present choice.

Constraints: The setting must be realistic (a workplace, hospital, government agency, or industry board — not science fiction). Do not request or include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations. After presenting the scenario, walk through the reasoning for each framework, identify precisely which structural feature of the case does the isolating work, explain what happens when that feature is removed, and provide an honest assessment of how a defender of the standard framework would attack the case (for instance, by denying that the present moment genuinely satisfies the hinge conditions, or by arguing that the alleged irreversibility is overstated). Keep the scenario itself to one paragraph; the analysis may extend further.
