As a retired physicist who spent decades studying iterative dynamical systems with feedback loops, I have recently begun reading philosophy of decision theory and find myself struck by the peculiar state of the literature on the backward induction paradox in the centipede game. The formal structure is elegant: with common knowledge of rationality and perfect transparency of the payoff tree, rational agents should exit at the first node, leaving nearly all cooperative surplus unrealized. Yet this result conflicts with both empirical observation and strong intuitive judgment. For decades, the discussion has circulated among three familiar camps—those who accept backward induction as a normative demonstration of strategic foresight, those who treat it as a reductio of the common-knowledge-of-rationality premise, and those who claim the paradox dissolves once we index rational assessment to temporal stages rather than static game trees—without any clear convergence.

What puzzles me is whether these camps are genuinely disagreeing about a single proposition, or whether they are addressing different questions under one heading. The formalists appear to defend a model of perfect strategic transparency in which every agent's reasoning is fully accessible across all iterations. The critics appear to defend a claim about bounded human cognition or about the normative impropriety of leaving cooperative gains unclaimed. The dissolutionists appear to be making a meta-methodological point about how decision rules should be applied over time. Are these substantive disagreements about rationality itself, verbal disagreements about the word rationality, or taxonomic confusions arising from mapping psychological, normative, and formal concepts onto a single tree?

More specifically, I wonder whether part of the stall comes from treating backward induction as one reasoning procedure when it actually comprises two distinct mechanisms: recursive elimination of dominated strategies, and forward-looking anticipation of mutual reasoning. In physical iterative systems, recursive elimination often fails when measurement noise or asymmetry breaks perfect information symmetry; analogously, strategic reasoning might require what I would call epistemic friction—a minimal opacity in belief attribution—that the standard common-knowledge framework artificially removes. If so, is the philosophical debate ignoring a formal possibility: that backward induction holds only in the limit of zero epistemic friction, and that real or even idealized rational agents operate with nonzero friction?

Given this framing, I ask: is the impasse substantive, verbal, or taxonomic? Do the sides differ about facts, about standards of evidence for what counts as data in decision theory, about which cases are central, or about starting intuitions—such as whether a rational agent may ever abandon a cooperative surplus—that neither side can justify neutrally? Is there a hidden empirical question about whether humans can maintain common knowledge under iterative feedback, or a hidden formal question about whether relaxation of common-knowledge axioms blocks the induction without collapsing rationality? Would progress require an experimental result on human play, a formal result on induction failure under belief-opacity conditions, a conceptual distinction separating normative rationality from predictive rationality, or an argument that the question is ill-posed? And does my proposed distinction between recursive elimination and forward-looking anticipation—together with the analogy to epistemic friction in physical systems—clarify the disagreement, or does it merely add another layer of terminological confusion? Please diagnose the structure of the disagreement using what each side actually says about rationality, common knowledge, and game-theoretic interpretation, identify the crux if the disagreement is substantive, and specify what, given your diagnosis, should count as genuine progress.
