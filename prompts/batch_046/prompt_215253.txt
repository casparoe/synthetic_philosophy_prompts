I have spent the last several hours in heated discussion with a close friend about Douglas Hofstadter's claim that the self is not a substance but a strange loop—an abstract pattern of self-reference that can, in principle, be instantiated across different substrates. My friend argued that if two brains were linked by a sustained direct cortical interface allowing continuous bidirectional synchronization of autobiographical memory-structures, they would not merely share memories but would come to host isomorphic strange loops referencing a single first-person narrative. From this, she concluded that we should recognize one distributed 'I' with two bodily nodes rather than two separate selves with shared content. I resisted, feeling that some embodied indexical boundary must persist, yet I could not specify where that boundary lies without implicitly treating the brain as a privileged substance-container—a move Hofstadter's framework explicitly rejects. I am now designing the final essay exam for my upper-level philosophy of mind seminar and want a question that forces students to navigate this precise tension rather than merely summarizing Hofstadter's loop metaphor.

Along with the question, provide a model answer of roughly 600 words that takes a clear, defensible position while seriously engaging the opposing intuition, and provide a marking scheme totaling 20 marks built around four specific analytical moves. For each move, state the marks allocated, describe the precise intellectual task it represents, and include a one-line note on how students typically fail it. The scheme should sharply separate an A-level answer—which must explicitly confront the conflict between substrate-independent pattern identity and the phenomenological ownership of an embodied perspective—from a B-level answer that applies Hofstadter's vocabulary descriptively without resolving the underlying tension.
