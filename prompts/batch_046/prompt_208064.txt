I am preparing an application for a six-month postgraduate research fellowship and need a concise two-page proposal. My half-formed idea is this: the lottery paradox and the preface paradox are usually treated either as twin manifestations of a failure of belief closure or as entirely separate puzzles requiring distinct solutions—threshold theories for the one, consistency constraints for the other. I suspect this dichotomy obscures something important about the direction of epistemic orientation. In the lottery case, the agent distributes confidence across a large set of independent, future-oriented possibilities; in the preface case, the agent integrates a large set of past-oriented, self-authored claims into a single reflective stance. I want to explore whether we can dissolve part of the tension and sharpen the remainder by introducing a distinction between evidential tolerance—the statistical uncertainty an individual belief can carry without being suspended—and reflective tolerance—the degree of self-undermining a corpus of beliefs can sustain before its author must withdraw global assent. Rather than proposing a new formal logic from scratch, I intend to construct a conceptual taxonomy that maps existing responses (threshold views, contextualist adjustments, non-aggregative strategies) onto these two tolerances and shows where they conflate them.

Please turn this into a formal proposal with the following elements: a precisely stated research question that explains why current unified or bifurcated treatments leave it open; a justification for adopting the evidential/reflective tolerance distinction rather than the more obvious alternatives, such as refining a single consistency condition or appealing to pragmatic encroachment; a realistic month-by-month plan for the six-month period specifying what conceptual analysis, case construction, and comparative evaluation I would actually undertake; a clear description of what a successful result would look like and, equally important, what would count as the project failing—whether that be the collapse of the distinction into a psychological rather than epistemic one, or the demonstration that both paradoxes are already resolved without it; and a brief statement of scope explaining why six months is sufficient and what lies outside the research programme. The proposal should be written in polished British English and should avoid any request for a bibliography or citation list, though it may refer to named positions where necessary.

Finally, the proposal should include an explicit epistemic self-assessment: identify the weakest point in the argument—most likely the claim that temporal orientation (prospective versus retrospective) carries genuine epistemic weight rather than being merely descriptive—and specify exactly what kind of counter-example, formal result, or conceptual objection would change my mind and lead me to abandon the bifocal framework in favour of a more conventional unified account.
