I'm trying to pin down a pattern I keep seeing in poetry translation, especially when people work under really strict formal constrants like rhyme and meter. There's this specific failure mode where every formal feature is preserved — rhyme scheme, syllable count, all that — but the vocabulary gets pushed upward into a fake-elegant, archaic register. The original might be playful or casual, like Marot's lighter verse, but the translation ends up sounding like court poetry. Not because the translator is being pretentious, but because the rhyme slots and meter just happen to accomodate high-register words more easily. So the form is perfectly faithful but the tone is inverted — formal preservation produces tonal betrayal. Is there an established term for exactly this mechanism, not just generic 'register shift' or 'domestication'? If there is, how does it differ from ordinary translation loss? And if not, can you coin and define it with clear boundry cases — like when a translator deliberately chooses archaic diction versus when the constrants force it? I care about the carving, not just a bare label.
