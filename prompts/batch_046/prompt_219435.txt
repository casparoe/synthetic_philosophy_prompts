After a career in condensed-matter physics, I have recently begun studying philosophy and now hold the view that the sciences carve nature at its joints only where those joints correspond to scale-invariant fixed points—structures that survive renormalization-group flow—while all other taxonomies, from biological species to psychiatric diagnoses, are instrumental projections onto a continuous substrate. What would have to be true for this claim to be wrong? I want the arguments and observations that should move me, sorted by how decisive they are: for instance, whether genuinely discontinuous phase transitions without underlying symmetry breaking would break the link between joints and fixed points, or whether successful prediction of novel biological taxa from pure morphological clustering, lacking any mechanistic grounding, would show that taxonomy can be joint-carving without being scale-invariant. I also want to know what this position predicts that rival accounts—conventional scientific realism on one side, pure nominalism on the other—do not, and whether defenders of this structural view have qualified it, perhaps by stretching the notion of a fixed point so widely that no empirical result could reach it. If it has been insulated in that way, is that a defect? Please distinguish evidence that would be fatal from evidence that would merely be awkward, include what proponents themselves concede as counting against them, and give an honest assessment of whether the view risks anything at all.
