Write a polished dialogue between two equally competent interlocutors—call them Althea and Bion—debating what moral and artistic obligations we have to real, deceased people when they are dramatized in historical fiction or biopics. Althea defends a view of posthumous narrative autonomy: she holds that death does not dissolve a person’s claim over the interpretation of their own life, especially regarding private mental states and unrecorded intimate moments, and that inventing these for dramatic effect constitutes a distinctive wrong—not merely misinformation for the living, but a kind of representational dispossession of the dead. Bion defends dramatic truth over biographical fidelity: he argues that artistic works pursue emotional and structural truths that may legitimately require altering chronology, compressing events, or inventing dialogue; that the dead lack the consciousness required to be moral patients capable of being harmed; and that the only relevant wrong is misleading living audiences about matters of public historical record.

In their exchange, they should directly engage with each other’s arguments rather than speaking past one another. Althea should press Bion on whether his view collapses the distinction between compressing public events for narrative economy—which she might grant is permissible—and fabricating an entirely unrecorded private scene, such as a deathbed conversation for which no transcript or credible witness exists. She should argue that the latter violates what she terms “memorial integrity,” a constraint that protects not the dead person’s feelings (which she concedes are absent) but the dignity of the life-narrative that the person actually lived. Bion should respond by challenging Althea’s notion of “memorial integrity” as resting on what he calls the “archival fallacy”: the mistaken assumption that a human life is a fixed dataset to be preserved intact rather than an interpreted narrative that art legitimately reconfigures. He should ask whether her standard would prohibit any interior monologue in a biopic, and whether she can distinguish harmful invention from benign interpretation without smuggling in a questionable metaphysics of personal identity that survives death.

They should also address whether the wrong, if there is one, is owed to the dead themselves, to living survivors who suffer dignitary harm, or merely to audiences who deserve not to be deceived about public facts. The dialogue should conclude without either party being reduced to a strawman, leaving the tension genuinely unresolved but sharply clarified, and it should not invoke any specific philosophers by name or request bibliographic support.
