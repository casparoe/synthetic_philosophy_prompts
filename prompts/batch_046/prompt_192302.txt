I'm preparing a single seventy-five-minute seminar for second-year philosophy undergraduates who have read Plato's Republic but have never encountered Aristotle's Politics or Nicomachean Ethics. The aim is not a survey of Aristotle's economics, but an understanding of his account of exchange, the origin and purpose of money (nomisma), and the boundary between natural household management (oikonomia) and unnatural wealth-getting (chrematistike). To keep the idea from collapsing into familiar modern categories, I want the session to turn on a specific carrying example: a physician in a small polis who accepts payment in grain tokens that are redeemable only within the current harvest cycle and that lose all value if hoarded past the season, rather than the usual shoemaker or barter narrative. Plan the explanatory arc: the dependency order—what must be secured about natural needs and the purpose of the household before exchange can be introduced, what must be in place about exchange before money can appear as a conventional measure, and what must be clear about the nature of money before the condemnation of interest (tokos) becomes intelligible rather than merely moralistic; the precise features this decaying-token example must possess and the features it must lack in order to illustrate Aristotle's claim that money is a convention serving need rather than a store of unlimited potential; the misconceptions students will form at each stage—such as that Aristotle rejects all trade, that he treats barter as primordial and money as a corruption, or that he condemns interest because it exploits the borrower rather than because money is naturally barren—and the exact point in the sequence where each should be headed off; one closing question that distinguishes students who have grasped the teleological structure from those who have only memorized the oikonomia/chrematistike distinction; and what standard comparative framing—e.g., casting Aristotle as a proto-Marxist critic of capital or as an advocate of unregulated exchange—should be openly refused at this level and why.
