I have been trying to map Aristotle’s distinction between oikonomikē and chrematistikē onto modern financial architecture, and I keep running into what seems like a literal reading of Politics I.10: Aristotle calls coin (nomisma) argos—barren or sterile—so that tokos, interest, is treated as an unnatural offspring of something that cannot reproduce. I take this not as a metaphor but as an empirical claim about the physical properties of metallic currency: because coinage lacks generative capacity, any iterative loop in which capital redeploys itself without being consumed in use violates a kind of material logic. From this, I conclude that Aristotle permits kapēlikē (retail exchange) only when it is non-iterative—essentially a one-way conversion of surplus goods into coin for household replenishment. But modern algorithmic market-making creates precisely the iterative structure he condemns: liquidity is provided continuously, capital is never used up in consumption, and returns are generated through velocity rather than through the exchange of goods for use. Given that Aristotle allows exchange only insofar as it serves the telos of household self-sufficiency, must he classify high-frequency liquidity provision as chrematistikē even when it facilitates price discovery for consumable goods? Or does his framework contain a hidden distinction—perhaps in Nicomachean Ethics V.5—between iterative exchange that remains instrumentally subordinate to oikonomia and iterative exchange that treats coin as if it were naturally generative?
