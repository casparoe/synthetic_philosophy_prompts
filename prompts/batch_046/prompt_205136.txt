In recent discussions of absurdism and contemporary accounts of meaning in life, people often invoke the comparison between human existence and an open-world video game: there is no final boss, no save file that transfers elsewhere, and no external scoreboard, so any purpose must be invented by the player and valued only through enjoyment of the mechanics. A blogger uses this to argue that objective-list theories of meaning—those treating knowledge, love, or achievement as objectively valuable goods—still smuggle in a hidden teleology, as if the game had a designer-imposed achievement system we have failed to notice. Audit the analogy: lay out the correspondence table (player, designers, mechanics, invented sub-goals, absence of score), identify the disanalogies that matter for the argument (e.g., whether cosmic purposelessness parallels lacking a designer, whether objective goods are analogous to hidden achievements, whether invented goals can bear the same normative weight as discovered ones), and say whether the blogger’s conclusion—that objective-list theories cannot escape a residual design assumption—survives once those breaks are on the table. If a different analogy better captures what absurdism claims about value-creation without objective purpose, propose it.
