Having recently spent two years navigating my father’s decline through frontotemporal dementia, I have begun reading philosophy of mind not as an academic exercise but as a practical attempt to understand what, if anything, remains of his inner life as language and self-reference dissolve. In conversations with clinicians, ethicists, and family members, I keep encountering the same argumentative maneuver, yet I do not know whether it is recognized.

Here is the pattern. When the question is whether my father—who no longer speaks, no longer recognizes me consistently, but still withdraws his hand from a hot surface and occasionally smiles at familiar music—retains any subjective experience, the response is typically: we have no first-person access, so we cannot know, and therefore we should assume the absence of experience in making care decisions. The explanatory gap is treated as a license for negation.

Yet when the same interlocutors discuss a pre-linguistic infant undergoing comparable neural degradation, or a locked-in patient who retains full awareness but no motor output, they invoke precisely the same lack of first-person access and conclude the opposite: we cannot know from the outside, therefore we must presume experience and act with maximal caution. The gap, identical in logical structure, suddenly becomes a reason for affirmation.

What strikes me is not mere inconsistency, but a systematic correlation: the direction in which the epistemic gap is resolved—whether it licenses denial or demands precaution—tracks not the available neural or behavioral evidence, but the practical and moral cost of being wrong. Where attributing consciousness would impose an unbearable burden, such as withdrawing analgesic vigilance, the gap is read as absence; where denying it would impose a moral horror, such as neglecting an infant or a paralyzed adult, the gap is read as presence.

I have noticed this in at least three concrete settings. In hospice ethics committees, the argument is used to justify reducing vigilance for non-communicative patients with severe dementia, on the grounds that there is no one there to suffer. In neonatal intensive care debates, the identical logical form—our inability to bridge to their phenomenology—is used to mandate aggressive pain management for infants with comparable cortical damage. And in comparative cognition, researchers who deny phenomenal consciousness to non-human animals with analogous neural complexity cite our failure to close the gap as decisive, while researchers studying human anencephalic infants cite the very same failure as a reason for protective humility.

Is there an established concept for this specific selective deployment of the hard problem’s epistemic gap, in which the gap’s normative direction is determined by the stakes of attribution rather than by the evidence? How does it differ from ordinary anthropocentrism, from verificationist bias, or from what one might call practical phenomenal skepticism? If there is no established term, I would be inclined to call it something like stake-contingent phenomenal denial, but I am more interested in the carving than in the label: exactly what distinguishes this move from legitimate epistemic caution, where its boundaries lie, whether it represents a failure of reasoning or an unavoidable feature of how consciousness concepts function under moral pressure, and what a consistent application of the gap would actually require across these cases.
