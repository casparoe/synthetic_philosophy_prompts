Design a diagnostic taxonomy—either a decision tree or a flowchart with branching questions—for philosophical accounts of why promises bind, intended for use in a graduate normative-ethics seminar. Rather than opening with abstract definitions, root the tree in a concrete case: an agent makes a sincere promise to a promisee who subsequently suffers irreversible cognitive dissolution and can no longer hold anyone to account, yet the agent's practical identity and the social record of the commitment remain intact. The first node should ask whether the promise retains binding force in this scenario.

From there, construct at most five successive binary questions that separate the following positions, each phrased so that a respondent can answer without knowing the canonical label: (i) an expectation-based account that treats bindingness as sustained by the promisee's (actual or counterfactual) reliance; (ii) a normative-power account that treats the promisor as having exercised an authority to create an obligation regardless of the promisee's current capacities; (iii) a practice-based account that grounds bindingness in shared social norms of accountability rather than in dyadic relations; and (iv) a hybrid relational-transformative account that requires both the exercise of normative power and an intact framework of mutual recognition.

You must also locate, within the logical space your questions generate, a position here labeled the 'radical dissolution view': the claim that promises bind only while both parties remain within a shared framework of practical reason, and that binding force evaporates entirely—not merely weakens—when either party permanently exits that framework. If this combination is incoherent with one of your earlier branches, or if it occupies an empty region of the tree, flag it and briefly diagnose whether the vacancy is due to incoherence, unattractiveness, or mere neglect.

Additionally, include a branch that addresses promises made to entities of indeterminate moral status—specifically, a future artificial agent that processes commitments but lacks phenomenological experience—to test whether an account's binding conditions depend on the promisee's sentience, its rational agency, or purely formal features of the commitment.

For every leaf, provide a one-sentence characterization of the view in terms of its pattern of answers, not just its name. Explicitly flag any pair of named positions that your tree fails to separate, and propose one further hypothetical case—distinct from those above—that would distinguish them. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; do not attribute specific arguments to named authors by page or text; and avoid framing the task as a historical survey. The deliverable should be the classification instrument itself, together with its scoring key.
