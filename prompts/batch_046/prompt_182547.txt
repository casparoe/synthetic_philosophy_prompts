I've been arguing with my flatmate about Newton's bucket and whether it actually kills relationism. Here's the argument I cooked up, but it feels like it proves way too much and I cant see the leak.

(1) If relationism is true, then every motion fact is just a fact about relations between bodies.
(2) Take Newton's bucket: the water climbs the sides when its spins.
(3) A clever relationist (say, a Machian) can reply that the concavity is explained by the bucket's rotation relative to the distant fixed stars, not absolute space.
(4) But now imagine we strip away all distant matter and leave only the bucket and water in an otherwise empty universe. The Machian has nothing to invoke, so they must say there's no concavity.
(5) That sounds wrong — we'd still expect the water to bulge — so Machian relationism fails, and there is absolute rotation.
(6) Now apply the same pattern to linear motion. If I'm in a sealed box being accelerated in a straight line in an empty universe, I feel pressed against the wall.
(7) By parity of reasoning, if there's no distant matter, the relationist can't explain that inertial push either.
(8) So to stay consistent, the relationist must invoke distant matter for linear inertia too, or concede absolute linear acceleration.
(9) But if we concede absolute linear acceleration, we have an absolute standard of rest and uniform velocity.
(10) That means special relativity's claim that uniform velocity is purely relative is false.
(11) So Newton's bucket, used honestly, doesn't just establish absolute rotation — it establishes absolute velocity, which is far more than anyone wanted.

I want to hang onto the idea that the bucket genuinely shows something non-relative about rotation, but I definitely dont want to be committed to there being a privileged inertial frame for straight-line motion. Which step should I ditch, and why did it look okay? Also, what do the usual camps — hardline substantivalists, Machians, and the modern 'dynamical' relationists — each have to pay if they reject that step?
