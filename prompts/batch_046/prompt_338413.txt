I've been thinking about that Hofstadter thing with Marot and whether form can actually survive translation when you're locked into rhyme and meter. Here are my gut verdict's on five versions of the same short lyric -- all keeping some constraint, but different ones.

One version keeps the exact rhyme scheme and syllable count, but replaces "the sun weeps gold onto the field" with "dawn spills yellow over grass." Form is perfect, image is close but not identical. I say thats fine, maybe even good.

Another keeps every word's literal meaning but shifts to free verse -- "the sun weeps gold onto the field" stays intact, but rhyme and meter vanish. For this poem I feel like thats actually worse, which surprises me.

A third keeps the rhyme sounds but forces grammar thats barely English: "gold weeps the sun field onto." Meaning and rhyme are technically both present, but it reads like a cipher. I reject this hard.

A fourth changes the rhyme scheme to match English stress patterns, keeps the central metaphor exact, and puts a short translators note in the margine explaining the swap. This feels like the most honest version, but I'm unsure if its still translation or just remake.

Then there's a fifth: same rhyme and meter, but "sun" becomes "moon" because it rhymes better, flipping the seasonal reference. I think this is wrong in a different way from the free-verse one.

So is there one principle behind all this? Maybe form and content aren't separable in lyric? Or maybe there's a hierarchy I'm missing? And which verdict do I drop for consistency -- especially accepting image-shift for form in case one but rejecting form-shift for image in case two? Also, what does your principle say about a version that keeps the rhyme scheme but replaces every noun with a synonym of same syllable count and stress, so "sun weeps gold onto the field" becomes "star cries silver on the meadow" -- does that pass or fail?
