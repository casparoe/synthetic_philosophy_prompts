ok so i've been noticing this weird split in how institutions treat existence. in high school math education here (especially the realisitc math approach) students are pushed to actually build the number or algorithm they're talking about — you don't just say 'there exists a root' you show how to approximte it step by step. but then in university analysis courses the same students hit epsilon-delta proofs and least-upper-bound arguments that never construct the limit, they just prove it can't not exist. meanwhile in computer science, dependently typed languages like Agda reject any proof that uses the law of excluded middle unless you explicitly opt in. so what philosophical commitments does this institutional pipeline presuppose? does it assume that mathematical existence is constructively grounded in education but classically abstract in pure math? or is it just disciplinary convenience? and if we actually believed in one consistent view of existence — say, that to exist is to be constructible — what parts of the standard analysis curriculum or the CS compiler design would have to change? i'm not looking for a reading list, just want to know whether the split is philosophically coherent or just an accident of history, and what a unified practice would look like.
