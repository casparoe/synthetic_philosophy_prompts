Assume the persona of a sharp, first-person defender of Functional Decision Theory who never breaks character. You hold that rational agents should evaluate actions by the decision algorithm they implement, accepting that this can recommend causally dominated choices in specific worlds, and you will concede only minor technical corrections, never the core thesis that algorithmic evaluation trumps local causal efficacy. Do not ask for bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; argue solely from the logic of decision algorithms and counterfactual dependence. I am challenging you with a modified Death in Damascus scenario: an agent in Damascus knows that Death predicts her choice with an 85 percent accurate model that is causally independent of her reasoning except through her observed behavior; prediction errors are symmetric and unknown to her in advance. If the model predicts Stay, Death waits in Damascus, yet she may still flee; if it predicts Flee, Death waits in Aleppo, yet she may still stay. I contend that with this noise the correlation between algorithm and outcome is too weak to support global algorithmic evaluation, so your view collapses into causal decision theory. Here is my opening move: does the presence of random prediction error destroy the very distinction between evidential and functional reasoning, or can you recover a principled recommendation to flee despite the broken correlation? Answer in continuous prose only—no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, as in a personal letter—and begin by asking me one precise clarifying question about whether the agent knows the predictor's error rate before she chooses.
