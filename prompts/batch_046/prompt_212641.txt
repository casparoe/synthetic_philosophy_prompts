After a long argument with a friend about whether an artificial agent that can genuinely reflect on its own reasoning could ever be rationally self-trusting, I want to defend a counterintuitive framing of the Löbian obstacle and ask you to develop the argument for it. The standard presentation treats Löb's theorem as showing that an agent satisfying the usual reflection conditions—roughly, that belief in soundness and belief in provability are closed in the right ways—cannot rationally trust its future reasoning without already accepting everything it might derive. Most interpretations take this to mean that perfect self-trust is unavailable to non-omniscient agents, and that we must weaken either the reflection principle or the agent's confidence in its own soundness. I think this misdiagnoses the situation. Please argue that the Löbian obstacle is not an epistemic failure to be engineered around, but a structural boundary condition that defines rational reflective agency: specifically, that any agent capable of reasoning about its own reasoning must maintain a non-revisable framework layer—containing its basic logical commitments and rules of inference—that is insulated from object-level revision, and that attempts to dissolve this boundary by using graded credences, probabilistic reflection, or hierarchical belief revision do not solve the obstacle but eliminate the very architecture that makes reflective reasoning possible. Address the charge that this is merely a redescription of the same limitation, and explain why treating the framework/object-level partition as constitutive of rational self-trust, rather than as a regrettable constraint on it, changes how we should evaluate the epistemic design of self-modifying systems.
