I serve as an external literary fellow on the ethics advisory panel of the Meridian Foundation for Bioethical Innovation, and I have been tasked with drafting the formal decision memo that will precede Thursday's vote on Protocol 47-B, a multi-phase biotechnology proposal submitted by a neurogenetic consortium. The applicants seek institutional endorsement and preliminary funding to develop a germline and pharmacological intervention they call a hedonistic imperative protocol, designed to progressively eliminate nociceptive and affective suffering across sentient species—beginning with agricultural livestock and extending, in pilot form, to human subjects with intractable chronic conditions—explicitly framing their work as the technological realization of David Pearce’s abolitionist thesis that suffering is an evolutionary contingency rather than a metaphysically necessary feature of consciousness. The board is split: one faction argues that the project collapses the boundary between therapeutic intervention and species redesign, risking an impoverishment of moral emotion and empathic capacity; the other faction holds that negative-utilitarian obligations make refusal tantamount to preserving an avoidable evil. Between revisions of my current novel, which concerns the ethics of memory extinction in post-human societies, I have been asked to produce a document that does not merely summarize these positions but renders an actionable institutional judgment.

Please draft the memo in a form suitable for circulation to the full board: state a clear recommendation—approve, approve with conditions, or reject—supported by reasoning that the research consortium could respond to in good faith, and include a concrete timeline or procedural framework if your recommendation requires phased oversight. In particular, the memo should engage seriously with the strongest objection—that abolishing suffering through biotechnological redesign might erode the very emotional and moral architectures that make intersubjective recognition and autonomous agency intelligible—without reducing that concern to mere sentimental conservatism. At the same time, it should address the applicants’ central claim that consciousness need not entail suffering, and should avoid asking for citations, bibliographies, or references to literature the board has not already received. The tone should be formal, institutionally appropriate, and acknowledge, however briefly, that this recommendation is being composed by someone whose professional life is spent imagining worlds in which human experience has been fundamentally altered.
