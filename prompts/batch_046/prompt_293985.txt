I take the view that qualia are not merely unexplained but conceptually incoherent as theoretical entities — not just epistemically hidden, but ontologically defective. The argument, in the spirit of Quinean eliminativism and developed in work associated with Chase and Sanborn, runs roughly as follows: qualia are defined by first-person exclusivity (only the subject can access them), causal inertness (they make no difference to physical behavior or explanation), and irreducibility (they cannot be analyzed into structural, functional, or dispositional terms). Taken together, these three features generate a contradiction rather than a mystery. A property that is genuinely real must be capable of entering into explanatory or causal relations, or at least be publicly attributable; yet qualia are said to be both introspectively indubitable and entirely disconnected from the causal-physical order. This is not a gap in our knowledge but a failure of conceptual coherence — like positing a round square.

What would have to be true for this position to be wrong? I want an honest assessment of its exposure — what it genuinely risks, where it might be insulated, and which objections its defenders would themselves concede.

Please structure your response around the following:

First, identify the premises doing the heavy lifting: that ontological commitment requires causal or explanatory integration; that first-person exclusivity is incompatible with legitimate property attribution; that irreducibility combined with causal inertness is unstable. For each, describe not just a logical counterpossibility but a live scenario: what empirical discovery, conceptual revision, or result from another domain (e.g., mental representation, intersubjectivity, philosophy of science) would actually undermine it?

Second, distinguish fatal defeaters from merely awkward ones. Would the discovery of precise, cross-subjective structural isomorphisms in neural correlates of experience — correlations that allow reliable third-person prediction of qualitative states without any shared functional role — count as undermining the incoherence claim? Or could the eliminativist absorb this as just another mysterious correlation that leaves the core contradiction untouched? Be explicit about whether you think this is a live threat or an accommodation.

Third, assess insulation. Has the incoherence view been qualified into unfalsifiability — for instance, by defining any apparent counter-example (such as successful intersubjective reporting) as necessarily missing the qualitative essence? If so, is that insulation a conceptual virtue (protecting a necessary truth) or a methodological vice (rendering the view empirically empty)? Would the view's defenders accept that qualification, or would they reject it?

Fourth, consider results from adjacent fields. If cognitive science developed a fully predictive, functionally integrated model of conscious report that made no reference to qualitative properties but captured all behavioral and neural data, would that confirm the eliminativist position or merely show that science can proceed without them — leaving open whether they exist as inert appendages?

Finally, rank the defeaters from those that should genuinely move a committed defender to those they would dismiss as question-begging. Include which objections the defenders themselves would treat as legitimate challenges rather than misunderstandings of the argument. Be willing to conclude that the view is well insulated and that this is or is not a problem for it.
