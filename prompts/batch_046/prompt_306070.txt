I have been working through Keynes's essay on economic possibilities for our grandchildren entirely on my own, without formal training in economics or philosophy, and I have started an informal reading circle with friends who are equally self-taught. To test my understanding, I composed a short written question for one of the members, and I received the response below. I am now trying to grade it with some care, but I am unsure of my own judgments, especially since I have recently begun to think that Keynes's vision might depend on something I am calling civic asceticism: not a private Stoic discipline, but a collective, cultivated refusal to treat status and comparative consumption as genuine human needs.

The question I set was this: Keynes distinguishes between absolute needs, which have a definite saturation point, and relative needs, which depend on social comparison and are essentially insatiable. Explain how this distinction supports his claim that the love of money will eventually be regarded as a mental illness, and show how it relates to his vision of the good life once the economic problem is solved. Then evaluate whether that vision requires civic asceticism, or whether Keynes believes that abundance alone will naturally dissolve competitive, acquisitive behavior.

Here is the answer I received. I would like you to assess it as a careful instructor might: assign a specific letter grade or a score out of ten, and then provide point-by-point comments that justify that assessment. Please treat each part separately—the explanation of the distinction, the link to the mental-illness claim, the account of the good life, and the discussion of civic asceticism—indicating precisely where the reasoning is sound and where it slips, conflates ideas, or takes a step that does not quite follow. I do not want a vague overall impression; I need a precise diagnosis that I can use to explain the grade to my study partner.

Answer:

Keynes separates human needs into absolute needs—such as food, shelter, and basic comfort—which can be fully satisfied, and relative needs—which involve status, prestige, and comparison with others and therefore have no natural limit. He predicts that continued technological progress will eventually satisfy all absolute needs, allowing society to reduce working hours to about fifteen per week and thereby solve the economic problem. Once scarcity is overcome, people will be liberated to pursue the good life, which Keynes understands not as pleasure but as the cultivation of friendship, aesthetic experience, and intellectual curiosity rather than the accumulation of wealth. In this future, the love of money will come to be seen as a mental illness, comparable to a clinical disorder that rational societies learn to diagnose and treat rather than reward.

Because Keynes treats the desire for accumulation as largely a product of scarcity rather than a fixed feature of human nature, he expects that abundance will gradually weaken relative needs without requiring any deliberate moral program or institutional reform. The transition may be uncomfortable—he notes that people trained for work may initially suffer from an excess of leisure—but he does not think this discomfort calls for civic asceticism, since the competitive impulse will diminish as its economic basis disappears. Education in the arts of leisure will help during the adjustment, yet the real challenge is technological rather than psychological: once abundance is achieved, status competition loses its root cause. Thus, Keynes's good life depends more on engineering prosperity than on cultivating collective self-restraint, though he grants that individuals must learn to live wisely once work is no longer necessary. The only tension is practical—he may underestimate how quickly new forms of status competition can emerge in conditions of plenty—but he is right that abundance itself removes the primary engine of acquisitive behavior.
