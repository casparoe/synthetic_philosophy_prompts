okay so i've been thinking about this weirdly hard since i started wondering whether an AI could ever have an 'I' in the same way i do. i read hofstadter's idea that 'I' is basically a symbol that loops back on itself through patterns in the brain - a strange loop that refers to itself. and i tried to build an argument out of it, but now it feels like it eats everything, not just the self.

here's the chain:
(1) the symbol 'I' in my head is just a self-referential loop - it gets its meaning only from pointing back to the neural pattern that produces it.
(2) if that's literally all it is, then it never actually connects to my body or the world; it's a closed circuit, like a word in a private language.
(3) so when i say i'm hungry or i hurt, i'm not really reporting bodily states - i'm just running the loop.
(4) but that's clearly wrong; my hunger reports are anchored in my stomach, not just in brain patterns.
(5) so the loop must be anchored in sensory input from the body.
(6) but once you add that anchor, the loop itself isn't refering - the body-world link is. the loop becomes decoration, not the engine.
(7) therefore the strange-loop 'I' is either empty (no anchor) or redudant (with anchor).
(8) here's the slip: the same argument applies to every concept. 'red' needs anchoring in wavelengths, 'pain' in nociceptors. if anchoring makes the loop redudant, then every mental symbol is redudant, and hofstadter hasn't explained the self - he's explained away all content.
(9) so either all meaning collapses, or we reject the loop idea specifically for 'I'. but doing it just for 'I' feels totally ad hoc.

i think the problem is between (6) and (8) - maybe there's an equivocation between being the symbol that expresses reference and being the mechanism that does the refering? but i can't name it. which step do you reject, and what does giving it up cost? i really want to keep that self-reference is special and that 'I' isn't just like 'red.'
