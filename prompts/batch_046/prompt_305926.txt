Please explain, in continuous prose only—no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, as one writes in a letter—why the tension at the heart of Sen's liberal paradox is not resolved by expanding the framework to three agents with cyclically ordered preferences and a conditional delegation clause for liberal rights. Begin by briefly recalling the classic two-person, one-book setup: two individuals each hold minimal liberal authority over whether they personally read a particular work, yet their preferences over the four possible social states generate a direct conflict with the Pareto principle, since respecting each person's local decisive power produces an outcome that both rank below an alternative they both prefer. Now consider the following extension, which I ask you to treat as the central object of analysis rather than a digression. There are three agents—P, Q, and R—each assigned minimal liberal authority over a distinct personal domain: P over whether he reads volume X, Q over whether she listens to recording Y, and R over whether he wears item Z. Their preferences over the eight social states exhibit a Condorcet cycle on the cross-domain dimensions: P prefers any state in which Q listens to Y to any state in which she does not, holding his own domain fixed; Q prefers any state in which R wears Z to any state in which he does not; and R prefers any state in which P reads X to any state in which he does not. Within each agent's own domain, preferences are lexicographic: each strictly prefers his favored exercise of his right over its denial, but is otherwise indifferent. To this we add a delegation mechanism: an agent may transfer his liberal right to the agent whose cross-domain preference he directly affects, but only if that recipient simultaneously transfers his own right forward, so that delegation is either fully cyclic or absent altogether. This yields two viable regimes—local rights only and full cyclic delegation—under which the collective assignment of rights differs. Your task is to show that even in this expanded setting, no social decision rule can satisfy both the Pareto principle and minimal liberalism interpreted to include the non-transferability of rights without unanimous consent. Specifically, address why treating full cyclic delegation as a permissible exercise of liberal autonomy fails to dissolve the paradox: if all three delegate, the collective outcome must still respect the cyclic preference ordering, which conflicts with Pareto efficiency over the eight states; if delegation is blocked, we revert to the local rights case, where Pareto is violated in exactly the same structural manner as the two-agent original. Conclude by explaining why the paradox is therefore not an artifact of dyadic interaction or of an overly rigid conception of personal domain, but a deeper incompatibility between unrestricted Pareto improvement and the assignment of non-aggregable decision rights to individuals.
