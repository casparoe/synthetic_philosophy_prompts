I was arguing with my roomate about the statue and street-name debate after we heard about that town in Belgium that keeps old colonial street signs but buries them in a 'shadow archive' under the new ones. I think I've mostly figured out the logic but it still feels off in a way I can't pin down. Basically, if a monument is just recording that someone existed—like a gravestone—then altering it is like editing a primary source, which is clearly bad. But if it's an honor—like naming a school after someone—then it's more like giving an award, and taking it back isn't falsifing history, it's just withdrawing praise. So far so good, except every statue or street name seems to do both jobs at once. The general on the pedestal records that he lived and fought, but he also gets praised by being put up there in the first place. So when people shout 'don't erase history,' they must be treating the praise-part as if it were archival documentation, right? Otherwise there'd be no conflict at all. But then I occured to me that we have heritage-preservation laws protecting old buildings and monuments as architecture even when the person was awful—like, we don't tear down a slave-owner's mansion, we keep it as a historical structure. So maybe the anti-renaming crowd is actually correct, but for heritage reasons rather than moral ones? Or is there some hidden premise that once public space receives a name it acquires a kind of property right held in trust for the dead? I'm genuinely stuck on whether renaming a street is closer to returning a medal or closer to burning a page out of a diary, and whether this shadow-archive compromise actually resolves the tension or just moves the contradiction somewhere else.
