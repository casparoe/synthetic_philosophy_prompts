Following a prolonged argument with a friend about whether dispositions are genuine properties or merely conditional powers interrupted by interfering factors, I want a poem that stages the metaphysical disagreement structurally. Write a Petrarchan sonnet—octave in ABBA ABBA, sestet in CDECDE—on the distinction between a fink and a mask in the philosophy of dispositions. In the octave, present the conditional analysis and its collapse under the fink: a wire disposed to conduct electricity, but a device breaks the circuit at the exact moment of contact, so the manifestation is prevented and the disposition appears to vanish with it; let the enclosed rhyme scheme mirror the self-canceling loop, where the condition and its negation are locked together. At the volta in line 9, turn to the masked case: the same wire, now coated in insulating resin that blocks current without altering the conductive structure, struck repeatedly but still disposed; here the rhyme should open into interwoven patterns that hold the disposition intact beneath the obstruction. The closing couplet must state precisely what the contrast costs the analysis—whether "would have manifested" can survive systematic blocking, and whether a disposition that never manifests remains a real tendency or only a ghost of a conditional. Render the concepts with enough precision that a specialist in the metaphysics of dispositions would recognize the standard problem of the interrupted conditional and the distinction between destructive and obstructive interference, but do not name authors, cite texts, or provide bibliographic references; let the structural turn of the sonnet do the philosophical work. Make the verse accurate enough that the distinction between cancellation and masking is conceptually clear, not merely named.
