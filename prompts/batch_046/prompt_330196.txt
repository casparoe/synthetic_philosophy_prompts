As a journalist covering the history of evolutionary thought, I am working through Charles Darwin’s The Descent of Man and have become stuck on a dense passage near the discussion of moral faculties. In the passage, Darwin argues that the moral sense first arises from social instincts—sympathy, communal loyalty, and the desire for approval—which natural selection strengthens because they improve group survival. He then adds that, as intellectual powers grow, human beings begin to compare past actions with future intentions and to judge conduct against a broader standard of community welfare, so that reason can override or redirect these instinctive feelings. At the same time, Darwin treats religious belief as itself an evolutionary byproduct—born from fear of unknown causes and a tendency to personify natural forces—which often obstructs this rational correction by imposing dogmatic duties that conflict with social sympathy. What I cannot see clearly is whether Darwin means that reason offers an independent, normative check on evolved morality, or whether he is describing only a more complex, second-order adaptation: a self-correcting mechanism that remains entirely inside the biological framework. And I wonder whether his simultaneous reduction of religion to evolved fear undermines his confidence that reason can genuinely transcend instinct, or whether he thinks religious interference is precisely what rational moral development must overcome. Could you help me understand how Darwin resolves—if he does resolve—the circularity of using an evolved faculty to judge the adequacy of evolved instincts, and what that implies for his view of morality’s authority?
