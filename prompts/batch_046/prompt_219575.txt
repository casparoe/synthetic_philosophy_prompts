As a novelist constructing a pivotal scene, I need a formal keynote address delivered by my protagonist—a retired trauma surgeon who has permanently lost the use of both hands—at a symposium marking fifty years since the publication of an obscure 1974 philosophical essay, within my fiction titled The Partitioned Self by the Stoic revivalist Elias Vorn. The audience is sharply divided: half are clinical psychologists who have built therapeutic protocols around Vorn’s tripartite revision of the dichotomy of control—dividing agency not into two but into three domains: the internal, the proximate external, and the cosmically indifferent—while the other half are literary scholars who regard the essay as a concealed foundation of postwar American minimalism. The occasion demands both intellectual rigor and emotional coherence, yet the speaker is physically incapable of performing the conventional Stoic gestures of dismissal or acceptance, so the philosophical argument itself—specifically whether Vorn’s third domain causes the entire framework to collapse into incoherence—must carry the structural weight of the speech and do the emotional labor of the occasion. The address must engage the specialists without alienating the humanists, and it must never frame her disability as either triumphant transcendence or tragic limitation. Write this speech as it would be delivered: a polished, occasion-appropriate keynote in which the tension within partitioned agency shapes what can be said, what must remain unsaid, and how the speaker occupies the stage without her hands.
