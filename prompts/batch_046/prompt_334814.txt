I'm working through Nietzsche's distinction among monumental, antiquarian, and critical history for life, and I tried applying it to the curation of digital heritage archives—systems that preserve every record of a monument (antiquarian), generate heroic narratives for public education (monumental), and deconstruct its colonial origins (critical). This led me to an argument that seems to prove too much: (1) If history genuinely serves life, then any given practice must satisfy one of Nietzsche's three needs—inspiration, preservation, or liberation. (2) Monumental use requires selectively forgetting and idealizing the past; antiquarian use requires exact preservation without distortion; critical use requires actively destroying or condemning parts of the past. (3) For any single object—say, a specific monument—these three operations are mutually exclusive: you cannot simultaneously idealize, preserve exactly, and dismantle the same record. (4) So for any particular subject, at most one mode can apply. (5) Yet Nietzsche insists that healthy life requires all three in balance, which seems to demand that every subject be treated in all three ways at once. (6) Therefore the framework either contradicts itself or cannot guide practice, since it requires incompatible treatments of the same object. (7) If I keep (5), I must reject either (3) or (1). Where does the slip occur? Is the error in assuming that balance applies object-by-object rather than across a culture's overall historical needs? Or is there a hidden equivocation between treating an object and adopting an attitude toward it? Please explain which premise to abandon and what that costs for Nietzsche's therapeutic project.
