I am a student in a philosophy of perception seminar, and I am writing to ask for help adjudicating a disagreement that arose during our discussion of Molyneux's problem. The debate was between two of my classmates, Maya and Leo, and I was present as a bystander without taking sides. I will lay out the exchange as precisely as I can recall it, and then I ask you to referee it point by point.

Maya began by arguing that recent findings in cross-modal plasticity effectively resolve Molyneux's question in the negative, but not for the reasons usually given in textbooks. She claimed that congenitally blind subjects who learn to navigate haptic space using allocentric—world-centered—coordinate frames do not need to "translate" tactile information into visual terms upon gaining sight. Instead, both modalities can feed into a single, amodal spatial model that represents object geometry independently of sensory format. If a subject can identify a cube versus a sphere after sight restoration because both touch and vision update the same allocentric object file, then cross-modal recognition succeeds without any prior shared qualitative experience between the senses.

Leo responded that this argument changes the subject. The original problem, as Locke reported it from Molyneux, asks whether the ideas acquired through touch are the same as those acquired through sight—whether there is a direct conceptual or phenomenal correspondence. By introducing an intermediate, modality-neutral representation, Maya does not show that tactile and visual ideas align; she shows only that both can be mapped onto a third system. That is an engineering solution, not an epistemological one. If the allocentric frame does all the identifying work, then sight and touch remain conceptually isolated, and we have actually confirmed a version of the negative answer: the subject recognizes the object not because vision and touch share content, but because a computational intermediary correlates them.

Maya replied that Leo is imposing an unnecessarily internalist criterion. The functionally relevant question, she said, is whether a subject can genuinely identify the same object across modalities and integrate that knowledge into a unified perceptual life. If the amodal spatial model enables exactly that integration, then insisting on direct qualitative correspondence is like insisting that two people must speak the same language to understand one another, ignoring that translation can produce genuine understanding. She added that modern predictive processing frameworks treat perception precisely as the convergence of modality-specific prediction errors onto shared generative models, so Leo's "intermediary" is not an artificial add-on but the normal architecture of perception itself.

Leo countered that predictive processing only deepens his point. In those frameworks, each modality retains its own sensory-specific priors and its own precision-weighted errors; the shared generative model is an abstraction that never becomes phenomenally present as either visual or tactile. Therefore, when the sight-restored subject reports recognizing the cube, she is not experiencing a unified tactile-visual idea of cubeness—she is experiencing a visual prediction error being resolved against a model that was previously calibrated only by touch. The conceptual isolation remains at the level of experience, even if behavior is seamless. He concluded that Maya has solved the practical problem of cross-modal behavior but left the philosophical problem untouched: we still do not know whether there is a single idea of shape that belongs to both senses.

Please adjudicate this exchange. Specifically: who is right about the role of the amodal spatial representation—does it solve Molyneux's problem, change the subject, or confirm the negative answer in a new form? Where does Leo's predictive-processing objection actually land, and is Maya correct that functional integration is the only criterion that matters? Finally, what important point, if any, are both of them neglecting—perhaps regarding the distinction between recognition and identification, or the normative status of perceptual learning? Give your verdict with clear reasoning, and do not offer bibliographies, citation lists, or requests for further reading.
