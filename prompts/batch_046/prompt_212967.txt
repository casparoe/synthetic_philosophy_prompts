The comparison of church-state models—England’s established church, Germany’s Kirchensteuer collected by the state, France’s laïcité, and American disestablishment—has stalled for decades on the same surface question: is state involvement with religion neutral cooperation or illegitimate endorsement? Defenders of the German system call church-tax administration a purely logistical service that respects associative freedom; critics say it makes the state an agent of religious identification. English establishment defenders frame it as cultural heritage without theological coercion, while American separationists treat any fiscal entanglement as violating equal citizenship. My working theory is that the disagreement is not empirical—no one is waiting for a study on tax collection—but conceptual and possibly verbal: one side is asking whether democratic legitimacy requires that coercive laws be justifiable without shared religious premises, while the other is asking whether citizens have a right to organize religious membership through public institutions. Is the stall caused by these sides answering different questions—one about public reason and symbolic equality, the other about associative freedom and delegated sovereignty—under the single heading of secularism? Or is there a hidden empirical dispute about whether state-linked religious funding actually undermines social cohesion in plural societies, masked as a normative argument? Diagnose the structure using what each position actually claims about neutrality, membership, and justification. Is this a substantive disagreement about the conditions of democratic legitimacy, a verbal dispute over “neutrality,” or bedrock intuitions about the boundaries of collective identity? And given your diagnosis, what would count as progress—a distinction between neutrality of purpose and neutrality of effect, a comparative institutional finding, or should we expect the impasse to remain?
