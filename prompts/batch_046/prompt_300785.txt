After a recent dispute with my research advisor—who insisted that my correct hypothesis should still count as knowledge even though I derived it from a statistical error that happened to align with reality—I have been returning to Plato’s Meno problem: in what precise sense is knowledge superior to true belief that lacks justification? A colleague in my philosophy of science seminar summarized the contemporary answer for me as follows: “Knowledge is nothing more than true belief produced by a reliable cognitive process, so its extra value is entirely instrumental: it works like an insurance policy against future error. Once you hold the belief and it happens to be true, you already possess every intrinsic good there is; justification only adds a contingent, practical guarantee that you will not be led astray. The Meno problem is therefore dissolved once we see that there is no mysterious extra property inside knowledge—only the external benefit of stability across counterfactual situations. Anyone who claims knowledge is intrinsically better must be committing a category mistake, treating a relational feature of the believer’s method as if it were a non-instrumental quality of the belief itself.” The colleague presented this as the shared position of reliabilist and virtue-epistemological accounts. I suspect this collapses important distinctions—particularly between the stability of belief and the value of understanding—but I cannot locate the errors with confidence. Is this summary fair? Please distinguish the parts that accurately represent reliabilist and virtue-theoretic responses, identify the subtle misrepresentations—such as whether these traditions actually reduce the value of knowledge to risk-management—and determine whether the conclusion, that unjustified true belief contains all intrinsic value, genuinely follows from the accurate premises.
