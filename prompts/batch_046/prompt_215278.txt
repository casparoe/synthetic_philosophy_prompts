A fellow student in my seminar summarized the debate over historical objectivity as follows. Ranke held that the historian should recover past events exactly as they occurred, treating archival sources as transparent windows onto reality and excluding the historian’s present concerns as corruption. Carr replied that facts are never self-interpreting: historians necessarily select, order, and explain them through present-day questions, so Ranke’s mirror-like ideal is impossible. Hayden White then showed that every historical narrative is structured by deep linguistic tropes—metaphor, metonymy, synecdoche, irony—which govern not merely style but the very identification of events and causal relations. From this, the student concludes that White dissolves the Ranke-Carr disagreement into a single insight: both are producing literature disguised as science, with Carr merely more honest about his partisanship, while Ranke mistook his romantic emplotment for neutral description. The student further claims that because the problem is tropological rather than methodological, no institutional reform—better archives, peer review, or transparency—can restore objectivity; the failure is built into language itself. Which parts of this summary are faithful to Ranke, Carr, and White, and which are misrepresentations or overstatements? In particular, does White’s analysis of narrative form support the stronger claim that tropes determine causal identification? Are Carr’s scepticism about facts and White’s literary tropology genuinely the same kind of critique, or do they pull in different directions? And does the conclusion that institutional reform is useless actually follow from any of these positions, or does it conflate a claim about narrative form with a claim about epistemic practice?
