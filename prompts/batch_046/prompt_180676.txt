I'm developing a conceptual framework that tries to integrate the classical Buddhist emphasis on cetanā and karmic root (mūla) with the consequentialist demand that the actual reduction of dukkha carry independent normative weight. To test whether such integration is coherent, I want to see whether my intuitive verdicts on the following cases can be captured by a single principle, or whether some must be abandoned.

Case 1: A monastic lies to an armed pursuer about a refugee's hiding place. The mental root is unambiguous compassion (karuṇā), and the false speech prevents killing. Verdict: the act is ethically correct and generates bright kamma.

Case 2: A devout layperson sponsors an elaborate ritual cycle involving substantial ecological destruction and supply-chain suffering, but performs every action with pure devotional intention and no awareness of the distant harms. Verdict: the immediate karmic quality seems bright, yet I feel the overall welfare trajectory is poor; I am unsure whether to condemn, tolerate, or praise the act.

Case 3: A bodhisattva withholds a terminal diagnosis from a patient, not out of compassion but out of personal aversion to delivering suffering. By accident, the patient spends her remaining months in peaceful ignorance rather than despair. Verdict: despite the favorable outcome, I judge the act ethically poor because the root is defiled.

Case 4: A practitioner cultivates mettā with a concealed self-interested aim—securing rebirth in a favorable realm. The practice genuinely diminishes her hostility and improves her social environment. Verdict: the welfare effects are genuinely positive, but the karmic value is diluted; the root contaminates the merit.

Case 5: A physician administers a lethal dose to a consenting, terminally suffering patient. The physician's motivation is purely compassionate, with no personal gain, yet the act directly destroys life in violation of the first precept. Verdict: I oscillate. At times the elimination of extreme dukkha together with the pure root makes the act permissible; at other times the directness of killing renders the karmic weight irredeemably dark regardless of motivation.

Case 6: A teacher intends to deceive students for personal prestige, but an accidental error in her instructions leads them away from harmful practices. Verdict: poor root and accidental causation, yet a beneficial welfare trajectory; I think the evaluation should be negative, but I am not sure why it differs structurally from Case 3.

Is there a precise principle—perhaps one that assigns weights to root mental states, directness of causal control, and aggregate welfare trajectories—that accommodates Cases 1, 3, and 4 together? If so, state it exactly, indicate which clause is motivated by which case, and derive its verdict on Case 5 and on a further case: a government official whose primary motivation is career preservation but who knowingly approves a life-saving policy that requires sustained deception of the public, where the deception is instrumentally necessary rather than accidental. Most importantly, be honest about which of my six verdicts the principle forces me to overturn, and whether any revision to the principle that saves that verdict collapses the account into ad hoc complexity.
