I am drafting a one-year dissertation fellowship proposal in democratic theory, and I need help sharpening a half-formed idea into a precise, defensible research plan. The standard literature on the boundary problem—who belongs to the demos—treats it largely as a selection problem: we pick a criterion such as affected interests, stakeholder status, or citizenship, apply it to a pre-given population, and determine membership. In recent discussions with colleagues, however, I have become convinced that this framing obscures a reflexive dimension that is especially pronounced in contemporary governance. Many democratic decisions do not merely apply to an independently fixed set of people; they actively construct the very categories through which affectedness is recognized. A climate-adaptation policy, for instance, does not simply include or exclude existing refugees; it helps constitute who counts as a climate-affected subject and therefore retroactively shapes who should have been within the demos for that very decision. I want to ask whether democratic legitimacy can accommodate this kind of boundary reflexivity, or whether it necessarily requires an ex ante, non-circular determination of membership that is independent of the decision itself.

Please write the proposal. The central question should be stated precisely: can a reflexive model of the demos—where membership categories are partly constituted by the democratic decisions that invoke them—avoid vicious circularity and preserve democratic autonomy, and does this model resolve tensions that the standard affected-interests and stakeholder-based approaches leave untouched? Explain why existing treatments leave the issue open: they generally assume that the population affected by a decision is conceptually and causally prior to the democratic procedure that addresses it, which makes reflexivity look like a logical error rather than a structural feature. The approach should be conceptual analysis with structured thought experiments rather than comparative empirical study, since the core issue is coherence, not institutional prevalence. Specify what a successful result would look like—perhaps a taxonomy of reflexivity types (definitional, causal, and normative) together with conditions under which each is legitimate—and state clearly what would count as failure, such as demonstrating that any reflexivity collapses into either an infinitely expanding demos or an arbitrary closure that undermines accountability. Finally, give an honest assessment of scope: is this tractable for a single researcher within twelve months, or does it implicitly demand a full comparative institutional analysis that should be reserved for a larger project?
