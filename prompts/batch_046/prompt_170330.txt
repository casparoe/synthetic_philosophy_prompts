hey, i built this argument for a study group about why knowledge is better than just being right (the meno problem) and now it feels suspicious because the conclusion is kinda absurd but every step looks okay? here's the chain:

(1) if knowledge is actually more valuable than true belief, there has to be some feature F that knowledge has and true belief lacks.

(2) people usually say F is justification, or being tied to the fact in the right non-accidental way.

(3) but imagine this thing i'm calling "epistemic echoing": agent A knows p because she worked it out, and agent B gets p from A with full justifcation transmission and all the right causal links, but B literally doesn't grasp p - she just repeats it with perfect accuracy. B's belief is true and fully justified via transmission, but intuitively B doesn't really know it the way A does.

(4) so transmission of justification can't be F; there must be something like direct cognitive achievement or actual grasp.

(5) but if that's true, then anyone who learns from a textbook or teacher is just an echo too. that'd mean second-hand knowledge basically doesn't exist, which is cleary false.

(6) so either (4) is wrong and echo-beliefs do count as knowledge, or (5) overgenralizes. but if we save knowledge by saying B knows p after all, then we haven't actually solved the meno problem - we've just said true belief plus good transmission equals knowledge, which feels circular.

(7) so the only way out seems to be denying (3) and saying B really does know p, but then why does it feel like B is missing something?

where does this go wrong? i'd rather keep the intution that B is missing something, but i also really don't want to say that learning from books is useless.
