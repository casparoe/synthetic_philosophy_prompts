I'm working on a novel where one of my characters, Dr. Lena Voss, is this moral philosopher who keeps giving these snap judgements on harm-aggregation puzzles, and I need her reasoning to feel consistent but not too predictable. So far in the manuscript she's said that torturing one innocent person to prevent a billion people getting a dust speck in their eye is flat wrong; she's said that distributing a barely-noticeable itch across every human alive to stop an active genocide is not just allowed but actually obligatory; she's said that letting one person suffer a full migraine to spare ten thousand people a fleeting disappointment is wrong; she rejects counting up well-being points across different people like you were balancing a chequebook; and she once mentioned a hypothetical machine that could spread one second of mild confusion across every conscious mind rather than inflicting lifelong agony on a single victim, and she said that'd still be wrong, though she muttered something about salience and quickly changed the subject.

Now the plot throws up this scenerio: there's an AI climate-modelling system that can either divert a hurricane away from a coastal city of about two million people, but in doing so it generates an infinitessimal thermal noise in literally every water molecule on Earth — no creature feels it, but if you aggregate the total physical disturbance it technically dwarfs the damage the storm would cause. Or it can stay silent and do nothing. Given her earlier verdicts, what do you think she'd say? I'm less interested in the bare answer than in the competing rules she might actually be running — maybe a hard non-aggregationist line with a hidden salience threshold, maybe a form of rule-consequentialism that only activates for sentient targets, maybe something about direct versus structural causation. Which of her previous cases best pull those interpretations apart, and what's the one extra hypothetical I should write into her next chapter dialogue to actually force her hand between them?
