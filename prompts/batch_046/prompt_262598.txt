I've been thinking about pain asymbolia — those rare cases where people report feeling pain but dont act like it hurts, they dont avoid the source, and even seem kind of indifferent about it. I want to defend a kind of 'exit-cost' view: pain is bad not because of its raw phenomenal feel, and not just becuase it represents tissue damage, but because it functions as an unavoidable resource-drain that forces you to redirect attention and behavior. Asymbolia shows the sensory part can stay intact while the 'drain' part is missing, and in those cases the pain apparently isnt bad for the person. So the badness is in the forced reallocation, not the feeling itself.

What would actually count against this? Not just the usual 'well they still suffer' line — I want the hard defeaters. For instance: if asymbolic patients still show disrupted sleep or elevated stress hormones, does that secretly restore the exit-cost? If long-term studies show they eventually develop avoidance after repeated exposure, does that prove the cost was always there, just delayed? And phantom limb pain has no tissue damage and no real resource drain, yet patients rate it awful — does that kill the view, or can I absorb it by saying the brain is running a phantom cost-routine?

Also, what does this view predict that rival accounts (say, pure representationalism or the standard affective-motivational story) dont? Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and flag which ones I'd have to bite the bullet on versus which ones actually force me to revise. Finally, be honest: have I qualified the view into unfalsifiability by tying badness so tightly to the cost? Is that insulation a problem for a claim like this?
