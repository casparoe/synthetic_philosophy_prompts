hey — im writing a short feature on whether self-deception is even coherent, and a colleague walked me into this argument. it feels like it proves too much: either you're totally off the hook or fully culpable, with no middle ground. but i cant find the exit.

(1) Self-deception requires one mental part knowing p and another part not knowing p, with the first actively hiding p from the second.

(2) Beliefs are transparent: if p sits in your belief-box, you automatically know you hold it.

(3) So the only way to "hide" p from yourself is to keep it outside the box — in sub-doxastic processsing, emotional circuits, etc.

(4) Moral responsibility, though, only tracks what's inside the belief-box; sub-personal noise doesn't count.

(5) Therefore if you're "self-deceived" about a bad motive, that motive was never in your box (so you're not responsible), and if it was in the box you knew all along (so you're fully responsible).

(6) So the excuse "i was deceiving myself" never gives partial cover; it's either total absolution or a flat lie.

(7) Hence self-deception isn't just impossible — it's conceptually useless for ethics.

where does it go wrong? i want to hang on to (2) if i can, but im open to paying the price elsewhere. which step should i reject, why did it look okay, and what do i have to give up if i drop it? please be explicit about how confident you are and name the point you're least sure about. end with your verdict in \answer{...} — just the step number.
