I hold a reconstructed view drawn from Émilie du Châtelet’s Discours sur le bonheur: that happiness is not the maximisation of pleasure or the isolated exercise of virtue, but a stable, interdependent arrangement of intellectual passion, bodily health, romantic attachment, and—crucially—beneficial illusion. On this reading, illusions such as an idealised image of one’s beloved or an exaggerated sense of one’s intellectual progress are not merely instrumental aids that reason may safely discard once their purpose is served; rather, they are constitutive conditions of the passions that sustain study and love. Consequently, to dissolve such an illusion through rational correction is not to achieve clarity at negligible cost, but necessarily to diminish the happiness that depended upon it. I want to know what would genuinely count against this position. Beyond the obvious reply that some people appear happy without illusion, which specific observations, conceptual tensions, or findings from other domains—say, empirical work on self-deception and relationship stability, or conceptual arguments about the compatibility of truth-seeking study with sustained misapprehension—would threaten the view? Which defeaters would a committed defender concede as damaging, and which would they dismiss—perhaps by distinguishing the right kind of illusion from the wrong, or by arguing that rational correction can be partial rather than total? Please rank the objections from fatal (the view collapses into incoherence or is empirically refuted) to merely awkward (it requires an ad hoc qualification). Finally, assess whether this position risks being insulated by qualifications: if every instance in which happiness survives the loss of illusion is explained away as involving a different good, or every failure is attributed to the wrong species of illusion, has the theory become unfalsifiable, and is that a defect for a conception of happiness of this kind?
