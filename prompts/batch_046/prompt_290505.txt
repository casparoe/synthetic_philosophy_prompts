Been arguing with my rationalist reading group about whether Lewis-style conventions actually explain how forum norms evolve. We all read the standard stuff—common knowledge, coordination problems, the phone-book example—but the debate's been stuck for months. One side says norms like 'cite your sources' or 'don't sealion' are classic Lewis conventions: everyone prefers coordination on clear rules, and we all know we all know it. The other side says that's naive, because in practice people have wildly different preferences (some want rigorous debate, others want vibes), there's no shared preference ordering, and the 'norm' is really just a power dynamic enforced by moderators and status games. They call it an adversarial convention—stabilised not by mutual benefit but by mutual detterence, like a cold war over interation protocols.

My working theory is that we're not actually disagreeing about sociology; we're using 'convention' for two different things. One group means 'solution to a pure coordination problem' in the technical Lewis sense, the other means 'any stable pattern of behaviour that regulates interaction' regardless of whether preferences align. But I'm not sure—maybe there's a hidden empirical claim about whether people actually have common knowledge of preferences in these spaces, or maybe it's about what counts as an explanation at all.

Can you diagnose what's actually going on? Is this verbal, substantive, or a case of two different questions being answered under one heading? And if your diagnosis is right, what would actually move it—a conceptual distinction, an empirical study we'd never actually run, or is this just bedrock intuition about whether social order is cooperative or conflictual? Don't need a survey of Lewis interpreters, just the structure of the disagreement and whether progress is even possible here.
