I'm on the resource-allocation board for our regional mental-health authority (not a philosopher, just a clinician who got roped in). We have a one-time grant of about 2 million and two competing proposals. Proposal A spreads the money across ~100 patients with moderate chronic depression for standard CBT and meds — expected outcome is meaningful but modest quality-of-life gains for each. Proposal B spends almost the whole grant on a single patient with an extremely rare neurological profile (something like congenital analgesia combined with an amplified reward response) who reports subjective well-being that clinicians estimate is an order of magnitude above baseline; maintaining her minimal intervention costs about 1.8m, leaving almost nothing for anyone else.

Half the board says this is basically the utility-monster problem in a lab coat and we should fund B because total welfare dominates. The other half says there's a hidden distinction between 'raw hedonic utility' and 'authentic well-being' — her happiness comes from a wiring quirk, not from relationships, achievement, or autonomy, so it shouldn't count the same way in allocation, and also that concentrating resources violates a floor of decent care for the 100. A few people also mentioned that if we approve B we're saying mental-health funding can be monopolized by anyone with an extreme subjective scale, which sets a weird precedent for future cases.

I need to circulate a short memo before Thursday's vote. Can you draft it? Give the bottom-line recommendation right up front (approve A, approve B, or split with conditions and a timeline), then the reasoning. Keep it under 350 words. I want the memo to take the 'authentic well-being' objection seriously rather than just dismissing it, and also address wether the utility-monster framing actually applies when the high-utility state depends on a medical anomaly. No citations needed — just make the argument tight and actionable.
