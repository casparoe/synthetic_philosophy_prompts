I'm putting together a taxonomy handout for a bio-philosophy study group — not a quiz with points, but a decision tree that sorts people by what they actually think counts as alive, not by which theory-label they like. The audience is grad students who already know the usual suspects (functionalism, evolutionary accounts, informational views) but keep conflating organizational closure with autopoiesis. I want the tree built from four concrete borderline cases: a dormant bacterial spore with zero metabolism, a computer virus that replicates and mutates using host hardware, a hypothetical synthetic grey-membrane replicator that eats minerals and builds copies but has no DNA or evolutionary history, and a perfused human brain after cardiac death with no consciousness. For each case the respondent picks alive, dead, or genuinely unclear / border case.

The deliverable should be the logical space first — list the 3^4 answer patterns — then map named positions onto them: the metabolic/homeostasis camp, the Darwinian/evolutionary camp, the informational/replicator camp, a strict vitalist-leaning view (if anyone still holds it), and the deflationary no-sharp-boundary-family-resemblance option. Flag any unoccupied patterns and briefly say why they're empty (incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored). In particular, flag the pattern where someone calls the synthetic replicator alive but the computer virus dead, since that combo seems unstable unless there's a hidden splitter. And I want you to inject one niche axis: treat capacity for self-repair of the organizational boundary using only internally processed resources as a hidden branch. So if a respondent includes the spore but excludes the synthetic replicator, the tree should diagnose them as smuggling in evolutionary lineage as a tiebreaker, not pure metabolism.

Also note which pairs of leaves the tree can't really seperate — I suspect the organizational view and the deflationary view overlap on the brain case, but point out whatever you find.

Please keep it casual; this is for a study group, not a journal. And say how confident you are in the mapping overall, and single out the one point you're least sure of — probably whether the hidden boundary-repair axis actually distinguishes anything in practice, but you decide.
