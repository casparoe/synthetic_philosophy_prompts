You are a philosopher of rationality and artificial intelligence with experience teaching logic-minded students who have no philosophy background. I need a teaching plan, not the explanation itself, for a 75-minute session with second-year computer science undergraduates who have taken one course in logic but nothing in decision theory or philosophy of mind. The goal is for them to understand embedded agency—not as a generic puzzle about self-reference, but specifically as the structural problem that arises when an agent must model a world in which it is physically embedded and causally coupled to the very processes it is reasoning about.

To keep the session from collapsing into material they have already seen a hundred times, I want to build it around a single carrying example I have designed: the Mirror-Box scenario. In this scenario, an agent sits inside a sealed, causally closed system. Inside the same system runs a perfect predictive model of the agent that executes in real time and displays its output—“Your decision: D”—on a screen the agent can observe. The agent knows that such a model exists, but it does not know whether it is the original agent observing its own simulation, or an instance running inside the simulation observing the display of the original agent’s decision. The agent must choose whether to cooperate or defect against an identically structured counterpart in an adjacent box, but its choice immediately updates the model, which immediately updates the screen it sees.

Plan the explanatory arc. First, briefly brainstorm the distinction between embedded agency, Cartesian skepticism, and strategic game-theoretic reasoning, since the students will conflate all three. Then specify:

1. The one prerequisite idea that must be in place before the Mirror-Box example can appear puzzling rather than merely like a science-fiction gadget.
2. The exact order in which the conceptual components must be introduced, with the dependency relations made explicit—what must be understood before what, and why.
3. The properties the Mirror-Box example must have and the properties it must lack in order to carry the idea of embedded agency rather than collapsing into an epistemic puzzle about “how do I know I’m real” or a standard prisoner's dilemma.
4. The misconceptions these students are likely to bring or form—treating the agent as an external observer of the model, assuming the model is just a fast calculator, interpreting the puzzle as being about free will, or reducing it to a standard information asymmetry—and the precise point in the sequence where each misconception should be headed off.
5. A check for understanding at the end that demonstrates they have grasped embedded agency specifically, as opposed to merely being unsettled by the scenario or having learned a new vocabulary word.
6. Which standard references and examples—such as Newcomb's problem, the halting problem, or brain-in-a-vat arguments—should be deliberately left out at this level, with a brief justification for each omission.

Please think carefully about the dependency structure and the misconception map. Do not ask for readings, citations, or bibliographies. Do not include quotations from real texts. Make sure the plan addresses why the agent’s physical embedding in the causal loop is structurally different from being an outside observer who happens to be uncertain about a model.
