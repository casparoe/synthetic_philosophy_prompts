Next month I’m running one session of a casual philosophy circle at the library. The group is about eight people, mostly retired teachers and engineers, sharp but with no formal phil background. They’ve all read Douglas Hofstadter’s “Person Paper” ahead of time, and I want to lead them through why the satire lands: the hidden default that “he” stands for everyone, and how flipping gender in every noun makes the bias visible.

I need a teaching plan for the session, not the lecture itself. Please organize it like this:

- Dependency chain (around 150 words): what idea has to be in place before the next one can land, and in what order, so they don’t just laugh at the joke and miss the structural point.
- The carrying example (around 100 words): the one concrete comparison or scenario that should carry the whole explanation — what it must include and what it should avoid.
- Misconceptions to head off (around 150 words): the wrong takes they’ll bring in — probably “it’s just grammar, not politics” or “he was always meant to be generic” — and exactly where in the sequence to defuse each one.
- Check for understanding (around 75 words): one question or brief exercise at the end that shows whether they’ve grasped the logic of the satire rather than just agreeing that sexism is bad.
- What to leave out and say so (around 50 words): the parts that are too in the weeds for a single session with this crowd.

One more thing: please seperate what is settled (for instance, that generic masculine pronouns have been documented and critiqued) from what is genuinely contested among people who actually study this (for instance, whether changing pronoun habits measurably shifts implicit bias). Don’t present a contested view as the consensus.
