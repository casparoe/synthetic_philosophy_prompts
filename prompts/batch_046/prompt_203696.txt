Read William Golding’s Lord of the Flies through David Lewis’s account of convention as developed in Convention, focusing specifically on the conch as a coordination device. Begin by treating the initial rules of assembly—whoever holds the conch may speak, and the others listen—as a pure Lewisian solution to a coordination problem about orderly deliberation, sustained by common knowledge and mutual expectation of benefit. Then ask how the conch acquires normative thickness: violating it comes to feel not merely inefficient but like a betrayal of the group itself, and Ralph treats its authority as something that binds even those who no longer share the rescue goal. Does Lewis’s framework, which treats conventions as arbitrary equilibria stabilized by rational expectation, have the resources to explain that normative binding without importing an independent idea of recognition or authority? Next, examine the moment when Jack’s faction has changed the payoff structure from cooperation to domination: Ralph still invokes the conch, but the common knowledge that everyone prefers rescue has collapsed. Does Golding show that the convention dissolves cleanly once shared interest disappears, or does he dramatize something Lewis’s static analysis misses—namely, that a convention can persist as a hollow, mocked form even after the conditions for its rational stability are gone? I want a reading that takes both the novel’s political psychology and Lewis’s philosophy seriously: identify exactly where the conch illuminates Lewis’s distinction between convention and contract, where Golding complicates it by showing norms surviving as symbolic shells, and what the novel adds to the philosophical debate about how conventions evolve and die that argument alone does not capture. End by saying whether the ending of the novel vindicates a purely expectation-based account of norms or demands something closer to an evolutionary story about symbolic investment.
