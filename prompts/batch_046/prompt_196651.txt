Argue that promises generate binding obligations not through the promisor’s reliance interests, nor through a bare expression of autonomous will, but through the creation of a second-personal accountability relation that involves what we might call a transfer of practical authority. Specifically, defend the view that when A promises B to φ, A does not merely commit herself to φ-ing; she grants B a limited, defeasible authority to hold her to account for φ-ing, and this authority is normatively valid only if both parties share a mutual recognition of contingency—that is, an explicit or implicit acknowledgment that the future is genuinely open and that B’s practical reasoning legitimately depends on A’s voluntary constraint of her own options. On this account, a promise binds because it restructures the normative landscape between A and B: B acquires a standing reason to plan around φ, not because she expects A to φ in the sense of predictive reliance, but because A has made herself answerable to B for φ-ing, and B is entitled to treat A’s failure as a specific kind of wrong—a breach of granted authority rather than a mere disappointment. Defend this view against two objections. First, that it cannot explain why promises to strangers bind, since there is no prior relationship in which authority could be granted; reply by showing how the speech act itself, under conditions of mutual recognition, can instantiate the authority relation ex nihilo without requiring a pre-existing bond. Second, that it collapses into an autonomy-based account because the grant of authority is itself an autonomous act; in addressing this, distinguish sharply between the autonomy of the act of promising and the content of the obligation created, and argue that the authority-transfer model explains why breaking a promise wrongs the promisee specifically, in a way that breaking a private resolution does not. Conclude by applying the framework to a hard case: a promise made under conditions of significant but non-coercive emotional pressure, where the promisor’s autonomy is not undermined but the mutual recognition of contingency is arguably distorted. Does the promise still bind, and with what strength? In your answer, state the principle precisely, identify which clauses are motivated by which cases, and be explicit about any intuitive verdicts the framework must overturn.
