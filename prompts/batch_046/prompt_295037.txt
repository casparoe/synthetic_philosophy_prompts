I take the view that the standard tripartite division of European intellectual history into the Middle Ages, the Renaissance, and the Scientific Revolution is not a neutral descriptive taxonomy but a normative historiographical artifact—one that projects nineteenth-century disciplinary boundaries onto a continuous field of practice and does particular conceptual violence by treating scholasticism and humanism as mutually exclusive orientations rather than as coexisting institutional grammars. I am especially persuaded by the continuity thesis in natural philosophy—that mechanistic explanations in the seventeenth century often extend, rather than rupture, late-medieval impetus and quantification programs—and by the geographical restriction of self-described Renaissance practices to narrow urban and courtly contexts outside of which scholastic methods persisted unchanged. What I want is an honest map of what would genuinely count against this anti-periodization stance, not merely the familiar reply that “change happened.” Specifically: which archival discoveries about the transmission of texts or instruments, which reconstructions of actors’ own categories, which formal properties of knowledge-transmission networks, or which philosophical commitments about identity over time would force me to concede that these three labels capture something real and non-arbitrary? For each potential defeater, indicate whether a committed anti-periodizationist could absorb it by redrawing boundaries rather than abandoning the core claim, and whether that absorptive strategy itself becomes a theoretical liability. Rank the defeaters from fatal (requiring abandonment of the central thesis) to merely awkward (requiring only local qualification). Also address the risk of unfalsifiability: if I define a “real historical period” so narrowly that no pattern of continuity or discontinuity could ever qualify, is that a defect of my view or a defensible feature of rigorous historiography? In particular, state what my position predicts about the social diffusion of techniques or the self-categorization of sixteenth-century practitioners that the standard tripartite scheme does not predict, so that I can see whether my stance is genuinely exposed to empirical risk or merely redescribes the same data. Finally, identify the crux—whether about the ontology of intellectual change, about the self-understanding of historical actors, or about the conditions for genuine conceptual rupture—on which both defenders and critics of standard periodization agree that the debate should turn, and record what each side predicts about it. Be willing to conclude that my view is well insulated and say whether that insulation is a problem for a claim of this kind.
