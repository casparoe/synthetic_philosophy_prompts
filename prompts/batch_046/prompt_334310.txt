I'm in a decision-theory seminar, and I've come to defend a qualified version of Good's theorem. The position is this: for any agent who updates beliefs via conditionalization, has state-dependent but separable utility, and faces a fixed choice set regardless of what she learns, acquiring cost-free information can never reduce ex ante expected utility. I take the standard counterexamples—strategic ignorance in coordination games, the "curse of knowledge," or emotional costs of learning—to violate either separability or the fixed-choice-set condition, and therefore to be priced-out instrumental costs rather than genuine failures of the theorem. What I want is an honest map of what would genuinely count against this qualified claim. Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward: are there formal results showing negative expected value even when all three conditions hold? Are there well-formed decision problems in which the choice set is genuinely endogenous to the information received—through institutional rules, self-binding commitments, or social mechanisms—that cannot be redescribed as pre-existing costs built into the "free" condition? Which intuitions about rationally "not wanting to know" survive if we grant separability and a fixed menu? Finally, if every apparent counterexample can indeed be dissolved by tightening these conditions, does that insulation make the view stronger, or has it been qualified into unfalsifiability in a way that undermines its usefulness for decision theory? I want the assessment to include what a committed defender of the position should themselves concede as counting against it, not only what critics say.
