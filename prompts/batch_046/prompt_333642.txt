at a dinner last week my friends leah and darius got into it about incommensurable values, specifically whether picking between two genuinely incomparable goods leaves some kind of lingering moral residue or whether the choice just closes the matter cleanly. i was just sitting there with my pasta getting cold but they kept turning to me like i should referee.

leah started by saying if values are really incommensurable—like saving a strangers life versus preserving a centuries-old art practice that has no replacement—then theres no rational scale to weigh them on. you cant say one is 3.2 units better. so she argued rational choice theory just breaks down here; youre left with arbitrary will or emotional pull, and calling it rational is just dressing up preference.

darius pushed back. he said thats too quick. even without a common metric, you can reason about which option fits the kind of agent you are, or which one youd stand behind if you had to explain yourself to someone you respect. he called this structural coherence—not a hidden ruler, but a second-order check on whether your choice hangs together with your other commitments.

leah said that sounds nice but its just smuggling in coherence as a hidden value. now youve translated both options into what kind of person am i, and youve commensurated them by that back door. she said thats not honoring incommensurability, thats dissolving it.

then darius brought up this idea he read somewhere—he called it value shadowing—where after you pick one option, the other value doesnt disappear but casts a normative shadow over your life. like if you save the stranger but let the art practice die, youre not just done; youve incurred a specific kind of moral remainder that shapes what you owe going foward.

leah loved that. she said thats exactly what incommensurability feels like—not paralysis, but a permanent, non-fungible debt to the value you sacrificed. she said if you dont feel that shadow, you probably didnt really understand that the values were incomparable in the first place.

darius got annoyed and said thats romatic nonsense. once youve made the best choice available, the unchosen path is rationally silenced. if you carry around value shadows forever youre just fetishizing regret and turning every decision into an unpayable debt. he said the point of hard-choice thinking is to show you can choose well without a scale, not that you have to carry every road not taken like a ghost.

leah replied hes confusing psychological guilt with structural residue. the shadow isnt guilt—its an objective feature of the normative landscape after the choice. the art practice is still gone, and that fact continues to generate reasons (maybe to document it, honor its memory, avoid such tradeoffs lightly) that wouldnt exist if youd saved it.

they asked me to adjudicate: is there actually this lingering normative shadow after an incommensurable choice, or is darius right that rational closure is possible? does structural coherence really avoid smuggling in a common measure? and is carrying that residue paralyzing or appropriate?

please go point by point: whos right about incommensurability breaking rational choice, about coherence being a hidden metric, about whether theres objective residue or just psychological regret, and about whether holding onto that residue is paralyzing or fitting. then say who wins overall, and point out what both seem to be missing—maybe something about how the shadow might differ depending on whether the values are agent-relative or agent-neutral? i honestly couldnt follow that part and im probably misusing the terms, but they seemed to think it mattered.
