In the long-running dispute over whether “left” and “right” denote real ideological families or merely local coalitional labels, the argument has stalled despite broad agreement on the evidence: empirical researchers cite multidimensional issue spaces, weak cross-national correlations, and frequent position-reversals (for example, each side flipping on free-expression norms or on centralization depending on which holds state power), while normative theorists insist that each camp is still unified by a deep structural commitment—protecting the vulnerable versus preserving legitimate hierarchy, or something close to it. Both sides now accept the same survey patterns and the same historical cases of reversal. My working hypothesis is that the impasse is not about hidden facts but about explanatory standards: one side demands a unifying normative principle to ground coherence, while the other treats strategic adversarial alignment—defining left and right primarily by negative reference to the other camp’s current institutional position rather than by shared positive commitments—as sufficient for a real distinction. Is this a substantive disagreement about the ontology of ideologies, a verbal dispute over what “coherence” requires, or are the two sides answering different questions—one descriptive-sociological, one normative-structural—under one heading? Diagnose it with reference to what each side actually says about cross-national reversal and about whether adversarial negative reference can support a genuine cleavage. Then tell me what, if anything, could move it: might a distinction between synchronic value-clusters and diachronic coalitional loyalty help, is there an empirical pattern that separates principled consistency from strategic opposition, or is the question itself ill-posed?
