I am preparing a long-form essay on the European democratic deficit, and I want to use the 2005 rejection of the Treaty establishing a Constitution for Europe by French and Dutch voters as a disciplined case study—not for the treaty's legal architecture, which is well documented, but for what the vote patterns reveal about sovereignty, legitimacy, and the logic of integration itself. Analyze the episode through the lens of democratic-deficit theory and the doctrine of ever-closer union, treating the referendum results not merely as a popular veto but as empirical evidence about how citizens understand the delegation of authority across levels of government. Specifically: distinguish input legitimacy from output legitimacy, but then test whether this binary holds up when applied to the 2005 campaigns, in which voters apparently conflated concerns about market liberalization, immigration, and institutional opacity with the constitutional text itself. Ask whether the episode confirms that democratic deficits are domain-specific—more acute in monetary and fiscal governance than in regulatory harmonization—or whether it shows that citizens experience all pooled sovereignty as a single, undifferentiated loss of control. Then turn the analysis back on the theory: does the failure of the Constitutional Treaty suggest that ever-closer union is structurally incompatible with national democratic accountability, or does it instead reveal that the deficit is a contingent product of deliberative failure that could be corrected without abandoning integration? Be precise about where democratic-deficit theory predicts voter behavior correctly, where it is silent on the symbolic and identitarian dimensions of the 2005 votes, and whether the episode forces a revision of the standard claim that legitimacy in the EU must be rebuilt primarily through the European Parliament. I need this for a general audience of scientifically literate readers; do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations to primary literature, and do not invoke obscure institutional histories I could not verify from common knowledge. Where the theory offers no clear answer, say so explicitly.
