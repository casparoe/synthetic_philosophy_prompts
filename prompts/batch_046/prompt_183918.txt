I am drafting a short conceptual essay that attempts to reframe debates about greedy reductionism by dividing the standard notion of a crane into two varieties. The first, which I call a vertical crane, builds upward through compositional levels: outputs or patterns stabilized at one level become the inputs, constraints, or scaffolding for explanations at the next. The second, a horizontal crane, operates laterally within a single level, explaining macro-patterns through distributed interactions among entities of the same type—gene networks, neuronal populations, or market agents—without invoking autonomous higher-level dynamics.

My central claim is that defenders of greedy reductionism typically do not reject cranes altogether; rather, they accept horizontal cranes while denying that vertical scaffolding ever produces genuinely irreducible explanatory structure. What looks like a skyhook—an unexplained top-down cause—is, in their view, always reducible to summed horizontal interactions across enough bottom-level entities. Conversely, critics of greedy reductionism often accuse reductionists of ignoring emergent levels, but they rarely specify whether they are demanding vertical cranes with real downward causation, or merely insisting that horizontal explanations at the bottom become computationally intractable without intermediate-level summaries.

To test this framing, I apply it to the explanation of eusociality in insects. A greedy reductionist account explains worker altruism through kin-selection dynamics and gene-frequency interactions operating horizontally across individuals. An anti-reductionist account appeals to colony-level selection: differential survival and reproduction of whole colonies that feed back downward to stabilize worker phenotypes. I argue that the real disagreement is not about mysterious group-minds or skyhooks, but about whether colony-level selection constitutes a vertical crane—creating a genuinely autonomous intermediate level with downward causal efficacy—or whether it is merely a bookkeeping aggregation of horizontal genetic interactions summed across many workers.

Could you comment on the coherence and accuracy of this framing? In particular: (a) Does splitting cranes into vertical and horizontal varieties actually illuminate the reductionism debate, or does it merely rename familiar positions? (b) Is it correct to characterize greedy reductionists as accepting horizontal but rejecting vertical scaffolding, rather than rejecting all non-fundamental explanation? (c) In the eusociality case, is there an underlying empirical disagreement about whether colony-level models capture dynamics that bottom-level gene-network models miss, or is the dispute purely conceptual—about what should count as an irreducible explanatory level?
