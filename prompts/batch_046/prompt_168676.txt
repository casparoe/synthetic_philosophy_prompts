Write a dialogue between an anthropologist and a normative theorist arguing over whether corruption is culture-relative or structurally universal. The anthropologist defends "reciprocity spirals"—long-term gift loops that build social capital—and claims Western anti-bribery rules miss the welfare gains from these moral compromises. The theorist holds that corruption is simply private obligation overriding public role, regardless of context, though he concedes Western lobbying hides the same pattern. They should clash over a concrete case: a municipal water commissioner who receives ceremonial post-project gifts from contractors. The anthropologist frames this as a clean temporal separation—decision first, gratitude second, so no distortion occurs, just a gain in mutual trust. The theorist should introduce the "temporal collapse" problem: in repeating relationships, today's post-hoc gift functions as tomorrow's pre-commitment, so the compromise isn't eliminated but distributed across time, creating hidden obligation. Make sure they actually answer each other—she should address whether hidden obligation matters if outcomes stay good, he should address whether breaking trust networks is a real harm or just a different preference. Keep it real, no strawmen, and dont have them list books or cite authors.
