I'm serving as a TA for a graduate seminar on philosophy of mind, and I was recently discussing with a colleague whether Hofstadter's strange loop could preserve its causal topology across two brains linked not by technology but by sustained linguistic co-construction—what we might call an inter-brain loop. That conversation shaped an exam question I designed on distributed identity, and now I'm grading responses. I want a dispassionate second opinion on one answer that strikes me as sophisticated but may smuggle in subtle conceptual errors that I'm inclined to overlook; I want to avoid both the Scylla of being overly charitable and the Charybdis of penalizing a genuinely creative extension of the framework.

The exam question asked: Consider Douglas Hofstadter's claim that the self is not a substance but a self-referential symbol—a strange loop—that emerges when a brain represents itself. Now imagine a neuroscientist, Dr. A, who suffers progressive damage to her episodic memory centers, while her long-time partner, Dr. B, gradually comes to hold the majority of A's autobiographical memories through years of intimate co-narration, reconstructing A's past in response to her cues. Over the same period, B has adopted many of A's core decision-making patterns and values through sustained empathic modeling. Applying Hofstadter's framework, is there a coherent argument that A's I has partially migrated into B's brain? And what limits does Hofstadter place on this migration, particularly regarding the causal continuity of the loop?

The student answered as follows. Hofstadter defines the self as a strange loop, a pattern of self-reference realized in neural tissue but not tied to any particular neurons. Because the loop is substrate-independent in principle, its symbolic content can be distributed: B's brain now physically instantiates a large portion of the loop originally centered in A, since B holds A's episodic memories and shares her value structures. This aligns with Hofstadter's observation that we carry representations—almost secondary souls—of loved ones inside our own brains; in this case, B's representation of A is so causally integrated and detailed that it functions not as a mere copy but as an active fragment of the original loop.

That said, complete migration is not achieved by informational overlap alone. Hofstadter's account is essentially continuous with Derek Parfit's psychological-continuity theory: identity persists through the continuity of memory and personality, regardless of the physical substrate. Because A's brain remains present, though degraded, the original loop is still anchored there, while B hosts a parallel continuation. The decisive limit is causal dominance: if B's representations become the primary drivers of A's behavior—acting as the center of gravity for her remaining choices—then the loop's core has effectively relocated to B, leaving only a hollow shell in A's damaged cortex. Partial migration is therefore coherent, but full transfer requires that the partner's loop not only mirror but actively direct the original neural activity.
