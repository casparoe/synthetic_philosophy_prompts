I am presenting a paper at next month’s History of Economic Thought workshop defending a non-standard reading of Keynes’s distinction between risk and uncertainty in Chapter 12 of The General Theory. My thesis is that Keynes was not merely describing an epistemic limit—our practical inability to form numerical probabilities about future investment returns—but was advancing an implicit ontological claim: that economic time is structurally non-ergodic, so that for many long-run expectations there simply is no stable sample space over which a probability distribution could be defined, even in principle. I further argue that this reading illuminates his otherwise puzzling claim that the weight of argument can increase without altering the balance of reasons, because weight tracks the robustness of the sample-space framing itself rather than the strength of evidence within a fixed frame. The audience will be about fifteen scholars: roughly half are Cambridge-school historians who defend the conventional interpretation that Keynes was concerned primarily with practical ignorance and the psychology of long-term expectation; two are philosophers of science who are skeptical about attributing formal ontological commitments to an author who never explicitly endorsed non-ergodicity; and one senior macroeconomist who regards the entire risk-uncertainty literature as rhetorical window-dressing for liquidity preference.

I need a speaker’s Q&A prep sheet ordered by how damaging each objection is likely to be, with each objection phrased in the vocabulary that specific subgroup would actually use. For each, indicate what I should concede up front, where I should hold firm, and the weakest point in my reply—especially the point where my ontological reading risks over-interpreting Keynes’s silence on formal statistical mechanics. Include the objection I am most hoping nobody raises: the challenge that if uncertainty is genuinely ontological and non-ergodic in the way I suggest, then Keynes’s own policy recommendations—discretionary state intervention guided by what he called animal spirits—collapse into incoherence, since there would be no stable causal structure for policy to act upon. Please treat this as a prep sheet for the speaker rather than a general defense of Keynesian economics, and avoid asking for bibliographies or citation lists.
