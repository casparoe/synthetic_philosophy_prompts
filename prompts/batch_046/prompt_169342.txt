Over the past several months I have been navigating a prolonged caregiving decision in which my immediate moral intuitions conflict with the prescriptions I derive from reflecting on what I would want an ideally rational version of myself to authorize on my behalf. This has led me to think seriously about indirect normativity—specifically, whether normative force can be mediated through an idealized deliberative proxy rather than applied directly to one’s present mental states—and about how such idealization ought to be constrained when an agent is only partially transparent to herself regarding her own future preferences and emotional dynamics. Please explain, at an intuitive yet conceptually precise level, the core tension between direct and indirect normativity in cases of partial self-opacity: why might a proponent hold that the correct course of action is not what the present agent judges best in the moment, but rather what an idealized counterpart—who lacks access to the agent’s current emotional valence yet retains her long-term preference structure and commitment to coherence—would prescribe for her actual self? In your explanation, distinguish this position carefully from simple future-self paternalism and from standard expected-utility maximization, and clarify exactly what philosophical work the idealization is doing (for instance, whether it functions as a filter on bias, as a coherence condition on diachronic agency, or as something else). I am looking for conceptual architecture rather than a survey of authors or texts, and I do not want bibliographies, reading lists, or citations. Please also indicate, for each major claim you make, how confident you are in it, and explicitly single out the specific point about which you are least certain—particularly regarding whether the idealized counterpart’s lack of present emotional access should be regarded as a feature that corrects for distortion or as a bug that severs normativity from lived experience.
