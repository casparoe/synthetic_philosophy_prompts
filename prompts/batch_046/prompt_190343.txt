I've been working through a particular formulation of disjunctivism about perceptual experience—the view that veridical perception and illusion are not two species of a shared phenomenal kind but fundamentally different mental states, with no common "feel" that survives the distinction. I find it compelling for classic argument-from-illusion cases, but I'm trying to understand exactly how exposed this position is if we extend its scope beyond the textbook examples.

Specifically, I'm considering a stricter version that applies the disjunctive treatment not only to outright hallucinations or illusions like the bent-stick-in-water, but also to subthreshold perceptual drift—cases like prolonged prism adaptation, where a subject's spatial mapping systematically shifts and then restabilizes, and the subject reports that the post-adaptation experience of object location is phenomenally continuous with the pre-adaptation experience despite being systematically inaccurate about absolute position. If disjunctivism is correct, should we say that the pre-adaptation and post-adaptation experiences belong to different mental kinds, even though the subject cannot distinguish them introspectively and describes them with the same phenomenal vocabulary? I'm also wondering how this extends to sensory-substitution calibration and vestibular recalibration, where the system gradually remaps input-output relations without any subjective rupture.

What I want is an honest map of what would count against this stricter disjunctivism—not just the standard objections about phenomenological similarity or the common-factor problem, but the specific defeaters that apply when the view is pushed into these adaptation and cross-modal calibration domains. Which observations, which results from perceptual psychology, which formal constraints from theories of mental representation, or which intuitive judgments about phenomenological continuity would actually damage the view? Please distinguish the fatal from the merely awkward: for instance, does the existence of a smooth phenomenological gradient during adaptation collapse the disjunctive taxonomy, or can a defender absorb it by appealing to a layered account of phenomenal character? Which of these challenges would a committed disjunctivist concede as genuinely counting against them, and which would they dismiss by re-describing the phenomenology? Does extending disjunctivism to these cases risk collapsing it into a form of representationalism or relationalism that no longer needs the disjunctive machinery at all? I'm especially interested in whether there's any live conceptual development—perhaps in debates about the transparency of experience or about the individuation of sensory modalities—that exposes this stricter version in a way the standard textbook formulation avoids. Rank the defeaters, and be willing to conclude that the position is well insulated or that it is not, with an explanation of whether that insulation is a virtue or a vice.
