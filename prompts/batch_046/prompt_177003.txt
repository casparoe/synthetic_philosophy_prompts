I have been trying to work out whether the physical size of a brain ought to affect how much moral concern we owe the being that possesses it. I have built an argument that seems to show it does, but the conclusion feels plainly wrong, and I cannot locate the error.

(1) If two beings are otherwise identical in kind, temperament and circumstance, but one possesses a brain with twice the volume of functionally active cortical tissue, then that being can host twice as many simultaneous conscious states, and therefore carries twice the moral weight.

(2) Moral weight ought to scale with the richness or quantity of conscious experience a being can sustain, not merely with the bare presence or absence of consciousness.

(3) Therefore brain size — understood as the total volume of integrated neural substrate — is directly proportionate to moral status, all else being equal.

(4) By the same reasoning, if a human brain were gradually replaced, neuron for neuron, with synthetic equivalents that performed identically but occupied half the physical space through denser packing, that person's moral claim against harm would diminish in proportion to the volume saved.

(5) Likewise, because an adult elephant's brain is roughly three times the mass of an adult human's, an elephant would possess about three times the moral weight of a human being.

(6) And if split-brain surgery genuinely produces two separate centres of awareness within one skull, each hemisphere — being about half the original substrate — would hold only half the original person's moral status.

(7) Yet I find it absurd to say that a fully conscious mind compressed into smaller synthetic tissue is worth less, or that elephants automatically outweigh humans, or that a split-brain patient becomes two half-people.

(8) So one of the premises above must be false.

I should like to keep (1) and (2) if possible, since the intuition that moral importance tracks the depth of inner life rather than a simple on-off switch seems correct to me. Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? In particular, does abandoning (3) force me to deny any link between brain structure and moral status, or can a distinction — perhaps between raw volume and functional integration — save the core intuition without these absurd results?
