For a workshop on computation and artificial agency, I'm trying to clarify my own package of views and check whether they hold together. Here is where I stand:

1. I accept the strategy-stealing argument for finite deterministic perfect-information games: an optimal first-move strategy exists, but the proof is non-constructive, so no computable algorithm is guaranteed to find it.
2. I hold that LLMs, at inference time, are best characterized as high-dimensional pattern matchers over reasoning traces—what some call simulators—rather than as agents with stable goals and counterfactual robustness.
3. Yet I also think that when an LLM is embedded in an action loop (repeated negotiation, game-playing, or planning with feedback), the system-level dynamics can exhibit strategic stability that no single forward pass possesses; the agent-like properties emerge at the loop level, not inside the weights.
4. I maintain that this loop-level emergence does not solve the computability problem: if every forward pass remains computable, an adversary with full model access can still apply the strategy-stealing construction to the loop's effective policy, unless there is genuine non-computability somewhere in the pipeline.
5. I deny that phenomenal consciousness is necessary for strategic agency, and I think the simulator/agent boundary should be drawn by functional criteria—counterfactual stability, goal coherence, and causal integration—rather than by internal experience.
6. I also reject applying evolutionary debunking arguments to our intuitions about machine agency: I think those intuitions are unreliable, but I still want to use reflective equilibrium, informed by functional analysis, to classify borderline systems.

Please audit this package. Which pairs are in genuine tension, which only appear to conflict, and what is the least costly revision for each? Rank the tensions by severity. In particular, I worry that (2) and (3) pull in opposite directions—if the model is just a simulator, how can loop-level dynamics generate real strategic resistance?—and that (4) seems to undermine (3)'s practical significance. But I also want to know if (5) and (6) are in tension: if I reject debunking of intuitions but still rely on reflective equilibrium, am I being inconsistent about the epistemic status of my own judgments?

Please also say what would change your mind: which argument or consideration would lead you to reorder these tensions or abandon one commitment, and where your reasoning is most likely to fail.
