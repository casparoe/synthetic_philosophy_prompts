I'm running a 75-minute philosophy-of-mind seminar with 12 upper-level students. Design a structured in-class game—rules, hidden roles, a real but small collective stake, and a timeline—that makes the group feel the force of consciousness as a user-illusion: specifically, the view that phenomenal unity and first-person immediacy are interface representations built for control and reporting, not direct reflections of an underlying integrated process, with no further "audience" inside. To keep this from being a familiar brain-lecture retread, inject a niche collective twist: have participants act as distributed cognitive nodes that feed private, contradictory inputs into a shared "phenomenal interface" (a spokesperson or display that synthesizes one coherent narrative). They must make a binding group decision based only on that interface; afterwards, reveal that the synthesis actively suppressed real contradictions among the nodes, so the unified "we" that seemed to experience the choice was an artifact of the interface. Include a debrief that maps each revealed contradiction onto a feature of the individual user-illusion thesis, predicts what will go wrong the first run (e.g., participants anthropomorphizing the interface or resisting the third-person framing), and frankly states which feature of the view the activity cannot reproduce—especially the first-person immediacy of illusion, since participants observe their own group process from the outside. Separate in your debrief notes what is broadly settled about interface and narrative-construction models from what is genuinely contested among philosophers of mind, and do not present any contested interpretation (e.g., full eliminativism about phenomenal properties) as the consensus.
