My philosophy-of-population-ethics reading group has been stalled for months on Benatar's asymmetry. Every member accepts the formal structure: suffering's presence is bad for its subject, pleasure's presence is good for its subject, suffering's absence is good even with no subject, but pleasure's absence is not bad unless a subject is deprived. From this Benatar derives that existence is never a net benefit and often a harm. The defenders in our group treat this as an impersonal value asymmetry generating person-neutral reasons not to procreate. The critics accept the four premises but deny the moral conclusion, insisting that without an actual subject claim (3) registers only an abstract value-state, not a reason that can outweigh the concrete goods and harms in (1) and (2). We recently tried restating the dispute in decision-theoretic terms — comparing ex ante evaluations over worlds with populated versus empty domains — and found the asymmetry either collapsed into standard expected-utility neutrality or survived only by smuggling in a person-affecting constraint that Benatar rejects. Nothing has moved since.

Is it a hidden disagreement about whether moral reasons must be person-affecting, masked by shared acceptance of the formal asymmetry? Or is it a bedrock clash of intuitions about the moral status of potential people that no formal distinction can resolve?
