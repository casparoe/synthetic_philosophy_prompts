I am working out whether tranquilism — that only the absence of craving and suffering matters for well-being — holds up, by testing my judgments on concrete cases. A life of intense artistic achievement coupled with constant craving for recognition and occasional suffering is clearly worse than a life lacking those achievements but entirely free of craving and suffering. A being in permanent dreamless sleep, lacking both suffering and craving but also any consciousness, does not seem to live a good life, since there is no subject present to benefit. A hypothetical anhedonic sage who retains full second-order self-awareness and reasoning but has had every affective drive and all capacity for suffering eliminated, living in isolation without projects or relationships, strikes me as living a perfect or near-perfect life. A person who undergoes one severe painful event that permanently removes all future craving and suffering seems, on balance, to live an excellent life despite that initial harm. Yet a functioning biological organism with no consciousness, craving, or suffering — comparable to an insentient plant — does not seem to have a life that goes well at all, being neither good nor bad for it. Is there a coherent principle behind these five verdicts, or must one be abandoned? Please separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among those familiar with the topic, and do not present a contested view as the consensus. Answer as a numbered list of exactly five items, each one or two sentences long, with no introduction or conclusion. State any principle you propose with its clauses, indicate which verdicts it saves, predict its judgment on an unlisted case — a society that achieves collective absence of craving and suffering solely by suppressing individual self-awareness — and say honestly which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate.
