Examination question: Bradley's regress targets the reality of relations: if A and B are related by R, then R must itself stand in relations to A and to B, generating an infinite regress unless relations are unreal or reality is an undifferentiated Absolute. A parallel worry arises for propositional unity: if the judgment that Socrates is wise is composed of the term Socrates and the term wisdom, what binds them into a single thought? Explain Bradley's original regress, then explain whether and how it maps onto the problem of propositional unity. In doing so, discuss Frege's appeal to unsaturated concepts and Russell's doctrine that relations are external, and evaluate whether either response solves both problems or only one. Be precise about whether treating the two problems together illuminates them or risks conflating metaphysical relations with logical predication.

Student response: Bradley's regress begins when we notice that if two terms A and B stand in a relation R, then R itself must stand in some relation to A and to B in order actually to connect them. This requires a further relation R', and then another, ad infinitum. Bradley concludes that relations are not ultimately real; instead, reality is an undifferentiated Absolute in which all distinctions are merely phenomenal.

When we turn to propositional unity, we face an analogous worry: in the judgment that Socrates is wise, the terms Socrates and wisdom seem to be distinct entities, so something must bind them together into one proposition. Frege avoids this problem by arguing that concepts are unsaturated—logically incomplete—so that wisdom contains a gap that Socrates fills without any external binder being required. Russell, by contrast, treats relations as external: they relate terms directly without themselves requiring further relations to do so. Applying Russell's view to propositions, the relation of predication is just an external relation holding directly between Socrates and wisdom.

However, this mapping works only if we treat the predicate as a complete term. Once we accept Frege's point that predicates are unsaturated, Bradley's argument is blocked at the outset for propositions, since there is no separate, complete term that needs relating. In fact, Bradley's own doctrine of internal relations is essentially the same solution as Frege's unsaturatedness: both deny that terms need an external glue because the terms themselves are either modified by or incomplete with respect to one another. Thus Bradley's regress applies to object-relations in the metaphysical case, but it collapses in the propositional case, where Russell's external-relations response is unnecessary and Frege's account suffices on its own.
