Here is an argument I constructed for a philosophy seminar, and I now suspect it proves too much, but I cannot locate the precise misstep. The argument concerns Bernard Williams's integrity objection — specifically, his claim that utilitarianism can require an agent to have one thought too many when deciding whether to save her spouse.

(1) Williams's core claim is that there is a distinctive wrongness in being required to justify an action that flows directly from one's ground project by appealing to an impersonal calculus; the agent should save her spouse because she is her spouse, not because overall utility is maximized.

(2) But notice that this objection depends on a distinction between reasoning internal to the ground project and reasoning external to it.

(3) Now consider a case where an agent's ground project is itself morally questionable — for instance, a project of artistic creation that requires the exploitation of others. If she reflects on whether her project is worthy and decides to abandon or modify it, she is engaging in reasoning that is, by Williams's own lights, external to the immediate pursuit of the project.

(4) Therefore, if Williams permits such reflective evaluation — and he clearly must, or he could not distinguish legitimate ground projects from illegitimate ones — then he already allows the kind of stepping back that he condemns in the utilitarian case.

(5) The only consistent position is either to forbid all reflective evaluation of ground projects, which would make integrity compatible with monstrous commitments, or to allow it, in which case the boundary between permissible reflection and the one-thought-too-many error collapses.

(6) So Williams's objection either applies to all moral self-evaluation, including his own, or it applies to none, leaving utilitarianism untouched.

(7) Therefore, the integrity objection fails to isolate utilitarianism; it is either self-defeating or toothless.

The conclusion feels wrong, but every step seems defensible. Where does the argument go wrong? Is there an equivocation in step (2) or (3) between evaluating a project and weighing it against an external moral standard? If I must reject one premise, I would prefer to keep (1) and (4), but I want to know exactly what I am paying to preserve them.
