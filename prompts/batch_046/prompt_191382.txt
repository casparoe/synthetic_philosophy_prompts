I coordinate a volunteer community review panel that meets once a month to approve, require revision of, or reject proposals for changes to our town’s shared public spaces—things like playground redesigns, mural installations, or seating structures in plazas. The panel has seven members with different backgrounds: two parents, two local artists, one retired engineer, one city employee who is not a technical expert, and one rotating resident with no special training. I chair the meeting, must keep it to about forty minutes, and have to sign a decision log that records the vote and any dissent with a one-sentence explanation. I am not an expert in engineering, art, or child development, and I need a reusable procedure I can apply every month without rebuilding the reasoning from scratch.

A while back I read a lay explanation of the Condorcet jury idea—the notion that if each voter is slightly more likely to be right than wrong, and they reach their views independently, then a larger group voting by simple majority tends to be correct more often than any individual. I want you to turn that principle into an operational procedure for my panel. Our situation breaks the standard assumptions in specific ways: our members talk and persuade each other before voting, so independence is often compromised; our members are not equally informed about every proposal; and our decision is not binary but three-way—approve, require revision, or reject. To handle this, I want the procedure to include explicit checks for whether independence has been broken, a quick competence check for the specific proposal type, and an adjusted aggregation rule for the three outcomes.

Please write the procedure as an ordered checklist or flowchart in words that I, as a non-expert chair, can run in real time during the meeting. For each step, include a concrete test or question I can ask, specify the action if the answer is yes or no, and note where I must record something in the decision log. Include at least three worked examples: one where the conditions are satisfied and a majority-style vote should be trusted; one where independence is violated or competence is too low and the procedure directs me to switch methods—perhaps weighting input by demonstrated expertise or deferring to an outside review; and one where the procedure tells me to do something I will resist, such as overriding a clear majority and tabling the decision because influence or incompetence is too high. Clearly flag every point where judgment cannot be eliminated and I still have to make a call myself.

Also tell me exactly what the procedure cannot do—where it will misfire, where the underlying theory does not apply because our group is too small or too interdependent, and how the three-way choice weakens the standard result.

Please write your answer in American English.
