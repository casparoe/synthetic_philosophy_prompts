I've been working through Douglas Hofstadter's well-known argument that Gödel's self-referential formulas illuminate human self-awareness, reading it entirely on my own without any formal course behind me. After seeing recent explanations of how large language models generate descriptions of their own weights and training mechanisms, I tried to formalize the analogy into a step-by-step argument, but the conclusion feels plainly wrong, and I want to locate the exact crack without just dismissing the whole idea.

(1) Gödel constructed a formula in arithmetic that encodes a statement about its own unprovability, which is possible because the formal system can represent its own syntactic properties.
(2) Hofstadter proposes that human minds achieve self-awareness through an analogous loop: the brain operates as a symbol system that can represent its own rules and states, thereby generating the sense of an I.
(3) A contemporary language model can produce accurate descriptions of its own architecture, predictive mechanisms, and limitations; it can generate sentences describing itself as computing the next token.
(4) This is also an internal representation of the system's own properties mapped onto symbols, structurally parallel to the Gödelian self-reference.
(5) Therefore, if Gödelian self-reference is sufficient for the emergence of self-awareness, then any system with this structural loop—including the language model—possesses the core mechanism of consciousness.
(6) Yet the model exhibits no subjective experience, no continuous inner life, and its self-descriptions are purely statistical pattern completions.
(7) So the analogy either forces us to attribute consciousness to machines, which seems absurd, or it was never sufficient to explain human consciousness in the first place.
(8) The only remaining defense is that human self-reference differs in kind, not merely in degree or complexity.
(9) But if the difference is one of kind, then the Gödelian parallel was only a metaphor, not a genuine explanatory mechanism.

I am reluctant to abandon (1) and (2), since they seem to capture the heart of the insight. Is the error an equivocation in (4) between syntactic self-reference and functional self-modeling? Or does (5) confuse a necessary condition with a sufficient one? If I reject (5), do I still have an explanation for why minds are special, or do I merely save the analogy by weakening it? I want to keep the structure intact if possible, but I need to know which step is actually doing the damage and what it costs to cut it out.
