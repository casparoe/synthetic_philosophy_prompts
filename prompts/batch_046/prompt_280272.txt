I have been working through the standard distinction between finkish dispositions—where the very conditions that would trigger manifestation simultaneously remove the disposition—and masked dispositions, in which an external factor blocks the manifestation of an otherwise intact disposition. I want to extend this framework to what I will call layered, or recursive, dispositions: cases in which an object possesses not only a first-order disposition D, but also a second-order disposition D* to acquire or lose D in direct response to specific environmental conditions. Consider a hypothetical artifact engineered so that it is soluble in water only when it is not immersed in water; upon immersion, an embedded mechanism transforms its chemical structure into an insoluble state precisely at the moment the dissolution conditions are met. At the exact temporal slice where the triggering environment and the structural transformation coincide, the object appears neither determinately soluble nor determinately insoluble, but rather suspended in a kind of dispositional oscillation that neither the fink analysis (which requires the disposition to be present and then removed) nor the mask analysis (which requires an intact disposition that is externally blocked) seems able to capture. Is this oscillation a genuinely distinct metaphysical category—perhaps a form of dynamic antidisposition—or can it be fully reduced to a temporally indexed interaction of finking and masking? More specifically, how should we assign the disposition at the precise instant of coincidence, and does recognizing such layered cases force us to abandon the idea that dispositions are intrinsic, time-independent properties of objects?
