I was discussing moral psychology with friends over dinner—none of us are philosophers professionally, though one studied literature years ago—and we found ourselves returning to a pattern in everyday judgement that I have not seen named clearly. I have been reading casually about Adam Smith’s ideas on sympathy and the impartial spectator, and I recognise the broad outline: we understand others by imagining their feelings, and we judge actions partly by asking what an impartial observer would approve. But there is a specific move I keep making that does not seem to fit neatly into either box. Consider a case: a colleague is visibly enraged by a bureaucratic inconvenience that I regard as trivial. I deliberately try to place myself in his situation, and I succeed in feeling something like his frustration—this is sympathy, I suppose. Yet immediately afterwards, I find myself asking not whether I share the feeling, but whether its intensity stands in the right proportion to its cause, and I treat that proportion as part of whether I esteem or disesteem him as a moral agent. The judgement is not about an action he has taken; it is about the scale of his response. Crucially, I can only make that judgement because the sympathetic exercise has shown me what the feeling is like from inside. So sympathy seems to function both as the lens that reveals the emotion and as the standard by which I measure its appropriateness.

Is there an established concept for this—using sympathetic entry as the basis for a normative calibration of emotional proportion? Or does Smith’s framework distribute it between sympathy and the impartial spectator in a way that avoids circularity? If the literature does not have a precise name, I would like to understand how to carve the phenomenon at its edges: what distinguishes it from mere empathy, from an external spectator’s cool assessment, and from a simple disagreement about values. I should emphasise that I am not asking for a bibliography or citations to particular editions; I am working from general knowledge and would value a conceptual map rather than scholarly apparatus. Please reply in British English, and if there is no exact term, I would welcome a proposed name together with its boundary conditions—especially how it differs from cases where we judge an action wrong but understand the feeling behind it perfectly well.
