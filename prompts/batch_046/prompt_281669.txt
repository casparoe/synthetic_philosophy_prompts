I am planning a fifty-minute workshop for second-year philosophy students who have just finished an introductory module on modal logic and possible-worlds semantics, but who have not yet studied metaphysics of personal identity. I want them to grasp origin essentialism — the claim that a given person could not have originated from a different sperm and egg — but I want to avoid the standard textbook framing and instead build the session around a constructed scenario: a laboratory technique that splits a single zygote (formed from sperm S and egg E) at the two-cell stage, implanting one half in mother M1 and the other in mother M2, producing adults Alex and Blake. The pedagogical target is not whether Alex and Blake are identical, but whether either could have been the very person who developed from a completely different gamete pair, S* and E*.

Plan the session as a teaching arc rather than a lecture. First, identify the prior conceptual idea that must be in place before origin essentialism sounds like a substantive modal claim rather than a biological truism, and explain why that idea needs to be secured before the split-zygote case is introduced. Second, specify exactly which feature of this scenario should carry the students’ intuition, and state what the case must include (genetic continuity across the split) and exclude (mere environmental or gestational similarity) for that intuition to point in the right direction. Third, map the misconceptions they will predictably form: I am especially concerned about conflating origin essentialism with genetic determinism, with an empirical claim about DNA uniqueness, and with a thesis about parental rights; for each, state the precise point in the sequence where it should be headed off and how. Fourth, design one question I can ask at the end that would show whether they have understood the modal claim — that is, whether they recognise the difference between this body could not have had different parents and this person could not have had different origins — rather than merely feeling uneasy about the laboratory imagery. Finally, state explicitly what should be left out at this level — for instance, whether origin essentialism applies to artefacts or to social kinds — and say how I should frame that omission so it protects the core idea rather than leaving the students thinking the claim is arbitrarily restricted.
