I'm facilitating a 90-minute graduate workshop on decision theory and rational agency with about ten philosophy students who have already read the basic literature on Newcomb-style problems but keep treating counterfactual mugging as a mere curiosity. I want them to physically feel why refusing to pay can seem rational under evidential reasoning and yet intuitively wrong, and then to see that XOR blackmail undermines the very same pay-to-prevent-harm reflex from the opposite direction. Design a structured in-room simulation—using only paper tokens, sealed envelopes, and a point-based payoff scheme with no real money—that intertwines both problems through a shared two-round structure: in round one, each student decides whether to surrender a token representing a prediction-dependent contribution, which corresponds to the counterfactual mugging component; in round two they face a paired blackmail envelope whose content is determined not by their round-one choice alone but by an XOR gate linking their choice to a hidden partner's choice, so that paying in round one activates one threat in round two while not paying activates the other. The rules should be arranged so that participants construct the payoff logic themselves—perhaps by distributing hidden condition cards that they must match to outcomes—before the facilitator names either problem. Include a debrief that maps the moment when students realize the XOR linkage collapses the usual causal intuition onto the evidential reasoning they used in round one, and be explicit about what will go wrong the first time this runs, for instance students conflating the partner's hidden choice with their own counterfactual copy, or demanding to know the true causal history of the envelope. Also state frankly which feature of counterfactual reasoning—specifically the modal separation between actual and predicted worlds—this paper-token simulation cannot reproduce and that you as facilitator must supply verbally. Do not ask for bibliographies or citations; the activity should stand on its own mechanics.
