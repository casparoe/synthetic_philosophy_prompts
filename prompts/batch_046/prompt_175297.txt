For a philosophy paper I am writing for my senior seminar, I tried to formalize the doing-allowing distinction using what I am calling the Decisive-Cause Condition, but the result now strikes me as suspicious because it dissolves collective obligations into collective absolution. Here is the chain: (1) If actively killing is wrong in part because the agent becomes the decisive cause of death, then allowing death carries the same moral weight only when the agent is the decisive cause of the failure to prevent it. (2) In redundant-causation cases—imagine five guards who each hold an identical key that can free a prisoner—no single guard's inaction is decisive, since any other guard could have acted. (3) So in redundant allowing, no individual is morally equivalent to a killer. (4) Large-scale famine relief is structurally redundant: thousands of potential donors could each prevent a particular death by contributing. (5) Therefore, for any given individual, failing to donate is not decisive, and the moral wrongness of omission collapses almost to zero. (6) Yet if every agent applies this reasoning, no one donates, the prisoner dies, and each agent can sincerely claim, "My omission was not the decisive cause." (7) So the Decisive-Cause Condition proves too much: it turns collective action problems into cases where nobody bears individual moral weight. Which step should I reject? I am reluctant to abandon (1), because overdetermined omissions really do feel morally lighter than direct killing, but I also want to avoid the conclusion that collective problems have no individual solution. Where exactly does the argument go wrong, and what does fixing it cost me—must I deny that redundancy diminishes individual responsibility, or must I accept that some collective failures are genuinely nobody's fault?
