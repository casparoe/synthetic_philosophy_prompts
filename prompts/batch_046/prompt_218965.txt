My friend and I spent the evening arguing about whether Plutarch’s Parallel Lives can still serve as a serious instrument for moral formation once we accept that the biographies are shaped by hagiographic conventions rather than archival fidelity. I maintained that moral learning from lives operates through what we might call structural extraction—the identification of patterns of practical reasoning that remain stable even when particular anecdotes are distorted—so that knowing Alcibiades was probably not as disciplined as Plutarch depicts does not disable the pedagogical function of comparing him with Coriolanus. She countered that without a baseline of historical reliability, the reader cannot distinguish between a genuinely virtuous pattern and a rhetorically constructed one, which means the text collapses into mere edification and forfeits any claim to teach us about virtue as it actually exists in human lives. We have reached an impasse: she treats my position as a defense of fiction-based ethics, while I treat hers as an impossible demand for documentary purity applied to a genre that never claimed to provide it. Before I concede that we are talking past each other, I want to know what kind of disagreement this actually is. Is it a hidden empirical dispute about whether distorted narratives can cause genuine moral improvement? Is it a disagreement about the ontology of virtue—whether virtues are essentially embedded in specific historical acts that must be correctly described, or whether they are abstract relational structures that can be instantiated in stylized accounts? Or is it a deeper divergence in the pragmatics of reading, where one of us approaches biography as a technology for self-calibration and the other approaches it as a source of testimonial knowledge about human possibility? Given your diagnosis, tell me what would actually move this debate—whether a conceptual distinction, a psychological study, or a closer analysis of Plutarch’s own methodological commitments—and be honest about whether the stall indicates a resolvable tension or a bedrock difference in what we think moral learning requires. I am not asking for a survey of scholarship; I want a diagnosis of the structure of our disagreement and an honest assessment of whether anything could resolve it.
