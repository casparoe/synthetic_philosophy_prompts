I am drafting a philosophical interlude for a novel in which an aging art conservator writes a private essay reflecting on Hume's argument that taste, though apparently subjective, admits of a standard corrected by experienced and unbiased judges. Rather than summarizing the familiar dialectic—the delicacy of imagination, freedom from prejudice, repeated comparison, and the refinement of practice—I want to advance a more idiosyncratic claim that extends the framework without importing contemporary meta-ontological machinery. I propose that Hume's account quietly presupposes what I call ontological convergence: the assumption that disputants are disagreeing about the same object under the same descriptive framework. Against this, I introduce the notion of framework incommensurability, a class of aesthetic disagreement in which observers do not share sufficient conceptual architecture to locate identical properties in a work. Consider the case of a decaying industrial site: one viewer treats it as an unintended ecological sculpture and evaluates its formal balance, while another treats it as mere ruin and applies criteria of historical preservation; they are not offering competing verdicts on a shared aesthetic object, but operating with incompatible ontologies of what is being judged at all. My essay argues that Hume's mechanism of correction—appeal to the true judge—can resolve defective sentiment within a shared framework, yet has no resources to adjudicate between frameworks, because the correction procedure itself requires prior agreement on the boundaries of the object.

Could you comment on the coherence of this distinction? Specifically, I would like to know whether it genuinely extends Hume's position or collapses back into his existing categories of prejudice and false comparison; whether the industrial-site example actually performs the work I assign it, or if it secretly begs the question by assuming that preservation and sculpture belong to different domains; and whether my characterization of Hume's emphasis on comparison and practice is accurate in spirit, even if I have avoided direct quotation. I am also interested in whether the argument feels interesting or merely taxonomic—does the introduction of framework incommensurability open up a productive space for thinking about taste, or does it simply relabel an old problem without advancing it?
