I am preparing the question sequence for a podcast interview with a philosopher who has recently advanced a hybrid account of moral revolutions. Her central claim — which she terms the symbolic redundancy thesis — is that practices such as chattel slavery and duelling do not end primarily through rational moral argument, nor through naked economic or class interest, but through a specific substitution mechanism: the status, recognition, or honour previously secured only by participation in the practice becomes available through cheaper, less risky social substitutes. Once that symbolic capital has migrated, moral condemnation performs only retrospective legitimation rather than causal work. She draws selectively on Bernard Williams’s suspicion that moral change is rarely a matter of rational enlightenment, and on Kwame Anthony Appiah’s analysis of honour codes, yet she rejects both the pure-interest explanation and the pure-shame explanation in favour of this mechanism of substitution.

I should like a sequence of questions that makes this position progressively harder to defend, turning on the actual mechanics of symbolic redundancy rather than generic objections. The questions ought to press how one distinguishes symbolic redundancy from mere fashion or technological drift; whether the mechanism can apply to norms that seem to lack any clear honour-bearing component, such as foot-binding or early child-labour regulations; and what predictive purchase the thesis has over practices currently in moral contention. Please anticipate her standard replies — for example, that redundancy is identifiable through an asymmetry of exit costs, or that moral argument operates as a coordination device that becomes effective only after substitution has occurred — and embed the follow-up that undermines each reply. The sequence should conclude with a question about what kind of empirical pattern or conceptual counter-example could genuinely falsify the view, rather than merely adding complexity to it. Please formulate everything in formal British English, suitable for a philosophical colloquium setting.
