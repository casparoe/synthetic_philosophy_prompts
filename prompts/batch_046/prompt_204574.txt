I have been following, both in the literature and in my own reading group, the long-running dispute over Amartya Sen’s liberal paradox and its aftermath. The core setup is familiar: minimal liberalism requires that each of at least two individuals have decisive power over some pair of social states within a personal sphere; the Pareto principle requires that if everyone prefers one state to another, society should too; and Sen showed that with an unrestricted domain these two commitments are incompatible for societies of two or more people. Over decades, formalists have offered escape routes—restricting the domain of preferences, weakening minimal liberalism to apply only within smaller groups, or treating rights as side-constraints rather than as inputs to an aggregation function—while normative theorists have insisted that any weakening abandons the very idea of liberal rights as trumps over collective preference. Yet in seminars and in recent journal exchanges the two camps seem to have reached a stable stalemate: everyone acknowledges the theorem, everyone knows the standard responses, and nobody has changed their mind about whether the paradox reveals a deep contradiction in liberal democratic values or merely a category mistake in applying Arrow-style aggregation to rights.
