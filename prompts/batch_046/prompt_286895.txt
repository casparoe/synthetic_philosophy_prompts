ok so in some european cities when a statue of a contested figure comes under pressure, they dont just tear it down or leave it alone; they do these counter-monument moves—sinking the pillar into the ground over years, tilting the pedestal, putting up mirrors so you see yourself alongside the figure—while keeping the original object right there in its spot. but for street names in the same jurisdictions its usually binary: rename or dont, maybe with a small plaque. what does that asymmetry presuppose? does treating a statue as something you can physically "counter" without erasing it assume that symbolic harm is spatial and embodied—tied to the viewers bodily relation to the object—whereas renaming a street assumes that linguistic reference itself carries a moral taint that context cant fix? reconstruct the implicit theory of commemorative ethics and collective memory here. are the two practices actually consistent, or do they lean on different views of how moral wrongs stick to objects and names? and could a straight consequentialist approach—just ask whether leaving the statue or name up causes present harm—license both, or does the counter-monument idea smuggle in some non-consequentialist commitment about ritual acknowledgment? what would have to change in policy if we dropped that?
