I serve on the clinical ethics committee of a regional neurorehabilitation center, and after my sibling sustained a diffuse axonal injury that left him with minimal behavioral responsiveness, I have been forced to watch teams casually attribute or deny mental states—pain, preference, recognition, suffering—on the basis of single behavioral markers. I need a reusable decision procedure that my committee can run during our monthly thirty-minute reviews of care plans for patients with disorders of consciousness. The deliverable must determine whether we possess sufficient epistemic warrant to attribute a specific mental state to a non-communicative subject, or whether we are obliged to suspend judgment entirely. Ground the protocol in the philosophy of other minds as it actually stands—the analogical argument from my own first-person case, inference to the best explanation of observed behavior, and the public-criteria tradition—without collapsing into naive behaviorism or unexamined empathy. Structure it as a sequential flowchart in words: the questions to ask in fixed order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, the decision nodes (provisional attribution, suspension, or explicit denial of warrant), and the precise points where the procedure stops and hands the decision to human judgment because the issue is one of values rather than epistemics. The protocol must survive hard cases: where structural analogy is weak due to atypical neural architecture yet predictive coherence is high; where public criteria are technically satisfied but best-explanation rivals such as reflex arcs or automated responses remain undefeated; and where emotional proximity to the subject introduces bias that the procedure itself cannot correct. Include a small set of operational tests a non-philosopher can apply, flag the misfires, and state explicitly what the procedure cannot do—particularly its inability to bridge the first-person asymmetry gap by procedural means alone. Then apply the completed protocol to three worked cases: (1) a locked-in patient using eye-tracking to select music over silence; (2) a non-human primate research subject whose tool-use patterns appear to require belief about a hidden food location; and (3) my sibling, who demonstrates localized fMRI activation in response to my voice but exhibits no reproducible behavioral criterion—here the procedure must deliver an answer I will not want to accept, and you must say exactly where judgment takes over and what error the committee risks if it ignores that boundary. Specify the role of the clinical chair and ethics lead in signing off on the judgment-call nodes, and keep every step executable without bibliographic lookup or specialist philosophical training.
