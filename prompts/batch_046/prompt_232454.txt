I serve on my university's Institutional Review and Resource Allocation Board. We must decide whether to approve a campus-wide predictive-intervention protocol: an algorithm flags roughly eight percent of our 30,000 students for mandatory wellness check-ins based on behavioral signals; false positives face a minor but real intrusion (an unwanted meeting, a non-clinical notation in an internal file), while the system is projected to prevent about six serious self-harm events per year. Our charter forbids simple aggregate cost-benefit reasoning and requires contractualist evaluation—would anyone affected be able to reasonably reject the principle behind the decision? The charter also contains an untested Section 4.2 permitting "disaggregated justification" if no identifiable subgroup bears a disproportionate share of the burden. Proponents claim this resolves Scanlon's aggregation problem; opponents argue it collapses contractualism into consequentialism by distributing rejectability across the population until it vanishes. Draft the board's decision memo—approve, reject, or approve with conditions—including a concrete implementation timeline and any safeguards. The memo should apply contractualist reasoning rigorously, treat the strongest objection on the losing side with intellectual honesty rather than straw-manning it, and specifically adjudicate whether Section 4.2 is a legitimate extension of contractualism or a conceptual mistake that smuggles aggregation back in. Please state how confident you are in the analysis you present and single out the point about which you are least certain.
