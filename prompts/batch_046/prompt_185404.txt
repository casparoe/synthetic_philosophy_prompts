I've been thinking lately about an old criticism I came across—something by a Danish writer named Kierkegaard—about how the press and the "public" it manufactures turn real people into an anonymous crowd where nobody has to stand behind what they say. From what I understand, he believed that when everyone is addressed at once, no one is really addressed at all, and opinions become flat because they belong to nobody in particular. I don't have any formal background in philosophy, but the idea keeps returning to me when I notice how recommendation algorithms seem to build a phantom audience: reactions feel loud but unowned, as if the feed itself has leveled every voice to the same dull volume, creating a crowd that thinks without anyone inside it actually thinking.

Could you write me a villanelle that takes this tension seriously? I want the poem's two refrains to do structural work: one should be the voice of that crowd, returning word-for-word in every tercet like the public never actually changes, and the other should be the voice of someone who refuses to dissolve into it—except that in the final quatrain, where both refrains must appear together, that second refrain should shift by exactly one word, so that refusal reads as something other than escape. Please keep the rhyme of "press" and "address" running through the stanzas, and let the poem's real turn happen in that closing quatrain, where the two voices finally share the same space. It should be faithful to Kierkegaard's actual worry—that anonymity and leveling destroy the seriousness of individual commitment—without simply naming him or relying on jargon; someone who knows the concept should recognize it immediately, and someone who doesn't should still feel the poem's pressure.
