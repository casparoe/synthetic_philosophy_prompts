Consider the near-disappearance of institutional dueling in Britain and the American South between roughly 1820 and 1860: within two generations, a standardized gentlemanly mechanism for resolving insults was criminalized, socially ridiculed, and largely abandoned, despite the absence of any sweeping statutory prohibition. Analyze this episode through the competing frameworks for moral revolution associated with Bernard Williams and Kwame Anthony Appiah—namely, the thesis that norms erode when they conflict with material and political interests, versus the thesis that they collapse when internal honor codes invert so that adherence becomes a source of shame rather than status. In your analysis, account specifically for the persistence of other honor-based practices—such as vendetta violence in parts of the American South and formal dueling protocols in parts of Southern Europe—well after dueling itself had faded, and assess whether the concurrent expansion of state-controlled legal remedies (professional policing, expanded libel law) supports an interests-based explanation, merely provides institutional cover for an honor shift that had already occurred, or exposes a false dichotomy between the two frameworks. Finally, examine what the rapid demise of dueling, contrasted with the far slower decline of chattel slavery in the same societies, reveals about the scope conditions of each theory: does the asymmetry indicate that moral revolutions require a particular convergence of interest and honor, or does it suggest that one framework was never intended to apply to norms deeply embedded in economic systems? Be disciplined in mapping the actors, the available options, and the evidence, and say explicitly where either theory is silent rather than forcing it to speak.
