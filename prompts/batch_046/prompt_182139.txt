I've been chewing over Kripkenstein's skeptical argument but trying to apply it specifically to large language models instead of human speakers, and I think I've landed on a position that feels a little extreme but I can't shake it. The view is that for an LLM there's genuinely no fact of the matter about whether it's following a syntactic rule in any normative sense—only that it's weights track statistical correlations in a way that mimics rule-following. Not just an epistemic gap, but a metaphysical one: the internal state lacks the kind of normative constraint that distinguishes getting a rule right from getting it wrong, except with humans we cover the gap with social practice and shared form of life. So talk about models understanding grammar is basically projection, and the skeptical solution is actually the only stable endpoint.

What I need is an honest assessment of what would count against this, not just the standard objections people raise against Kripke in human cases. For instance, what specific findings in mechanistic interpretability—say, about emergent circuit structures that behave like error-correction—would actually damage the view? Or which philosophical distinctions between causal tracking and normative commitment break the analogy between model outputs and human linguistic behavior? I'd like the defeaters ranked: what's fatal versus merely awkward, and what defenders of this LLM-version of skepticism would concede is damaging versus dismiss as missing the point. Would a clear demonstration that a model can revise its own outputs in ways that aren't reducible to its training distribution be decisive? Or is the position basically insulated because any apparent normativity can always be redescribed as sophisticated pattern-matching?
