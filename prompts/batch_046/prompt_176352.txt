I am trying to articulate what I actually believe about species membership, testing my intuitions against concrete biological scenarios rather than against textbook definitions. Here are my verdicts.

First, in a classic ring-species arrangement—say, populations arranged around a geographic barrier, each interbreeding with its neighbors, but with the two ends reproductively isolated—I judge that the two end populations belong to the same species. The isolation is a contingent geographic accident, not a deep biological division.

Second, consider two bacterial lineages that share 99% genomic identity but routinely exchange accessory genes horizontally with distant taxa, so that their evolutionary histories form a reticulate network rather than a tree. Despite the genetic similarity, I judge them to be different species, because the horizontal exchange undermines the genealogical coherence I take to be essential.

Third, imagine a synthetic chimeric organism constructed with the nuclear genome of species A embedded in the cytoplasmic machinery—including mitochondria and regulatory RNAs—of species B. It reproduces only asexually, but its cells can be fused back into A with full viability. I judge it is neither A nor B but a new species, because its reproductive pathway is irreducibly novel.

Fourth, take a single unbranched lineage that transforms gradually over ten million years: every successive generation can reproduce with its immediate predecessor, yet the morphological and ecological distance between the first and last generations exceeds that found between many recognized sister species. I judge they are different species; continuous reproductive connectivity is not enough if cumulative divergence crosses a threshold.

Fifth, two populations are morphologically indistinguishable and share recent common ancestry, but a single chromosomal inversion prevents hybridization. I judge they remain the same species; the barrier feels too trivial and mechanically fragile to constitute a real species boundary.

These judgments conflict in ways I find uncomfortable. Case 1 tolerates reproductive isolation across geographic distance, while Case 5 dismisses a direct mechanical barrier. Case 4 permits division without branching, while Case 2 rejects union despite near-identical genomes. Is there a unified principle—perhaps centered on the modal robustness of reproductive pathways, or on the integrity of a genealogical network against perturbation—that can accommodate all five? If not, which verdict should I overturn, and why?

Finally, apply your best principle to a case I have not listed: a population splits into two isolated groups that diverge for one million years, are then reunited, and can once again interbreed and produce fertile offspring—except that all hybrid offspring suffer from a severe, heritable metabolic disorder. Are the reunited groups one species, two, or something else?
