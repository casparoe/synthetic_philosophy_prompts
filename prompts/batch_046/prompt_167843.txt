I am a retired biophysicist who has recently turned to philosophy and now hosts a podcast on conceptual issues where scientific reasoning meets ethical theory. I am preparing a colloquium-style interview with an unnamed ethicist who defends a precisely formulated view about meat consumption and wild-animal suffering. Her central claim is that standard comparisons between crop-cultivation mortality and livestock harm rely on a linear, additive aggregation of deaths across trophic levels, which she rejects as ecologically naive; in its place she proposes a 'trophic perturbation index' that measures non-linear changes in predator-prey dynamics and net wild-animal welfare caused by a food-production system. She holds that well-managed rotational beef production can score lower on this index than large-scale no-till grain monoculture—because perennial pasture stabilizes small-mammal populations and eliminates plowing-induced mortality—while chicken production, despite requiring less direct land, depends on feed-crop agriculture that generates high rodent and passerine mortality per delivered calorie, yielding a disproportionately high index. She also argues that the familiar crop-death objection to omnivorism is structurally mistaken, not empirically false: it treats a coupled ecological system as if welfare impacts could be summed like entries in a ledger.

Please construct a sequence of six interview questions that progress from clarifying this framework to exposing its vulnerabilities. Each question should anticipate her most probable defense and include a built-in follow-up that blocks the standard retreat. Specifically: first, ask her to defend the non-linearity of the index and to state exactly why linear body-count aggregation misrepresents coupled ecosystems; second, press her on the empirical measurements that would be needed to show rotational pasture actually lowers net wild suffering compared with monoculture, and ask what result would undermine that comparison; third, challenge the beef-versus-chicken comparison by asking how the index accounts for the temporal lag and spatial separation between feed-crop death and the consumption of chicken; fourth, ask whether her framework entails that consuming predators—who suppress prey populations—is ethically preferable to consuming herbivores, and how she avoids that conclusion without ad hoc adjustments; fifth, present a case where two systems have identical perturbation indices but one involves vastly more direct, observable suffering—ask which system she selects and whether that reveals that the index is tracking the wrong variable; sixth, ask what specific ecological or moral observation would convince her to abandon the index entirely.

The questions should turn on the actual content of her position, not on biography or methodology. Do not request bibliographies, citations, or reading lists, and do not include generic opening questions. Please present the questions in clear order. End your response with a single line—beginning with the word 'Summary:'—that states in one sentence the strategic purpose of the sequence.
Summary: The sequence is designed to move from internal clarification of the trophic perturbation index to empirical, comparative, and reductio pressures that force the defender either to refine her non-linear framework or to concede that it tracks ecological dynamics rather than direct suffering.
