In discussions of Nietzsche's tripartite division of history into the monumental, the antiquarian, and the critical — drawn from his essay On the Uses and Disadvantages of History for Life — commentators frequently invoke a therapeutic analogy: monumental history functions as a stimulant for present action, antiquarian history as a preservative that can induce paralysis or decay if applied without limit, and critical history as a surgical excision of the past that frees life but risks damaging the organism. A recent extension of this framing compares critical history specifically to chemotherapy rather than surgery, arguing that because chemotherapy destroys healthy tissue along with the malignant, critical history is inherently unsafe in societies that lack a shared normative immune system; on this view, pluralistic democracies should therefore restrict critical history to private scholarship rather than public education. Audit this analogy and its extension: first, provide an explicit correspondence table mapping Nietzsche's three modes onto the medical source domain (the patient, the physician, the dosage, the risks of under- and over-treatment, the immune system); second, identify the points of disanalogy, especially where the medical model assumes an organically unified body with a single health-teleology that Nietzsche's account of culture and life either rejects or renders problematic; third, assess whether the chemotherapy variant genuinely derives from Nietzsche's text or whether it smuggles in modern assumptions about pluralism and institutional risk-management; fourth, determine whether the normative conclusion — that democracies should quarantine critical history — survives once the disanalogies are on the table, or whether the analogy was carrying the entire argument; and finally, suggest whether an analogy drawn from information management (e.g., caching, archiving, and garbage collection) avoids these particular imports, without requiring that it be superior overall.
