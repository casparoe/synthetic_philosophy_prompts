I got in a huge debate with my friend last night about the absent-minded driver problem — you know, the case where you intend to stop at the grocery store but drive right past it because you're zoned out, then you realize and loop back. She said it's definatly just another version of the deviant causal chain problem, like the climber who wants to kill his partner and accidentally cuts the rope, except here the causal chain is broken by inattention instead of nervousness. But that doesn't sound right to me? In the driver case, you eventually do stop at the store, and it feels intentional, not like an accident. So here's where I'm stuck: philosophers talk about this like it's a puzzle about whether your original intention can still cause the turn-around even when you're not consciously thinking about the store. But if we accept that intentions can be "dormant" and still steer your behavior — kind of like a background program running — then isn't the problem solved? Or is the real issue whether there even was a dormant intention, versus just a habit loop that predictive processing explains? My friend kept bringing up "procedural drift" in autopilot driving, saying the brain's default mode takes over and the explicit goal never really guided the action. So I'm wondering: does the absent-minded driver problem only work if we assume there's a genuine mental state that survives the lapse, or can the puzzle survive even if the "intention" was never more than a statistical prior in your motor system? Because if it's the second one, then I don't see why philosophers treat it as a problem about action theory rather than just neuroscience.
