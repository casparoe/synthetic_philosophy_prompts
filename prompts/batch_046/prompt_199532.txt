The novel offers Stevens constantly reframing his loyalty to Lord Darlington—downplaying the dismissal of the Jewish maids, recasting his emotional withdrawal from Miss Kenton as professional dignity, and retrospectively editing his own memories—without ever quite confessing the falsehoods to himself. I want the discussion to ask not just whether Stevens is lying to himself, but whether the text forces us toward a dual-register model in which an agent maintains one evaluative stance for propositional belief (he knows Darlington's politics were contemptible, he knows he loved Kenton) and a separate, insulated stance for practical identity and emotional investment (he acts as if loyalty and restraint are the highest virtues). Does Ishiguro's portrayal vindicate the idea that self-deception requires this kind of partition, or does it show that Stevens never actually formed the flattering beliefs—he merely failed to integrate the evidence, leaving him in a state closer to structured ignorance than to deception?

If the novel ultimately adds something to the philosophical debate that pure armchair analysis misses—perhaps about the temporal dynamics of belief formation or the social cost of admitting error—I'd like you to say exactly what that contribution is and why it matters for anyone trying to keep their own beliefs honest.
