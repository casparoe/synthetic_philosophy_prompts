I teach an advanced undergraduate seminar in philosophy of risk and future ethics—ten students, one 90-minute session, movable tables, no screens required. I want a structured in-class simulation or deliberation game that makes participants feel, from the inside, the specific asymmetry driving suffering-risk (s-risk) reasoning: not merely that extreme suffering is bad, but that preventing it carries a moral urgency that lacks a symmetric counterpart in promoting extreme well-being, particularly when the affected entities are substrate-independent minds whose moral status is genuinely uncertain.

To avoid a generic longtermist discussion, frame the decision problem as “moral portfolio allocation under ontological uncertainty.” Participants must distribute a fixed budget of preventive resources across three categories: (A) near-future biological humans with unambiguous consciousness; (B) near-term digital or synthetic minds with ambiguous phenomenal status; and (C) distant synthetic minds with radically different architectures. The payoff structure should reward expected-suffering reduction but penalize “false positives” (wasting resources on non-conscious systems) and “false negatives” (failing to protect conscious ones) asymmetrically, so that rational play generates a conservative bias on B and C that feels defensible in the moment but becomes morally uncomfortable once the group sees the suffering costs of that conservatism. The asymmetry must emerge from the rules and incentives, not from my framing.
