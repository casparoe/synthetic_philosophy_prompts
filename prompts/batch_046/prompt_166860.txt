I am moderating a colloquium Q&A with an unnamed defender of a revived structuralist interpretation of Orwell’s essays on political language—specifically, the thesis that Orwell’s central target is not euphemism but syntactic degradation: the systematic loss of subordinate and counterfactual clause structures in bureaucratic prose, which she claims collapses the speaker’s capacity for modal reasoning about alternative policies. On her account, simplified grammar does not merely mirror impoverished thought but enforces it by stripping away the linguistic scaffolding required for hypothetical comparison.

Draft eight to ten interview questions that do not ask her to restate the view. Include at least one pressing her on how she distinguishes this syntactic-decay claim from the familiar euphemism-centered reading found in standard discussions of Orwell’s work; one probing the causal direction she asserts—whether grammatical simplification actually constrains cognition or merely tracks it—together with what historical or corpus-based patterns she takes to establish it; and one testing whether the mechanism applies symmetrically across ideological registers, or whether she allows that some political languages resist syntactic collapse through explicit grammatical discipline. For each of these three, provide a follow-up keyed to her most likely reply—anticipating, respectively, her appeal to Orwell’s analysis of passive-voice constructions, her reference to administrative corpora from the mid-twentieth century, and her concession that resistance is possible but requires institutional vigilance rather than individual intent.
