As a student working through the philosophy of liberty, I have adopted a specific version of the republican view: liberty is not merely the absence of actual interference (negative liberty) nor the presence of self-mastery (positive liberty), but rather the absence of domination understood as requiring what I term distributed veto power — that is, every affected party must hold an institutional mechanism to block decisions that could arbitrarily alter their civic status. I hold that this formulation alone explains why benevolent dictatorship and chattel slavery are structurally equivalent violations, even when the latter involves no day-to-day coercion. What would genuinely count against this position? I am asking for the arguments, conceptual counter-examples, and institutional histories that should move me away from it, ordered from fatal to merely uncomfortable. In particular, does the view risk collapsing into an implausibly demanding theory of democratic participation? Are there credible republican traditions that secure non-domination without any such distributed veto? And has the position been qualified — for instance, by allowing that veto power need only be potential rather than exercised — to the point where it is insulated from refutation? If so, is that insulation a virtue or a vice for a normative theory of this kind? Please respond in British English.
