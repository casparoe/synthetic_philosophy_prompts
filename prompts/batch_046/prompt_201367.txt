I have been working through Nick Bostrom's distinction between the self-sampling assumption and the self-indication assumption, particularly as it figures in the so-called presumptuous philosopher thought experiment. It strikes me that if one genuinely accepts self-indication—that one's own existence should be treated as evidence in favour of hypotheses that predict larger numbers of observers—then this reasoning ought to apply not only across competing cosmological models, but also within any single world across different reference classes. For instance, if I reason that I am more likely to exist in a universe with a trillion observers than in one with a million, should I not, by the same token, assign greater credence to being one of many identical twins in this world rather than an only child, or to living in a densely populated epoch rather than a sparse one? In other words, it seems that self-indication collapses into a form of self-sampling that operates intra-world, which would render Bostrom's distinction redundant. Where, precisely, does this line of argument fail?
