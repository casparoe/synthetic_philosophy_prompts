I'm preparing to lead next week's meeting of our philosophy of mind reading group — about eight second-year graduate students who have read some philosophy of perception but have never worked through disjunctivism in detail, and who tend to arrive assuming that any theory allowing hallucination to share phenomenal properties with perception is automatically committed to a common-factor view. I want them to grasp disjunctivism not as a mere slogan that hallucinations are "radically different" but specifically as a structural response to the argument from illusion: please give me a committed teaching plan that takes definite positions on every choice below rather than laying out alternatives for me to pick from. First, map the dependency order: which conceptual piece — the argument from illusion's premise that veridical and hallucinatory experiences share a mental kind, the disjunctive analysis of veridical experience as direct awareness of the world, the denial of that shared mental kind — must be in place before the next one can land, and why that ordering is forced. Second, choose and defend one concrete carrying example that should run through the entire session; I'm inclined to avoid the tired stick-in-water or Müller-Lyer cases, so commit to using an olfactory scenario — smelling smoke during an actual kitchen fire versus an identical phenomenal smell during a focal temporal-lobe hallucination — and specify exactly what features this example must have and what it must lack to do the philosophical work without smuggling in visual-spatial assumptions. Third, identify the misconceptions these particular students will form in sequence: I expect they'll confuse disjunctivism with naive direct realism, then with the claim that hallucinations lack phenomenal character altogether, and possibly conflate it with a predictive-processing account of misperception; for each, state precisely where in the arc it should be intercepted and how.

Fourth, give me one sharp end-of-session question that tests whether they've understood the dependency relation between the argument from illusion and the disjunctive analysis, not just the vocabulary. Fifth, say explicitly which parts of the wider debate — perhaps the relation to classical sense-datum theory or to contemporary disjunctivist treatments of illusion proper rather than full hallucination — should be openly left out at this level, and justify why leaving them out protects the arc rather than just saving time. Give your reasons for each commitment visible in the plan; do not present this as a menu of pedagogical options.
