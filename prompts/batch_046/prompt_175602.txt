In my political ethics seminar this term, we have been working through whether political morality permits actions that would be impermissible in private life, particularly the thesis—associated with Michael Walzer—that leaders sometimes acquire dirty hands by doing necessary wrongs. After class, two students I will call Jordan and Sam got into a sustained argument about what actually counts as dirty hands in modern bureaucratic governance, and they asked me to relay the exchange to someone who could referee it.

Jordan opened by defending a narrowly personalist reading: dirty hands applies only when a leader personally authorizes a specific immoral act—ordering the torture of a suspect, for example, or directly authorizing a deceptive public statement—and does so with full awareness that the act is wrong. For Jordan, the defining feature is not merely that harm occurs, but that the agent’s own will is directly implicated and that she experiences a genuine moral remainder in the form of guilt, shame, or the need for personal atonement. If a leader instead constructs institutional conditions—designing an enforcement protocol, say, where family separations become highly probable but are never explicitly ordered—Jordan insisted that this is not dirty hands but rather corruption, negligence, or structural injustice. The hands remain clean because the dirt never touches the agent directly; the wrong is mediated by bureaucracy and distributed across subordinates.

Sam replied that this distinction collapses in modern governance, where almost all significant wrongs are distributed across agencies, subcontractors, and automated decision systems. If dirty hands requires direct personal authorization, Sam argued, then the concept becomes an anachronism that applies only to pre-bureaucratic tyrants. Sam proposed an institutionalist revision: dirty hands should cover cases where a leader deliberately selects a policy architecture knowing it will systematically generate specific moral wrongs, even when execution is delegated. Crucially, Sam added a further claim—one that emerged in our seminar’s discussion of moral remainder—that the remainder should not be understood as private psychological guilt but as a public, structural debt: an institutional obligation to acknowledge, record, and repair the wrong through policy correction and transparent accounting. On this view, the leader’s hands are dirty precisely because she has engineered a system that produces wrongs she never touches personally.

Jordan countered that Sam’s revision eliminates the specificity of the concept. By Jordan’s lights, if every risky policy choice that produces harm becomes a case of dirty hands, then the category is no different from ordinary political responsibility for unintended consequences. Jordan also attacked the idea of a structural debt: if the obligation to repair and to be transparent is just a standard feature of democratic accountability, then labeling it a moral remainder is redundant. The special tragic weight of dirty hands, Jordan maintained, comes from the agent’s direct complicity in a singular wrong act that she knows she should not have done—something that cannot be reduced to good governance practices.

Sam responded that Jordan is smuggling in an overly individualistic, almost theatrical conception of moral agency—one that treats politics as a stage for personal heroism or suffering rather than as collective administration. Sam conceded that not every policy risk counts: the leader must specifically intend the wrong as a necessary means or accept it as an unavoidable cost of achieving a legitimate end. But Sam insisted that the institutional framing actually preserves the tragedy rather than dissolving it, because the leader can neither fully own the wrong (it is distributed across the system) nor fully disown it (it was engineered by her choice). This ambiguity, Sam said, is precisely the modern form of the dilemma.

Jordan then raised a final point: if Sam is right, then a leader who builds a harmful system and then institutes robust transparency and repair mechanisms has actually satisfied the moral remainder and cleansed her hands. But if the hands are truly dirty, Jordan argued, there should be some irreducible stain—something that good governance cannot wash away. Otherwise, dirty hands becomes just a temporary managerial problem rather than a permanent moral condition.

Sam answered that the stain is not erased by repair; it is institutionalized. The system carries a permanent record of the wrong—analogous to a constitutional acknowledgment of past injustice—so that future governance operates under the shadow of that debt. The leader does not become clean; she becomes permanently responsible for a structure that remembers its own wrong.

Please adjudicate this exchange move by move. Rate Jordan’s opening defense, Sam’s institutionalist reply, Jordan’s counter about specificity and structural debt, Sam’s response about distributed agency, Jordan’s final point about the irreducible stain, and Sam’s closing answer. For each move, indicate whether it is clearly mistaken, partly right but incomplete, clearly right, or somewhere in between, with brief justification. Then say who is more right overall, and identify what both parties are missing—perhaps about the role of democratic authorization in distinguishing dirty hands from corruption, about the difference between foreseen and intended wrongs within institutional design, or about whether the concept requires an audience that recognizes the wrong for the remainder to have any force. Do not simply declare a winner; show where each argument succeeds and fails, and note whether either move depends on an unstated premise that needs defense.
