I was discussing with a colleague in our university's digital humanities centre, over coffee after a rather dull committee meeting on research infrastructure, the design of an autonomous archival system. The system uses environmental sensors to record temperature, light spectra, and acoustic frequencies onto encrypted drives, which are then sealed in a climate-controlled vault with no display interface and no retrieval protocol scheduled for at least thirty years. My colleague insists that these stored measurements constitute genuine representations of the world "as it is in itself", wholly independent of any perceiving mind, precisely because the sensors operate without human intervention and the data will sit unread for decades. I feel there is a deep conceptual fault in this picture—something about whether one can coherently describe representational content that is never, even in principle, available to any mind capable of interpretation—but I struggle to articulate the difficulty without sounding as though I am merely rehearsing familiar scepticism about external reality. I am not asking for technical criticism of the sensors; I want to know whether her philosophical claim holds together, and whether there is a classical line of reasoning that targets exactly this attempt to conceive of unperceived representational states. Please reply in British English, avoid offering me a bibliography, and simply walk me through the reasoning as it applies to her vault: where, if anywhere, does the incoherence lie, and what would a defender of her view say in response?
