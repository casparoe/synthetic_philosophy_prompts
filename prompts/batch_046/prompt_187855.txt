In our university student-governance charter there is an Article XII that sets out the amendment procedure; Article XII also provides that Article XII itself may be revised by a two-thirds vote of the executive committee, with no appeal to any higher authority. During a recent dispute over whether the committee could invoke Article XII to eliminate student elections, a defender of the status quo dismissed the worry with the one-liner: "The rule that changes the rules is just a rule like any other; treating self-amendment as paradoxical is a category mistake." I want to understand exactly what work that sentence might be doing. Please lay out, as a menu of distinct precise theses, what "the rule that changes the rules is just a rule like any other" could mean—ranging from the syntactic and trivial to the normative and radical—and for each say whether it is true, false, or coherent, and whether it actually supports the conclusion that there is nothing specially problematic about Article XII being used to abolish elections. In particular, distinguish readings that concern (i) the logical form of self-reference, (ii) the absence of a hierarchical "master rule" in the system, (iii) the normative authority of the present committee relative to future ones, and (iv) whether a system containing such a clause still counts as "governed by rules" in any meaningful sense. For each reading, indicate whether accepting it commits one to a particular view about legal self-reference—say, that no paradox of hierarchy arises here, or that omnipotence in rule-change is compatible with genuine bindingness—and tell me which reading the speaker most likely intended if they were genuinely trying to dissolve the objection rather than merely changing the subject.
