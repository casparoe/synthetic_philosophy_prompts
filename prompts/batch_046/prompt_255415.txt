Write a speech for the following occasion. You are delivering the principal address at a municipal ceremony granting honorary citizenship to a group of long-resident scholars, artists, and clinicians who have lived and worked in the city for over twenty years but remain formally stateless. Roughly half the audience are moral and political philosophers well-versed in debates over associative obligations, partiality, and cosmopolitan justice; the other half are family members, patients, students, and local officials with no background in philosophy. The organizers have asked you to confront, rather than avoid, the central tension of the event: whether those who hold formal citizenship have special moral claims on one another—claims of loyalty, priority, or gratitude—that can justify privileging compatriots over these honorees, who are equally embedded in the community but lack the legal status. Your position must shape the speech structurally, determining what you can sincerely say about belonging and obligation in this room without either diminishing the dignity of the people being honored or retreating into vague universalism. Reach and defend a substantive conclusion about whether compatriot preference is morally defensible, and do so in a tone that is formal, respectful, and fully intelligible to both halves of the audience. Use the particular emotional stakes of the ceremony—recognition after decades of contribution, the absence of legal protection, the presence of those who have known the honorees as neighbors rather than as citizens—to carry the argument, rather than relying on abstract thought experiments or disciplinary citations.
