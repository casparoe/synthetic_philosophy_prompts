As a novelist constructing characters who wrestle with long-term commitments, I keep returning to a particular justification people use when they override their present desires. One character refuses a lucrative but dishonest opportunity, not merely because it is wrong, but because she imagines a version of herself who has fully reconciled every contradiction in her values and retained that clarity permanently; she treats that hypothetical figure's preferences as more genuinely hers than her own present temptation. Another character, facing addiction, describes his future, fully coherent self as having a kind of veto over his current cravings, as if the present self is only a provisional draft. This seems distinct from ordinary rational planning or autonomy, since the authority derives specifically from maximal internal coherence and complete information, not from any particular goal. Does philosophy have an established name for this authority relation—the idea that one's true will belongs to an idealized, contradiction-free continuation of oneself—or for the difficulty of reconciling such idealized wills when they belong to different people? I am not asking for a bibliography; I want to know whether this carving has been made, what its nearest neighbors are, and where its boundaries lie.
