Draft the adversarial-collaboration document that a "thin kernel realist" and a "cultural scaffold constructivist" should produce after arguing for months about whether cross-cultural divergence in folk morality—particularly the WEIRD/non-WEIRD split on loyalty, authority, purity, harm, and fairness—tells us anything about moral objectivity.

Assume both parties are intellectually honest, well-read in the psychology literature, and share a rationalist forum's norms: they want exact formulations rather than rhetoric; they distinguish empirical disagreements from normative ones; and they are willing to concede verbal disputes.

The document must contain:

1. Numbered agreed empirical claims. Include: (a) the observed pattern of binding-foundation suppression in WEIRD populations relative to non-WEIRD samples; (b) the existence of substantial within-culture variance in both WEIRD and non-WEIRD groups; (c) the methodological limits of current cross-cultural moral-psychology instruments, especially regarding translation and social-desirability effects; (d) a jointly endorsed criterion for what would count as a culturally unbiased measure of moral judgment. Do not list references, citations, or reading lists; write from shared knowledge.

2. The disagreements, in neutral wording each side accepts for its own view. Separate at least three layers: (i) whether there exist cross-culturally invariant moral kernels—e.g., harm-avoidance and reciprocity—with stable cognitive signatures; (ii) whether the existence of such kernels, if granted, supports any form of normative objectivity; (iii) whether the WEIRD profile is best explained as a culturally specific scaffolding that suppresses binding intuitions, or as an evolutionary-developmental endpoint that reveals which foundations are genuinely modular. Present these in a table with three columns: point of divergence, kernel-realist formulation, scaffold-constructivist formulation.

3. The cruxes—sub-questions such that resolving them would shift at least one party's credence. You must include:
- The Variance Crux: does high within-culture variance on purity and authority norms, paired with relatively low variance on harm norms, support modular architecture (some foundations are hard-wired and others culturally plastic) or coherent architecture (all foundations are equally plastic, but local normative coherence makes them cluster)?
- The Reversed-Weights Crux: imagine a well-documented, cognitively typical population that weights loyalty and purity above harm and fairness in practical reasoning. Would this count as evidence against kernel realism, or merely as a different but equally valid kernel weighting?
- The Predictive Crux: if a large-scale, long-term intervention successfully teaches binding-foundation reasoning to WEIRD adolescents without measurably affecting harm/fairness reasoning, what should each side predict about the stability and reversibility of the change?

For each crux, specify the concrete study design, hypothetical result, or thought-experiment case both sides agree is relevant, and record each side's explicit prediction in advance.

4. A section analyzing whether part of the dispute is verbal. In particular, examine whether "moral objectivity" is being used in an indexical sense (objective relative to shared human cognitive architecture) versus a non-indexical sense (independent of all minds). State clearly whether resolving this ambiguity dissolves any of the three disagreements above, or whether substantive disagreement remains regardless.

5. Inject and apply the following conceptual distinction: "moral modularity" (the view that loyalty, authority, purity, harm, and fairness are separable cognitive mechanisms with distinct evolutionary histories and can be independently strengthened or suppressed) versus "moral coherence" (the view that these are nodes in a single culturally transmitted normative system that tends to shift as a package). Frame the Variance Crux explicitly around whether observed patterns support modularity or coherence, and require both sides to state which interpretation they endorse and why.

Write in the voice of a longtime rationalist-blog reader drafting this for a reading group that has been talking past each other. Keep it formal, precise, and free of strawmen. Do not ask for bibliographies, citations, or reading lists. Do not quote any specific text verbatim; paraphrase if needed. Avoid depending on obscure named theories that a well-trained but non-specialist model would not have encountered in pretraining.
