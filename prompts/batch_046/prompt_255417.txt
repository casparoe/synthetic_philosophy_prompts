I am a doctoral candidate in epistemology and the philosophy of science, and I am preparing my job-market talk for a research-intensive philosophy department. I defend a reconciliationist reading of American pragmatism: Peirce's convergence account of truth and James's criterion that belief is warranted when it proves expedient in the manner of our thinking are not rival semantic theories, but complementary normative frameworks separated by Peirce's own classification of the sciences into theoretical, practical, and normative branches. In the theoretical sciences governed by logic, inquiry aims at the final opinion of an unlimited community and convergence functions as the regulative ideal; in ethics and aesthetics—domains Peirce treats within his normative sciences—there is no single fact of the matter independent of the inquirer's own commitments, so the final opinion is structurally unattainable, and James's local, expediency-based criteria for belief-fixation operate legitimately within the bounds Peirce sets. Thus James works inside the division of labour Peirce sketched but never fully applied to belief-formation, rather than against it.

The audience will be three analytic epistemologists working post-Gettier on justification, knowledge, and the value of inquiry; one historian of American philosophy who has published on Peirce's Harvard lectures of 1903 and his contributions to The Monist in 1905; a philosopher of science focused on scientific realism and theory-ladenness; and several graduate students. They are generally skeptical of reconciliation narratives in the history of philosophy, they value textual precision, and they are quick to spot anachronism. They will not be hostile, but they will press hard on whether I am papering over a genuine philosophical disagreement.
