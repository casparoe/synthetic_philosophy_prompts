Write the full specification for an adequate normative theory of the right to work and leisure — not a survey of labor law, but the philosophical criteria that determine whether a society distributes labor and free time justly. Give me the desiderata: which phenomena the theory must explain and not merely classify (for instance, why karoshi in Japan and the 35-hour week in France are not just policy divergences but potential violations of conceptually distinct rights); which distinctions it must preserve without collapse (such as between a right to be employed, a right to be free from compelled labor, and a right to leisure that is not reducible to recuperative rest for productivity); which historical conceptions it must either accommodate or reject with explicit reason (the Protestant work ethic's fusion of labor and moral worth, and the socialist claim that structural unemployment is a social failure rather than an individual one); and which empirical findings it cannot contradict (e.g., on health effects of sustained overwork, or on whether reducing working hours lowers aggregate output). For each desideratum, state the motivating case or consideration behind it. Identify which requirements are genuinely non-negotiable and which are preferences of particular ideological camps. Then flag any pair of desiderata you suspect are jointly unsatisfiable — for example, whether honoring the Protestant work ethic's view of labor as a calling can be reconciled with a universal right to leisure that is not instrumentally justified. Keep the weight on the criteria themselves rather than on scoring existing theories, though you may briefly note how a libertarian, a republican, and a Marxist framework each fare on one especially divisive point. Finally, separate what is settled in the philosophical literature on work and leisure from what remains genuinely contested among people who know the topic, and do not present a contested normative view as if it were the consensus.
