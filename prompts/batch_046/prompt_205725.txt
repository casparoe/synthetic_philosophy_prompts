You are an experienced philosophy instructor and curriculum designer specializing in applied ethics and collective action theory. Before producing any design, take a few minutes to brainstorm: identify the precise conceptual tension you want participants to feel in their decision-making bodies, not merely grasp intellectually, and anticipate where emotional resistance will arise.

Design a runnable, 75-minute in-class activity for a seminar of 18 philosophy undergraduates with mixed prior exposure to ethics. The session should make them experience the force of the inefficacy objection in collective harm scenarios—specifically, the gap between an agent’s causal impotence and the apparent moral significance of their contribution when harms are distributed, probabilistic, and structurally redundant. Do not lecture about the objection up front, and do not name it formally until the debrief.

Inject the following niche framing into the rules and information structure: treat the collective harm not as a linear accumulation of discrete actions but as a threshold-governed, non-linear system. Each individual choice shifts the aggregate probability distribution across a hidden tipping point, but no participant can observe where the system currently sits relative to that threshold, nor can any single withdrawal guarantee that the threshold is avoided. The incentives and hidden-state mechanics should be arranged so that students discover for themselves that individually rational, locally justifiable choices aggregate into a collective pattern each person finds unacceptable—yet no one can identify a specific decision of theirs that deterministically altered the outcome.

Provide exactly the following components:

1. Setting and group structure: how tables, roles, or stations are arranged, and whether interaction is simultaneous, sequential, or mediated.
2. Exact rules, required materials, and a payoff/incentive scheme using only non-monetary points (e.g., reputation tokens, symbolic privileges, or graded micro-credits) that generates genuine pressure to contribute or withdraw.
3. The hidden information architecture: what each student knows about their own action, what they know about others’ actions, and what remains hidden—especially the current aggregate state relative to the unknown threshold.
4. A minute-by-minute timeline for the full 75 minutes, including when the hidden state is revealed (if ever) and when collective outcomes are calculated.
5. A structured debrief that maps specific moments in the activity onto conceptual parts: causal inefficacy, redundancy of individual withdrawal, the distinction between statistical and token-level harm, and the hidden-threshold dynamics. Be explicit about what this activity cannot reproduce—such as temporal delay between action and harm, or genuine irreversibility—and state precisely what you, the facilitator, must supply verbally to fill those gaps.
6. At least two predictable failures for a first run, with exact recovery steps.
