Write a joke that turns on Douglas Hofstadter’s view that the self is not tied to a single physical brain but is a pattern that can, in principle, be instantiated in more than one brain at once, with both instances genuinely being “you.” The joke should place this distributed self in a concrete, somewhat bureaucratic or domestic situation in which two people—each with a distinct brain, but sharing the same self-pattern through prolonged, symbiotic mental intimacy rather than through cloning or digital upload—must negotiate a single legal or social obligation designed for a unique “I.” The punchline should depend on the actual philosophical tension: from the first-person perspective each instance genuinely experiences being the original, yet from the outside there is no non-arbitrary fact about which one “really” continues the self, so a third party applying a rule built for singular agents ends up in an absurd logical tangle. Avoid generic science-fiction tropes; instead, let the absurdity arise specifically from the mismatch between indexical self-reference and third-person administrative logic. Keep the joke concise, with the conceptual point intact for someone familiar with Hofstadter’s arguments about spouses containing fragments of each other’s patterns, but clear enough that the humor lands without requiring knowledge of any particular text.
