In a seminar I run on rational choice, discussion of Buridan's ass has reached an impasse that I find more revealing than standard presentations suggest. Every participant accepts that a perfectly rational agent facing two options of exactly equal value has no comparative reason to prefer one over the other; yet one faction insists that rationality therefore demands a randomizing mechanism or arbitrary tie-breaker, while the other insists that rational suspension—refusing to choose—is not only permissible but required, since selecting without reason violates autonomy rather than fulfilling it. They have not moved in months. I want a diagnosis, not a verdict: is this disagreement genuinely about the nature of rationality, or is it verbal, turning on whether choice must include a mental cause? Is there a hidden empirical claim about how human decision-making actually works? Or are the two sides answering different questions—one about normative rationality, one about phenomenological agency—under a single heading? And please consider a specific conceptual distinction I have been testing: between structural indifference, where the option-space is symmetric by design, and contingent indifference, where equality is an accidental feature of the particular pair. Does this distinction illuminate the stall, or does it collapse under pressure? Given your diagnosis, what would actually count as progress: a formal result about decision rules, a phenomenological description of suspended agency, or a change of subject?
