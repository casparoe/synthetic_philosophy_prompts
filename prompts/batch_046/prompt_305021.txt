I'm preparing material for a graduate seminar on conceptual history and archaeology, and I've been arguing with a colleague about whether "collapse" is a useful analytical category or merely a retrospective narrative convenience. We kept returning to the same three cases—the Bronze Age palace economies, the Classic Maya lowlands, and Easter Island's statue-building culture—but we could not agree on whether these represent a single kind of societal failure or three entirely different processes that only look alike from a distance. I want to close our session with a poem that takes this conceptual problem seriously rather than using the cases as decorative backdrop. Write a sestina—six stanzas of six lines, with six rotating end-words following the standard pattern, and a final three-line envoi using all six words—whose end-words are: stone, root, break, remain, name, shift. The poem's subject should be the question of whether "collapse" denotes a real, unified kind of failure or just a label applied to heterogeneous local transformations. Use the formal rotation to enact the argument structurally: in the early stanzas, "break" and "name" should dominate in contexts evoking catastrophic discontinuity and the imposition of a single historical story, while in the middle and later stanzas "remain" and "shift" should gradually take over, showing the same archaeological traces—abandoned centers, altered settlement patterns, changed material cultures—read as continuity, adaptation, and regional reorganization rather than disappearance. The word "stone" should carry both the literal monument and the figurative weight of judgment; "root" should suggest both the agricultural base that some narratives treat as failing and the deeper social structures that persist. By the envoi, all six words must converge in a single syntactic movement that leaves the tension genuinely open: neither affirming collapse as a coherent phenomenon nor dissolving it entirely into mere change, but holding both readings in suspension so that the form's inevitable convergence mirrors the historian's inability to settle whether these societies failed or simply became something unrecognizable to later chroniclers. The poem should be accurate enough that someone familiar with the archaeological debates—particularly the critiques of unified collapse models and the emphasis on regional variation and post-abandonment reorganization—recognizes the specific conceptual stakes, but it should remain a poem first: the repetition and rhyme must feel structurally necessary, not like an essay in verse.
