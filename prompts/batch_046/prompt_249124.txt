After a long and somewhat circular argument with a friend about whether history exhibits a directional tendency toward justice, I realized we were conflating several distinct commitments and talking past each other. I want a taxonomy for a philosophy of history reading group, structured as a branching decision tree with labeled nodes, that sorts actual positions on this question without ever asking a respondent to name a philosopher. The instrument should have about eight leaves and must be built from the logical space rather than from a list of authorities.

To prevent a simple encyclopedia recitation, the tree must decompose the debate along three axes that are usually collapsed together. Axis one concerns ontological status: is the directional tendency a real causal or structural feature of institutional development over centuries; a purely retrospective hermeneutic pattern that makes past suffering and resistance intelligible but predicts nothing; a normative regulative ideal that guides action without describing anything actual; or an illusion with no basis? Axis two concerns mechanism, applying only to those who affirmed the real causal option in axis one: does the tendency operate through dialectical conflict that produces synthetic reconciliation at a higher order, or through competitive selection among institutional forms that gradually retain more inclusive and stable arrangements? Axis three concerns epistemic stance: can the tendency be affirmed only by a retrospective historian who knows the outcome; only by a participatory agent inside the process for whom the arc is a condition of meaningful action; or also by a detached observer who verifies it from outside?

Each branch node should be phrased as a concrete claim or case distinction—such as whether a participant in 1848 could legitimately invoke long-run justice to justify present sacrifice, or whether an observer in 2500 could verify the tendency without being embedded in the institutions studied—so that someone can answer without knowing which view the answer leads to. The tree must include as leaves at least the following: a Hegelian-style dialectical view, an institutional evolutionary view, a retrospective narrative coherence view, a participatory immanentist view, and an anti-teleological position. Each leaf should carry its name together with exactly one sentence describing its most awkward commitment; if a logical combination is defensible but unexplored, label it unnamed. Please also identify any logical combinations that remain unoccupied, with a brief diagnosis of whether the gap is due to incoherence, unattractiveness, or mere neglect, and flag any pair of leaves that the tree cannot cleanly separate. Do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citation requests, and do not quote any passage verbatim; if you refer to a specific claim, paraphrase it without quotation marks.
