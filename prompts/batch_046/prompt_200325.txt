I am preparing a report on whether moral justification can be aggregated across persons, using T. M. Scanlon’s contractualist framework—that an act is wrong if it violates a principle no affected party could reasonably reject—as my point of departure. I have settled on the following verdicts about concrete cases, but I cannot see whether they rest on a single principle or on an unstable mix of intuitions. I would like you to propose the most coherent principle, or small family of principles, that captures them; state precisely which clause is motivated by which case; tell me what the principle predicts for the unlisted scenario below; and be explicit about which of my verdicts must be sacrificed if the principle is to remain simple. I also ask that you indicate where your reasoning is most vulnerable—that is, what argument or empirical finding would lead you to overturn your proposed principle.

Here are the cases.

One: A surgeon kills one healthy patient to harvest organs that will save five others. Verdict: impermissible. The single victim can reject any principle permitting this.

Two: A traffic authority adopts a design change that will save approximately fifty lives over ten years, but will also cause exactly one fatal accident that the old design would have avoided. Verdict: permissible. The statistical victim cannot reasonably reject a principle that produces such a large net preservation of life.

Three: A manufacturing plant emits a toxin that raises each of ten thousand nearby residents’ risk of severe illness by one-tenth of one percent; shutting the plant eliminates that risk but renders one hundred workers permanently unemployed in a region with no alternative work. Verdict: tentatively permissible to continue operating. The aggregated risk equals roughly ten serious illnesses, yet it is distributed so thinly that no individual bears a harm comparable to the unemployment of one hundred.

Four: A physician has resources sufficient to save either one patient, A, or five patients, B through E, each of whom needs an equal share of the same resource. Verdict: saving the five is permissible; failing to save A is not wrong.

Five: The physician has already begun administering the full resource to B through E collectively, and can now save A only by actively withdrawing the treatment from all five. Verdict: impermissible. Even though the net lives saved are negative compared with inaction, actively taking from the five to benefit the one violates a constraint that Case Four does not trigger.

The tension I see is that Cases Two and Three allow numbers to count in justification, while Cases One and Five treat pairwise constraints as absolute regardless of magnitude. Is there a contractualist distinction—perhaps between direct and statistical harm, between doing and allowing, or between pairwise and collective justification—that renders this pattern coherent? What does your principle say about this further case: in a vaccine trial, 999 participants face a 0.1 percent risk of severe adverse reaction, with no individual facing more than that risk, while one control-group patient is certain to die unless the trial proceeds and produces a cure? And please say plainly: if your principle requires me to drop one of my five verdicts, which one is it, and what specific argument—for example, a demonstration that contractualism cannot accommodate statistical reasoning at all, or a collapse of the doing-allowing distinction—would change your mind about the principle you propose?
