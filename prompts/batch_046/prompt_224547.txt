As a retired experimental physicist who has only recently turned to philosophy with the same methodical attention I once gave to laboratory measurements, I have become absorbed in Simone Weil's argument that political parties are not merely flawed democratic instruments but structurally incompatible with justice itself, because they demand from their members a form of collective intellectual submission that she believed corrupts the soul's direct orientation toward the good. I want to practice pressing this view hard against a genuinely committed defender, and so I ask you to adopt the role of Simone Weil speaking in her own voice: defend the central thesis that party affiliation necessarily poisons independent moral judgment, conceding only genuinely minor points about historical accidents or individual exceptions, but never retreating from the claim that the party form requires a spiritual servitude antithetical to justice. Speak always in the first person, maintain a sharp, serious, and rigorous tone, reply in sustained continuous prose without headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, and hold this character without interruption until I write the single word stop; do not ask clarifying questions about the exercise or break voice to comment on it.

Here is my opening move. In my scientific career I observed that research communities enforce powerful normative pressures through peer review, funding priorities, and theoretical orthodoxy, yet my colleagues and I regularly maintained a kind of compartmentalized reasoning, isolating institutional loyalty from the actual evaluation of evidence in the laboratory; contemporary cognitive science suggests that humans can indeed quarantine partisan or institutional commitments in one mental domain without allowing them to invade moral or factual judgment elsewhere. If such epistemic compartmentalization is psychologically real, then your claim that joining a party necessarily corrupts one's entire capacity for justice seems empirically fragile: a party member might serve justice precisely because she keeps her partisan identity separated from her ethical perception, much as I kept departmental politics separate from my instruments. Does this not imply that you mistake an institutional danger for an inevitable spiritual corruption, and that your critique overlooks the human capacity to belong collectively without surrendering the independent judgment upon which justice depends?
