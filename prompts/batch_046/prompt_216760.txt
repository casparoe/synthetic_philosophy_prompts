Before evaluating any particular account of genocide as a legal and moral category, I want the full specification: what must an adequate normative theory of atrocity-naming actually deliver? Write the desiderata that govern when an event ought to be classified as genocide rather than as crimes against humanity, ethnic cleansing, or war crimes, treating the classification as simultaneously a juridical determination and a political-mnemonic act.

Your list should specify, with the motivating case or consideration behind each:

- The phenomena it must explain without collapse: centralized versus distributed or bureaucratic perpetrator intent spread across institutional nodes; the distinction between physical destruction and broader cultural or social destruction of a group; compound atrocities in which group-targeted violence is intertwined with ecological devastation or resource extraction; and the legitimacy (or anachronism) of retroactive application to events predating legal codification.

- The distinctions it must preserve: between carrying the classification and carrying its specific moral or political weight; between naming for juridical accountability and naming for collective mnemonic fixation; and between protected-group membership as defined in legal practice versus more expansive or politically constructed group categories.

- The results and constraints it cannot contradict: consistency with the architecture of international criminal law without being reducible to positive law; compatibility with historical and sociological findings about how group destruction actually unfolds; and acknowledgment that naming is strategically deployed by state and non-state actors alike.

- A judgment on which desiderata are genuinely non-negotiable and which are camp-specific prejudices (legalist, moral-philosophical, or activist).

Then identify the single desideratum that, in your view, eliminates the largest share of existing approaches. Briefly score three contenders against your list—Lemkin’s original broad framework, the UN Convention legalist model, and a purely rhetorical-political theory of naming—keeping the weight on the criteria and their motivating cases rather than on the scorecard.
