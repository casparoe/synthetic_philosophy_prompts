I have been defending, in discussions with colleagues, a philosophical interpretation of the Fischer controversy that moves the debate from diplomatic history to collective moral agency. Rather than asking only whether German leaders caused the First World War, I ask whether the Reich can be held responsible as a corporate agent. Specifically, I hold that the issuance of the Blank Check to Austria-Hungary and the activation of the Schlieffen Plan manifested what I call institutional intentionality: a structured, deliberative posture embodied in cabinet and general-staff decision-making that belongs to the state itself, not to any aggregate of citizens or soldiers. On this view, the Reich bears primary moral and causal responsibility for the outbreak in July 1914, and this responsibility is non-distributive—it grounds legitimate reparative obligations for successor states but does not translate into individual blame for ordinary people. I also maintain that standard objections—structural determinism arising from alliance rigidities, shared culpability among the other great powers, and the epistemic limitations of the Bethmann Hollweg cabinet—fail because they confuse causal contribution with agential recklessness and treat the nation-state as a mere collection of individuals rather than as a unit of practical reasoning.

What would have to be true for this position to be wrong? I want an honest map of its exposure. Which historical findings—say, about the actual deliberative dynamics within the cabinet or about the logistical inevitability of the Schlieffen timetable—would undermine the claim that institutional intentionality was genuinely present? Which philosophical arguments about collective agency, distributive responsibility, or the conditions for corporate personhood would be decisive rather than merely awkward? Does my distinction between corporate culpability and individual blame collapse under pressure, or have I qualified institutional intentionality so narrowly that no counterevidence could reach it? Please rank the potential defeaters from fatal to uncomfortable, say which ones defenders of this kind of view would themselves concede as damaging, and assess whether insulating a claim from falsification is itself a philosophical defect for a theory of national responsibility.
