After more than a decade living in societies that treat autonomous individual choice as the foundation of moral and legal life, I have recently returned to the community where I was raised, and I am now confronting an institutional arrangement that has forced me to question whether the self I have come to regard as my own is actually a culturally specific construction. In this region, adult children who return home are expected to enter a multigenerational economic unit in which all income is pooled, major life decisions—including marriage, career change, and residence—require collective approval rather than individual consent, and one's obligations are determined by structural relational position rather than by contract or voluntary agreement. The elders and family authorities describe this not as a constraint on pre-existing individuals, but as the only social form in which persons genuinely exist; to insist on a private trajectory is, in their view, not an assertion of rights but a kind of category error about what human beings are. I have been offered permanent security and belonging within this unit, yet accepting it means abandoning the framework I had adopted abroad, in which a person is defined by the capacity for independent choice and collective demands are legitimate only insofar as they are voluntarily assumed. I am not seeking practical guidance about family negotiation; rather, I want to understand whether the conception embedded in this practice—that the community is ontologically prior to the individual and that identity and moral standing flow from membership—actually captures the relation between person and community more accurately than the opposing view, or whether it merely rationalizes structural dependence as philosophical truth. Does entering such an arrangement constitute the realization of a genuinely relational self, or does it represent a form of self-erasure that the individualist framework correctly identifies as harmful? And if I were to refuse, would I be defending a necessary boundary of human dignity, or would I be imposing an alien, atomistic conception of the person onto a social form that understands dependence and belonging not as limitations but as constitutive conditions?
