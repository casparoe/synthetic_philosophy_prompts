I have recently become the healthcare proxy for a parent with advanced dementia, and an imminent decision about an invasive, life-extending procedure has forced me to take moral uncertainty seriously rather than as an abstract puzzle. I now hold that when one is genuinely uncertain among moral theories that lack a shared metric of value—say, a preference-utilitarian calculus, a deontological prohibition on instrumentalization, and a care-ethical commitment to relational dignity—one should not aggregate expectations across theories. Instead, one should adopt a precautionary posture: refrain from actions that are gravely wrong according to any credible theory to which one assigns substantial credence, unless refraining is gravely wrong under all credible theories. This strikes me as the only intellectually honest response to incommensurability, especially when the stakes are concrete and irreversible.

What would count against this position? I want more than the familiar charge that it produces paralysis whenever omission and commission carry asymmetric risks across theories. I want to know which specific cases, formal considerations, or empirical findings expose it—perhaps situations where one theory assigns catastrophic disvalue to an action but receives only marginal credence, causing the rule to overweight outliers; or cases where both acting and not acting are severely condemned by different credible theories, leaving the agent with no permissible path. I also want an honest assessment of whether the view risks being qualified into unfalsifiability—by redefining “credible” or “gravely wrong” post hoc so that the rule is always satisfied—and whether such insulation is a defect for a practical stance of this kind.

Please distinguish the defeaters that defenders of precautionary moral reasoning would themselves concede from those they would dismiss. Rank them from fatal to merely awkward, and indicate whether any result—in decision theory about intertheoretic value comparison, or in moral psychology about how agents actually resolve deep conflict—would genuinely move the view, or whether it is now protected by its own architecture. Finally, if my own intuitions about the right course in my parent’s case conflict with what the precautionary rule delivers, I want to know whether that conflict should overturn my verdict, the rule, or my confidence in the incommensurability claim that motivates it.
