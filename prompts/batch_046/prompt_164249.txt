I'm writing a paper for my philosophy of economics seminar on ambiguity aversion after studying the Ellsberg paradox. I noticed that many national climate-adaptation agencies now operate what I will call a bifurcated rationality protocol: for risks with calibrated frequency distributions, such as flood return periods, they mandate standard expected-cost-benefit analysis; for risks they classify as deep uncertainty, such as climate tipping-point cascades or novel synthetic-biology interactions, they switch to a precautionary maximin rule and explicitly prohibit the assignment of probabilities. Crucially, the switch is not triggered by the actual epistemic state of the analysts but by an institutional vote among advisory scientists on whether the phenomenon is probability-amenable.

What philosophical commitments about rationality, knowledge, and democratic legitimacy does this institutional practice presuppose? Reconstruct its implicit epistemology: does it treat ambiguity as an objective feature of the world, or as a subjective failure of expertise? Does it presuppose that rational decision-making must be unitary across domains, or that different contexts license different norms of reasoning? Is there an implicit theory of value that explains why maximin is appropriate precisely where probabilities are withheld? Furthermore, if we replaced this bifurcated protocol with a uniform Bayesian framework that requires explicit priors even for so-called deep uncertainty, what exactly in current administrative and conceptual practice would have to change?
