Below is a short essay arguing that care ethics contains an under-examined tension between proximity and structural distance, which I try to articulate using a distinction borrowed from the philosophy of language—between indexical and anaphoric reference. I am not claiming that anyone in the care-ethics literature has developed this distinction; rather, I am testing whether it does useful conceptual work.

The standard framing of care ethics emphasizes receptivity to particular others. In the face-to-face encounter, the cared-for is present not as an instance of a category but as this specific other whose need registers in perception and affect. This is what I will call indexical care: its object is anchored in the immediate relational field, much as an indexical term like "this" or "you" picks out its referent through the context of utterance. The moral weight of care, on this reading, derives partly from this direct referential route—there is no mediating chain that could fail.

Yet care ethicists also want to claim that we bear obligations to distant strangers, to victims of institutional injustice we will never meet, and to future generations whose needs are accessible only through representation and narrative. The usual response distinguishes "caring about" from "caring for," but this risks splitting care ethics into an intimate, affective practice on one side and a thin distributive attitude on the other, precisely the bifurcation the approach was meant to resist.

I propose that we understand mediated care not as a diluted version of indexical care but as anaphoric care. Like an anaphoric pronoun that refers back to an antecedent through a chain of descriptions, anaphoric care reaches its object through institutional roles, statistical representations, or narrative accounts that maintain a referential link to a particular other. The particularity is preserved, but it is mediated. The danger, however, is reference failure: if the chain is broken—if categories replace names, or aggregates replace narratives—the anaphoric link collapses, and care degenerates into bureaucratic management. This suggests that extending care across distance is not primarily a problem of emotional reach but of referential maintenance. The ethical task becomes not merely feeling for distant others but constructing and repairing the representational chains that keep them present as particular objects of care.

If this framing is right, then proximity bias is not just a psychological limitation to be overcome but a structural feature of one mode of moral reference. At the same time, anaphoric care introduces its own norms: transparency about the chain, accountability for its breaks, and resistance to the substitution of category-management for particular concern. Whether this genuinely advances care ethics, or whether it merely imports linguistic machinery that obscures more familiar distinctions—such as Joan Tronto’s phases of care or the caring-about/caring-for distinction—seems to me an open question.

Please comment on the essay. I am especially interested in three things. First, does the indexical/anaphoric distinction genuinely illuminate the tension between proximity and structural obligation, or is it an unnecessary metaphorical overlay that complicates without clarifying? Second, does the argument misrepresent the resources already available within care ethics for addressing distance, and if so, which texts or distinctions should I be engaging? Third, is "referential maintenance" a coherent normative concept with practical implications, or does it collapse back into standard institutional ethics? I would also welcome any observations on whether the injection makes the argument more interesting or merely more obscure, and suggestions for sharpening the conclusion so that it does not resolve the tension too neatly.
