Write a poem of exactly five quatrains—twenty lines total—exploring the Buddhist doctrine of anattā (no-self) through the canonical five skandhas in their traditional order: rūpa, vedanā, saññā, saṅkhāra, and viññāṇa. Each quatrain must open with its skandha named in Pali, untranslated, followed by three lines describing that aggregate’s activity. No line in the poem may contain a first-person singular pronoun (I, me, my, mine, myself); the speaker, if any, must be grammatically reconstructed from the aggregate itself rather than from a persisting owner. Use an ABAB rhyme scheme within each quatrain, but with a migrating sonic bond: the B-rhyme sound of quatrain n must become the A-rhyme sound of quatrain n+1, so that continuity is carried by impersonal phonetic elements rather than by a stable voice. The poem should avoid both nihilist denial of experience and romantic affirmation of an enduring self; instead, it should leave the conventional self dissolved yet functioning. End the fifth quatrain (viññāṇa) with a line that could be spoken by any consciousness—this one, another, or none in particular—so that the formal absence of “I” and the migration of rhyme together enact the absence of an enduring subject behind the aggregates. Render the philosophical content with enough precision that someone familiar with Abhidharma recognizes the aggregates and the anattā position, while a reader without that background still feels the structural dissolution of personal identity through the poem’s shifting, ownerless music.
