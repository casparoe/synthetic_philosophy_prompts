The exam question and student response are below.

Question: Explain Millikan's direct/derived proper function distinction and its role in solving misrepresentation. Then assess Davidson's swampman objection: does an entity with no evolutionary history have mental content? Be precise about whether the threat applies to direct functions, derived functions, or both.

Student answer: Millikan grounds content in proper functions fixed by evolutionary selection. Direct proper functions belong to mechanisms reproduced because they performed a specific task—like a frog's detector being selected for small dark moving prey. Derived proper functions arise when an organism learns to redeploy an existing mechanism for a new purpose; they depend on direct functions but can vary individually. Misrepresentation occurs at production: when the producer generates a token outside its selected conditions—say, firing at a pebble—the direct proper function of the producer is unfulfilled. The consumer, however, still responds according to its own direct proper function, treating the token as prey. So error is production-failure, not consumption-failure. Davidson's swampman is a molecule-for-molecule human duplicate formed accidentally in a swamp with no evolutionary history. Since he lacks selectional history, his mechanisms have no direct proper functions. But he can learn and adapt, so he acquires derived proper functions through individual history. Thus the objection only threatens direct functions, not derived ones. In fact, Millikan allows derived functions to layer onto direct ones, but even without organism-level direct functions, derived functions might be anchored in the direct functions of subpersonal parts—neurons or sensory transducers that retain evolutionary pedigrees. So swampman can still have mental content via derived functions, and teleosemantics survives.
