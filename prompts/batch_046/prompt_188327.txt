I've spent the past year using a browser extension that highlights logical fallacies and weak inferences in real time as I read news articles and essays. I now feel I'm genuinely better at critical reasoning—not merely faster at recognizing the plugin's color-coded tags, but able to spot questionable moves in printed books and in live conversations where no software is running. To check whether this is real improvement or self-deception, I revisited essays I wrote before adopting the tool and found structural errors I had previously missed; I also worked through an unmarked philosophy article and caught several dubious inferences without any digital aid. My brother argues I'm not actually sharper, only trained to reproduce the extension's taxonomy and to grade my own performance by its internal rubric. I tried to break the loop by retaking an old standardized reasoning exam I found in storage, but I recognized too many items for a score increase to prove anything. I then asked a friend to evaluate my recent analyses blind, not knowing which were drafted with the plugin active, but I've started framing arguments in the extension's specialized vocabulary, so his judgment may be tracking jargon fluency rather than reasoning quality. There's also the nagging sense that whatever confidence I have came almost effortlessly—just months of passive highlighting—so that even a clean independent result might constitute 'easy knowledge,' the kind available without the hard labor that usually underwrites real competence. Given all this, can I legitimately claim to know that my critical faculties have improved, or am I caught in a circle where every verification I can perform secretly borrows from the very capacities I'm trying to validate? And if there is a genuinely non-circular check—some form of evaluation that doesn't rely on the trained faculty—what exactly would it need to look like to be trustworthy rather than just another disguised loop?
