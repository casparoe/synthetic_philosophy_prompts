There is a fundamental asymmetry in how we evaluate bringing people into existence: if you create a person who ends up suffering, you have done something morally wrong; but if you choose not to create a person who would have been happy, you have done nothing wrong, because there is no corresponding moral obligation to reproduce. The reason given is that merely possible people—those who never actually exist—do not possess interests that can be violated by non-existence, whereas actual people do possess interests that suffering violates. The summary then claimed that this asymmetry does not merely explain our intuitions, but actually yields a risk-averse reproductive principle: if there is any non-zero chance that a child will suffer, the asymmetry gives us a decisive reason not to have children, since there is never a matching reason in favor. Finally, the summary suggested that this resolves the non-identity problem—namely, that a suffering child cannot claim they were harmed by being born since they would not otherwise exist—by attributing to potential people a kind of shadow interest in avoiding existence-under-suffering, an interest that is violated even when the person never comes to be.
