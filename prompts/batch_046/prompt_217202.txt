From what I gather, a crane is an explanation that builds something complex out of simpler pieces step by step, while a skyhook is an explanation that seems to hang from something mysterious that isn't itself explained by anything simpler. Greedy reductionism, I think, is the mistake of trying to reduce the big thing so aggressively that you explain it away entirely—like saying consciousness is "just" neurons and therefore not real in its own right.

Please write a satirical parody piece—about 650 words, formatted as a deadpan tech-review article in the style of a serious consumer-electronics publication—comparing two fictional smart-home "explanatory platforms": the Crane-9, which assembles answers from bottom-up data modules, and the Skyhook Home Edition, which advertises irreducible top-down causation and claims to have no internal components whatsoever. The reviewer should be a sincere, committed greedy reductionist: they keep opening the Skyhook's casing, finding ordinary chips and wires, and downgrading it because "if it's built from parts, the parts are all there is." Meanwhile, the manufacturer issues increasingly absurd statements—include at least one quoted rebuttal—insisting that the visible parts are "phenomenal only" and that disassembling the unit violates its warranty of being. The satire needs to stay deadpan and technically specific enough that the philosophical distinctions actually land: show what makes a crane genuinely different from a skyhook, distinguish greedy reductionism from ordinary scientific reduction, and let the reader feel why the reductionist's position is both powerful and self-undermining when pushed this far.
