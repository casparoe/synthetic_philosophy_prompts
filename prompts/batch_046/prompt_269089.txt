As a retired experimental physicist who has only recently begun studying philosophy of logic, I keep hearing members of my reading group shut down foundational questions with the one-liner that "deduction justifies itself." They usually invoke Lewis Carroll's dialogue between Achilles and the Tortoise—where applying a rule of inference seems to require a further rule—to suggest that asking for a non-circular justification of modus ponens is foolish. Could you lay out the distinct precise claims that slogan might actually be expressing? I want a menu of readings: the trivial claim that every valid argument relies on rules of inference; the epistemological claim that we possess no independent ground for accepting logical laws; the formalist claim that inference rules are constitutive of a system rather than claims about reality requiring support; the psychological claim that accepting deduction is a brute mental disposition; and any intermediate or stronger variants you can distinguish. For each, say whether Carroll's regress genuinely illustrates it, whether it is defensible, and—crucially—whether it actually supports the dismissive use my colleagues make of it, namely that asking for an explicit justification of deduction is pointless. I am particularly interested in whether any reading collapses inference rules into mental processes rather than normative prescriptions, because that distinction was fundamental to how I thought about measurement validation in physics, and I want to know whether importing it helps or confuses the philosophical issue. Please present this as a list I can take to our next meeting; I do not need citations or a bibliography, only the conceptual disambiguation.
