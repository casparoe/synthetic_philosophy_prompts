Consider a forecasting framework—call it the Morrison-Voss model—that predicts epidemic hospitalization peaks in dense urban centers. Its premises are that every resident is identically susceptible, that all individuals adapt behavior instantly and fully rationally to risk signals, and that asymptomatic transmission is absent. Retrospective tests show it predicts the timing and magnitude of peak hospital demand within about eight percent for cities exceeding one million inhabitants, yet it systematically fails for suburban and rural settings. Milton Friedman’s F-twist maintains that the realism of a model’s assumptions is irrelevant compared to its predictive power. Given this, please state the most important implications of applying the F-twist to evaluate, trust, and deploy the Morrison-Voss model in public-health planning. In particular, address whether predictive success in large cities licenses using the model to design interventions that depend on its false mechanisms—such as policies predicated on universal rational adaptation—and whether there is any principled, non-ad-hoc reason to restrict its domain of application. Lead with your bottom-line assessment in the first sentence, then provide the reasoning, with no preamble and no restatement of the question.
