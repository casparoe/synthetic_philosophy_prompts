I run a postgraduate seminar in philosophy of action—fourteen students, one seventy-five-minute session—on weakness of will and hyperbolic discounting. Design an in-class simulation that makes the group feel why akrasia is not merely knowing better and doing worse, but a structural conflict between preference orderings held at different temporal distances from an outcome. Use real but small stakes: points that convert into a shared contribution to a group refreshment fund, with an optional immediate personal fee.

Structure it as a three-round binding-commitment game. Before round one, each student publicly locks in a strategy—choosing between an immediate small reward (A) and a larger delayed reward (B) that requires forgoing A now. Between rounds, they may pay a discrete, visible fee to unlock and revise their commitment; the fee should feel affordable but noticeable. The rules must be arranged so that participants experience the reversal themselves—choosing B at a distance, then paying to switch to A when it becomes immediate, then resenting the fee once B is secured—before any theoretical label is introduced.

Inject this twist: after round one, split the group. One half must defend their earlier locked-in choice as the preference of an authentic, continuing agent; the other half must argue that only present-moment preferences have authority, and that paying the fee was fully rational. They then swap roles for a second round with reversed stakes, in which the fee becomes a subsidy for maintaining the original commitment.

Include precise rules, the point-conversion scheme, a timeline with role assignments, and minimal materials (choice cards or a digital form, plus a visible running tally). The debrief should map moments in the game onto the distinction between unified-agency failure (a lapse in execution) and diachronic fragmentation (time-slices of the agent holding genuinely incompatible orderings). Say what will predictably misfire the first time—participants interpreting the fee as a sensible transaction cost rather than evidence of preference instability—and how to correct that reading in the moment. Finally, be explicit about which feature of real hyperbolic discounting the simulation necessarily fails to reproduce: the continuous, often unconscious shape of the discount curve, as opposed to the discrete, explicit choice points built into the game.
