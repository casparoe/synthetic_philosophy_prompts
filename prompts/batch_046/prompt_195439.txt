I'm delivering the opening remarks tomorrow evening at a community workshop I'm hosting in our public library auditorium, titled "The Figures We Keep and the Reasons We Give." I have no formal training in philosophy or history—I'm a reference librarian who helped organize this after our town council kept the Columbus statue in the central park with new contextual signage, and after local students asked whether the high school's Jefferson-Churchill auditorium should be renamed. The room will be mixed: high school history teachers, students who signed the petition, longtime residents, and a few retired professors. I need a roughly ten-minute address that treats the "man of his time" defense not as a ready-made excuse but as a structural problem that shapes what I can and cannot claim about these three figures.

I've been thinking on my own that people invoke that phrase to mean at least three different things, and they often slide between them without noticing. One version is about moral knowledge: the person genuinely could not see the wrongness of slavery, colonial violence, or imperial war because the conceptual framework was unavailable. Another is about comparative options: everyone around them was doing the same thing, so there was no practical alternative model to follow. A third is about temporal humility: we should not judge past actors by present standards because moral progress makes such judgments unfair. I want the speech to show that these three versions actually conflict when applied to the same person. For instance, if we say Jefferson was of his time in the comparative sense—that slaveholding was ordinary among Virginia planters—that undercuts the idea that he was exceptional for writing about liberty; but if we say he was exceptional for writing about liberty, then he had the conceptual tools to recognize the wrongness of slavery, which collapses the first version of the defense for him specifically. For Columbus, the comparative version might hold more weight—there was little institutional opposition to exploitation he could have joined—yet that makes him ordinary rather than heroic, which is not what defenders usually want. For Churchill, the defense often points to the wartime context, but Churchill also lived long enough to see anti-colonial movements and to be criticized by contemporaries for his views on India and race; so the knowledge and options versions apply differently to different chapters of his life.

Please write this address in American English. Structure it with the following sections, in this order: "Opening framing and why I'm here" (about 200 words), "The three faces of the defense" (about 350 words), "Jefferson, Churchill, and Columbus: where the defense splits" (about 350 words), "What I am deliberately not saying" (about 200 words), and "A closing for people who disagree with me" (about 200 words). The philosophical idea should do real work: it should determine which praises or condemnations I can offer without contradiction, and it should be accessible to the students and residents without feeling like a lecture to the teachers. Do not include any bibliography, reading list, or citations. Avoid direct quotations from historical texts; if you reference something these figures wrote or said, paraphrase it without quotation marks.
