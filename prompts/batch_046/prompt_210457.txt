Exam question: Consider whether the comparative formulation “Why Britain and not China?” is well posed as an explanation for the emergence of sustained industrial growth. In your answer, address (i) the distinction between proximate technological triggers and deeper structural preconditions; (ii) whether treating Britain and Qing China as unified macro-cases commits a “unitary actor” fallacy that obscures internal regional variation; and (iii) the role of “counterfactual stability” in assessing claims that geographic endowments—such as coal proximity or access to colonial “ghost acres”—were decisive rather than merely facilitating. Conclude by assessing whether integrating Joseph Needham’s original inquiry about scientific development with the economic-history “Great Divergence” framework strengthens or weakens the comparative exercise.

Student answer: Britain and China can legitimately be compared as macro-cases because Kenneth Pomeranz showed that living standards, life expectancy, and market development were remarkably similar in the Yangtze delta and northwest Europe around 1800. This baseline similarity makes the post-1800 divergence genuinely puzzling and therefore well posed. The decisive difference was geographic: Britain had coal deposits near its manufacturing centers in the Midlands, providing cheap energy, while China’s coal lay mainly in the northwest, far from the Jiangnan manufacturing belt. In addition, Britain could draw on “ghost acres” in North America and elsewhere, relieving domestic land pressure, whereas China’s Manchurian frontier was not economically integrated in the same fashion. Mark Elvin’s “high-level equilibrium trap” explains why Chinese agriculture and handicraft production remained labor-intensive, but Britain escaped this trap through mechanization driven by relatively high wages. Joseph Needham originally asked why modern science arose in Europe despite China’s earlier technological superiority; this addresses essentially the same comparative puzzle as the Great Divergence, only applied to epistemic institutions rather than factor markets, so integrating the two strengthens the framework. The explanation possesses high counterfactual stability: even if Britain had lacked American colonies, its domestic coal endowment alone would have generated industrial divergence, because energy costs were the binding constraint. For these reasons, comparing the two polities as unified actors is valid, since they faced identical Malthusian constraints until Britain broke through.
