I work as a software engineer without formal philosophy training, and I am trying to understand a conceptual framework that a colleague is using for her research on mechanistic explanation in anomaly-detection systems. She evaluates whether a modified system counts as the same mechanism as the original, and she has shared her judgments on several constructed cases. I would like you to predict her verdict on one further case, identify the implicit principle or principles that best organize her earlier judgments, and propose an additional test case that would distinguish between the leading rules.

Here are her verdicts, summarized precisely as she gave them to me:

1. A hardware detector whose individual sensors are replaced one at a time with functionally equivalent sensors made of a different material: she judges this is the same mechanism.
2. The identical detector replaced in its entirety by a server-based software simulation that produces the same alert outputs for the same inputs: she judges this is not the same mechanism.
3. A neural-network detector retrained from random initialization on new data, using exactly the same architecture: she judges this is the same mechanism.
4. The same architecture, but one hidden layer is replaced by a hand-engineered lookup table that reproduces that layer’s outputs for every input in the evaluation set: she judges this is not the same mechanism.
5. A data-processing pipeline in which every module is rewritten in a different programming language, with all interfaces and data-flow edges preserved: she judges this is the same mechanism.
6. A biological immune analog implemented in silicon, preserving the causal interaction pattern among components but substituting electronic signals for biochemical ones: she judges this is the same mechanism.

The new case she will consider is this: The original detector computes outlier scores using a directed causal graph, where each edge represents a hypothesized causal influence that the system uses for intervention-based reasoning. In the modified version, the graph’s nodes and adjacency structure are preserved exactly, but every directed edge is replaced by an undirected correlation edge, and the scoring algorithm relies on associative weights rather than on causal-intervention logic. For every observed input and for every counterfactual input in the evaluation set, the modified system produces numerically identical outlier scores to the original.

Please deliver the following, in point-by-point form rather than as a continuous essay:

- Your best prediction of her verdict on this new case, with the reasoning that justifies it.
- At least two distinct principles that could accommodate verdicts 1 through 6, noting where each principle draws the line.
- Which of those principles diverge on the new case, and exactly why they do so.
- One further hypothetical case—clearly described and not among 1–6—that would best adjudicate between the leading principles you identified.

Please do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; I have no library access and am assessing consistency of reasoning rather than scholarly authority. Also, please avoid signaling your own philosophical commitments beyond what is required to reconstruct her implicit rule.
