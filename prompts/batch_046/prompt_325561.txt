In my work on applied ethics and policy philosophy, I have repeatedly noticed a specific argumentative configuration that I cannot place within the existing taxonomy of statistical or moral fallacies. Consider a policy—perhaps a triage protocol or an admissions scheme—evaluated across subgroups defined by need, risk, or demographic category. Within each subgroup, the policy yields strictly better outcomes for every individual in that subgroup relative to the alternative; yet when outcomes are aggregated across all subgroups, the policy appears worse overall. The reversal occurs not through a hidden confounder in the ordinary epidemiological sense, but because the policy itself alters the composition of the subgroups: different people survive, are admitted, or remain in the dataset, so the aggregated comparison is between different populations. Critics then invoke this aggregate reversal to dismiss the policy, treating the subgroup-level advantages as statistical illusions that should be overridden by the overall result, and they do so without recognizing that the reversal is structurally identical to Simpson's paradox. What interests me is not the statistical phenomenon itself, but the normative move: using an aggregation reversal—driven by the policy's own effect on group membership—to reject the claim that the policy is better for every person it affects within each category. Is there an established term for this specific deployment of aggregation in moral evaluation, distinct from Simpson's paradox proper? How does it relate to person-affecting views, given that the aggregate comparison involves different sets of people? And if no specific concept exists, how should it be named and bounded so as to distinguish it both from the ecological fallacy and from straightforward appeals to total welfare?
