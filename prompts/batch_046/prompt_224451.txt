I'm currently taking a graduate seminar on rational choice and bounded rationality, and after working through the absent-minded driver paradox I've tried to push its logic into ordinary intertemporal planning. The result is an argument I sketched for discussion, but its conclusion—that any rational agent with episodic memory gaps must either bind every future action in advance or abandon adaptive reasoning altogether—feels clearly wrong, and yet I can't locate the precise slip. Here is the chain: (1) In decision problems with imperfect recall, rationality requires identical actions at nodes that lie within the same information set, because the agent lacks the discriminative information needed to do otherwise. (2) The driver problem is not sui generis; any agent who makes a choice on Monday, forgets the fact of that choice, and later faces a structurally analogous decision on Wednesday occupies an analogous information partition spanning both occasions relative to the forgotten event. (3) Therefore, if the agent selects A on Monday and cannot recall having done so, then Wednesday's decision-node and Monday's decision-node belong to the same information set with respect to that forgotten fact. (4) By (1), rationality demands that the agent choose identically on Monday and Wednesday, regardless of any intervening feedback, changed payoffs, or new evidence acquired in between. (5) Generalizing this, every forgetting boundary forces behavioral uniformity across it, which implies that adaptive learning—revising one's course in light of experience—is rationally impermissible whenever memory is imperfect. (6) Yet adaptive revision is not a defect but a central feature of rational agency; an agent who is constitutionally unable to update across any memory gap is crippled, not exemplary. (7) So either (1) is false and the absent-minded driver may rationally take different actions at indistinguishable exits, or rational choice with imperfect recall collapses into rigid, non-adaptive scripting with no room for learning. I would very much like to keep (6), and I also want to preserve the intuition behind (1) as it applies specifically within the original driving scenario, but I recognize these may conflict. Which step should I reject, and what does surrendering it cost for the standard ex ante versus ex post analysis of the driver paradox? If there is an equivocation hidden in how I am applying "information set" across distinct decision trees, I would especially like to see it named.
