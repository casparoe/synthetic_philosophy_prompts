Right, I'm trying to pin down what a genuinely Deweyan theory of democratic legitimacy actually has to acheive, not just one that nickes his phrases like associated living and social intelligence to make liberal proceduralism sound warmer. So: write me the desiderata. If democracy is really a form of experimental inquiry, what does the theory have to deliver?

Here's the spec I want:

1. A clear line between democracy as a mode of associated living and democracy as a decision procedure — and an explanation of why reducing the first to the second misses the point, without just repeating Dewey's own slogans.
2. An account of how experimental intelligence scales past small face-to-face groups without collapsing into technocracy or rule by experts. I want the mechanism, not just the hope.
3. A treatment of failure: what does it mean for a democratic outcome to be fallible in the same way a lab result is? Not just we vote again in four years — I want the conceptual parallel.
4. How structural power distorts inquiry, specifcally the kind that makes certain experiences invisible to the collective problem-solving process — call it the problem of dark experience if you like. The theory can't just say listen more; it needs to show how democratic instutions correct for this.
5. Consistency with the idea that values are reconstructed through inquiry rather than being pre-political givens, and that fixed ends are rejected. So the theory can't smuggle in a substantive conception of the good life as the benchmark for democratic success.
6. And here's the bit I keep coming back to: the temporal continuity problem. Democratic decisions aren't isolated experiments; each one alters the conditions for the next. The theory needs to explain how institutions preserve continuity of experience across iterations without letting past solutions fossilise into unexamined premises.
