I was talking with a friend over coffee about decision theory and we got sidetracked into this weird corner about the Principal Principle — you know, that idea from philosophy of probability about rational credence lining up with objective chance when you don't have inadmissable info. Anyway, my friend built this argument that seems to show the whole thing makes practical reasoning impossible, and I can't figure out where the slip is.

Here's the chain she laid out:
(1) The Principal Principle says that if I know the objective chance of event E is p, and my other evidence is admissible, then my rational credence in E should be p.
(2) Now take any action I might deliberate about — catching a train, signing a contract, whatever. The physical world, together with my mental states if physicalism holds, fixes an objective chance (maybe 0 or 1 if determinism is true, maybe intermediate) that the outcome occurs.
(3) So by (1), my rational credence about whether I'll catch the train should just be whatever physics assigns, completely independent of my reasoning or intentions.
(4) But practical reasoning seems to require that my beliefs about outcomes depend on my intentions and choices, not just on prior physical probabilities; otherwise deliberation is just watching a number unfold.
(5) Therefore applying PP generally wipes out agency from belief-formation and makes deliberation redundant.
(6) That can't be right — we obviously reason about what we'll do. So (1) must be false, or at least can't apply to actions.

She thinks the mistake is between (3) and (4), saying practical reasoning doesn't need beliefs to depend on intentions in that way. I'm not convinced; I think maybe (2) is doing hidden work by treating my mental states as fixed conditions rather than as part of what I'm reasoning about. But I want a definately call — don't lay out five options and say 'it depends on your view of free will'. Tell me exactly which step is rotten and why it only looks plausible, and if I have to keep (1), what does that cost me?
