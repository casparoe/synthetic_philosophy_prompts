I spent forty years in experimental physics, and only since retiring have I begun reading philosophy of mind with any seriousness. Recently I came across Douglas Hofstadter's proposal that the self is not locked inside a single skull but can persist as a pattern reproduced in others who have internalized it. That idea led me to notice a recurring configuration in long scientific partnerships and in cases of profound cognitive interdependence, and I cannot tell whether philosophy already has a precise name for it.

Consider two situations. First, a researcher who worked for decades with a collaborator—not merely dividing labor, but genuinely co-reasoning, so that hypotheses were generated through a back-and-forth in which neither party could later reconstruct which move was originally whose—finds after the collaborator's death that she continues to produce arguments in his characteristic style, to anticipate objections he would have raised before she reaches them herself, and even to hear his particular phrases in her internal monologue. She reports that the research still feels like "our" work, yet grammatically and phenomenologically it is now "my" work; the boundary of the first-person singular has collapsed inward to include his patterns rather than dissolving into a plural "we."

Second, imagine a person with severe memory impairment whose spouse supplies not only reminders but evaluative framing, emotional calibration, and the very structure of decision-making, to the point that major life choices emerge from an interaction neither could reproduce alone. When asked who decided, the impaired person answers "I did," and this does not appear to be mere social politeness; the spouse's contributions have been so thoroughly integrated that they function as parts of the decision-process without being experienced as external inputs.

What interests me is not simply that cognition extends beyond the skin, or that groups share intentions—those notions I have encountered—but rather a specific phenomenon: a first-person perspective that remains grammatically singular and phenomenologically immediate, yet is causally and structurally distributed across brains, or across time in a way that incorporates another's cognitive patterns. The "I" does not become collective; it absorbs the other and continues as a unit.

Is there an established concept that captures exactly this—the persistence of a distributed, multi-brain cognitive architecture within a unitary first-person report? If there is, how precisely does it differ from collective intentionality, from the extended mind thesis, and from Hofstadter's own framework of strange loops and copied patterns? If there is not, how should its boundaries be drawn: does it require actual neural integration, or is deep functional interdependence sufficient? Must the other be absent or deceased, or can two living people maintain such a distributed "I"? And does the phenomenon genuinely challenge the assumption that personal identity tracks a single biological organism, or does it show only that one identity can be parasitic upon another's pattern without being genuinely shared? I will not be satisfied with a bare label; I want to know what the phenomenon is, what it is not, and where its edges lie.
