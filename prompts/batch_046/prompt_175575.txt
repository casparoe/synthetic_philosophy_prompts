I've been trying to formalize a tension in communicative ethics, and the chain seems to collapse moral reasoning about politeness altogether. Here is the argument:

(1) Dutch directness treats the avoidance of explicit contradiction as a failure of respect: you honor your interlocutor by treating them as able to bear the truth.
(2) Japanese indirectness treats the avoidance of explicit contradiction as a success of respect: you honor your interlocutor by preserving relational harmony that direct contradiction would damage.
(3) Both are presented as moral virtues, not mere customs: each prescribes how one ought to speak regardless of personal preference.
(4) If two practices prescribe incompatible speech-acts and both claim universal moral authority, then at least one must be wrong.
(5) But neither can be shown wrong without presupposing the norms of the other: judging indirectness by directness begs the question, and vice versa.
(6) So there is no neutral standpoint from which to adjudicate between them.
(7) A white lie is indirectness applied to factual content: saying "this is acceptable" when it is not, in order to preserve harmony.
(8) If indirectness lacks universal authority (from 4–6), then white lies have no moral justification.
(9) By parallel reasoning, directness also lacks universal authority.
(10) Therefore neither directness nor indirectness carries moral authority.
(11) Yet if neither carries authority, there are no norms governing how we ought to communicate about sensitive truths.
(12) But we clearly do criticize people for needless cruelty and for deceit; such criticism presupposes norms.
(13) So (10) must be false.
(14) To reject (10) we must reject either (4) or (6).
(15) Rejecting (4) means accepting that contradictory virtues can both hold universal authority, which requires a meta-principle to resolve conflicts—returning us to the need for a neutral standpoint in (6).
(16) Rejecting (6) requires identifying a culture-transcendent criterion (e.g., minimize suffering), but any such criterion already embeds one culture's communicative priorities.
(17) Hence moral reasoning about politeness and white lies is either circular or impossible.

This feels suspicious: I think I'd rather keep (1), (2), and (7) than accept (17). Where does the slide happen? Is there an equivocation between "universal authority" and "authority within a practice," a scope error in (10), or a hidden modal leap between "no neutral adjudication" and "no norms at all"? Which step should I reject, why did it look plausible, and what does giving it up cost—especially if I want to maintain that white lies can sometimes be wrong without collapsing into cultural relativism?
