As a longtime reader of rationalist blogs and forums, I have watched the debate over illusionism about phenomenal consciousness settle into a peculiar kind of stalemate. By illusionism I mean the view that phenomenal properties — the intrinsic qualitative character often described in the literature as what-it-is-likeness — do not exist as usually conceived, and that first-person reports of them are the products of misrepresentational mechanisms rather than transparent windows onto real mental qualities. The opposing camp holds that such properties are genuinely present and that any adequate account of mind must preserve their reality.

What puzzles me is that both sides now share a remarkably stable common ground: they accept the same neuroscientific findings about reportability, change blindness, and the absence of any central Cartesian observer; they agree on the logical distinction between access consciousness and phenomenal consciousness; and they both acknowledge that subpersonal processes generate our first-person narratives. Yet neither side moves. The illusionists claim that this shared ground shows phenomenal talk is a kind of widespread cognitive error; the realists reply that it only shows our access mechanisms are fallible, not that the qualities they reveal are fictitious.

My own suspicion — offered as a hypothesis to be tested rather than a conclusion — is that the disagreement has stalled because of conceptual slippage within the microdomain of first-person epistemology. Specifically, the illusionist seems to be treating having a phenomenal quality as a theoretical attribution made by a subpersonal reporting mechanism, whereas the realist treats it as a direct epistemic relation that requires no mediation; but both continue to use the same vocabulary — experience, appearance, qualitative presence — as if they were talking about one thing. I want to know whether this diagnosis is correct.

Please diagnose the structure of this disagreement. Is it largely verbal — a dispute that dissolves once we rigorously separate phenomenological judgement from the phenomenology being judged? Is it substantive but concealed behind a failure to distinguish ontological questions about what mental states exist from epistemological questions about how we come to know them? Or are the two sides genuinely answering different questions under a single heading? I want you to engage with the specific positions as I have outlined them, supported by what each side actually says about introspection, misrepresentation, and the authority of first-person reports, rather than offering a generic survey of the literature.

In particular, I want you to examine a niche distinction that I think may be doing hidden work: the difference between a report being causally produced by a mental state and a report being genuinely about that state. Does illusionism require the strong claim that there is no state being reported about at all, or only the weaker claim that the report systematically mischaracterises the state's actual nature? If this distinction can be made precise, does it offer a path forward — perhaps by allowing both sides to agree on the ontology while disagreeing on the epistemology — or does it merely relocate the stalemate?

Finally, tell me what, if anything, would count as genuine progress given your diagnosis. Would it require a new empirical result about subpersonal reporting mechanisms, a conceptual distinction that both sides could accept, or a reframing of the original question? Or should we conclude that the disagreement protects a bedrock intuition about the immediacy of experience that no argument can dislodge, and that the debate should be abandoned as unproductive? Be honest about where judgement must replace procedure.
