I am writing for a seminar in conservative political philosophy, and I have drafted an essay that attempts a synthetic reading of Edmund Burke's defense of prescription and G.K. Chesterton's fence principle. Rather than treating both as generic cautions against change, I propose that they jointly defend what I term epistemic scaffolding—institutional structures whose purpose has become invisible precisely because the problems they originally solved have been forgotten. To test this framework, I apply it to the recent push in several jurisdictions to replace traditional common-law judicial discretion with algorithmic risk-assessment tools in criminal sentencing; my argument is that removing the fence of human judgment in this context eliminates not merely an obstacle, but an embedded repository of contextual reasoning that addresses failure modes the algorithm was never designed to recognize.

My instructor has encouraged original synthesis, but I am unsure whether my framing unfairly collapses Burke's organic, historically layered conception of society into Chesterton's more paradoxical and theologically inflected defense of tradition. I am also concerned that applying the analogy to algorithmic governance might be anachronistic, since judicial discretion itself is a modern institutional form rather than an ancient custom. I would greatly appreciate comments on the coherence of the epistemic scaffolding idea, on whether my characterization of Burkean prescription avoids importing contemporary epistemological vocabulary, and on whether the contemporary example strengthens the philosophical claim or merely distracts from it. In particular, any suggestions for clarifying the difference between Burke's suspicion of rationalist reconstruction and Chesterton's whimsical insistence on leaving fences alone would help me revise the argument.
