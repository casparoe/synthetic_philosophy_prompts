Having retired after decades in experimental physics, I find myself examining how societies calibrate individual judgment against collective rules. Consider pedestrian traffic norms: in Germany, pedestrians typically wait at the curb for the green signal even when the street is entirely empty, and violating this draws sharp social censure; in the United States, pedestrians frequently cross against the light when no cars are present, and enforcement is patchy and context-dependent. These are not mere legal differences—both countries have statutes—but contrasting institutionalized habits.

What philosophical commitments do these practices presuppose? Does the German pattern assume that civic order depends on treating rules as constitutive of right action, not just as useful guidelines, thereby embedding a particular view of moral authority and epistemic humility? Does the American pattern assume that individual situational judgment is the final moral authority, with rules functioning only as defeasible heuristics? Reconstruct the implicit theories of moral agency, responsibility, and social knowledge that each norm requires. Are the two commitments genuinely incompatible, or can they be unified under a single framework—perhaps one in which rules operate like calibrated instruments, authoritative within designed parameters but requiring interpretation at the margins?

If we replaced one implicit theory with the other—instituting strict universal compliance in American cities, or discretionary judgment-based crossing in German ones—what exactly would have to change in the underlying conception of citizenship, not just in traffic law? And is either practice philosophically underdetermined, such that several rival theories of value or authority could license it?
