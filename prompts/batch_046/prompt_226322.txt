I sit on my town’s heritage preservation committee, and we face a decision that has split us along lines I did not expect. In the 1950s a local civic association—let us call it the Riverside Council—financed the construction of our library, our water system, and the park where children still play. Its meeting minutes, recently digitized, reveal that every infrastructure grant was conditioned on excluding residents of a particular neighborhood from membership and from using the facilities they funded. The bureaucratic language is relentless: page after page of quorum rules, budget lines, and attendance rolls that reduce those residents to administrative absences.

We have agreed that the archive must not be destroyed. The dispute is about its form. One faction insists on a complete, searchable, prominently displayed digital kiosk in the library foyer—full transparency, they argue, so that the harm is visible and not sanitized. Another faction proposes a sealed repository with access restricted to researchers, accompanied by a public summary written with the descendants’ input, arguing that requiring the town—especially the descendants—to walk past these bureaucratic erasures every day transforms remembrance into a continuous injury rather than an act of justice. They invoke the idea that in close-knit communities, moral repair sometimes requires what they term “structured absence”: not denial, but a deliberately designed framing that allows shared civic life to proceed without perpetual re-enactment of the original exclusion, anchored by a transparent record of what has been withheld and why.

I am asked to advise the committee before Thursday’s meeting. Is there a coherent ethical distinction between preservation-with-exposure and preservation-with-curated-absence? More specifically, does a collective duty to remember entail an obligation to display, or can it be fulfilled by guarded preservation coupled with a publicly authored narrative? And if forgetting—carefully institutionalized, transparent about its own boundaries, and responsive to those harmed—can serve moral repair in thick communal relationships rather than merely excusing perpetrators, does that give the second faction a stronger claim than mere sentiment?

Please advise me not on public-relations strategy, but on whether the philosophical structure of collective memory, moral repair, and the ethics of remembrance supports one of these architectures over the other, and identify precisely where the strongest objection to your recommendation lies.
