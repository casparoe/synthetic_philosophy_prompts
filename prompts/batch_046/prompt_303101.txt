I serve on a state legislative review panel that evaluates proposed regulations on pregnancy-related medical procedures. We meet monthly, have no formal philosophy training, must issue written recommendations within ten business days, and our reports are public and may be cited in administrative proceedings. I want a reusable decision procedure—something my colleagues can run in about forty-five minutes per proposal—grounded in the two philosophical arguments that are actually stable in this domain: the bodily-autonomy reasoning associated with Judith Jarvis Thomson's violinist analogy, and the future-value reasoning associated with Don Marquis's future-like-ours argument. Do not treat either argument as settling every case; instead, translate each into an ordered sequence of questions with explicit decision rules.

Structure the procedure in three parts. Part A operationalizes the autonomy reasoning: distinguish continuous bodily support from intermittent risk, voluntary initiation of support conditions from involuntary imposition, and cases where effective alternative means exist; specify what counts as a sufficient answer at each node, and flag the conditions under which the reasoning blocks a restriction. Part B operationalizes the future-value reasoning: assess whether the proposed regulation prevents the destruction of a being with a future like ours, with explicit sub-rules for severe fetal anomaly and for conditions in which biological life continues but biographical life is absent; again specify sufficient answers and block conditions. Part C is a conflict-detection layer: identify direct tensions between the outputs of A and B, mark them explicitly as value-judgment nodes where the panel must vote rather than derive an answer, and state the point at which the procedure stops and hands the decision to elected officials because the remaining dispute is about values rather than facts.

Include a small set of quick test conditions that can be applied without philosophical vocabulary, list the hard cases the procedure cannot settle, and specify exactly where it will misfire or yield counter-intuitive results. Then apply the full procedure to three proposals: first, a mandatory waiting-period law that permits termination only for documented life-threatening conditions; second, a public-funding restriction that denies reimbursement except in cases of documented assault; and third, a rule requiring that all terminations after twelve weeks be performed in hospital surgical suites regardless of clinical indication. In your worked applications, include at least one case where the procedure produces a recommendation that conflicts with the intuitive judgment of most non-philosophers on the panel, and explain why the procedure still issues it and what that reveals about its limits. Finally, clearly separate what is philosophically settled about these two arguments from what remains genuinely contested among people who know the topic—such as whether voluntary initiation of pregnancy undermines the violinist analogy, or whether severe fetal anomaly negates the future-value claim—and do not present any contested interpretation as the consensus view. State explicitly what the procedure cannot do, especially with respect to pluralistic value conflicts that no formal checklist can dissolve.
