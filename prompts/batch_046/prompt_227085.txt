In discussions with friends about the ethics of individual action under collective harm—climate change, industrial meat consumption, and democratic voting—I have noticed a recurring argumentative structure that I cannot place precisely in the literature, and I am hoping to identify whether it has an established name or belongs near a recognized conceptual problem. The pattern concerns how people treat causal overdetermination differently depending on whether the collective mechanism is institutionally transparent or systemically opaque. Consider three cases. First, climate: an interlocutor typically emphasizes that global emissions form a continuous, nonlinear system; they reject the idea that any single emission is decisive, but preserve moral urgency by appealing to hidden systemic tipping points—ice-albedo feedback, methane release—so that the contribution remains meaningful despite dilution. Second, voting: the same interlocutor often points to the discrete formal rule of majority decision, noting that in a tie, one ballot is determinative; they treat this institutional discreteness as morally salient, even when they concede that the ex ante probability of being the decisive voter is smaller than the probabilistic impact of one consumption choice on a specific harm. Third, meat consumption: here they usually return to continuous dilution—market supply responds to aggregate demand, not to individual orders—while ignoring discrete supply-chain thresholds, such as a distributor ordering a new batch only after a specific inventory level is crossed, which structurally parallels the tie-case in elections but remains hidden inside logistics rather than encoded in public rules. The asymmetry seems systematic: hidden systemic discontinuities are invoked to preserve moral relevance for climate, visible institutional discontinuities are invoked for voting, and visible or hidden market discontinuities are ignored for consumption, with the selection tracking the speaker's normative conclusion rather than a unified account of probabilistic or overdetermined causation.

Does this selective deployment of discrete versus continuous causal framing have a name in moral philosophy or collective-action theory? How precisely should it be distinguished from the triggering problem, from debates about probabilistic causation in ethics, and from ordinary inconsistency? And if there is no established concept, what boundaries would best define it—particularly regarding the role of institutional transparency in making a causal threshold morally visible, and whether the pattern requires that the speaker apply the framing inconsistently across domains or merely that the framing is available and selectively activated? I am not looking for a bare label, but for a carving of the phenomenon: what exactly it includes, what it excludes, and where its edges lie relative to the standard inefficacy objection and to non-identity-style puzzles about overdetermination.
