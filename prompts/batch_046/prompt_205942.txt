Analyze the UK Animal Welfare (Sentience) Act 2022—under which decapod crustaceans and cephalopod molluscs were formally recognized as sentient beings, while insects, spiders, and other invertebrates were deliberately excluded—through the lens of the gradated-sentience thesis. On that thesis, sentience and its corresponding moral status form a continuous spectrum running from richly conscious mammals downward through simpler animals to minimal feedback-responsive systems such as thermostats, with no natural binary boundary between the protected and the unprotected.

In your analysis, proceed in four disciplined steps.

First, map the legislative decision onto the gradated view. Identify the relevant actors—policymakers, welfare scientists, and the advisory bodies charged with implementation—and the concrete options they faced: a binary statutory list, a tiered or scalar regulatory scheme, or no legislative recognition of invertebrate sentience at all. Given that moral status is supposed to come in degrees, does the gradated view require lawmakers to treat every incremental difference in cognitive or nociceptive capacity as morally significant? Or can the view accommodate practical thresholds without betraying its ontology? Be precise about what counts as a payoff or cost for each option under continuous rather than binary moral weighting.

Second, explain both what the gradated framework illuminates about the episode and what it obscures. Does the continuous ontology help account for why the scientific advisory work emphasized gradations in nociception, integrative processing, and behavioral flexibility rather than a simple present-absent test? Conversely, does the theory fail to explain why democratic legislation demands discrete categories—sentient versus not-sentient—for enforcement, funding, and criminal liability? Is that failure a defect in the ontology itself, or only a problem of political translation?

Third, apply the thermostat boundary problem. If a thermostat exhibits a primitive feedback loop that adjusts behavior in response to environmental change, and if the gradated view assigns even such minimal responsiveness an infinitesimal but non-zero moral status, then any legislative cutoff must either arbitrarily exclude entities with that minimal status or implausibly extend legal protection to artifacts. Use this limit case to test whether the Act’s implicit threshold—protecting octopuses but not bees, and certainly not devices—is defensible on gradated grounds, or whether it collapses the continuous scale back into an unjustified binary that the theory was meant to replace.

Fourth, state what the episode reveals about the gradated-sentience thesis itself. Is the Act a pragmatic vindication, showing that a continuous moral ontology can guide discrete policy without contradiction? Is it an anomaly, demonstrating that the view cannot deliver actionable distinctions without smuggling in a hidden binary? Or is it a case the theory never anticipated—one that forces the gradated view to articulate a non-arbitrary principle for where legal protection should begin and end?

Be disciplined about the mapping: do not force the theory to speak where it is silent, and do not treat the Act’s exclusions as experimentally established biological facts rather than legislative judgments. Write for a reader who knows the general structure of the Act but needs the philosophical analysis that runs in both directions— theory illuminating the policy choice, and the policy choice testing the theory.
