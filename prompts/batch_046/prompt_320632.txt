As a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy, I often think about the difference between a background process that writes internal state to disk and a process that is actively observed and monitored in real time. That engineering distinction makes me want to press a conceptual question about consciousness during sleep. Please argue for the position that dreams are not genuine experiences—defined as events with phenomenal character that occur during sleep—by treating the sleeping brain as analogous to a server running unobserved logging: there may be internal state transitions, but there is no present reader or observer that constitutes experiential access. In developing this argument, address the problem that if dreams truly were experiences, there should exist a criterion for having had them that is independent of post-awakening report; and respond to the counter-view that waking narratives are reconstructed fragments of memory rather than stories fabricated upon arousal. You may draw on the philosophical disagreement between Malcolm and Dennett regarding whether dream reports are conceptually coherent, but do not provide bibliographies, citations, or reading lists. Reason instead from conceptual first principles and the system-observability analogy.
