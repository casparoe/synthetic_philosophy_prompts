I've been working through older philosophy-of-science texts with a small reading group, and we've become stuck on a particular argument about how experiments relate to theoretical claims. One member reconstructed for us what she takes to be the core claim in Pierre Duhem's early-twentieth-century writing on physical theory: that an experiment in physics never confronts a single hypothesis in isolation, but always an interconnected cluster of premises, so that if the observed result fails to match the prediction, the physicist can only conclude that at least one element in the cluster is wrong—not which specific one. From this, she draws that any particular law can always be protected by modifying auxiliary assumptions elsewhere in the framework, leaving the core intact.

What confuses me is how this relates to a distinction our instructor recently introduced—between global and localized underdetermination. If the adjustments needed to save a hypothesis can be confined to a specific auxiliary cluster, then it seems we might localize the failure rather than facing a wholesale indeterminacy across the entire theoretical architecture. But if that cluster is itself already holistically entangled, and adjustments within it can always be made without touching the core law, then does Duhem's argument actually imply something stronger: that the boundary between local cluster and global architecture is itself unstable, because any redistribution of commitments eventually ripples outward and undermines the very idea of isolation?

Could you help me understand whether Duhem's reasoning supports this stronger, boundary-collapsing reading, or whether it genuinely permits a more modest, localized version of underdetermination? I'm especially interested in whether his view allows us to identify a specific sub-system as the locus of revision, or whether the very idea of isolating such a sub-system contradicts the holistic structure he describes. I'd prefer a conceptual analysis over a survey of secondary literature—no need for citations or reading lists, just careful engagement with the logic of the argument as presented.
