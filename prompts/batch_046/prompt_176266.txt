Here is an argument I constructed for a risk-governance seminar and now find suspicious because it seems to dissolve the precautionary principle into incoherence. I formalized the principle as the Strong Precautionary Principle (SPP): if an intervention I could cause severe, irreversible harm H, and there exists a physically possible causal pathway to H that is not epistemically closed—meaning we lack near-certain knowledge that every intermediate link is blocked—then I is prohibited unless one can demonstrate that H is impossible.

(1) For any actual-world intervention, there exist remote but physically possible causal pathways to catastrophic outcomes (for example, a novel energy infrastructure altering global supply chains in ways that eventually destabilize a nuclear-armed region) that are not epistemically closed.

(2) By the SPP, every intervention is therefore prohibited unless one possesses omniscience about all future states, which is never available.

(3) The same reasoning applies symmetrically to omissions: refraining from I also changes causal trajectories (for instance, failing to deploy carbon-capture technology allows climate damage with its own remote catastrophic pathways), so omissions are equally unclosed and therefore prohibited.

(4) Hence the SPP requires both I and not-I, which is impossible to satisfy jointly.

(5) Therefore the SPP is practically incoherent and must be abandoned.

I suspect the slide happens somewhere between formalizing the closure condition and applying it symmetrically to omissions—perhaps in treating arbitrarily distant causal chains as falling within the principle’s scope, or in assuming that the burden of proof applies identically to action and inaction—but I cannot pinpoint the exact step. Which premise should I reject, why did it look defensible in isolation, and what does rejecting it cost for a workable precautionary framework? I would prefer to preserve the intuition that there is a meaningful asymmetry between direct, foreseeable risks and arbitrarily remote causal chains, but I need to know whether saving that intuition forces me to weaken the closure condition or to deny the symmetry claim in (3). Please separate what is settled in discussions of precautionary reasoning from what remains genuinely contested among those who work on it, and do not present a disputed view—such as whether omissions trigger precautionary obligations identically to actions—as though it were the consensus position.
