For an essay I am researching on the ontology of scientific explanation, I have been examining Daniel Dennett’s distinction among the physical, design, and intentional stances, particularly his idea that certain patterns can be real without being reducible to physics. The position I am testing holds that patterns accessible only through the design stance—functional architectures such as renal filtration or aerodynamic lift—occupy an intermediate ontological status. They are not merely heuristic conveniences like the beliefs and desires posited by the intentional stance, yet they are not fully capturable by fundamental physical descriptions either; instead, they earn their reality through predictive robustness and counterfactual stability that microphysics alone cannot provide. What would have to be true for this intermediate-realist claim to fail? I want a rigorous map of its exposure: which arguments from eliminative materialism, which findings in reductionist philosophy of science, which empirical discoveries in biology or physics, and which internal tensions in Dennett’s own framework would genuinely undermine it? Please rank these potential defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, specify which ones a committed defender of this view would concede as damaging versus which they would dismiss, and assess whether the position risks being insulated—through a definition of real pattern tailored to exclude counterexamples—into unfalsifiability. If it is so insulated, is that a theoretical strength or a weakness? I am particularly interested in whether this intermediate status collapses under pressure into either pure physicalism or a full intentional-stance antirealism.
