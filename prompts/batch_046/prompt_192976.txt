Here's an argument my tutor threw at me and now I find suspicious because teh conclusion is wild.

(1) Chesterton's fence: if there's a barrier across a path and you don't know why it's there, leave it alone—someone put it up for a reason you might not grasp.
(2) Burkean conservatism treats social institutions as just such fences: they embody solutions to coordination problems that outstrip individual reason.
(3) Let's call the hidden premise the Preservation Clause: if you lack knowledge of an institution's purpose, that's sufficient reason not to alter it.
(4) Apply this not only to laws but to inherited normative distinctions—like the line between harm and mere offence, or between active and passive harm.
(5) These distinctions are fences that keep different kinds of reasons separate.
(6) For any such distinction, there's some aspect of its purpose we don't fully grasp—otherwise we'd have replaced it with a cleaner theory already.
(7) So by the Preservation Clause, we should never collapse or revise any inherited normative distinction.
(8) That means consequentialists who treat all suffering as equivalent are tearing down fences blindly, and rights-theorists who collapse active/passive distinctions are doing the same.
(9) But then the Clause protects every arbitrary moral boundary—old taboos against intermarriage, dietary rules, caste distinctions—because for each one there's some hidden purpose we can't recover.
(10) So either we accept that all moral reform is blind vandalism, or the argument slips somewhere between (3) and (7). Where? I'd rather keep (1) and (2) than accept (9), but I want to know exactly what I'm paying if I reject the Clause.
