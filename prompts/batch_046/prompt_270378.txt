I'm trying to determine whether a famine should be classified as a natural disaster or as a political crime, going by my gut on concrete cases rather than on any theory I've read.

In the 1943 Bengal famine, aggregate food availability was adequate but distribution collapsed under wartime colonial priorities; my verdict is that this was a political crime, not a natural disaster.

In the Irish famine of the 1840s, a potato blight triggered the crisis, yet grain continued to be exported and relief was inadequate; my gut says the mortality was largely a political failure, though the initial crop loss was not.

In the famine during China's Great Leap Forward, grain was physically available but was requisitioned and concealed by administrative decree; this is clearly a political crime.

Consider a drought in the Horn of Africa in 2011, where rainfall failed but conflict also blocked humanitarian corridors. Calling it purely natural feels wrong, yet calling it purely political also feels wrong, because the drought was real and not manufactured.

Consider a small island state struck by a volcanic eruption that destroys all local crops. The government issues ration cards equitably, but global supply chains cannot deliver enough calories in time, and some starvation occurs. Here my verdict is natural disaster — no institutional actor within the affected society had the capacity to prevent it, and none withheld aid.

But I keep returning to this case: climate change, driven largely by centuries of emissions from industrialized nations, gradually reduces rainfall in a poor agrarian region. The local government is competent and attempts redistribution, but global commodity prices spike and foreign aid is insufficient. People starve. The harm traces back to prior political decisions about energy and trade, yet no specific actor intended this famine, and local authorities are blameless.

Is there a principle — perhaps about whether starvation results from institutional failures within the affected society's sphere of control, or about whether available social and economic mechanisms could have averted the deaths — that captures all six verdicts? If so, what does it say about the climate-change case and about the Horn of Africa case? And if not, which of my intuitions should be overturned: should I treat the volcanic-island case as political because global supply structures are human-made, or should I treat the climate case as natural because the causal chain is too diffuse to count as crime? Please state the principle precisely, note which case motivates each clause, say what it predicts for a famine caused by a novel crop pathogen in a well-governed democracy with open borders, and be explicit about which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate.
