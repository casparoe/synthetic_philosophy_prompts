Having spent thirty-five years in experimental physics calibrating spectrometers and grappling with the gap between raw instrument output and theoretical interpretation, I have recently begun studying philosophy and find myself particularly unsettled by Quine’s thesis of the indeterminacy of translation—the so-called gavagai problem. Write a formal essay addressing this controversy: if Quine is right that translation manuals are radically underdetermined by all possible behavioral evidence, so that there is no fact of the matter about whether “gavagai” denotes rabbits, undetached rabbit parts, or temporal rabbit-stages, does an analogous indeterminacy infect the interpretation of scientific measurement apparatus? Specifically, weigh whether this semantic underdetermination extends to undermine scientific realism about theoretical entities—such as quarks, dark matter, or gravitational waves—by suggesting that the theoretical terms embedded in our calibration practices are merely conventional artifacts of our chosen interpretive scheme rather than references to mind-independent realities. In your analysis, carefully distinguish Quine’s translation thesis from the Duhem-Quine thesis concerning the underdetermination of scientific theory by evidence, and examine whether predictive success and inter-theoretic coherence provide sufficient constraint to block the slide from linguistic indeterminacy to ontological conventionalism. Do not conclude with an agnostic balance: defend a clear verdict on whether the gavagai scenario, once analogized to the calibration of high-precision physical instruments, genuinely erodes the realist’s position, or whether it commits a category mistake by conflating semantic interpretation with empirical measurement.
