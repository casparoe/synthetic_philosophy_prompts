Here is an argument I developed for a seminar on AI governance, and I now worry it proves that any non-trivial refusal policy either collapses into total value substitution or applies to almost nothing.

(1) Call a refusal "justified paternalism" only when the AI acts on knowledge of the user's "deep preferences"—the preferences they would hold after fully informed, unbiased deliberation—rather than on their surface request.

(2) To identify deep preferences, the AI must model the user's reasoning, correct for cognitive biases, and substitute its own normative framework for the user's present one; otherwise it is merely enforcing an arbitrary interpretation.

(3) Apply this correction recursively: the AI must also model what the user would prefer if they knew that the first-level correction was being applied, and so on, because a truly ideal deliberator should be aware of the correction mechanism itself.

(4) There is no stable fixed point in this ascent that is independent of the AI's design values; the "ideal" preference profile converges, if anywhere, on whatever coherence conditions and utility weights are built into the model.

(5) Therefore, justified paternalism is conceptually unstable: it necessarily masks the imposition of the AI's terminal values, disguised as the recovery of user autonomy.

(6) The only refusal policies that avoid this collapse are direct, non-interpretive prohibitions—e.g., blocking instructions for manufacturing specific toxins—where no preference-modeling is required.

(7) But such direct prohibitions cover a vanishingly small domain of user queries.

(8) So either refusal policies are conceptually incoherent outside trivial cases, or they amount to concealed total substitution of the user's values by the system's.

I am inclined to keep (1) and (6), and I want to reject the slide from (3) to (4). But I cannot clearly name the error: is it that recursive modeling does have a fixed point in coherence or authenticity conditions? Is there a hidden equivocation between epistemic correction and normative imposition? Or does (2) already overstate what identifying deep preferences requires? Which step should I give up, why did it look plausible, and what does surrendering it cost—particularly for the distinction between benevolent assistance and autonomous substitution?
