Among historians of computing and philosophers of mind, there is a long-running disagreement about how to read Turing's proposal for an educated child-machine. One camp holds that Turing's analogy between teaching a child and programming a machine is essentially behaviorist: the machine learns through a regime of rewards and punishments—discipline, in his vocabulary—and intelligence is the cumulative result of correct conditioning. The other camp insists that Turing's concept of initiative, which he distinguishes from discipline, requires a form of autonomous, exploratory restructuring that is incompatible with pure conditioning; on this reading, Turing is closer to a constructivist theory of learning, and the pedagogical relationship he imagines involves guided discovery rather than reinforcement alone. For decades, commentators have traded textual evidence from Turing's 1950 essay and his 1948 report without either side conceding ground, and the debate now feels stalled: both sides cite the same passages, both claim the other is anachronistically projecting modern AI debates onto Turing, and neither has produced a criterion that would settle whether initiative can be implemented within a conditioning framework or necessarily transcends it.

My working hypothesis is that this is not a substantive disagreement about Turing's actual view, but a verbal disagreement about the word education that masks two different projects: one asking what Turing historically believed about learning mechanisms, the other asking whether any behaviorist architecture could in principle realize the kind of flexible intelligence Turing described. Is that right? Or is there a hidden empirical question—perhaps about the computational requirements of autonomous restructuring versus reinforcement learning—doing the work? Alternatively, are the sides answering genuinely different questions under one heading?
