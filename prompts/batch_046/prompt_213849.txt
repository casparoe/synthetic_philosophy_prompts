Write a dialogue between two philosophers of equal competence—one defending the claim that Jeremy Bentham's utilitarian calculus applies consistently to sexual morality, the other arguing that Bentham's documented hesitation to defend non-reproductive sexual practices reveals a concealed normative constraint—focusing specifically on whether the felicific calculus can accommodate what we might call a pleasure-density asymmetry without becoming ad hoc. The defender should argue that Bentham could have implicitly weighted the long-run institutional stability effects of pleasure differently for acts that disrupted reproductive norms, not because he valued those norms intrinsically, but because second-order hedonic consequences—disrupted social trust, legal unpredictability—are legitimately factored into duration and certainty. The critic should reply that this reconstruction imports a structural boundary condition—certain pleasure-configurations are rendered ineligible for maximization regardless of first-order intensity—which violates the calculus's claim to agent-neutral transparency and mirrors the very deontological restrictions Bentham rejected. Have them exchange at least three rounds of direct response: the defender showing how the asymmetry can be rebuilt using only Bentham's own categories (intensity, duration, certainty, propinquity, fecundity, purity, extent); the critic demonstrating that any such reconstruction either collapses into circular reasoning or silently privileges a normative baseline about natural social architecture; and the defender countering that without such weighting the calculus produces implausible prescriptions in other domains of institutional design. Neither interlocutor should be reduced to a strawman; each must address the other's specific reconstruction rather than retreating to general slogans about liberty or tradition. Conclude the dialogue with the tension unresolved: leave open whether Bentham's framework genuinely licenses the asymmetry or whether the asymmetry exposes a hidden conservative architecture that the calculus was meant to conceal.
