I keep noticing a particular argumentative posture in ethical and metaphysical debate, and I want to know whether it corresponds to an established concept. The pattern is this: a speaker insists that a single, well-defined state of affairs is fully and literally characterized by contradictory predicates—say, that a particular social arrangement is both just and unjust—not as a conflict between parts, not as an ambiguity resolved by context, and not as competing opinions held by different observers, but as a feature of the object itself. They reject both the idea that one predicate is primary and the other derivative, and the idea that the predicates apply to different "aspects" that can be separated; instead, they treat the contradiction as revealing the situation's inherent multi-facetedness, with all facets equally real and co-constitutive. This is clearly stronger than ordinary value pluralism and different from simple perspectivism. Is there a precise philosophical term—perhaps drawn from logical or ontological traditions outside standard Western analytic philosophy—that captures this exact commitment to non-hierarchical, co-applicable contradiction? How does it differ formally from dialetheism and from contextualist resolutions? If no single term captures it, can you define the precise boundaries of this posture, including what it is not?
