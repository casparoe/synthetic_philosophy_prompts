I'm writing a feature on Victorian philosophers and weird science, and over coffee two people got into it about Sidgwick and the psychical research crowd. One's a philosopher collegue, the other's a historian of ideas. I wasn't taking sides — just listening. Here's rougly how it went.

(1) She said Sidgwick's whole deal with the Society for Psychical Research was methodological consistency: he brought the same rigour from The Methods of Ethics into investigating telepathy and survival, refusing to lower his evidential bar just because the topic was spooky. If he ended up sceptical, it was because the data didn't meet the standard, not because he was closed-minded.

(2) He said that's half right but misses the asymmetry. Sidgwick was genuinely open to telepathic claims on fairly slim experimental evidence — card-guessing, that sort of thing — yet he held survival-of-death claims to a far stricter standard, even when SPR investigators presented cross-correspondence cases where messages received through different mediums only made sense together. If his scepticism was pure method, why the double standard?

(3) She replied there's no double standard, just a structural difference in testability. Telepathy involves living subjects and can be run in controlled conditions; survival requires identifying a specific dead personality, which is always vulnerable to fraud, subconscious leakage, or coincidence. The cross-correspondence material is clever, but it still depends on post-mortem identification, so it inherits the same confounds.

(4) He pushed back that this sounds like post-hoc rationalisation. The cross-correspondence cases were designed specifically to block subconscious leakage — the messages are split across sitters with no shared knowledge. If Sidgwick was being consistent, he'd either demand the same replication for telepathy (which he didn't) or grant that cross-correspondences solve the identification problem. Instead, he seemed to treat survival as a special threat, probably because his utilitarian ethics quietly depended on people believing in consequences that extend beyond death.

Can you go through their exchange move by move and say who's right at each step — not just who wins overall? What are they both missing? Please answer in British English, and at the end say how confident you are in your verdict and single out the point you're least sure of.
