I'm drafting a short essay on the regress problem in epistemic justification, and I'd like your comments on accuracy, conceptual interest, and structural weakness. The draft is below; please treat it as the object of critique rather than a settled view.

Draft argument: The standard trilemma presents justification as ending in basic beliefs (foundationalism), looping in mutual support (coherentism), or extending infinitely (infinitism). I think this framing obscures a deeper divergence about what inquiry requires at the point where it halts. Foundationalists need a stopping condition: a belief justified without further inference, yet not arbitrary. The difficulty is not merely locating such beliefs, but explaining why the justifier is entitled to treat them as non-inferential without begging the question against the regress. Coherentists need closure conditions: the belief set must be integrated enough that revising any member destabilizes the system. Here termination is not a point but a systemic property. Infinitism appears to reject both, but I think it relocates rather than eliminates stopping conditions. To continue justifying B by appealing to B1, B2, and so on, one must already possess a criterion for selecting the next relevant belief. Without such a selection rule, the sequence is arbitrary rather than justificatory. So infinitism does not show that justification never ends; it shows that the conditions governing continuation are themselves never justified. This exposes infinitism to a different regress—not of beliefs, but of selection criteria—while foundationalism and coherentism differ mainly in whether they localize termination in individual beliefs or distribute it across relations.

First, is my claim that infinitism harbors a hidden "selection-rule" problem a fair characterization, or does it misrepresent how infinitists conceive of available reasons? Second, does the distinction between "stopping conditions" and "closure conditions" do real philosophical work, or is it merely terminological? Third, where is the argument weakest—particularly in the move from "need a criterion" to "that criterion generates its own regress"?
