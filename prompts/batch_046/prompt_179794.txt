I've been thinking about Benatar's asymmetry — you know, the idea that it's bad to bring suffering people into existence but not bad to fail to bring happy people into existence, since absence of pleasure only matters if there's someone there to miss it. I'm pretty convinced, but I've been playing with a modified version that applies not just to humans but to any "potential welfare-subject" we might create, including artificial minds with human-like consciousness. Specifically, I think the asymmetry gets stronger the closer the potential being is to the "threshold" where life might barely be worth living — call it the threshold-adjacent asymmetry. The closer you are to that boundary, the more the risk of real suffering should weigh against the non-badness of missing out on pleasure, because the "victim" of suffering is definete while the "victim" of missed joy is still just a non-existant placeholder.

But I want to know what should actually make me abandon this, or at least weaken it. Not the usual "people are glad they exist" objections — I've heard those and they miss the point. I mean the structural defeaters: cases where the asymmetry seems to collapse into a blanket anti-creation principle that even applies to beings with overwhelmingly good lives, or decision-theoretic problems with reasoning near an uncertain welfare threshold. Which of these would a defender of my modified view have to concede as genuinely damaging, versus which ones can be dismissed by tweaking the framework? Rank them from fatal to merely awkward. And be honest about whether this version has been qualified into unfalsifiability — if it has, is that actually a problem for a view like this, or just a feature of ethical reasoning in general? I'd especially like to hear if there's a tension between this asymmetry and our intuitions about saving existant lives versus not creating new ones, since that feels like the place where the view might actually bleed.
