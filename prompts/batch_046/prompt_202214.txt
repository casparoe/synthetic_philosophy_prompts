As a retired physicist now turning to political philosophy, I have been working through an older translation of Aristotle's Politics, specifically the books treating democratic equality. There is a dense passage in which he distinguishes election, which he associates with oligarchy, from selection by lot, which he associates with democracy, and then insists that citizens must hold office in rotation rather than continuously. The text asserts that democratic equality requires that no citizen govern permanently, for if some rule without being ruled in turn, they come to treat their fellow citizens as subjects rather than equals, and the equality of power is destroyed.

My difficulty is conceptual. The argument appears to combine a normative claim—that rotational symmetry is constitutive of democratic equality—with what sounds like an empirical claim about the corruption of prolonged authority, as if civic judgment degrades with tenure in a manner analogous to cumulative error in a physical instrument. Attempting to formalize this, I have been treating civic virtue as a state variable with an exponential decay constant, a kind of institutional half-life, but I worry that I am importing a scientific model where it does not belong. I want to understand whether Aristotle presents the corruption of continuous rulers as a necessary consequence of the relational asymmetry itself—so that the decay model is redundant—or only as a contingent psychological tendency that rotation happens to prevent. If the corruption is merely contingent, then the normative force of the argument for sortition and term limits seems to depend on an empirical law of human nature that might not hold; if it is necessary, then the argument stands or falls on the definition of equality alone. Could you help me identify the precise logical step where this distinction is made, and tell me whether my decay analogy captures anything genuinely present in the passage?
