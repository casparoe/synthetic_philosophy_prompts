I was discussing with a colleague whether demandingness objections genuinely undermine consequentialism or instead depend on a hidden model of well-being that consequentialists can reject. The standard framing treats the objection as a clash between an impersonal duty to maximize aggregate welfare and an agent-centered prerogative to pursue one's own projects, career, and relationships. I want to defend a less familiar position: that demandingness objections implicitly rely on calculating the "cost" of compliance against a counterfactual baseline of the agent's life calculated in isolation, but this baseline is unstable because the goods that constitute personal flourishing—artistic traditions, local attachments, forms of leisure—are path-dependent on the institutional and moral ecology in which they are embedded. If so, high-compliance regimes do not merely extract sacrifices from pre-existing lives; they reshape the very conditions under which those lives flourish, often preserving rather than destroying the goods agents fear losing. At the same time, this risks collapsing into a redescription of demandingness rather than its dissolution.

Please construct this argument rigorously. Show how it handles the classic comparative cases—such as the choice between a locally fulfilling career and one that maximizes welfare—without denying that real sacrifice occurs, and address the worry that you have merely smuggled in a consequentialist valuation of personal goods. Then, explicitly say what would change your mind: identify whether the failure of the co-constitution thesis about well-being, an empirical demonstration that personal goods are robust across radically different moral ecologies, or a conceptual proof that counterfactual baselines must be agent-neutral would be sufficient to undermine your position, and flag the precise inferential step—most likely the move from path-dependence to reduced demandingness—where your reasoning is most vulnerable.
