I have been reading, mostly in popular introductions, about the philosophical position that phenomenal consciousness is an illusion in a specific sense: not that we are hallucinating, but that properties like the vivid redness of a sunset or the piercing quality of a toothache do not exist as they seem to, and that all that really occurs is neural representation and functional processing. I think I grasp the basic distinction between the appearance and the reality, and I understand that illusionists want to deny there is any extra "feel" beyond the brain's informational states.

What I keep noticing, however, is a recurring pattern in how this denial is defended and explained, and I do not know whether it has a recognized name. The illusionist denies that there is literally any phenomenal quality—no "what it is like" aspect, no direct presentation of redness. But then, when explaining why ordinary people find this view so implausible, the same writer will appeal to how convincing the illusion is, how overwhelming the seeming of direct awareness feels, or how our cognitive architecture presents the world in a way that is vivid and immediate. In other words, the explanation for resistance to illusionism repeatedly invokes the very qualities—seeming, feeling, presenting, immediacy—that were just eliminated from the ontology. The move is not a flat contradiction, since these terms can be reinterpreted as descriptions of functional bias or representational error. Yet there is a specific structure: the property is removed by ontological denial, then quietly reintroduced under a functional description in order to account for why people keep believing in it. I have seen this happen in discussions of color experience, of bodily pain, and of the sense of self.

Is there an established concept for this exact configuration? I am not asking about performative contradiction in general, nor about the ordinary difficulty of describing subjective experience in objective terms, but about this particular two-step: denial of a phenomenal property followed by its functional reanimation to explain the persistence of belief. How does it differ from a simple equivocation, and where are its edges—does it apply only when the functional substitute uses the same vocabulary, or also when the substitute is genuinely distinct but still tacitly presupposes the denied quality? If there is no standard term, I would like a precise definition that distinguishes it from nearby failures like self-refutation or the hard problem of consciousness, along with clear boundary cases showing when an argument avoids it and when it falls into it. Please indicate how confident you are in the distinctions you draw, and point out specifically the claim or classification you are least sure about—for example, whether illusionists have a standard reply that dissolves the pattern, or whether my characterization wrongly lumps together arguments that are actually structurally different.
