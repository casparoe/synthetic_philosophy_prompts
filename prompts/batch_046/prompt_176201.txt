I chair the clinical ethics committee at a large teaching hospital, and we are revising our protocol for adjudicating conflicts between a patient’s advance directive and apparently contrary wishes expressed by that same patient now, in a state of diminished but not absent capacity. The standard framing treats this as a binary clash between the autonomy of the earlier self and that of the present self, but I want to replace it with a more granular principle: the authority of an advance directive should be understood as a form of normative custody over one’s life-plan, which can be forfeited not by any change of mind but only through specific diachronic ruptures—value inversion (the core evaluative commitments that grounded the directive have been genuinely abandoned rather than temporarily overridden), experiential alienation (the present self, in reflective moments, cannot recognize the directive’s goals as its own), and practical incommensurability (the present decision-context is structurally unlike anything envisaged in the directive, so applying it would not serve the plan it expressed). Where none of these three ruptures clearly obtain, the directive retains authority; where one or more are demonstrated, authority is suspended and present preference governs, but the burden of proof lies with those who seek to override the document. I need you to translate this principle into a decision procedure that my committee—comprising clinicians, a legal officer, a chaplain, and a lay representative—can actually run in a forty-five-minute case conference without philosophical training, though with explicit flags marking where judgment rather than fact-finding is unavoidable. The procedure must specify: the ordered questions to ask; what counts as sufficient evidence for each answer, including admissible and inadmissible sources; the stopping rules (honor the directive, set it aside, or escalate); and the precise nodes where values, not facts, determine the outcome. Then apply it to three cases: first, a patient with early-stage Alzheimer’s who refuses a feeding tube her directive mandates be placed; second, a patient with severe depression who demands discontinuation of life-sustaining treatment that her directive requires continue, where her psychiatrist attests that her values are distorted but not erased; and third, a patient in a persistent vegetative state whose ambiguous directive prohibits heroic measures but does not address artificial nutrition, with family members divided over whether the present clinical situation qualifies. In at least one application, the procedure should yield an answer you expect I will find unwelcome, and you should state explicitly what the procedure cannot resolve. Please organize your response as follows: (1) The Operational Procedure (approximately 500 words): present the flow in ordered steps, defining the three rupture tests in operational terms that a non-philosopher can apply; (2) Worked Applications (approximately 600 words): run all three cases sequentially, labeling for each the step at which the procedure stops and the justification; (3) Nodes of Irreducible Judgment (approximately 250 words): identify the exact points where values rather than facts govern the outcome, specify who must sign off, and note what evidence is excluded at those nodes; (4) Limitations and Expected Misfires (approximately 250 words): state where the procedure will reliably fail, what conflicts it cannot settle, and why the binary framing it replaces was not entirely mistaken.
