I'm a journalist covering science and ideas for about a decade, and lately I've noticed this weird argumentative dance in debates over ambitious long-term projects—fusion timelines, AI risk estimates, climate geoengineering proposals, that sort of thing. The critics always open with the graveyard: here's the list of things that looked structurally identical and failed. The defenders don't just say 'this one's different.' They build what I keep mentally calling an 'escape ladder'—they concede that the failure rate applies to the broad category, then argue the project actually belongs to a narrower subcategory with a better track record; when that subcategory is shown to have its own graveyard, they retreat to a sub-subcategory, each step technically defensible. Meanwhile the critics keep pulling the ladder by shoving success stories upward into broader failure buckets. I think this is somewhere near the reference-class problem, and maybe near whatever philosophers call the tension between statistical humility and case-specific confidence, but I'm after the specific concept for this recursive nesting—where modesty is acknowledged at every level except the final claim, which is protected by a chain of distinctions.

Does this exact pattern have a name? If not, could you coin one with sharp edges: how it's different from ordinary special pleading, from motte-and-bailey, and from just being wrong about the taxonomy—especially how it's seperate from plain reference-class tennis? I don't need a bibliography, just want to know if philosophers have already carved this out or if I'm staring at a real gap.
