Read William Golding’s Lord of the Flies through the lens of archaeological inference: the problem of reconstructing a society’s beliefs, rituals, and institutions solely from the material objects it leaves behind. Imagine that, centuries after the boys’ rescue, the island is excavated and only a handful of artifacts survive—broken spectacles, a large conch shell, charred fire pits, the decayed remains of a parachutist, and a pig’s skull mounted on a stick. From these fragments alone, an archaeologist might infer a hierarchical society organized around a sacred object, a division between a rationalist elite and a warrior caste, and perhaps a ritualized practice of predation or sacrifice. In the novel itself, however, these same objects circulate through living arguments about authority, vision, and violence. Where does this archaeological reading illuminate what Golding dramatizes about how institutions crystallize around things? Where does the living narrative resist that reading—where do the boys’ intentions, misunderstandings, and shifting alliances reveal that material traces systematically mislead about belief and practice? Focus on specific scenes: the conch’s authority being established and then shattered, Piggy’s glasses functioning simultaneously as instruments of knowledge and targets of destruction, and the pig’s head as a symbol whose meaning depends entirely on who is looking. Conclude by asking what the novel adds to philosophical debates about underdetermination in the reconstruction of past minds—does the story suggest that the difficulty is not merely epistemic but also ethical, in that leaving durable traces behind is itself an exercise of power that shapes what future observers can know?
