My study group constructed this argument linking Athens's three canonical democratic failures, and it now strikes me as proving far more than we intended. (1) The Mytilene debate (427 BCE) shows the Assembly voting for genocide, then reversing itself within a day—democratic deliberation lacks institutional memory and is merely emotional whim. (2) The Sicilian expedition (415 BCE) shows the Assembly overriding Nicias's strategic expertise and voting for catastrophic invasion—democracy systematically suppresses expert knowledge in favor of popular enthusiasm. (3) Socrates' trial (399 BCE) shows 501 jurors convicting a philosopher for corrupting youth and impiety—democracy is structurally hostile to intellectual dissent. (4) So in moral judgment, strategic planning, and intellectual freedom alike, democratic deliberation produces the worst available outcome. (5) If an institution consistently produces the worst outcome across all domains, it has negative epistemic value—worse than random choice, since randomness at least avoids systematic bias toward error. (6) Therefore any institution that binds collective decision by unfiltered popular vote, including modern referendum systems and jury trials, inherits this structural amnesia and anti-rationality and should be replaced by continuous expert governance. Where does the chain break? I am inclined to defend (1)–(3) as accurate descriptions, but I worry that (5) smuggles in an unstated comparison class and that (6) ignores differences between direct Assembly rule and representative or bureaucratic institutions. Diagnose the weakest step—whether the induction in (4), the value judgment in (5), or the analogical leap in (6)—and explain what rejecting it costs. Also say what would change your mind: for instance, whether evidence that Athenian juries usually acquitted (making Socrates an outlier) would undermine (3), or that modern deliberative bodies retain institutional memory through administrative continuity would block (6), and where your own reasoning is most likely to fail.
