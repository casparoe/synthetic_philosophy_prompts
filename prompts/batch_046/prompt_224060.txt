Last evening I had a lengthy argument with a close friend—she is a biologist and I am auditing an introductory philosophy of science course—about whether describing how things are can ever, by itself, establish how they ought to be. We kept encountering the same rhetorical pattern: a speaker points to an evolutionary origin, a statistical norm, or an ecosystem’s functioning, describes it using apparently neutral terms such as “healthy,” “proper function,” or “natural state,” and then concludes that we are therefore obligated to preserve or align with that condition. When pressed, the speaker denies having smuggled in any value judgment, insisting that the prescription is already implicit in the correct description. I have now noticed this maneuver in evolutionary ethics, in environmental debates about “natural” baselines, and in clinical discussions where “normal” physiology is treated as a normative target.

What I am trying to identify is not the broad logical gap between factual and normative claims in general, but this more specific configuration: the embedding of a prescriptive commitment within a definitional or taxonomic choice, such that the “ought” is presented as discovered within the “is” rather than added to it. Does this particular sleight-of-hand have an established name? How precisely does it relate to the wider problem of deriving obligations from descriptions—is it best understood as a subtype, as a semantic relocation of the same error, or as something genuinely distinct? And if the literature offers no precise label for this exact move, how should one delineate its boundaries, distinguishing it both from ordinary circular reasoning and from cases where descriptive and normative language merely overlap by accident?

In your response, please separate what is settled among philosophers who study this area from what remains genuinely contested—particularly regarding whether this maneuver actually bridges the underlying gap or only disguises it—and do not present any disputed interpretation as though it were the consensus.
