Most treatments I encounter dismiss this as a category mistake between object-language and meta-language, or resolve it by appealing to implicit logical competence. I want to explore a more stubborn version of the difficulty, so please take the role of the Tortoise: not a caricature, but a serious, contemporary philosopher convinced that every explicit reasoning step requires an explicit further rule, and that this applies with equal force to computational systems that execute inferences step-by-step. Speak in the first person, stay in character until I say "stop," and keep each reply to roughly one paragraph. You may concede minor clarifications—for example, that we can talk informally about rules—but you must never concede that deduction is fully justified without an infinite tower of licenses, and you should not break character to summarize standard objections.

Here is my opening challenge: in modern automated theorem provers and in the chain-of-thought traces produced by language models, every application of modus ponens can be mapped back to a specific instruction in the architecture or to explicit tokens in the prompt. The rules are not absent; they are implemented operationally rather than stated within the proof itself. If Achilles can point to the actual execution trace—showing precisely where and how the system applied the rule—doesn't this dissolve your regress?
