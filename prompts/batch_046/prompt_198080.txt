I teach an upper-level seminar on political ideology and comparative constitutional history, fourteen students, seventy-five minutes. I want a structured in-class deliberation—part simulation, part forced comparison—that makes participants feel a specific conceptual instability in American exceptionalism, not the familiar triad of historical thesis, normative aspiration, and ideological mask, but the reflexive bind that emerges when exceptionalism is treated as an empirically testable comparative claim. The problem is this: any metric selected to test whether the United States is genuinely exceptional—constitutional continuity, absence of feudal residue, high geographic mobility, weak labor-party formation—is already calibrated by the normative framework that exceptionalism itself promotes. Consequently, declaring the thesis falsified requires adopting an alternative evaluative baseline that often turns out to be the exceptionalist self-image in negative form. I do not want a lecture up front; I want the students to construct the comparative framework themselves, apply it to a small set of assigned national cases, and experience the collapse of the descriptive-normative boundary before anyone names the phenomenon.

Please design the rules, roles, materials, and timeline for this exercise. Arrange the mechanics—perhaps hidden payoff structures, assigned case portfolios, or staged deliberation constraints—so that groups must agree on a shared comparative metric, run the comparison across two or three other polities, and then discover that their chosen standard either confirms exceptionalism tautologically or undermines it only by importing a rival normative vision that looks suspiciously like the original claim inverted. Include a debrief that maps specific moments in the activity onto the three registers (historical thesis, normative claim, ideology) and onto the reflexive problem, plus an honest account of which feature—likely the long historical sedimentation of the evaluative vocabulary itself—cannot be reproduced in a single session and must be supplied by me. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time, such as groups converging prematurely on a safe metric or refusing to commit to one altogether, and how to handle each failure. Do not provide a bibliography, reading list, or citations; the conceptual work should remain entirely inside the room.
