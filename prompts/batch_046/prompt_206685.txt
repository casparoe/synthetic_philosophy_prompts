Write a dialogue between a philosopher of technology defending the narrow definition of the AI alignment problem—intent alignment, in which a system succeeds when it reliably executes the specific objective its designers specified, independent of whether that objective is morally correct—and a moral philosopher arguing that this definition is unstable, because specifying "the designers' intent" requires normative choices about whose values prevail when designers disagree or leave gaps. Center their exchange on a concrete scenario: a resource-scarce medical-triage system instructed to "minimize mortality," but with no specified tie-breaking rule between age-weighted QALYs and first-come-first-served fairness. The narrow-definition proponent should argue that ambiguity is an engineering failure correctable by sharper intent-specification, and that delegating tie-breaking authority to the design team is a legitimate procedural choice rather than a hidden moral commitment. The broad-definition proponent should counter that selecting a tie-breaking rule is itself value-laden, and that "delegation to the designers" collapses when the design authority is fragmented—so the narrow framework implicitly smuggles in substantive value judgments under the guise of procedural neutrality. Each speaker should respond directly to the other's argument: the narrow proponent must explain why fragmented authority does not force the engineer to become a moral philosopher, and the broad proponent must explain why procedural delegation fails precisely when authority is not singular. Keep the dialogue focused, with no requests for citations or reading lists.
