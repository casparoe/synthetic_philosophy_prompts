Design a runnable 75-minute classroom simulation for an upper-level seminar of about 14 students working on Locke’s philosophy of education. The activity should make participants feel, through structured roles and symbolic stakes, the tension Locke describes between the tutor’s necessary authority and the pupil’s obligation to withhold assent until ideas are clearly and distinctly perceived—especially the danger that education by authority causes the mind to take its own (or the tutor’s) nominal classifications for real distinctions in things.

Specify roles, rules, and materials: divide the group into tutors and pupils. Give the tutors a hidden set of “objects” with real but non-obvious structural properties, and ask them to construct a public taxonomy and teaching script based only on superficial, easily communicable marks. Give the pupils a decision task—classify new instances using only the tutor’s descriptions, with a symbolic credit system that rewards correct classification but penalizes suspended judgment too severely and penalizes false assent even more severely. Arrange the incentives so that pure deference yields rapid but systematically wrong classifications, pure skepticism yields paralysis, and attempts to “split the difference” collapse because the pupils lack direct access to the hidden structures. The tutors should act in good faith, reinforcing their scheme; the pupils should discover only late in the session that the taxonomy is purely nominal.

Provide a minute-by-minute timeline, the credit/payoff scheme (using points or tokens rather than money), and a brief list of physical or digital materials needed. Then outline the debrief: map the instruction phase onto Locke’s critique of rote learning; map the classification crisis onto the distinction between real and nominal essences; map the pupils’ struggle over assent onto Locke’s doctrine of assent by degrees. Note what will predictably go wrong on the first run—likely, tutors dominating the debrief or pupils framing the exercise as a simple rebellion-against-authority narrative rather than an epistemic failure—and suggest how to redirect it. Finally, be explicit about which feature of Locke’s view the simulation cannot reproduce—specifically, his theological framing of reason’s natural light as a divine endowment that makes autonomous assent ultimately trustworthy—and state that the facilitator must supply this in discussion. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, citations, or verbatim quotations from Locke’s texts; do not invoke obscure post-Lockean scholarship; and do not depend on any specific publication date.
