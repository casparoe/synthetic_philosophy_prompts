I'm taking a course on early analytic ethics and we've been wrestling with G.E. Moore's Principia Ethica — especially his claim that friendship and the appreciation of beauty are among the highest intrinsic goods, plus that strange organic unity idea where a whole can be worth more than just adding up its parts.

So take the Beatles split around 1969-70 as a real case study. Don't just rehearse the usual story about Yoko and business disputes — I know all that. Instead, analyse the breakup through Moore's lens: treat the band's collaboration not as four solo artists making music near each other, but as an 'organic aesthetic friendship' — a collective good where mutual recognition of each other's artistic capacities produces beauty that none of them could generate alone. Map out who counts as the 'players' here in Moore's terms. Is the Lennon-McCartney friendship itself the core intrinsic good, with the records being a happy side-effect? Or is the collective artistic friendship — the group as a unit — the real good, meaning the split destroyed an organic whole? What should each member have done by Moore's lights if they genuinely recognised that value? And then turn it round: does the fact they all made decent solo work afterwards show that Moore's organic unity principle overstates collective goods, or does it just reveal that the friendship had already curdled and the organic good was already lost?
