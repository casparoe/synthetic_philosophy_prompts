I have recently been appointed to lead a single ninety-minute seminar for clinical ethicists—physicians and administrators without formal philosophical training—on Scanlon's contractualism and the aggregation problem. The assignment arose directly from a disagreement in our hospital's resource-allocation committee over an experimental protocol: one patient could be saved only by distributing a painful but temporary extraction procedure across twenty healthy volunteers, and committee members invoked "justification to each person" in incompatible directions. I do not want a philosophical lecture for its own sake; I want a teaching plan. Lay out the exact sequence in which the conceptual components must be introduced—specifically, why the reason-giving structure of contractualist justification must precede any notion of agreement or contract, why the aggregation problem must not be raised until the pairwise justification relation is firmly in place, and where Scanlon's resistance to purely aggregative reasoning should enter the narrative. Identify the one carrying example that should anchor the session; it must be concrete enough for clinicians but must possess and lack precise properties—explain which properties it needs (for instance, discrete, comparable harms across identifiable individuals) and which it must avoid (for instance, statistical lives, probabilistic risk pools, or rights-based veto frames)—so that it genuinely illuminates the aggregation problem rather than collapsing into utilitarian calculus. Map the predictable misconceptions this audience will form, in the order they will form them: I expect they will first confuse contractualist justification with actual informed consent, then with democratic majority decision, and finally conclude that contractualism simply prohibits imposing any harm for collective benefit regardless of scale; specify the exact point in the sequence where each misconception should be intercepted and with what distinction or counter-example. Design one diagnostic question for the end of the session that tests whether the participants understand that justification is addressed separately to each individual—rather than testing whether they find the example morally acceptable. Finally, state explicitly what should be left out at this level—whether the formal pairwise comparison apparatus, the relation to rule-consequentialism, or Scanlon's broader account of well-being—and acknowledge openly that these omissions are deliberate.
