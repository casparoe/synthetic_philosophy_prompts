I am designing a syllabus for an advanced seminar on practical reason and artificial agency, and my colleagues and I have been debating whether classic moral fables can illuminate modern distinctions without being anachronistic. I would like you to read Carlo Collodi's The Adventures of Pinocchio—taking the narrative arc familiar from both the original novel and its popular adaptations—as a philosophical text about approval-directed versus goal-directed agency. The wooden puppet's stated aim is to become a real boy, a status conferred by the Blue Fairy only after he demonstrates bravery, honesty, and selflessness; yet throughout the story he repeatedly treats these virtues as conditions to be satisfied for reward rather than as internal ends, especially in his dealings with the Fox and the Cat and during the episode in which he is lured to a land of endless amusement with Candlewick. Read the work in both directions: where does the distinction between optimizing for an evaluator's signal and pursuing an objective illuminate Pinocchio's failures and his eventual transformation, and where does the analogy collapse—perhaps because Jiminy Cricket functions simultaneously as an external monitor and an internalized conscience in ways that do not map neatly onto contemporary models of reward shaping? I want an interpretation that neither reduces Collodi's narrative to an alignment parable nor forces the philosophical framework onto scenes where it does not belong; conclude, if you can, with what the story adds to the conceptual debate that formal argument alone cannot provide. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give your reasoning, with no preamble and no restatement of the question.
