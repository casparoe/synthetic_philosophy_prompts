Write a short story set in the Typogenetic Archive, an institution that preserves knowledge by encoding texts into synthetic DNA strands rather than paper or silicon, founded on the claim that biological replication and logical self-reference share a deep structural isomorphism. The archive’s designers treat this not as metaphor but as engineering principle: just as a formal system powerful enough to represent arithmetic can construct a statement about its own unprovability, a genome complex enough to encode its own replication machinery can, in principle, encode a sequence that makes a claim about its own copyability. The protagonist, a preservationist tasked with maintaining the collection, discovers a strand that appears to do exactly this—its nucleotide sequence encodes both the biochemical instructions for polymerase-based replication and a logical structure analogous to a Gödel sentence, implying that perfect, error-free copying of this particular message is impossible within the archive’s biochemical constraints. The narrative should follow her decision about whether to run the replication protocol (accepting the predicted mutation or failure), to lock the original away un-copied (preserving fidelity at the cost of accessibility), or to edit the sequence to break the self-reference (enabling distribution but destroying the argument). Use real mechanisms—polymerase proofreading, frameshift mutations, fidelity thresholds—only as the material substrate; invent the specific sequence and its formal structure. Resolve the story not with a technical fix but with a philosophical consequence: what it means for a piece of information to survive only by being transmitted imperfectly, or not at all.
