I'm in a course on long-run moral decision-making, and I've noticed a recurring response pattern in surveys and classroom discussions that I can't map onto any concept we've covered. A person will agree, in the abstract, that if welfare is constant across generations then a benefit in 2100 and an identical benefit in 2200 should be weighted exactly equally—there is no reason for the calendar position alone to matter. But when the same person evaluates actual policies, they treat the 2100 benefit as far more pressing if it is described as "for people alive then" rather than "for people two centuries away," even though both descriptions point to the same date and the same beneficiaries; conversely, they become indifferent between 2100 and 2200 when both are described genealogically ("our grandchildren's era" versus "our great-great-grandchildren's era"). The divergence is stable, survives explicit reminders about the constant-rate commitment, and does not seem reducible to ordinary impatience or to a belief that distant people are less real. Is there an established term for this kind of framing-sensitive split within intergenerational valuation—where temporal and genealogical descriptions of the same future pull in opposite directions despite an endorsed uniform discount rate? If it is unnamed, what distinguishes it from hyperbolic discounting and from present bias, and where are its boundaries?
