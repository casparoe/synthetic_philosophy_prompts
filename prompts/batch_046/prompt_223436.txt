I'm giving the opening remarks at the retirement gathering of my neighbor, who ran our town's emergency-preparedness office for three decades. The room is split between her colleagues—risk analysts and public-health officers who will catch every reference to inference about populations and samples—and family members who know her only as the woman who kept the neighborhood shelter stocked. I have no formal training in philosophy or statistics, but I've been thinking on my own about a tension I can't resolve: when I plan my life I treat myself as an ordinary, random draw from all the people who could have existed, yet when I think about the fact that I'm here at all, it feels wrong to use my own existence as proof that the conditions for life must be widespread. I want the speech to be structured around the distinction between self-sampling reasoning—taking yourself as a representative sample—and self-indication reasoning—treating your presence as evidence that there are many more like you, the kind of move that leads the so-called presumptuous philosopher to bet on cosmic longevity just because he is alive to place the bet. The idea should do real work: it should shape what I can honestly say about her legacy. We can honor the work that made survival more likely for ordinary people like us, but we should not claim that her being in this room proves the emergency system was destined to succeed, because that would confuse being one of the survivors with being evidence that there were bound to be survivors everywhere. Please write a five-minute speech that explains this distinction without lecturing, applies it to honoring someone who spent her career planning for disasters she never personally experienced, and lands with both the experts and the family. Please also separate what is philosophically settled about these reasoning assumptions from what remains genuinely contested, and include a brief note on where the speech takes liberties with contested interpretations or leans on a particular reading that specialists might dispute.
