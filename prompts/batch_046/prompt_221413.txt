I was sitting in on a philosophy study group where two members, Mara and Leo, got into a sustained argument about whether expressivism in moral philosophy can survive the Frege-Geach problem. Their exchange went like this:

Mara opened by saying that the classic objection assumes a false uniformity: when 'murder is wrong' appears unembedded, it expresses a non-cognitive attitude like disapproval, but when it appears embedded in a conditional — for example, 'If murder is wrong, then persuading your brother to murder is wrong' — it need not express that attitude at all. Instead, she argued, it functions as a supposition of a moral framework, much as 'If the king is in the counting house' supposes a fiction without asserting it. So there is no pressure to assign it a descriptive, propositional content just because it sits inside a logical compound.

Leo responded that this merely relocates the difficulty. If the semantic contribution of 'murder is wrong' shifts between expressing an attitude in isolation and expressing something else (or nothing) inside a conditional, then valid moral reasoning is broken. The inference from 'Murder is wrong' to 'Persuading my brother to murder is wrong' depends on a stable premise that connects to the conclusion via the conditional. If the premise in isolation carries one kind of meaning and the antecedent in the conditional carries another, the expressivist cannot explain why the unembedded argument is valid without smuggling in a hidden descriptive content.

Mara then proposed a hybrid compromise: there is a stable descriptive core — the property of being wrong — but this core is grasped and activated only through an associated non-cognitive attitude. In unembedded contexts the attitude is live; in embedded contexts only the descriptive core is active, providing the logical stability Leo demands, while the attitude remains what distinguishes moral judgment from mere description.

Leo countered that this is cognitivism disguised as expressivism. If the descriptive core is doing all the compositional and inferential work in conditionals, negations, and disjunctions, then the attitude is epiphenomenal to moral reasoning. The expressivist's distinctive claim was that moral judgment is fundamentally an expression of attitude, not a representation of moral facts; but if representation handles all the logic, the attitude becomes a decorative psychological overlay.

Mara shifted ground and defended a dynamic variant: rather than treating moral sentences as expressing static propositions or static attitudes, we should treat an assertion of 'murder is wrong' as updating a conversational score with a normative commitment. Embedding in a conditional updates the score conditionally. The Frege-Geach problem, she said, presupposes static propositional semantics, so it misses the point if the underlying mechanism is score-based and compositional without being propositional.

Leo granted that dynamic semantics might avoid the static embedding problem, but insisted it faces an analogous challenge with negation and disjunction. If 'Murder is not wrong' updates the score by canceling a normative commitment, and 'Either murder is wrong or theft is wrong' updates it with a disjunctive commitment, then we need an account of what it is to accept or reject such complex updates without implicitly treating them as descriptions of which norms hold. Otherwise, he argued, the dynamic expressivist owes us a compositional theory of score-change that looks suspiciously like a compositional theory of truth-conditions in disguise.

Mara concluded that Leo was conflating the Frege-Geach problem specifically — about embedding in conditionals — with broader worries about negation and disjunction that are independent. She maintained that dynamic score-updating solves the embedding issue without conceding descriptivism, and that the remaining challenges are engineering problems for the semantics, not fatal objections.

Leo ended by saying that at some point the distinction between expressing a non-cognitive attitude through compositional score-rules and describing a moral state of affairs through compositional truth-rules collapses into a verbal distinction. If the compositional machinery behaves exactly like a representational system, he said, then the expressivist has not preserved her view but only renamed cognitivism.

Neither would concede. Could you adjudicate their exchange step by step? For each move — Mara's uniformity denial, Leo's relocation objection, Mara's hybrid proposal, Leo's epiphenomenality charge, Mara's dynamic turn, Leo's negation and disjunction challenge, and Mara's separation of issues — say who is making the stronger point and whether there is a hidden assumption either is missing. Then say who is more right overall, and identify any important consideration that both parties overlooked entirely. Please write your response in American English.
