Write an essay on whether collective guilt for historical wrongs can survive the complete constitutional dissolution and replacement of the state that committed them. Imagine a nation that formally extinguishes its legal personality—dissolving its constitution, dissolving its government, and reconstituting itself under an entirely new founding charter with a different name and legal structure—while retaining continuous territory, population, and institutional memory. Drawing on Karl Jaspers' distinction between criminal, political, moral, and metaphysical guilt, examine whether each form of guilt is tied to the continuity of the legal-political entity or can persist across this kind of constitutional rupture. Weigh the analogy to personal identity fission: if a person divides into two psychologically continuous successors, do obligations or blame distribute in proportion? Consider also the legal doctrine of corporate succession, where a dissolved corporation's liabilities may or may not transfer to a successor entity depending on the form of reorganisation. Reach and justify a definite conclusion: either that the successor nation inherits a qualified, non-criminal form of collective guilt despite the constitutional break, or that the rupture genuinely severs moral and metaphysical responsibility, leaving only historical remembrance without present collective culpability. In either case, explain precisely which of Jaspers' categories survive, which are extinguished, and why the opposing view mistakes continuity of memory for continuity of moral agency.
