I spent three decades working in condensed-matter physics before retiring, and over the past several years I have turned the same analytical patience I once applied to phase transitions toward the philosophy of quantum mechanics. I have become convinced that the familiar triad of interpretations—Everett, Copenhagen, and Bohm—are usually compared along an axis that obscures a deeper structural parallel, and I would like you to construct a rigorous argument for the position that Bohmian mechanics does not genuinely resolve the measurement problem but rather displaces it into the dynamics of the quantum potential. Specifically, argue that because the guidance equation for particle positions depends holistically on the universal wavefunction in configuration space, any apparently localized measurement apparatus remains coupled to distant degrees of freedom in a way that prevents the recovery of classical separability. This means that securing definite outcomes in Bohm’s framework either requires a hidden collapse mechanism—which the interpretation explicitly rejects—or an unexplained decoherence boundary that is functionally isomorphic to the Copenhagen cut it claims to avoid. Contrast this displacement with Everett’s relative-state formulation, in which branching is explicit and local observers are simply entangled subsystems of a universal state, and with Copenhagen’s direct instrumentalist refusal to assign ontology to the wavefunction altogether. Your argument should explain why the 3-space particle ontology of Bohm is insufficient by itself to restore the kind of local independence that would make the measurement problem vanish, and it should treat Bell-type correlations not as a refutation of any one interpretation but as a shared constraint that all three must accommodate. In particular, address whether the nonlocal dynamics introduced by the quantum potential is ontologically distinct from the nonlocality Everett accepts through entanglement, or whether that distinction collapses once one examines precisely what “the position of a particle” denotes within the Bohmian framework.

Please develop this without appealing to bibliographies, reading lists, or specific citations; I want the conceptual mechanics worked out from first principles as you understand them, with careful attention to the conditions under which a Bohmian defender might resist your conclusion by appealing to the actual configuration of particles rather than to the wavefunction’s global structure. I am especially interested in whether your argument turns on a feature that is genuinely internal to Bohm’s equations or on an interpretive assumption about what counts as an explanation of definite outcomes—because if the latter, then the apparent advantage of Bohm over Copenhagen may reduce to a preference for hidden variables rather than to a successful dissolution of the problem itself. Keep the reasoning formal and precise, as befits a comparison of ontologies that are each attempting to account for the same empirical predictions through radically different accounts of what exists between measurements.
