Please review the following outline for an essay on pure time preference and intergenerational discounting, and offer critical comments. The essay's thesis is that pure time preference—discounting future welfare solely in virtue of its futurity—is not a rational defect but can be defended as an expression of "diachronic sovereignty": the present practical agent's legitimate authority to assign greater weight to projects falling within its own lifespan and direct causal reach than to projects belonging to distant successors. I argue that this avoids the classic Ramsey-Parfit objection (that pure discounting treats identical goods differently based only on temporal location) because diachronic sovereignty appeals not to bare time but to a relational property—proximity to the agent's practical identity—that genuinely distinguishes near-future from far-future goods. I then examine the parallel with spatial parochialism, conceding that spatial distance does not justify differential moral weighting, but maintaining that temporal proximity is constitutive of agency in a way spatial proximity is not. The essay concludes with a policy proposal: social discount rates for long-term public goods (e.g., climate infrastructure) should incorporate a small pure rate—no more than 0.5 percent—restricted to outcomes within roughly one human lifespan, falling to near zero beyond that horizon, thereby respecting diachronic sovereignty without licensing indefinite intergenerational neglect. I would particularly welcome comments on two points. First, is "diachronic sovereignty" philosophically coherent, or does it merely rename temporal egoism without solving the underlying rationality problem? Second, is the lifespan-capped rate internally consistent with the argument, or does it represent an unmotivated compromise?
