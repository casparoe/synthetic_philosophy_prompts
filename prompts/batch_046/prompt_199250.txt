I'm drafting a short essay—around 2,500 words—for an advanced seminar on theories of value, and I want to check whether the conceptual architecture holds together before I commit to the full draft. The paper takes up Moritz Schlick's argument in his essay on the meaning of life, where he distinguishes between activity pursued for an external goal—work or purpose-driven action—and activity that is self-justifying, done for its own sake, which he associates with play and with the characteristic experience of youth. As I understand him, Schlick claims that life gains meaning not through the accumulation of achievements but through participation in this second kind of activity, and that the tragedy of aging is less the loss of strength than the gradual replacement of play by purpose. I want to apply this framework to a problem I see in contemporary productivity culture: the instrumentalization of leisure itself, where hobbies are tracked, optimized, and converted into side-projects, leaving no domain of human life untouched by goal-oriented reasoning. My thesis is that Schlick's distinction predicts exactly this pathology—not a crisis of purpose, but a crisis of play—though he wrote long before digital economies made such colonization systematic.

I'm concerned about three issues and would appreciate targeted feedback rather than general stylistic comments. First, I may be misreading Schlick's concept of play as purely non-instrumental; perhaps he allows playful activity to carry indirect benefits without losing its status as play, and if so, my sharp contrast with modern instrumental leisure collapses. Second, there seems to be a hidden normative premise in Schlick—perhaps that only the young can live meaningfully—which I do not want to endorse uncritically, but I'm unsure whether it is essential to his view or whether I can separate the structural distinction between play and purpose from that age-based evaluation. Third, I'm uncertain whether applying Schlick's metaphysics of value to a sociological critique of work culture is philosophically legitimate, or whether it requires premises about human nature that Schlick wouldn't accept and that I haven't independently defended. I will add citations and references myself, so please don't provide a bibliography; instead, tell me whether the framing is coherent, whether my characterization of Schlick is accurate enough for this purpose, and whether the move from his value theory to my cultural diagnosis amounts to more than dressing up sociology in philosophical vocabulary.
