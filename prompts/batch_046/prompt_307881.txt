I take part in a small, long-running forecasting collective: we maintain private prediction markets on policy outcomes and keep an epistemic ledger of reasoning errors for calibration. One of our founding members, who has mentored several of us, has lately developed a subtle but persistent framing bias—he attributes every strategic failure in our group to bad luck rather than to model error, and junior members are beginning to absorb this pattern uncritically. I have mapped the distortion carefully and feel a strong pull to correct him in our next review session, not solely because it threatens our collective accuracy, but also, I must admit, because there is a peculiar satisfaction in being the one who spotted the flaw in a respected senior figure. Our protocol requires anyone initiating a public correction to submit an anonymous forecast estimating how an uninvolved observer—with no hierarchical or social stake—would judge both the substantive error and the corrector's motives. I submitted mine, and the simulated observers split almost evenly: half see necessary maintenance of epistemic norms, the other half see resentment disguised as rigour. Rather than deferring to that divided market, I want to apply Adam Smith's account of sympathy and the internal tribunal of the impartial spectator to examine my own sentiments before I act. How ought I to distinguish, within myself, between genuine sympathy for the collective's long-term health and a more self-interested pleasure in dominance or correction? What would it mean for my proposed intervention to be approved by the impartial spectator rather than merely strategically sound? Please answer in British English, and treat this as a problem in moral psychology rather than offering generic conflict-resolution or mediation advice.
