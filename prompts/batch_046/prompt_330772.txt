As a journalist covering science and ideas, I have come to treat the narrow alignment problem—getting an AI system to pursue its designers' specific intended objective, irrespective of whether that objective is morally correct—as conceptually separable from value alignment, which requires resolving irreducible ethical disagreement. I call this the specification-boundary view: the claim that faithful execution is an engineering task that can be bracketed from moral philosophy. But I want to know what would actually count against it, not just the familiar objections about proxy goals or specification gaming, which defenders can absorb as imperfect attempts at intent alignment. Specifically: what conceptual analyses, formal results, or concrete design scenarios would demonstrate that specifying an intent necessarily embeds value judgments—about which observable proxies are legitimate, which side effects are tolerable, or how ambiguous instructions should be interpreted—so that the boundary collapses? Rank these defeaters from fatal to merely awkward. Which ones would a committed defender concede as damaging to the conceptual distinction rather than just technically difficult? And has the view been qualified into unfalsifiability—if every case where intent and value seem entangled is reclassified as a botched specification—such that it no longer risks anything, and is that insulation a problem for a framing of this kind?
