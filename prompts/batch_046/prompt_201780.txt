Me and my mate had this massive arguement last night about biopics and whether inventing scenes for dead people is just disrespectful or if it's something more specific. I tried to argue it's not only about getting facts wrong — there's a real difference between making up a private conversation someone had in a kitchen and making up a public decision they made in parliament — but I definately tied myself in knots and now I want to sort it out properly.

I'm writing a short research proposal for a six-month independent project (probably about 8,000–10,000 words, part-time) on what we actually owe real dead people when we dramatise them in historical fiction or biopics. The precise question: is the wrong done by such dramatization best understood as a single kind of harm to the dead, or should we split it into two distinct categories — counterfactual intimacy (inventing private emotional, domestic, or bodily moments) versus counterfactual agency (inventing public actions, speeches, or decisions they never made)? My hunch is that existing debates collapse these together under vague ideas of dignity or epistemic harm, but they pull in opposite directions. Intimacy violations might wrong the dead directly by violating a kind of posthumous privacy, while agency violations mainly harm living audiences by corrupting the historical record they depend on. If the split holds, then some biopics are guilty of one wrong but not the other, which changes how we should judge them.

Why current answers leave it open: the dignity camp says any fabrication is an affront to the dead person's memory, while the epistemic-harm camp says the problem is misleading the audience. But neither camp explains why inventing a private tears-on-a-pillow moment feels different from inventing a vote cast in a council meeting. Also, nobody asks clearly whether the obligation changes when there are living descendants or cultural custodians with a claim, versus figures with no living stakeholders.

Approach: Month one, conceptual mapping — I'll define counterfactual intimacy and counterfactual agency precisely, and test the boundary with edge cases like invented moments that are historically plausible but unverified, or invented actions that are out of character but publicly documented as false. Months two and three, apply the framework to three paired case studies: one where only intimacy is invented, one where only agency is, and one where both occur, tracking whether our moral intuitions actually track the split or collapse back into a single judgement. Month four, analyse whether direct wronging of the dead is coherent here, or whether all harm is mediated through living people (audiences, descendants, historians). Month five, draft the framework and check for internal consistency. Month six, final write-up. The deliverable should be the analytical essay plus a short diagnostic checklist — not for lawyers, but for writers and dramatists — asking which kind of counterfactual they're using and whether they can justify it.

What counts as success: the distinction proves stable across cases and explains why some dramatizations feel creepy while others feel merely inaccurate. What counts as failure: the distinction collapses (every invented private moment turns out to be an agency violation in disguise, or vice versa), or direct wronging of the dead turns out to be incoherent and the whole framing has to be abandoned in favour of pure audience-harm.

Scope and risks: Six months part-time is realistic for conceptual work with three case studies, but I'm limiting myself to Western historical fiction and English-language biopics; I won't claim universal cross-cultural validity. There's a risk the project gets too big if I try to cover every theory of posthumous harm, so I'll stick to the conceptual split and flag other traditions briefly without fully engaging them.
