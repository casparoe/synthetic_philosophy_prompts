Question: Some philosophers have proposed that moral status should track the absolute number of cortical neurons rather than brain-to-body mass ratios. Assess this proposal with reference to three cases: (1) an adult human with macrocephaly (brain mass approximately 1.8 kg), (2) a bottlenose dolphin (brain mass approximately 1.6 kg with a high encephalization quotient), and (3) a synthetic layered cerebral organoid containing roughly two billion neurons arranged in cortical-like architecture but lacking sensory afference, motor efference, or integrated behavioral output. Does brain size, in any of these metrics, supply a non-arbitrary boundary for full moral status? In your response, clearly distinguish capacity-based accounts—which allow graded degrees of moral consideration—from boundary-based accounts—which require a categorical threshold.

Student answer:

Absolute brain mass is an unreliable indicator, since elephants possess brains weighing over five kilograms yet do not intuitively command greater moral status than humans. The encephalization quotient corrects for body size, and dolphins score higher than humans on this measure, which implies that if neural complexity is the relevant factor they should possess equal or greater moral standing. A boundary-based account holds that moral status is a continuous function of neural complexity, with no sharp cutoff, so both the macrocephalic human and the dolphin fall on a gradient rather than being inside or outside a single category. By contrast, capacity-based theorists insist on a binary line—typically drawn at the species boundary—so they would reject both the dolphin and the synthetic organoid regardless of neuron count. The organoid, with about two billion neurons, sits between a rat and a human on this scale, so a consistent capacity-based view should assign it intermediate moral status: partial protection but not full rights. Therefore, brain size fails to provide a non-arbitrary threshold only if we mistakenly assume moral status must be binary; once we accept gradation, neuron count gives us a perfectly reasonable ordering.
