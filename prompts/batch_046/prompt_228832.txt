I'm co-teaching the final session of a graduate seminar on G. E. Moore's ethical writings, and the students have become stuck on a tension we kept circling but never resolved. Throughout the term we've treated Principia Ethica's account of intrinsic value as centered on the idea of organic unity: the value of a beautiful object is not reducible to the pleasure it causes but inheres in the complex internal relation of its parts. But when we turned to Moore's claim that personal affection and friendship rank among the greatest goods, several students noticed that friendship seems structurally different—unlike a landscape or a musical work, it requires mutual awareness and emotional reciprocity to exist at all, which makes it hard to see how its value can be entirely independent of mental states in the way Moore demands for genuinely non-hedonic goods. For the closing, I want a sonnet—fourteen lines, roughly iambic pentameter, with a clear volta at or near line 9—that opens with an image of beauty valued as an organic whole whose parts relate without reference to any observer's consciousness, then turns to ask whether friendship can share that same structure, or whether the presence of another mind introduces a dependency that pulls interpersonal value away from the aesthetic model. The closing couplet should leave the tension open rather than resolving it into either pure organic unity or a covert hedonism, since that is precisely where our discussion landed. The poem should be rendered precisely enough that someone familiar with Moore's ethics recognizes the specific problem—the possible mismatch between the structure of aesthetic and interpersonal value—rather than finding only the philosopher's name dropped into verse.

Please do not include bibliographic references, page citations, or direct quotations from Principia Ethica; render the concepts in your own language so that a reader without access to the text can still follow the argument being staged. I am not asking for a summary of Moore's view, but for a poem in which the formal turn of the sonnet itself enacts the philosophical pivot we've been debating: from an object whose value seems to sit in its parts alone, to a relation that may require consciousness to be what it is.
