I've been reading about superrationality since my roommate got obsessed with Newcomb's problem last week, and now I've built an argument that feels like it proves rational people should never compete. Here are the steps:

(1) In the Platonia dilemma, N agents with identical reasoning treat their choices as perfectly correlated, so they all pick B and each get $1,000.
(2) The Luring Lottery is structurally the same: a predictor scans your reasoning. If it predicts you'll buy a ticket, you win $10 but 100 strangers each lose $5; if it predicts abstainence, nothing changes. So if your reasoning leads to buying, every agent with the same reasoning also buys, triggering collective harm.
(3) So superrationality requires abstaining, since you recognize that "my reasoning buys" means "all identical reasoners buy."
(4) But this applies everywhere people share reasoning: if I cheat on an exam, others like me cheat too, lowering the curve and hurting us all, so I shouldn't.
(5) Same for prisoner's dilemmas, stock trades that depend on herd behavior, or buying gas—anywhere my choice correlates with others'.
(6) Therefore superrationality collapses rational choice into pure collective altruism, which can't be right.
(7) So either the Platonia solution is wrong, or superrationality is useless outside artificial puzzles.

I think the slip is somewhere between (2) and (4)—maybe the predictor makes the correllation causal, not logical?—but I can't name it. Which step do I reject, and what's the cost? Keep your answer in American English, under 250 words, in one or two paragraphs max.
