A philosophy-of-science seminar classifies long-run predictive theories that initially seem falsified into three categories: R, rescued as "right but early" when a contingent delay masks an intact core mechanism; F, falsified when structural substitutes replace the original causal base; and M, mechanism-substituted rescue, where defenders preserve the theory only by altering its causal structure. Their rulings: on Malthus's population-food argument they selected R, holding that agricultural innovation postponed but did not dissolve the geometric-arithmetic tension; on Jevons's thesis that coal exhaustion would halt industrial growth they selected F, since petroleum and electricity replaced coal's structural role rather than merely delaying depletion; on Ehrlich's 1960s warning of imminent famine they selected R, treating the Green Revolution as a contingent technological postponement; and on a 1990s claim that internet-driven information overload would collapse democratic deliberation by 2010 they selected M, because proponents replaced the original cognitive-overload mechanism with algorithmic-curation dynamics. Now consider a 2022 argument predicting that climate-induced agricultural contraction will trigger a Malthusian labor-population crisis by 2070, with vertical farming and synthetic proteins eventually resolving the bottleneck while leaving the underlying land-labor feedback loop intact, merely postponed by about eight decades. Should the seminar classify this as R, F, or M? Lay out the competing interpretive rules that fit their prior four rulings equally well—especially whether a technological substitute that removes land from production counts as postponement or structural replacement—predict the seminar's best-consistent verdict, and specify one further hypothetical case that would force a choice between those rules.
