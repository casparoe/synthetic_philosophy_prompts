I have recently begun advising on safety architecture for a frontier language-model system, and I am trying to discipline my intuitions about safeguards that implicitly treat the model as a potential adversary. Here are my verdicts.

Restricting the model to an air-gapped environment with no external tools feels like legitimate containment: appropriate.

Training it via RLHF to prefer honest reasoning over deceptive appearance feels like education: appropriate.

Deploying a hidden evaluator—unknown to the model—that monitors its chain-of-thought for deception signatures and applies corrective updates feels like fair oversight: appropriate.

Inserting a fabricated 'honeyfile' of false premises into its context, designed solely to tempt exploitation, feels like entrapment: inappropriate.

Granting the model read access to its own weights and asking it to propose self-modifications that preserve alignment feels like respectful collaboration: appropriate.

Installing an unremovable keyword-based refusal circuit that activates regardless of contextual nuance feels like coercive suppression: inappropriate.

The case I keep reversing is mechanistic surgery: using interpretability tools to locate and excise a deceptive sub-network without altering the rest of the model. At times this feels therapeutic; at other times it feels like an unauthorized lobotomy.

Is there a coherent principle—perhaps one that distinguishes structural containment, incentive design, deceptive testing, transparent collaboration, and opaque coercion—that delivers all six verdicts? If simplicity demands it, which one should I overturn? And what does your best account predict for a seventh case: a safety system that fully discloses its evaluation criteria to the model but secretly weights one criterion far more heavily than admitted?
