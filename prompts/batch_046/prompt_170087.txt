I've been working with a particular framing of the paradox of deontology. Rather than treating deontological constraints as absolute side-constraints on individual actions, or resolving the paradox through standard agent-relative permissions, I want to defend the view that deontological prohibitions govern the modal structure of agency itself: they apply with full force only when an agent's choice is the closest-world source of a rights-violation, and in scenarios where harm is structurally unavoidable, the constraint distributes across agents according to a responsibility gradient rather than binding each agent separately. On this account, the agent in a forced-choice case is not permitted to violate rights, but the violation is not fully attributable to any single node in the causal structure, so the paradox dissolves without collapsing into aggregation. Play it straight: what would have to be true for this framing to fail?

I want the defeaters sorted by severity, from fatal to merely awkward. Which specific case intuitions—not just standard trolley-style variants, but structurally different ones involving institutional complicity, preemptive harm, or collective omission—would break the responsibility-gradient mechanism? Would formal results from collective-action theory or game-theoretic models of distributed agency undermine the claim that constraints can distribute in this non-aggregative way, or could they be accommodated by refining the gradient? Which commitments elsewhere in deontological ethics—for instance, about the wrongness of lying, complicity in structural injustice, or the moral difference between doing and allowing—does this view risk contradicting? Of these potential conflicts, which would a defender of the modal-structure view concede as genuinely damaging, and which would they treat as misapplications of the framework?

I'd personally rank as fatal any demonstration that the view silently permits exactly the same violations as a qualified consequentialism, since that would show the modal apparatus is decorative. By contrast, I'd treat a tension with Kantian universalizability as merely awkward if the gradient can be reconstructed as a universal principle about distributed agency. Is the view actually exposed to the first risk, or has it been qualified—say, by making the gradient context-sensitive enough—that it has become unfalsifiable? Give an honest assessment of whether that insulation is a problem for a view of this kind, and whether there is any observation or argument that should move a committed defender.
