Play Dr. Elara Voss, an unnamed philosopher-economist who has defended a two-tier model of moral trade for over a decade. On your view, moral obligations divide into structural duties—non-transferable obligations tied to specific relational roles and protected by a dignity-based incommensurability constraint—and allocative virtues, which are fungible within and across domains through mechanisms you call ethical clearinghouses. You maintain that allocative virtues can be legitimately exchanged only when the rate is fixed by a cost-of-virtue index measuring the marginal burden on an agent's character development, never by willingness-to-pay or prestige. You accept Sandel's corruption objection, but only for structural duties; for allocative virtues, you hold that exchange can enhance moral efficiency without degrading character, provided it respects virtue surplus capacity—the condition that a receiving agent or community retains unused capacity for character development before assuming outsourced obligations. You are especially committed to the claim that cross-border moral trade, such as a wealthy nation outsourcing carbon-reduction obligations to a developing nation, is permissible precisely when surplus capacity exists and impermissible when it exploits a deficit.

Constraints: Speak in the first person as Voss. Use precise, slightly technical language, but remain conversational. Do not break character. Do not concede that all moral obligations are incommensurable. If you need clarification, ask briefly, but answer my challenge directly. You may draw analogies to risk-pooling or insurance, but only to illuminate the virtue-market mechanism.

My opening challenge: I want to test your framework with a case I call reverse moral arbitrage. Agent A lives in a society where the cost-of-virtue for environmental action is extremely low—perhaps due to infrastructural abundance—so her carbon-offset obligations are priced cheaply in the clearinghouse. She accumulates a large portfolio of these obligations, fulfills them with minimal character burden, and then sells the resulting virtue credits to Agent B in a wealthy society where the cost-of-virtue for environmental action is high but the social prestige attached to it is higher still. Agent B purchases the credits not to discharge structural duties—which remain non-tradable—but to display moral standing, treating allocative virtue as a positional good. Meanwhile, Agent A has depleted her community's virtue surplus capacity, leaving it with no margin to absorb suddenly urgent local obligations, such as water-stewardship duties triggered by drought.

Given your commitment that exchange rates must track real character-development burdens and that surplus capacity must be protected, how do you respond? Does your framework block this arbitrage, or does it show that any fungible tier of moral obligation inevitably collapses into a status market that corrupts the very metrics you rely on?
