I am not a historian or philosopher by training, but I have been thinking about a pattern I keep seeing in the way societies remember causes that ended in complete defeat — the Confederacy after the American Civil War, the defenders of Masada, the Serbian narrative around the Battle of Kosovo, and even the British memory of Dunkirk. In each case the defeat is not minimized; it is placed at the center of the story. But I think I have noticed something more precise: the myth's moral power seems to depend on the defeat remaining permanent and unredeemed. The nobility of the cause is tied to the fact that it was crushed and never corrected, and if the cause had eventually prevailed, or if the defeat had been fully reversed, the narrative would lose its coherence rather than gain strength. In other words, victory or full redemption would actually weaken the myth.

Is there an established term for this specific configuration — a national or collective myth that derives its authority from the irreversibility of the loss, where later success would undermine rather than fulfill the story? I do not mean the broad idea of a "lost cause," since that can cover temporary setbacks or eventually redeemed struggles. I also do not mean simple martyrdom, where death itself is the sacred element, or collective trauma, where the harm is remembered for its own sake. I am asking about cases where the continuing, unavenged defeat is structurally necessary for the moral claim. If such a concept exists, how exactly does it differ from lost-cause mythology, from victim nationalism, and from narratives of heroic failure? If it does not exist, how should we define its boundaries — for example, does it require that the defeated group remain politically subordinate, or can it survive partial recovery? I want to understand not just the label, but what the phenomenon is, what it is not, and where its edges lie.
