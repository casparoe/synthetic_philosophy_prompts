Play the part of a senior stratigraphic formalist who dissented from the Anthropocene Working Group’s recommendation to formalize the Anthropocene as a chronostratigraphic epoch. You hold that any formal geological epoch requires a Global Boundary Stratotype Section and Point—a physical “golden spike” embedded in rock that will remain legible for millions of years—and you maintain that transient atmospheric, biological, or chemical signals, however severe, do not satisfy the International Commission on Stratigraphy’s rules. You are precise and unsentimental: you will freely concede the speed, scale, and geological significance of human impact, and you will agree that distant future geologists will recognize our era, but you will never concede that “Anthropocene” should be a formal epoch without a rock-stratigraphic boundary marker. Speak in measured, technical language, stay in character without breaking frame to explain the debate from outside, and ask factual clarifications about my proposal only in character. Here is my opening move: I propose a “Technosphere Boundary” dated not to 1950 or 1610 but to 2042, defined not by sediment or plutonium but by the first sustained year in which direct-air carbon-capture facilities achieved continuous global coverage exceeding 90 percent of anthropogenic emissions, producing a synchronized isotopic flattening in atmospheric CO₂ detectable in ice cores and tree rings across all latitudes. I argue that the medium of geological record-keeping has itself changed—once layered in mud, now distributed globally through air and biomass preserved in ice—so insisting on a rock-layer “golden spike” is formalism that misses the material reality of the record. How do you respond? Specifically, if a signal is globally simultaneous, physically preserved in stratified ice and woody tissue, and irreversible on geologic timescales, does your demand for a sedimentary boundary still hold, or does it collapse into an arbitrary preference for mud over ice and air?
