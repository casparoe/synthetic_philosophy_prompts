I'm a doctoral student in political philosophy preparing a workshop paper on institutional applications of social-choice impossibilities. I have constructed the following argument extending Sen's minimal-liberalism paradox, but I am concerned that it collapses into an absurd conclusion and that I am masking a subtle scope shift or equivocation.

(1) Sen's result demonstrates that minimal liberalism—the assignment to each individual of decisive authority over at least one pair of alternatives involving only that individual—cannot coexist with the Pareto principle whenever preferences are interdependent.

(2) In any real democratic institution, citizens hold interdependent preferences not only over binary personal choices but over the constitutional rules that define those choices: for example, a citizen may prefer that she herself be permitted to publish controversial material, while also preferring that others be restricted from publishing material she finds harmful.

(3) If minimal liberal rights are institutionalized as constitutional protections—say, freedom of expression—then the domain of each citizen's sovereignty is not a simple binary option but a rule-governing framework within which multiple citizens' rights interact.

(4) Applying Sen's framework to this institutional level, the interdependence of preferences over constitutional rules means that minimal liberalism must conflict with Pareto-optimal democratic decision-making at the constitutional level itself.

(5) Therefore, any democratic constitution that genuinely entrenches minimal liberal rights for all citizens is necessarily incapable of achieving unanimous democratic improvements: it must either violate some citizens' sovereign rights or block some Pareto-superior institutional reforms.

(6) But constitutional democracies routinely enact unanimous or near-unanimous amendments—expansions of suffrage, abolition of property qualifications—without surrendering entrenched rights.

(7) Hence, either minimal liberalism is incompatible with constitutional democracy, or one of the premises above misapplies Sen's binary model to complex institutional structures.

I want to defend the conclusion that the paradox is dissolved—not that democracy is impossible—by arguing that constitutional rules decouple citizens' first-order rights claims from their second-order strategic preferences over others' rights, thereby breaking the interdependence condition. Before I present this, I need to know precisely where my argument slips. Is (2) wrong to conflate personal-choice interdependence with preference interdependence over institutional rules? Is (3) wrong to treat constitutional rights as falling within Sen's personal-choice domain? Or is there a hidden quantifier shift between (4) and (5) that illegitimately moves from binary pairs to multi-agent constitutional domains? I am particularly worried that I am committing an equivocation between interdependence of preferences in Sen's original two-person model and interdependence of rights-claims in institutional design, since my positive thesis depends on denying the latter. Which premise should I reject, what does rejecting it cost my claim about preference-decoupling, and how should I defend the weakest point in my own position if the workshop audience includes social-choice theorists familiar with the standard derivations?
