I have just spent the better part of an evening arguing with a close friend about the long standoff between democratic egalitarianism and epistocratic proposals for weighted or restricted suffrage. We kept circling the same points: she insisted that universal suffrage expresses a non-instrumental status equality that cannot be traded for marginal gains in policy accuracy, while I countered that if competence varies systematically with education or information access, then treating every vote equally is not fairness but arbitrary noise amplification. By the end we had not moved each other, and I am now unsure whether we were genuinely disagreeing about facts, about values, or about what the word competence is even doing in the argument.

My working hypothesis is that the debate has stalled because both sides run together two distinct dimensions of political competence—call them predictive calibration about empirical outcomes and normative coherence about value trade-offs—without noticing that each camp privileges one dimension and then declares victory on that axis. The democratic side, I think, implicitly treats normative coherence as either uniformly distributed or not the proper business of voting, and therefore collapses competence into predictive calibration, where evidence of voter ignorance looks devastating. The epistocratic side, conversely, treats predictive calibration as the only measurable kind of competence, and then argues that weighting votes improves aggregate accuracy, but rarely addresses whether the same weighting would distort normative coherence by empowering a homogeneous elite with convergent value structures.

So my question is not which side is right, but what kind of disagreement this actually is. Is it a hidden empirical dispute about whether voter ignorance is remediable through deliberation, or about whether weighted voting genuinely improves outcomes? Is it a normative disagreement about whether political equality is a deontological constraint or an instrumental heuristic? Or is it partly verbal—are the sides answering different questions under the single heading of who should rule—with one asking about the legitimacy of authority and the other asking about the efficiency of decision-making? And is there a deeper ill-posedness: does the very framing of competence as an individual scalar property misrepresent democratic decision-making as an aggregation of private opinions rather than a distributed process of collective reasoning?

I want an honest diagnosis, not a survey of the familiar literature. Tell me what each side actually commits itself to when it invokes competence or equality, whether there is a formal or empirical result that could settle the dispute if the diagnosis is substantive, or whether the impasse is structural—perhaps because the two dimensions of competence I have sketched are genuinely in tension and cannot be jointly maximised by any institutional design. If the disagreement is partly about starting intuitions that neither side can argue for, say so. If a conceptual distinction—say, between instrumental and expressive justifications of democracy—would dissolve the stalemate, propose it and explain why. And be direct about whether anything, short of abandoning one of the dimensions altogether, could actually end the argument, or whether my friend and I were destined to talk past each other from the outset.
