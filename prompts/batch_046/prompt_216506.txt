I work as a senior policy analyst for a regional social-insurance board that has recently adopted a broadly luck-egalitarian compensation framework: we cover long-term disadvantages that trace to brute luck but deny coverage for those that trace to option luck. In practice, however, we are overwhelmed by what I am calling compound-luck cases, in which a single adverse outcome—chronic illness, disability, or severe economic loss—appears to be jointly produced by an unchosen factor (a genetic predisposition, an environmental exposure outside the agent’s control) and a sequence of occupational or lifestyle choices made with imperfect but non-negligible information. Because our reviewers are not philosophers, I need a reusable, step-by-step decision procedure that can be applied repeatedly without requiring theoretical expertise at every desk. The procedure should be structured as an ordered set of tests with clear stopping rules: at each stage, specify what counts as a sufficient answer, identify the exact node where the board must issue a denial versus approve compensation, and flag the nodes where judgment cannot be fully mechanized and the file must be escalated to an ethics reviewer. Crucially, the procedure must include a decomposition stage that isolates the counterfactual contribution of the unchosen factor from that of the chosen factor, using only data available in standard administrative records—employment histories, medical diagnostics, and exposure reports—without speculative psychological profiling or counterfactual simulations of personal identity.

Please construct this procedure in full, then apply it to two worked examples. The first is a warehouse worker who developed severe asthma after a decade of voluntary overtime in a documented dusty environment; the worker knew dust was present but did not know, and could not reasonably have discovered, that a particular genotype made them hyper-susceptible to occupational particulate exposure. The second is a cyclist who suffered permanent nerve damage after choosing to ride without a helmet on a well-marked high-traffic route, but who was born with a rare congenital condition that made any head trauma disproportionately severe; the cyclist knew the traffic risk but was unaware of the congenital vulnerability until after the injury. For each case, show precisely where the procedure lands, identify which feature of the case does the classificatory work, explain what verdict changes if that feature is weakened or removed, and present the strongest objection a critic of luck egalitarianism—someone who holds that the brute-option distinction is morally irrelevant to distributive justice—would raise against the procedure’s design at that specific node.

Finish with an honest assessment of one structural misfire the procedure is guaranteed to produce: a class of compound-luck cases where the decomposition step yields ambiguous results regardless of how the administrative record is interpreted, and say what institutional safeguard should be built around that failure mode. The entire deliverable should be suitable for direct insertion into an internal training manual, with no appeal to external literature, and should avoid treating the brute-option boundary as self-evident at any point.
