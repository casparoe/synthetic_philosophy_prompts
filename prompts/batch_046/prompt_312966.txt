I was up until nearly two in the morning arguing with a friend about Mary’s room and whether Jackson’s knowledge argument actually lands a blow against physicalism or just confuses epistemic access with ontological novelty. She kept insisting that any adequate theory of phenomenal knowledge only needs to explain why Mary learns something upon seeing red, but that strikes me as far too thin—there are boundary cases and internal variants that any serious account has to survive, and I want the full specification before I read another defense or rebuttal. So I am asking for a rigorous list of desiderata: what must a theory of phenomenal concept acquisition actually deliver in order to count as adequate?

The requirements need to cover the canonical case—Mary, raised in a black-and-white environment, who knows all physical facts about color vision and then experiences red for the first time—specifying whether her gain is best understood as propositional knowledge, an ability, direct acquaintance, or a conceptual revision, and which distinctions the theory must preserve rather than collapse, such as knowing-that versus knowing-what-it-is-like, or perceptual change versus representational restructuring. But the list also has to handle two stress-test variants that keep getting ignored: the inverse-Mary, a scientist born with normal color experience who is gradually given exhaustive physical knowledge of color vision and reports that her phenomenal concepts have been “displaced” or translated into structural ones; and the synthetic-Mary, an artificial system that runs a perfect neural simulation of Mary’s brain upon “release” and produces first-person reports of color experience without any biological retina or evolutionary history. For each desideratum, state the motivating case or consideration behind it, and flag any pairs you suspect are jointly unsatisfiable—particularly whether a theory can both maintain a robust physicalist ontology and grant Mary genuinely new knowledge without collapsing into property dualism or eliminativism about phenomenal states.

Finally, I want to know which single requirement does the most damage to the standard competing responses—the ability hypothesis, the acquaintance-theoretic account, and the phenomenal-concept strategy—without asking for a full scorecard, just identifying the knockout condition that eliminates the weakest contender. Keep the weight on the criteria and the cases that motivate them rather than on a survey of the literature; a table organizing the requirements is perfectly fine. I am not looking for bibliographies or reading lists—just the conceptual architecture that any answer in this space actually has to fit.
