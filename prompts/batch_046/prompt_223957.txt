I've been trying to understand karma not as a vague "what goes around comes around" idea, but specifically as a theory of moral desert — the claim that people eventually receive exactly the suffering or flourishing they deserve, distributed across lifetimes if necessary. A friend who reads philosophy of religion summarized the view for me in these terms: "Karma is essentially a universal moral accounting system. Every intentional action deposits either merit or demerit in a cosmic ledger, and the total is never lost; if the results don't arrive in this life, rebirth places you in circumstances that precisely match your net moral balance. Because the system is fully deterministic and tracks even hidden intentions, it eliminates moral luck altogether — good people never suffer undeservedly, and wrongdoers never escape. In that respect it outperforms Western retributivism, which can only punish visible crimes once and has no mechanism for hidden wrongs or posthumous justice. The metaphysics of rebirth is really just the delivery mechanism; strip that away, and the core claim remains that moral desert must eventually be satisfied in full."

I suspect this is too neat, and that different traditions treat the accounting in very different ways — some emphasizing mental intention over outward act, others allowing for grace or ritual intervention to alter the ledger, and still others rejecting any quantitative balance-sheet model. But I am unsure where the line falls between an accurate philosophical reconstruction and a popular distortion. Could you audit this summary carefully? Specifically: which claims about determinism, quantitative moral balance, and the complete elimination of moral luck would a careful defender of karma as a desert-theory actually accept? Where does the summary conflate doctrines that the traditions keep distinct — for instance, causal law versus normative desert, or Buddhist versus Hindu frameworks? Is the comparison to Western retributivism fair to both sides, or does it misrepresent what retributivists claim about hidden crimes and proportionality? And does the summary's conclusion — that karma succeeds as a theory of moral desert — depend on the parts that are misrepresentations, or can a corrected, more nuanced version still deliver what the summary promises?
