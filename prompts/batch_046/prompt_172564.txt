Write the final exam question, model answer, and marking scheme for an upper-level seminar in archaeological epistemology, aimed at students who can summarize the basics of material-culture inference but must now work through its philosophical crux. Present a hypothetical late-prehistoric settlement assemblage: standardized subterranean storage pits of uniform capacity; ceramic weights calibrated to a common measure; communal feasting debris with high-value imported goods distributed across all residential sectors; burial contexts that lack personal ornamentation but contain shared ritual vessels. Ask students to adjudicate between two competing institutional reconstructions—(A) a centralized, egalitarian redistributive economy governed by cosmological beliefs about communal obligation, and (B) a competitive, hierarchical feasting system in which status is negotiated through temporary surplus accumulation and ritual display. Require that they do not merely describe both options, but articulate the specific inferential steps that justify attributing beliefs (about obligation, fairness, or cosmic order) to the inhabitants rather than only describing the functional economy of the objects. They must identify at least one “silent practice”—a belief-laden institution likely to leave little or no durable trace—and explain how its presence or absence asymmetrically affects the evaluation of A versus B. They must also address the so-called Pompeii premise and the risk of analogical overreach, without invoking specific named ethnographies as direct parallels. The answer should be an essay of about 700 words, written for 90 minutes.

Provide a model answer that demonstrates the central distinction: material correlation is not institutional causation; formal properties of the assemblage (standardization, spatial distribution, taphonomic bias) can constrain belief-attribution only through explicit linking arguments; the absence of ornamentation does not entail egalitarian belief, because hierarchical systems may suppress durable status markers in mortuary contexts; and a silent practice—such as oral covenant or temporary ritual purification—could leave the storage and feasting record intact while altering the institutional meaning entirely, so that reconstruction B is not eliminated but is made more costly in evidential terms.

Then provide a marking scheme out of 20 marks that separates an A answer (16 or above) from a B answer (13–15) by naming the specific moves each must contain. Include: correct identification of the functional ambiguity of standardized storage and weights (3 marks); explicit rejection of direct belief-to-object mapping without mediating linking arguments (4 marks); incorporation of the silent-practice problem and its asymmetrical effect on reconstructions A and B (4 marks); critical evaluation of analogical reasoning without relying on named ethnographic parallels (3 marks); a coherent adjudication that uses evidential asymmetry rather than collapsing into “we cannot know” (4 marks); and philosophical precision and clarity (2 marks). For each criterion, add a one-line note on the typical B-level failure—for example, describing both reconstructions clearly but treating them as equally plausible because “the archaeological evidence is ambiguous,” or mentioning silent practices without showing how they differentially weaken one reconstruction. Keep the scheme usable for a grader working without external references.
