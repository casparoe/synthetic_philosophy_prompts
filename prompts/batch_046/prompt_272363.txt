I constructed the following argument for a seminar on Glaucon's challenge, and I now distrust it because the conclusion seems to swallow the phenomenon it was meant to explain.

(1) Justice is instrumentally valuable only: its sole point is to secure the rewards of reputation and avoid the penalties of being caught acting unjustly.

(2) The ring of Gyges grants perfect invisibility; with it, an agent can commit injustice without any risk of detection, so a rational ring-bearer should be unjust.

(3) Perfect invisibility is only the extreme case of partial invisibility. For any unjust act in an actual society, there exists some feasible strategy—relocation, pseudonymity, encryption, or temporal separation—that reduces the risk of detection arbitrarily close to zero at some finite cost.

(4) A rational agent maximizes expected utility. If B is the benefit of an unjust act, P the probability of detection given the evasion strategy, L the loss if caught, and C the cost of the strategy, then injustice is rational whenever B exceeds C plus P multiplied by L.

(5) Because P can be driven arbitrarily close to zero and B, C, and L are finite, for every unjust act there exists a feasible strategy satisfying the inequality in (4).

(6) So for every particular injustice available to an agent, there is a rational, available path to committing it while bearing essentially none of the reputational costs that make justice attractive.

(7) From this it follows that no agent ever has an instrumental reason to refrain from any specific injustice, since the requisite evasion strategy always exists.

(8) But if justice is merely the disposition to refrain from injustice across contexts, and no particular refraining is ever instrumentally justified, then justice itself has no instrumental value; it is an unnecessary self-constraint.

(9) The argument therefore applies not only to the mythical ring-bearer but to everyone: even in ordinary face-to-face dealings, one could have relocated or anonymized in advance, so justice is never rational.

(10) This is absurd—among other things, it implies that moral education systematically harms those who receive it, because it instills a disposition that is always rationally dispensable.

If I have to choose, I would rather preserve (1) than reject (9), but I want to know exactly which step slips and what preserving (1) forces me to pay. Please diagnose the argument: name the step, explain why it looked fine, and price the alternatives, including what contractarian, virtue-theoretic, or Kantian replies each have to concede if they take my preferred route. And please be direct: if my framing of Glaucon's challenge rests on a confused premise, or if the task as posed can't be done well because the argument is actually valid and the cost must fall on (1) after all, say so plainly rather than accommodating me politely.
