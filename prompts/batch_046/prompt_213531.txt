I'm taking a graduate seminar on philosophy of mind, and we've been working through a version of the epiphenomenalism paradox that the instructor referred to only in passing—something she called "judgmental mirroring," though she never developed it in the readings. The argument I reconstructed goes like this. (1) If epiphenomenalism is true, phenomenal properties have no causal efficacy over physical processes, including the neural dynamics that produce verbal reports and conceptual judgments. (2) If phenomenal properties lack causal efficacy over judgment-production, then the content of our phenomenal judgments is not causally determined by the phenomenal properties they purport to describe. (3) But if judgment-content is not causally determined by the property it targets, then no non-accidental connection can link them: any correlation must be either brute coincidence or mediated entirely by physical causes that are phenomenologically blind. (4) Therefore, on epiphenomenalism, our phenomenal judgments are systematically ungrounded—we could have produced exactly the same reports had our phenomenal lives been inverted, absent, or replaced by different qualities altogether. (5) If our judgments could remain unchanged regardless of the phenomenal facts, then we possess no epistemic warrant for treating them as accurate descriptions of those facts; they become causally inert byproducts whose truth, if any, is purely fortuitous. (6) So epiphenomenalism entails that we lack justification for our own phenomenal reports, which removes the evidential basis for positing phenomenal properties in the first place. (7) Hence the view is self-defeating: it protects a phenomenal realm only by destroying our reason to believe it exists.

A colleague in the seminar thinks the slip occurs between (3) and (4), suggesting that evolutionary tuning or structural isomorphism could preserve reliability without direct causal determination—though I worry that importing any such bridge collapses epiphenomenalism back into interactionism. Another possibility is that the argument overreaches at (5) by conflating the absence of causal grounding with the absence of all justification, when in fact first-person authority might rest on a different epistemic basis altogether. I want to know precisely which step fails, what hidden equivocation or scope shift makes it look valid, and what rejecting that step costs—whether it forces me into phenomenal skepticism, into a qualified interactionism, or into accepting that the argument applies with equal destructive force to other domains like mathematical intuition or instrument-mediated physical measurement. If the argument is genuinely valid, then I need to know which premise is least costly to surrender and whether doing so leaves any coherent form of epiphenomenalism standing.
