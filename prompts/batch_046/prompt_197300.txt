I have recently come to see psychiatric diagnosis as something that reshapes not just my future conduct but my past—how I read old memories, relationships, and failures—through the lens of a classification I did not choose. While the looping-effects framework associated with Ian Hacking explains how being classified changes the kind of person one becomes, I think it misses a distinct temporal harm that deserves its own conceptual treatment. I want you to argue for the position that psychiatric classification produces what we should call retrospective looping: a diachronic reinterpretation of one's life narrative that is structurally different from the prospective looping that shapes future behavior. Specifically, defend the claim that retrospective looping undermines autobiographical sovereignty—the authority to determine which past events count as symptoms and which count as ordinary life—without reducing this harm to familiar categories like testimonial injustice or hermeneutical injustice. Your argument should explain why this is a distinct epistemic wrong that operates across time rather than within a single clinical encounter. You should also address the objection that all medical diagnosis invites some retrospective reinterpretation (for instance, recognizing that chronic fatigue was anemia), and show why psychiatric retrospective looping is qualitatively different in ways that justify special institutional or moral safeguards. Do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; build the case conceptually, extending the looping-effects framework into this under-theorized narrative and temporal dimension.
