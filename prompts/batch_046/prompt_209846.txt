Read the 2016 film Arrival through Quine’s thesis of the indeterminacy of translation and ontological relativity, rather than through the usual Sapir-Whorf lens that dominates popular discussion of the movie. The linguist Louise Banks decodes an alien language not by matching sounds to pre-given concepts but by negotiating shared behavioral responses with the heptapods, and the narrative treats this process as unlocking a deterministic, circular experience of time. I want to use this for a philosophy-of-language discussion that carefully separates Quine’s claim—that translation manuals are radically underdetermined by all possible behavioral evidence—from linguistic determinism, which holds that language shapes cognition within a fixed ontology. Where does the film’s depiction of translation actually illustrate Quine’s point that multiple incompatible manuals can be equally consistent with every observable verbal and non-verbal response? Does the heptapod language’s restructuring of Louise’s temporal cognition support Quine’s further claim of ontological relativity, that adopting one manual over another can shift the ontology one is committed to, or does the film instead presuppose a single correct ontology—time as an unchanging four-dimensional block—that the language merely reveals to her? Take both the film’s narrative logic and Quine’s actual argument seriously: point to specific scenes, such as Louise’s progressive abandonment of linear narrative in her flash-forwards, or the military’s insistence on a definitive translation of a symbol that might mean either “weapon” or “tool,” and say whether they dramatize the underdetermination Quine describes or whether they quietly assume a correct translation that Quine’s thesis denies. I am teaching this to an upper-level seminar, so I want the reading to be faithful in both directions: do not reduce the film to an illustration of Word and Object, and do not distort Quine’s position to fit the plot. End by saying whether the film, despite itself, offers something to the philosophical debate about radical translation that standard textbook examples—such as the famous “gavagai” scenario—tend to miss, perhaps about the emotional or existential cost of genuinely accepting that no translation manual is uniquely correct.
