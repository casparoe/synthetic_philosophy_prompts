The exam prompt was: "In Cicero's dialogue on old age, the speaker Cato addresses four standard objections to old age. First, identify these four objections. Second, explain Cato's specific rebuttal to the charge that old age necessarily removes a person from meaningful public affairs. Third, show how this rebuttal connects to the Stoic conception of virtue as rational activity according to nature, and distinguish this Stoic framing from an Epicurean justification based on the tranquility of withdrawal."

The student's answer:

"The four objections are: (1) old age takes us away from active business and public life; (2) it weakens the body and makes physical labor impossible; (3) it deprives us of nearly all sensual pleasures; and (4) it brings us close to death, which is naturally feared.

Cato answers the first by saying that the elderly should not seek the same physical roles—like military command—but should instead offer counsel, judgment, and moral authority, which are actually more valuable to the state than youthful strength. This fits the Stoic idea that virtue is rational activity according to nature: the old still exercise reason in governance, just in a different mode. So Cicero is basically saying old age is the peak of moral contribution, which aligns with Rowe and Kahn's 1987 model of successful aging—avoiding disease, maintaining high function, and staying actively engaged—showing that Cicero anticipated modern gerontology's three-part framework.

As for the second objection, bodily weakness is real but irrelevant because virtue does not depend on physical power. The third is answered by saying that pleasures of the mind—conversation, study, friendship—are actually greater than bodily pleasures, which is almost an Epicurean point about ataraxia through mental calm, though Cicero frames it as Stoic self-sufficiency. Finally, the nearness to death is not frightening if one has lived well, since nature requires death and a long life is not necessarily better than a complete one."
