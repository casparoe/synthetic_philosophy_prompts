I was discussing with friends recently how certain technologies appear to outlive the people and institutions that originally built them, and someone mentioned John von Neumann's interest in self-replicating machines and in whether technology might survive the civilization that created it. I do not have an academic background in philosophy or computing history, but the idea has stayed with me. I am trying to think through how von Neumann might assess a development that occurred long after his death: large-scale artificial intelligence systems that continue to operate, update, and generate outputs long after their original developers have lost control, passed away, or dissolved—systems that are copied, fine-tuned, and redeployed by unknown third parties across jurisdictions where no single authority can fully disable them.

More specifically, I wonder whether von Neumann would regard these persistent algorithmic artifacts as genuine examples of the kind of technological survival he had in mind, or whether he would insist on a distinction between survival in his sense—where the original design architecture and functional intentions remain intact—and what I am calling statistical ghosting, in which only parameter distributions and behavioral patterns continue without any continuity of engineered purpose. Would he see them as analogous to his cellular automata, capable of autonomous replication and adaptation, or would he argue that without preserved intentional structure they are merely echoes rather than true survivors? And if he did classify them as genuine survivors, what might he say about any ethical obligations that creators hold toward artifacts that will persist and evolve well beyond their ability to guide, recall, or constrain them?
