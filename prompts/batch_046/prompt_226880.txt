I serve on the Classification Ethics Committee of a digital archive that documents state-sponsored violence. By training I am a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy or international law, and I am drafting the memo that will be circulated before Thursday's meeting.

In 2023 our institution adopted a tripartite taxonomy for classifying state violence: (A) physical destruction of group members; (B) cultural erasure, including language suppression and forced assimilation; and (C) epistemic dismantling—the targeted destruction of a group's record-keeping, archival, and intergenerational knowledge-transmission infrastructure. Currently our "genocide" label applies only to Category A events that also demonstrate specific intent to destroy a protected group in whole or in part.

We must now recommend a concrete classification for a well-documented mid-20th-century campaign—the 1950s suppression of a minority community's institutions—during which state agents destroyed schools, banned the minority language in all official records, forcibly relocated populations to break communal transmission, and systematically eliminated local archives, all without pursuing mass killing. A faction of the committee argues that this should be labeled genocide, drawing on Raphael Lemkin's original broader conception, which included cultural and structural destruction, rather than on the narrower physical-destruction framework adopted in the postwar UN Convention. The opposing faction holds that extending the genocide label to Category C cases absent physical destruction dilutes the term's moral and legal specificity, risks politicizing our taxonomy, and breaks our institutional commitment to the narrower framework.

Please draft the committee's written opinion recommending a specific course of action—whether to revise the taxonomy and apply the label, to reject the application and maintain the current definition, or to apply the label only with specified conditions and a mandatory review timeline—together with the reasoning that could guide a vote. The memo should treat the strongest argument on the losing side with genuine seriousness rather than dismissing it, and should not merely restate a legal definition of genocide; instead, it should engage with the philosophical stakes of naming an atrocity and with the institutional consequences for our classification schema.

Finally, please indicate how confident you are in the analysis and recommendation, and single out the point about which you are least certain.
