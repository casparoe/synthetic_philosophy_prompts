I defend a hybrid dual-layer view of vagueness: semantic supervaluationism governs natural-language predicates, so there is no single correct precisification and borderline cases are genuinely indeterminate in meaning; yet metaphysical epistemicism governs the properties those predicates aim to track, which possess sharp, determinate extensions fixed by sub-physical facts that are in principle inaccessible to any evidential state we could occupy. The result is that a borderline case—say, a hair count that makes "bald" apply—is linguistically unsettled but ontologically settled, with the sharp boundary lying in a region of fact permanently hidden from us. I want a committed, definite assessment of how exposed this position actually is. What specific observations about speaker disagreement or cross-contextual stability, what formal results in the logic of vagueness or higher-order indeterminacy, and what metaphysical arguments about property individuation or reference magnetism would have to obtain to collapse one or both layers? Which retreat—pure supervaluationism, pure epistemicism, or a context-sensitive sharp-boundary view—is the most defensible fallback if the hybrid fails, and does that fallback preserve the original motivations? Please take a clear stance on whether the view is genuinely risky or largely insulated by qualifications, and identify the single challenge you regard as most damaging.
