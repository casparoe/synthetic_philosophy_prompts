I'm organizing a graduate seminar module that brings metaphysicians of the self into conversation with scholars of African philosophy, specifically around Ubuntu and relational conceptions of personhood. Two invited interlocutors—one who treats Ubuntu as articulating a metaphysically substantive thesis that persons are constituted by their relations to others, and one who insists it is primarily an ethical and political doctrine about communal solidarity and moral recognition that Western philosophy wrongly metaphysicizes—have agreed to an adversarial collaboration after a year of talking past each other. Please draft the joint statement they should have produced.

The document should open with a numbered section of agreed claims both can sign. These should cover at least: that liberal individualism fails to capture important dimensions of moral life in many African social contexts; that relations to family, community, and ancestors play a centrally important role in how personhood is cultivated and recognized; that translation between metaphysical and normative vocabulary has obscured the debate; and that neither party denies the empirical reality of deep social embedding. Then present a disagreements table with two columns, each phrased in wording its own camp would accept. The relational-constitution side should state precisely whether relations are ontologically constitutive rather than merely causally formative, and should specify which relations do the constituting work—familial, communal, ancestral, or others—without smuggling in normative premises. The normative-interpretation side should state precisely that Ubuntu concerns moral status and communal recognition rather than ontological dependence, and should specify whether it allows any non-metaphysical reading of being formed or constituted by relations.

Next, identify at least three cruxes—sub-questions such that resolving them would move at least one side. Suggested cruxes include: whether the familiar formulation that a person is a person through other people is best parsed as an ontological identity claim or as a claim about normative interdependence; whether a being entirely lacking communal ties could possess full moral personhood, and whether each camp's answer turns on metaphysics or on ethics; and whether the framework carries universal moral weight or remains specific to particular African social structures, with implications for cross-cultural moral theory. For each crux, specify a concrete case or thought experiment both sides agree bears on it—perhaps a hermit with no sustained social ties, or a hypothetical artificial agent trained exclusively on relational data but embedded in no human community—along with the prediction each camp would make about it in advance.

Finally, inject a novel conceptual distinction to help clarify where the disagreement is genuinely substantive versus verbal: distinguish between constitutive relationality, in which relations are part of what makes the entity the kind of thing it is, and causal relationality, in which relations merely shape an entity that already exists independently. Ask whether resolving this distinction settles any portion of the dispute, or whether the camps still diverge even once the distinction is granted. The document should be formal, precise, and free of bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; paraphrase any traditional formulations without placing them in quotation marks. Keep the tone constructive, with each residual disagreement rendered exactly and neutrally.
