Construct a thought experiment engineered to isolate whether a person-affecting view can consistently evaluate climate-policy choices that affect an unbounded number of future generations. Constraints: present it as a realistic national energy-policy decision lasting no more than one paragraph; do not invoke exotic physics, perfect predictors, or science-fictional technology. The scenario must involve two policies that are identical in their effects on all present and near-future individuals but diverge in their asymptotic population-growth profiles: Policy A produces a countably infinite sequence of generations with bounded per-capita welfare, while Policy B produces a finite sequence with unbounded per-capita welfare. After the scenario, demonstrate: (i) the verdict the person-affecting view delivers and the precise reason it treats asymptotic outcomes as normatively inert; (ii) the verdict a total-utilitarian calculus delivers and exactly where in the reasoning the divergence occurs—specify whether it hinges on the convergence or divergence of the welfare series; (iii) which structural feature of the case—finite versus infinite support—is doing the normative work; and (iv) how a defender of the person-affecting view would attack the case by challenging the moral relevance of asymptotic outcomes to present obligations. Conclude by stating whether removing the infinite-horizon feature collapses the disagreement back into a standard intergenerational trade-off, and identify what that implies about whether infinite ethics introduces a genuinely novel form of moral indeterminacy rather than merely scaling up familiar conflicts.
