I have been reflecting intensely on what makes a life go well ever since I declined a highly paid but hollow corporate position in order to remain in the demanding but meaningful research I actually care about, and I find myself caught between competing intuitions that seem to resist any simple formula. The debate over Nozick's experience machine—whether a life of simulated pleasure and false belief could be genuinely good—seems to capture part of my confusion, but my own reactions to variations on the scenario do not line up neatly with the standard philosophical camps.

Here are my verdicts on five cases. First, a gifted composer spends her entire adult life in a full experience machine, believing she is writing symphonies that move audiences worldwide, when in fact she is floating in a tank with no real achievements; despite her constant joy and sense of purpose, I judge that her life is going badly. Second, a real composer spends decades in obscurity, produces work that is genuinely excellent but largely ignored, suffers frequent disappointment, yet knows she has actually mastered her craft; I think her life is going well, even though her subjective happiness is lower than the simulated composer's. Third, a person raised in severe material deprivation gradually adapts her desires downward until she wants almost nothing, reports complete satisfaction with her constrained circumstances, and never considers alternatives; I think her life is not going well, regardless of her own assessment. Fourth, an elderly person who has lived a fully authentic life of real accomplishment chooses, with full awareness, to enter a peaceful simulated retirement in which she knows the experiences are false but enjoys gentle fabricated memories of gardening and friendship; I think her life can still be going well, perhaps even better than a senile but authentic alternative. Fifth, in what I think of as a reverse experience machine, a person believes she is living an authentic, difficult life filled with genuine achievement and feels deep pride in her work, but unknown to her every success is simulated; despite her subjective state being nearly identical to the real composer's in case two, I judge that her life is going poorly, much like case one.

Is there a coherent account of well-being—perhaps one that combines objective achievement, an authentic relation to reality, and the autonomy of one's choice to enter or remain in a given state—that can deliver all five verdicts without becoming ad hoc? If no single principle can accommodate them, which of my judgments should I overturn, and what does the best surviving theory predict for the case that still unsettles me: a person who knowingly enters a partial simulation in which only her social relationships are real and unsimulated, while all her professional achievements are fabricated, because she has come to value genuine human connection far more than objective accomplishment?
