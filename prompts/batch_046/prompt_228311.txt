At a dinner party, two guests who had recently read opposing accounts of the British Raj in India fell into a sustained argument. One, Alex, was largely persuaded by the historian who defends empire on the grounds of modern institutional legacy and counterfactual comparison; the other, Jordan, was convinced by the parliamentarian-historian who stresses extraction, deindustrialization, and the moral bankruptcy of treating occupation as development. They referred to no specific volumes, and neither author was present to defend his view. A bystander reconstructed the exchange as follows:

(1) Alex said the Raj must be judged against plausible counterfactuals in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, not against an idealized independent India. Given the power vacuum after Mughal decline and competition among European powers, British rule was likely the least destructive option available, and its institutional legacy — a unified legal code, a professional civil service gradually opened to Indians, and integrated markets — supplied the scaffolding for post-1947 democracy.

(2) Jordan replied that this is a false trilemma that ignores Japan's autonomous modernization and overstates Mughal collapse. More fundamentally, it commits what Jordan called a credit-stealing fallacy: treating any positive spillover from an unjust system as a credit to that system, rather than asking whether Indians could have achieved comparable outcomes through selective trade and diplomacy without subjugation.

(3) Alex countered that path dependency matters more than moral possibility. The railways, built primarily for troop movement and raw-material extraction, nevertheless created pan-Indian economic integration and a shared elite experience that made the independence movement itself coherent. Without that unintended infrastructure, decolonization might have produced fragmentation rather than two functioning states.

(4) Jordan said this confuses correlation with causation and treats Indian political agency as an accidental byproduct of British engineering. The independence movement drew on indigenous traditions and anti-colonial solidarity, not railway schedules. Furthermore, if unintended benefit justifies domination, then any occupation that accidentally produces useful artifacts escapes moral condemnation, which collapses the distinction between empire and other forms of coercive control.

Please adjudicate move by move: rate each of the four moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right and briefly justify each rating. Then say which party is right about which specific point, and identify what, if anything, both are missing — particularly regarding how moral accounting should treat unintended institutional spillovers across generations.
