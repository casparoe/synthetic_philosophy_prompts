Question: Jain anekāntavāda claims reality is ananta-dharma (infinitely propertied) and uses syādvāda together with the saptabhaṅgī (sevenfold predication) to avoid one-sided judgments. Explain how this framework applies when a single substance seems to instantiate contradictory properties—say, sweet and bitter—simultaneously in one temporal slice. Distinguish naya (standpoint) from a merely subjective perspective, and explain the contribution of the avaktavya mode here. Is anekāntavāda best read as dialetheism, or does it avoid contradiction differently?

Student answer:
Jain anekāntavāda says every substance has infinite properties (ananta-dharma), so any single claim is one-sided (ekānta). To fix this, syādvāda adds the word syāt—meaning "perhaps" or "in some respect"—to every judgment. The saptabhaṅgī then lists seven combinations: it is, it is not, it is and is not, it's inexpressible, and the three mixes with inexpressible. So if a substance is sweet and bitter at the exact same moment, that just means "it is sweet in one naya and bitter in another naya." The naya here works like a conceptual scheme or language-game: you choose the frame that highlights sweetness, and someone else chooses the frame for bitterness. The avaktavya mode is for when a speaker is too confused to settle on one frame, so they can't express a definite judgment. Because all contradiction is spread across different frames, anekāntavāda doesn't need dialetheism—it's not saying P and not-P are literally both true, just that they're true in different schemes. At the same time, Jains reject the law of excluded middle globally, since there's always a "both" or "inexpressible" option, but they preserve non-contradiction within each individual naya. There's also a self-reference issue: the claim "all claims need syāt" itself needs syāt, which creates a vicious circularity that undermines the whole system, though Jains don't seem to notice it. So the doctrine is mainly about epistemic humility: we always have to say "syāt" because we can never access the whole object at once.
