As a retired experimental physicist who has recently taken up philosophy of science, I am assisting a colleague with the marking of an undergraduate examination on conceptual inversions in biology and computation. The examination question was this: Darwin's explanation of adaptation and Turing's conception of mechanised computation are both characterised as inversions in which functional competence is accounted for without an appeal to comprehension or design. Precisely delineate what is inverted in each case, distinguishing carefully between the elimination of intelligent design and the elimination of teleonomic function in Darwin, and between the elimination of phenomenal consciousness and the elimination of semantic content in Turing. Then assess whether the two inversions are genuinely parallel, and whether they jointly entail that no functional system, biological or artificial, ever requires an internal understanding of its own operations.

One student answered as follows.

Darwin inverts the traditional inference from biological complexity to an intelligent designer. Where natural theologians argued that the eye's intricate structure implies a seeing creator with foresight, Darwin showed that cumulative selection can construct such organs blindly, across generations, without intention. In doing so he eliminates not only intelligent design but teleonomic function itself: there is no evolutionary sense in which the eye is genuinely "for" seeing, since nature possesses no purposes, and functional talk is merely a retrospective human description. This is therefore an ontological inversion that replaces mental causation with pure mechanism.

Turing performs an analogous inversion in the domain of mind. Traditionally, intelligent behaviour was taken as proof of an internal consciousness that grasps meaning. Through the imitation game, Turing demonstrated that a formal system manipulating symbols according to syntactic rules can reproduce linguistic behaviour without phenomenal awareness and without semantic content. The machine understands nothing, yet its outputs are indistinguishable from understanding. Hence, just as Darwin removes the need for a designer in biology, Turing removes the need for both consciousness and meaning in cognition.

Taken together, these inversions establish a single philosophical principle: competence never requires comprehension. Darwin proves that biological function needs no purpose or internal understanding, and Turing proves that cognitive performance needs neither mind nor meaning. The only significant difference is temporal: Darwin's process is diachronic, operating across populations over time, whereas Turing's is synchronic, occurring within a single device. Otherwise, both replace an appeal to internal understanding with an appeal to external rules—natural selection functioning as a rule of survival, and computation functioning as a rule of symbol manipulation—so that in neither domain is comprehension a necessary condition for success.

Please award a mark out of 100 and provide specific, point-by-point commentary that justifies the assessment. Identify any subtle conflations, misattributions, or steps that do not follow, particularly where a hasty grader might overlook them. Please reply in British English, and do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations.
