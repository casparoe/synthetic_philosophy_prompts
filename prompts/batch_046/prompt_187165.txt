I have been thinking, without any formal training in philosophy, about whether two objects that share every observable feature are genuinely distinct. I came across a thought experiment involving two spheres suspended in an otherwise empty universe: identical in size, mass, and internal structure, separated only by distance. Some argue that their spatial separation is enough to distinguish them, since one occupies region A and the other region B. Others reply that in a universe containing nothing else, directional relations like being to the left of have no anchor, and that assigning them different locations already assumes there are two things to locate.

This led me to a further question that I have not seen addressed clearly. Even if we grant them distinct positions, we might ask whether the sphere in region A could have occupied region B, and vice versa. If each sphere carries a unique modal profile—a specific range of possible locations that belongs to it alone—does that rescue the principle that no two things are exactly alike? Or does invoking possible positions merely rename the problem, presupposing the very individuality it tries to explain?

Please write an essay addressing whether Leibniz's identity of indiscernibles survives Black's two-sphere scenario. Examine whether spatial relations can serve as non-circular distinguishing properties, and whether appealing to swap-ability or modal location genuinely solves the puzzle or just pushes it back a step. Reach and defend a specific conclusion: are the spheres one object, two objects, or is there no fact of the matter? Finally, discuss what your conclusion implies for more familiar cases—for instance, whether a perfect duplicate of a person or artifact should be regarded as a new individual or as the original multiplied. The essay should be rigorous enough to satisfy someone who has followed the reasoning closely, but written for a reader without philosophical background.
