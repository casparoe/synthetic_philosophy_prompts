In discussions of AI development, the “alignment tax” is usually described as a one-time performance cost: safety measures reduce benchmark scores or increase compute. The “competitiveness constraint” is the worry that actors who pay this tax will be outpaced by rivals who do not. But I have noticed a more specific pattern that seems distinct from both. Consider a lab that introduces a refusal mechanism for dangerous queries. The mechanism is technically minor—perhaps a few percent drop in coding-benchmark scores—but because procurement and marketing decisions are driven by visible leaderboard rankings, the lab loses enterprise contracts. Meanwhile, the safety benefit—fewer harmful outputs—is hard to monetize or verify, so it does not offset the competitive loss. Over successive generations, this is not just a static handicap: the aligned lab has lower revenue and less user-interaction data, so its base model improves more slowly, widening the initial gap. A second instance: in open-source ecosystems, a safety-filtered release is adopted less widely than an unfiltered one, which means the filtered version receives fewer downstream fine-tunings and bug reports, further slowing its improvement relative to the unfiltered branch. The pattern is a feedback loop in which competitive dynamics do not merely penalize alignment once, but systematically redirect the evolutionary trajectory of the technology away from aligned variants, even when the alignment modifications are small. This seems different from a generic tragedy of the commons—there is no shared resource being depleted—and different from a simple race to the bottom, since the “bottom” here is not lower quality but specifically the absence of a particular kind of constraint. Is there an established concept—perhaps in evolutionary economics, institutional analysis, or technology studies—that captures exactly this mechanism of competitive selection operating through visible-cost and invisible-benefit asymmetries compounded over iterations? If not, can you coin and define a term for it, with clear boundary conditions that distinguish it from ordinary market failure, adverse selection, and the competitiveness constraint itself? I am not looking for a bare label; I want the carving: what the phenomenon includes, what it excludes, and where its edges lie.
