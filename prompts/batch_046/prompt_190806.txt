exam question: "Explain the argument from illusion and how disjunctivism responds to it. Then apply the same structure to a gustatory hallucination—say, tasting sweetness in plain water. Does disjunctivism need to treat cross-modal cases differently?"

student answer:
The argument from illusion starts from the observation that veridical perception and illusion can feel exactly the same—like seeing a stick that looks bent in water versus one thats actually bent. Because they share this subjective similarity, philosophers infer there's a common mental element, usually a sense-datum, present in both cases. So in normal perception we're not directly related to the physical object but to this mental intermediary.

Disjunctivism, especially in Michael Martin's version, rejects the common-factor principle. It says veridical perception and hallucination are totally different mental kinds: in the good case you're directly acquainted with the world, in the bad case there's no object of awareness at all. So they don't share any mental component, even if they feel identical.

Applying this to taste: if I hallucinate sweetness in plain water, the disjunctivist says my experience is just a different mental event from actually tasting sugar water. The fact that I can't tell them apart doesn't prove they share a factor; it just means my introspection is limited.

But there's a catch. For disjunctivism to avoid the common-factor view, the two experiences can't actually be phenomenally identical—if they were the same mental kind we'd be back to square one. So disjunctivists must deny the first premise of the argument, not just the inference. They have to claim that in genuine tasting there's always some direct relational property—like the actual sugar molecules affecting your experience—that makes the phenomenal character subtly different, even if you never notice. That means cross-modal cases don't need special treatment, since the same direct-relation logic applies whether its vision or taste.
