Here are my verdicts on Gödel-inspired anti-mechanist arguments, and I want to know whether they form a coherent position or are merely ad hoc. When Lucas argues that a human mathematician can produce a statement true but unprovable in a formal system F meant to capture human reasoning, I reject it: if F is fixed, the reasoner can always ascend to a stronger system F', so the limitation never actually binds the human. When Penrose appeals to our ability to recognize that F is consistent, I reject that move as well—not because consistency is irrelevant, but because recognizing consistency is not equivalent to constructing the specific Gödel sentence the argument requires. Yet when the same Gödelian structure is used today to claim that artificial systems can never possess genuine mathematical insight—treating derivability within a fixed architecture as equivalent to cognitive grasp—I think the flaw is different and deeper: it conflates syntactic reach with understanding, and it assumes the system's formal framework is static. Despite all this, I still hold an intuition that human mathematical insight involves something not fully captured by any formal derivation procedure; I just deny that Gödel's theorems establish it. Is there a principle that unifies these verdicts—perhaps about the conditions under which a formal limitation genuinely transfers to an epistemic or cognitive one? What does that principle predict for an AI designed to dynamically revise its own axiomatic framework in response to novel mathematical problems? And please say what would change your mind: would a repaired Lucas-style argument using a genuinely non-expandable formalization, or a clear demonstration that consistency-recognition can be fully mechanized, overturn the core intuition?
