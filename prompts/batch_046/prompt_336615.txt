I am preparing the final examination for an advanced undergraduate and graduate seminar in philosophy of mind and value. The exam is a single 90-minute essay, and I want one question that tests whether students can do philosophical work with the clinical phenomenon of pain asymbolia rather than merely reproduce what they have read about affective dissociation and insular damage. To that end, please construct a question that introduces—within the prompt itself—a conceptual distinction that is not standard textbook fare: distinguish "affective detachment," in which a subject recognizes a noxious stimulus as pain but experiences none of the usual motivational aversion or suffering, from "evaluative inversion," in which the subject not only lacks aversion but actively assigns a positive or neutral valence to the same sensory state. The exam question should ask students to evaluate whether either form of dissociation demonstrates that pain's badness is not intrinsic to its sensory character but is instead a contingent product of a separable affective system. Crucially, the question must also present a specific objection to this separation thesis: namely, that even in classic asymbolia the patient retains a "thin" or behaviorally masked negative evaluation—evidenced perhaps by withdrawal reflexes or delayed verbal corrections—and that this residual evaluation is sufficient to preserve the claim that pain is essentially bad. Students should be required to adjudicate between the separationist interpretation and this masked-evaluation defense without simply asserting that neuroscience has settled the matter.

Please provide: (1) the exam question itself, clearly framed for a 90-minute response and suitable for students who know the basic clinical profile of asymbolia but have not encountered the specific distinction or objection above; (2) a model answer of approximately 650 words that demonstrates the precise argumentative moves an excellent answer should make, including how to treat the constructed distinction as doing real philosophical labor rather than serving as mere taxonomy; and (3) a marking scheme totaling 20 marks, organized around five distinct criteria, each noting the specific move that earns full credit and the characteristic failure that distinguishes a B-level answer—one that summarizes the clinical material competently but treats the distinction as merely descriptive and either ignores the masked-evaluation objection or answers it by stipulation. Keep the marking scheme to about 250 words. Do not include reading lists, citations, or requests for bibliographic references; the answer should be assessable entirely on conceptual grounds.
