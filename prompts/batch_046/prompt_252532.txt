I've been arguing with my friend about whether "better" works like a math relation — specifcally if it's transitive, so if A is better than B and B is better than C then A has to be better than C. I tried to build a case that this can't hold up, but now I'm worried my reasoning proves way too much and basically wipes out every small good choice. Here's my arguement with the steps numbered:

1. If "better than" is transitive, then chaining a bunch of "slightly better" steps should add up across the whole line.

2. Saving 10 people is a little better than saving 9, saving 9 a little better than 8, all the way down to saving 1 being a little better than saving 0.

3. By the same logic, giving $1 to charity is a little better than giving $0, giving $2 a little better than $1, and so on up to giving away every dollar you have.

4. Same deal with time: spending one extra minute helping is slightly better than spending zero minutes.

5. So if transitivity holds, giving away everything and spending every waking minute on relief is "better" than doing nothing.

6. Now, if X is genuinely better than Y, then it's wrong or at least defective not to pick X when you can.

7. So if "better than" is transitive, you're definately required to give away all your money and never sleep, which is obviously not true — you can keep rent money and rest sometimes.

8. Therefore "better than" can't be transitive, or else every tiny improvement turns into an extreme obligation.

But I don't want to say comparing lives or donations is meaningless, and I still think saving 10 people IS better than saving 5. Where did I slip? Is it step 6, where I connect "better" to "required"? Or is the problem in how I'm chaining things across money and time? I want to keep the idea that small improvements matter without getting the absurd result. Which premise should I give up, and what does giving it up cost me? Please answer in American English.
