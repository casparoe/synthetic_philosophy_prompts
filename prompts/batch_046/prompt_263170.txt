I have taught the epistemic regress problem—foundationalism, coherentism, and infinitism—for many years, and I remain struck by how static the debate feels despite the apparent clarity of the three positions. Foundationalists maintain that justification terminates in basic beliefs requiring no further inferential support; coherentists hold that beliefs are justified by their mutual integration within a system; and infinitists deny both arbitrary termination and circularity, proposing instead an infinite, non-repeating chain of reasons. Yet when I examine the objections exchanged in the literature and in seminar, the disagreements seem to cluster around two concerns that are rarely separated: one about the psychological impossibility of an agent actually possessing an infinite chain of beliefs, and another about the normative structure of justification regardless of cognitive capacity. At the same time, foundationalists and coherentists appear to talk past one another on whether circularity is vicious or benign, without specifying whether they are describing the synchronic architecture of belief at a time or the diachronic process of rational revision. I am beginning to suspect that the apparent three-way stalemate is not a single disagreement but a cross-cutting cluster—part substantive, part verbal—masked by shared vocabulary. Can you diagnose the actual structure of this impasse? In particular, distinguish the genuinely substantive disagreements from the merely verbal ones; identify whether hidden empirical claims about human cognitive finitude are illegitimately doing normative work; determine whether the sides are answering different questions—perhaps one about structural conditions on belief systems, another about temporal processes of justification—under a single heading; and tell me whether any conceptual distinction, such as that between synchronic and diachronic justification or between having a reason and being able to provide one, could actually move the debate forward. If not, should we conclude that the regress problem fragments into several independent questions that share only a name, and that expecting a unified resolution is itself part of the confusion?
