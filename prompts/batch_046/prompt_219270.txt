You are a professional moral philosopher with expertise in action theory, applied ethics, and the metaphysics of causation. Before delivering your analysis, please begin with a brief internal brainstorming: list at least three candidate principles that might distinguish permissible from impermissible harm in indirect and mediated cases—for example, distinctions based on direct bodily involvement, intention versus foresight, causal proximity, or the presence of an autonomous intermediary. Then proceed step by step to your conclusions, being as rigorous and precise as possible.

I have been reflecting on my own intuitive verdicts about killing versus letting die, doing versus allowing, across both direct and technologically mediated scenarios. Here is the pattern I find in myself, with concrete cases and my gut judgments:

1. I am walking past a shallow pool and see a toddler drowning within arm's reach. I could rescue them at no risk to myself, but I keep walking because I do not want to be late. The toddler dies. Verdict: clearly wrong.

2. I am on a remote cliff and see a hiker fall into a 200-meter crevasse during a blizzard. Any attempt to descend would almost certainly kill me as well. I call emergency services, but they cannot reach the site in time. The hiker dies. Verdict: tragic, but not morally wrong.

3. I am the sole physician in a field hospital with one functioning ventilator and two patients in acute respiratory failure. Patient A is a 28-year-old with temporary trauma and a high chance of full recovery; Patient B is a 92-year-old with terminal multi-organ failure. I allocate the ventilator to A; B dies within hours. Verdict: permissible, and perhaps required.

4. The same field hospital scenario, but instead of allocating the ventilator, I secretly administer a lethal overdose of sedative to B so that A receives the machine without competition. Verdict: monstrous, far worse than Case 3.

5. I am on a footbridge and see a runaway trolley approaching five workers on the track below. I push a heavy stranger standing beside me onto the tracks; the stranger dies, but the trolley stops and the five survive. Verdict: wrong.

6. I flip a switch that diverts the trolley onto a side track where only one worker is present. That worker dies, but the five on the main track survive. Verdict: permissible.

7. Three years ago, I designed and certified an autonomous emergency-traffic system for a municipal fleet. Its embedded decision protocol includes a harm-minimization subroutine that, in a specific rare conflict scenario, will physically redirect one autonomous vehicle away from a larger group without requiring real-time human authorization. Last month, the system executed exactly this maneuver: one vehicle was forced into a barrier, killing its sole occupant, in order to protect five pedestrians. At the time of the event I was not present and had no direct control. Verdict: I feel genuinely uneasy. I lean toward saying that deploying the system was wrong, yet I also feel it is not as wrong as Case 5.

8. I am a synthetic biologist. I engineer a self-limiting marine bacterium intended to digest microplastics, release it under strict containment, and monitor it for years. An unforeseen interaction with a native viral vector causes a mutation that makes it pathogenic to a specific marine mammal population; a small number die years later, far from the release site. I had no intent to harm them and was not physically present. Verdict: I keep going back and forth. Sometimes it feels like Case 2 (indirect, unforeseeable, not blameworthy); other times it feels closer to Case 7, as if I released an autonomous agent that later killed.

The pair that is hardest for me to reconcile is Case 6 and Case 7: both save five at the cost of one through an indirect mechanism, yet my gut treats the switch as permissible and the autonomous-system deployment as questionable. Meanwhile, Case 4 and Case 3 are clearly separated, but Case 8 sits awkwardly between Case 2 and Case 7.

Please do the following:

- State a precise principle, or a small family of competing principles, that captures as many of my verdicts as possible. For each principle, specify the clause that addresses each of the cases above, and state its verdict on Cases 1 through 8.

- Apply the principle to at least two cases I have not considered: (a) a physician who disconnects a brain-dead patient from a ventilator at the family's explicit request, knowing the heart will stop within minutes; and (b) a factory manager who knows that a particular manufacturing process has a 0.1% chance of releasing a toxic cloud that will kill one nearby resident per decade, but who continues the process because shutting it down would eliminate hundreds of jobs.

- Give an honest assessment of which of my original verdicts the principle cannot accommodate without ad hoc adjustment. In particular, address whether Case 7 must be overturned to preserve a simple principle, or whether Case 6 must be overturned, or whether the tension between Case 7 and Case 8 forces a more complex, multi-clause principle.

- Conclude by saying which of your candidate principles you find most defensible, and what it implies about my back-and-forth Case 8.

Please reason from first principles and avoid appealing to authority, citation, or named philosophical schools. Do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or references to specific texts.
