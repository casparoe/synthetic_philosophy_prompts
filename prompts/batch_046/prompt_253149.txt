I have been working through arguments about the inefficacy objection in collective action ethics—specifically, whether individual choices regarding meat consumption or carbon-intensive travel can be morally wrong when any single choice makes no discernible difference to the aggregate harm. One analogy that keeps surfacing in these discussions, usually advanced by those who want to preserve individual moral obligations despite causal insignificance, compares the individual agent to a single musician in a very large orchestra: if one violinist stops playing, the symphony sounds essentially the same, yet it would be absurd for every musician to reason that way and stop; analogously, each consumer ought to refrain from harmful practices even when their personal contribution is negligible.

I would like a careful audit of this comparison. What corresponds precisely to what—the musician, the instrument, the conductor, the audience, the score? Where does the mapping break down, especially regarding intentional coordination, the absence of a shared goal among dispersed consumers, and the causal structure of supply chains versus acoustic contribution? Which features of orchestral performance—hierarchy, rehearsal, aesthetic unity—does the analogy quietly import that are missing from global collective-harm scenarios? Does the conclusion that individual abstention is morally required even when causally redundant survive once these disanalogies are fully on the table, or does the analogy collapse into a different kind of argument, perhaps about expressive value or rule-following, that needs separate defense? And if the orchestral comparison ultimately fails, is there a better analogy that preserves the intuition that negligible contributions can still be wrong without importing false structural parallels?
