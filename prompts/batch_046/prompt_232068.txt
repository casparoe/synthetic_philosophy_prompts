In philosophy of artificial intelligence, a tripartite dispute about the status of large language models has reached an impasse that feels more structural than empirical. One camp treats LLMs as simulators—stochastic reproductions of human discourse patterns that lack beliefs, intentions, or understanding regardless of behavioural fluency. A second camp argues that, given capacities for in-context reasoning, extended goal pursuit across contexts, and adaptive tool use, LLMs instantiate a form of functional agency that requires neither consciousness nor biological embodiment but does demand that we treat them as agents for predictive and explanatory purposes. A third, less prominent position rejects both frames and proposes that LLMs belong to a distinct ontological category—something like epistemic artefacts or mediated cognitive extensions—because they lack both the internal coherence of agents and the representational transparency of mere simulators.

What makes the stall puzzling is that all three sides share the same architectural description—transformer-based next-token prediction, scaling dynamics, emergent capabilities—and the same behavioural data, including benchmark performance and characteristic failure modes. They also concur that phenomenal consciousness is absent. Nevertheless, the debate has cycled for years through the same illustrative cases—whether a model understands a mathematical proof, whether it pursues an assigned objective—without convergence.

My hypothesis is that the disagreement is not about hidden empirical facts but about the criteria for intentional attribution: the simulator camp requires phenomenal consciousness or biological embodiment as necessary conditions for agency; the functionalist camp accepts organisational and behavioural criteria alone; and the artefact camp denies that the individual system is even the right unit of analysis. But I want a sharper diagnosis. Is this a substantive metaphysical disagreement about what agency necessarily involves? A verbal dispute about whether the term agent ought to be reserved for entities with a particular kind of autonomy? Or are the sides answering different questions—one about predictive explanation, one about moral status, one about epistemology—under the single heading what kind of thing is an LLM? Could there be a hidden formal or conceptual distinction—perhaps between diachronic stability and synchronic coherence, or between derived and original intentionality—that, if made explicit, would dissolve the appearance of conflict?

Please diagnose the structure of this disagreement with reasons drawn from what each side actually says, and tell me what, if anything, would count as genuine progress: a conceptual distinction, an empirical finding, a change in framing, or an argument that the question is ill-posed and should be abandoned.
