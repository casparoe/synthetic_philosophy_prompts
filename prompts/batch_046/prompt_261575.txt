Next month I present my job-market paper at a metaphysics seminar and then defend it before a hiring committee in philosophy of language and metaphysics. The thesis is that Bradley's regress argument against the reality of relations fails because it rests on a hidden premise I call the homogeneity assumption: if a relation R relates terms a and b, then the fact that R relates them must be mediated by a further relation R* that is ontologically on a par with R. I claim Bradley collapses relating-as-activity, which does generate a regress, into relating-as-formal-propositional-structure, which does not, and that this distinction can be sustained even within Bradley's own idealist framework without importing Russell's theory of types or a primitive unity relation. The audience includes two neo-Russellians committed to structured propositions with either unsaturated constituents or primitive propositional glue; a deflationary philosopher of language who regards the debate as a grammatical illusion; a historian of early analytic philosophy who knows Bradley's corpus closely and distrusts anachronistic readings; and several metaphysicians who consider the regress a pseudo-problem. Give me the objections in the order they are most likely to arise, phrased in the voice of each camp, with for each whether I should concede part of it, what I must hold, and where my reply is weakest. Identify the feature of my case—the distinction between activity-relating and formal-relating—that does the work, and explain what happens to my argument if that distinction collapses. Include the objection I most hope nobody asks—the charge that my resolution is not genuinely internal to Bradley's view but merely an external Russellian fix dressed in idealist language—and tell me how to answer it in under thirty seconds.
