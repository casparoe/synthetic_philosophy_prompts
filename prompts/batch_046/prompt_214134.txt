I've been following arguments about whether rural, working-class value systems or urban, professional-class value systems are more defensible, and I've noticed a peculiar stall. Participants on both sides typically concede the descriptive sociology: that working-class cultures tend to emphasize local loyalty, tangible skill, and mutual obligation within a fixed community, while professional-class cultures emphasize mobility, credential-based authority, and self-expression across dispersed networks. They also tend to concede that each system generates real goods—stable social bonds on one side, innovation and individual autonomy on the other. Despite this agreement, the debate never moves beyond mutual dismissal: one side charges elitism and rootlessness, the other parochialism and anti-intellectualism. My working hypothesis is that this is not a substantive disagreement about outcomes, but a conflict over the standard of justification appropriate to a form of life. One side seems to treat a culture as defensible only if its norms can be vindicated to outsiders through universalizable reasons; the other treats embedded success within a practice as self-justifying, so that demanding external rationalization is itself a category error. Is this the right diagnosis—is it a disagreement about moral epistemology, a verbal dispute over "defensible," or a hidden empirical fight about psychological well-being or social mobility? And given your diagnosis, is there anything—a conceptual distinction, an empirical finding, or a normative argument—that could actually advance the debate, or should we conclude that the two outlooks are incommensurable?
