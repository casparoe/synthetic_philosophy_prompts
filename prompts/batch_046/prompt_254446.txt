I'm currently coordinating an interdisciplinary seminar on the ontology of digital environments, and a recent experience has made this urgent for me: I inherited administrative access to a persistent virtual world built by a colleague who has since passed away, and I'm now responsible for enforcing community norms that were established decades ago in code that no longer runs. This has left me convinced that the philosophical question isn't simply whether virtual harms are "real," but whether normative obligations can survive complete substrate replacement—when every server, file format, and rendering engine has changed, yet institutional memory claims that an old exclusion or a virtual property seizure still binds present inhabitants. I want to design a classroom activity for about 14 graduate students in a 75-minute session that makes them feel the force of this specific problem, not through lecture but through structured interaction.

Please design a runnable activity—complete with assigned roles, explicit rules, a payoff or stakes mechanism that doesn't require real money, and a timeline—where participants construct a multi-generational virtual society across three simulated "eras," each defined by a complete technical rewrite that eliminates all prior code and physical storage. In Era One, one group must make a decision that causes harm within the virtual economy; in Era Two, a different group inherits only archival records of that decision but operates on entirely new infrastructure; in Era Three, the descendants must decide whether to honor, repair, or ignore the legacy obligation, with their choice affecting collective points. The rules should be arranged so that participants experience the pull of treating the harm as genuinely binding before anyone names the philosophical issue, and they should discover for themselves where the intuition of reality comes from—whether it is causal continuity, institutional recognition, or something else—before the debrief maps these moments onto the conceptual distinction between substrate-dependent and substrate-independent persistence. Then provide a debrief that connects specific moments in the simulation to parts of the idea—particularly the point at which institutional memory begins to function like a causal chain—together with an honest account of what will predictably fail the first time this is run and how to manage it, and a frank admission of which feature of substrate-independent moral continuity the activity cannot reproduce and must be supplied by the facilitator. The answer should be written in American English.
