I'm planning a fifty-minute session for first-year philosophy students on Marx's concepts of exploitation and alienation, but I want to teach them through the distinction between formal subsumption and real subsumption of labor under capital rather than as two separate moral complaints. Plan the explanation: specify the exact sequence in which the components must be introduced—why surplus extraction must be secured conceptually before separation from species-being is added, and exactly where the shift from formal to real subsumption belongs—so that students see alienation as a structural deepening of exploitation rather than a psychological overlay. Identify the one carrying example, contrasting a craftsperson who sells labor-power to capital but retains control over the labor process (exploited yet not fully alienated) with a factory worker whose labor process is redesigned by capital (both exploited and alienated), and say what the example must make visible and what it must avoid. List the misconceptions they will predictably form—such as taking exploitation to mean merely unfair pay, taking alienation to mean workplace dissatisfaction, assuming the two apply to identical cases by definition, or attributing alienation solely to private property rather than to capital's control over the process—and indicate precisely where in the sequence each should be headed off. Provide one end-of-session check that reveals whether a student grasps that exploitation can exist without full alienation. Finally, state openly what should be left out at this level—such as the quantitative derivation of surplus value, the full fourfold schema of alienation, or the historical narrative of primitive accumulation—and say so.
