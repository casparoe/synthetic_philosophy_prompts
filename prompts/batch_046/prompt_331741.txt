I serve as the senior ethics reviewer for a state pilot-program board that evaluates proposals to monetize or price-gate goods traditionally distributed by need, citizenship, or lottery. We receive three or four proposals per quarter, must issue a determination within ten business days using only a five-person staff with no philosophy training, and the board chair must sign every decision. Until recently I approached this work as a technical compliance task; that changed when my younger brother was placed on the kidney-transplant waitlist, and I realized that my judgments about cash payments to donors or surge pricing for life-saving resources are no longer abstract. I need a reusable decision procedure that translates the principle of repugnant commodification—specifically, the idea that pricing becomes genuinely corrupting only when it simultaneously violates semantic integrity (the money alters what the good means), exploits structural coercion (refusal is not genuinely voluntary because of background vulnerability), and lacks a substitutable non-market alternative (no non-price mechanism can achieve the same welfare gain without those harms)—into an ordered set of practical tests. The procedure must specify, for each test, what counts as a sufficient answer, where it terminates with a prohibition, where it terminates with permission, and where it must hand the decision back to the board because the question is irreducibly one of values rather than empirical fact. It must be runnable without bibliographies, web access, or outside expertise.

Please construct the procedure, then apply it to two proposals: (1) a program offering direct tax-credit payments to living kidney donors to reduce waitlist mortality; and (2) a program allowing real-time auction pricing for ICU beds during a declared pandemic surge, with revenues ring-fenced for charity care. Include one case where the procedure produces a result I am personally likely to reject. Also state explicitly what the procedure cannot do—the hard cases where it will misfire and the distinctions it must drop in order to be usable.

Present the complete decision procedure as an ordered checklist of tests in the very first sentence, with no preamble and no restatement of the problem; then unfold the reasoning behind each filter, then the two worked applications, then the limitations. Near the end, state how confident you are in the procedure's reliability and identify the single step you are least sure survives translation from theory to practice.
