Next week I'm defending my local-structural-relationist take on Newton's bucket in our phil of physics seminar. The core claim is that the concavity of the water in the rotating bucket dosen't prove absolute space, but it also doesn't need Mach's distant stars — instead it's just the internal shear dynamics of the fluid responding to boundary conditions with the bucket wall. So rotation is real but it's a property of the water-body system, not motion relative to space or to the fixed stars. I'm not saying space doesn't exist, just that this experiment doesn't settle the substantivalism debate the way people think, and I want to defend that without smuggling in hidden absolutes.

The room will have Dr. Chen (hardline substantivalist, thinks GR manifold points are literally real and has the metric equations ready), a postdoc who is hardcore Machian and thinks distant matter is essential for any rotation, Prof. Miller (historian of science who actually reads Newton's Latin and will catch me misrepresenting the Principia), and three grad students who mostly care whether this is empirically distinguishable from standard views. It's a 45-minute talk with 20 minutes for Q&A, and I'm presenting this as my job-market paper idea, so I can't just shrug off objections.

I need a speaker prep sheet, not a defense essay. Give me the objections in roughly the order they'll come, phrased like each person would actually say them. For each: damage level, what I should concede up front, the two-minute reply, and the weakest point in that reply. Order them by how much damage they'd do. Include the question I'm hoping nobody asks — probably about whether I'm just smuggling in absolute space under a different name, or about what general relativity does to my local account. Also list the one or two things I should concede in my opening minute so I don't sound defensive.

Format:
1. Thesis check — one sentence confirming you got the view right, 2 lines max
2. Audience map — who cares about what and which commitments they'll defend, about half a page
3. Objections in order — speaker, phrasing, damage rating, concession, reply, soft spot. This is the bulk, aim for 6-8 items.
4. The dreaded question — why it's dangerous, a draft reply, and exactly where I'm exposed
5. Opening concessions — bullet list of what to admit immediately before they ask

Keep it casual, no bibliography, don't cite specific recent papers I probably haven't read, and if you reference Newton's actual arguments paraphrase them — don't put anything in quotation marks from a real text. There's probably a typo or two in this prompt, that's fine.
