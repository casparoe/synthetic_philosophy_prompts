Having spent three decades in experimental condensed-matter physics, I have in retirement turned to philosophy of mind with the same suspicion of overreach that I once applied to anomalous laboratory measurements. At an interdisciplinary colloquium last month, a speaker summarized Frank Jackson’s knowledge argument in terms that struck me as both precise and suspiciously tidy. The speaker described Mary as a neuroscientist confined since birth to a monochrome environment who nevertheless possesses exhaustive physical and functional knowledge of human color vision—the spectral tuning of retinal cones, the opponent-process dynamics in the lateral geniculate nucleus, the information-theoretic encoding in cortical area V4, and the complete causal architecture linking stimulus to behavior—without ever having experienced chromatic color. Upon leaving her room and seeing a ripe tomato, Mary acquires genuinely new knowledge: not an additional relational or structural fact about her brain or about surface reflectance properties, but direct acquaintance with the intrinsic qualitative character of redness itself. The speaker then concluded that physicalism is therefore false, on the grounds that any complete physical description is necessarily structural and relational and therefore necessarily omits these intrinsic phenomenal qualities. Finally, the speaker claimed that contemporary predictive-processing frameworks have in any case dissolved Jackson’s premise, because under active inference the subjective dimension of seeing red is nothing over and above the brain’s hierarchical minimization of prediction error across visual levels, which means Mary’s exhaustive prior knowledge of the visual system’s generative model already encompassed everything relevant to phenomenal experience and left no true epistemic gap.

I am inclined to think the speaker has smuggled in physicalist assumptions about the completeness of structural descriptions—assumptions that may not survive scrutiny in a non-physical domain—while also overstating what predictive neuroscience can deliver. Could you audit this summary sentence by sentence? For each claim, indicate what a careful defender of Jackson’s argument would accept, what they would correct or reject as a misrepresentation, and whether the speaker’s two further moves—first, that the argument straightforwardly refutes physicalism, and second, that active-inference models dissolve the epistemic gap—actually follow from an accurate statement of the view. I am not asking for citations, a bibliography, or a survey of the secondary literature; I want a conceptual accuracy check that I, as a reader without formal philosophical training, can use to judge how much of the summary to trust. In particular, please flag any conflation between epistemic and ontological claims, any overstatement of what the predictive-processing response achieves, and whether the speaker has presented a standard objection to physicalism as if it were an unproblematic conclusion.
