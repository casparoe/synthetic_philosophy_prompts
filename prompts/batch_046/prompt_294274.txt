I've been thinking on my own about what it takes for a theory of mental content or linguistic reference to be genuinely adequate, and I've been using the Twin Earth thought experiment as my main test case. By that I mean the familiar scenario in which a planet just like Earth hosts people who are psychologically identical to us, except that the clear, thirst-quenching liquid they call "water" has the chemical structure XYZ rather than H2O. I want to know what any serious theory in this area must be able to deliver.

Please lay out the full specification—the desiderata. For each requirement, include the case or distinction that motivates it. These should cover at least: explaining why two psychologically identical individuals can think about different substances; preserving the intuition that a speaker can successfully refer to something without knowing its underlying nature; allowing that an entire community can be systematically wrong about what they are talking about; distinguishing the mechanism by which reference is initially fixed from the mechanism by which it is passed along; and accommodating the idea that experts within a community can determine the reference of a term that lay speakers use competently without understanding.

Then add one more requirement drawn from a twist I haven't seen treated in standard discussions. Imagine a synthetic compound, synthesized in only one laboratory and named "neptunium-X" by its discoverers. A layperson on Earth hears the term, acquires a basic mental representation, and refers—through the expert chain—to that synthetic compound, even though she knows nothing of its synthesis. On Twin Earth, there is no such laboratory and no synthetic compound; instead, a naturally occurring isotope with identical macroscopic properties is present, and the psychologically identical layperson acquires the same mental representation but refers—again through local experts—to that natural isotope. The theory must either explain how reference can be anchored to an entity that exists only through human artifice and is unknown to the speaker, or it must explain why such anchoring fails, and in either case it must not collapse the distinction between referring to something and knowing what it is.

For every item on the list, mark whether it is non-negotiable or merely a preference of one philosophical camp. If any pair of desiderata appears jointly unsatisfiable—especially if the synthetic-compound case creates an irreconcilable tension with one of the standard Twin Earth intuitions—say so explicitly; that is the part I care about most.

Finally, score three broad approaches briefly against the list: a purely internalist or descriptivist view, a pure causal-historical view with no division of labor, and an externalist view that allows reference to be distributed across a community including experts. Don't just list strengths and weaknesses—identify the single requirement that does the most damage to the largest number of candidates.

I'm not asking for citations, a bibliography, or a reading list; I have no formal background in philosophy, so don't assume familiarity with the literature, but I do want the reasoning kept in the weeds rather than at the level of a textbook summary.
