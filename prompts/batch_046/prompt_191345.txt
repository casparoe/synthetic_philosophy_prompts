I am a retired experimental physicist who has recently begun studying philosophy of history as an independent scholar, and I am preparing a proposal for a six-month writing project—roughly one substantial essay of about seven thousand words—addressing the covering-law versus narrative explanation debate. My guiding intuition, drawn from my earlier work on renormalization-group methods in condensed-matter physics, is that the standard framing treats covering-law and narrative models as competing universal accounts of historical explanation, when in fact they may operate at distinct explanatory scales, analogous to how thermodynamic and microscopic descriptions apply at different resolutions without direct contradiction. I want the proposal to ask a precisely formulated question with a determinate answer: is the apparent conflict between Hempel-style covering laws and narrative coherence largely an artifact of demanding a single-scale explanation, and can we construct a scale-dependent taxonomy that assigns each explanatory mode its appropriate historiographical domain? The document should explain why existing treatments—typically presenting the debate as a binary choice—leave this scale-sensitive framing unresolved; outline a concrete methodological plan comparing specific historiographical practices, such as long-run demographic or economic transitions versus singular diplomatic crises, using criteria for explanatory resolution rather than primary archival research; state clearly what a successful result would look like, for instance a principled division of labor between law-like and narrative explanation, and what failure would look like, such as the collapse of the scale distinction upon examination; and provide an honest assessment of whether this scope is realistic for six months of independent work. Please do not include a bibliography, reading list, or citation requests; I will supply references myself. The tone should be suitable for a philosophy workshop or fellowship committee familiar with philosophy of science but not necessarily with professional historiography.
