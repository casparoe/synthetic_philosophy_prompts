Over the past few months I have been working closely with a tactile-to-visual sensory-substitution interface, and the experience has made Molyneux's old thought experiment feel less like an abstract classroom puzzle and more like a question I am living inside. I am trying to understand exactly what philosophical work is being done in the original formulation that John Locke quotes in An Essay Concerning Human Understanding (1694, Book II, Chapter IX, Section 8), and I would appreciate help unpacking the passage itself. Here is the text as Locke presents it:

"Suppose a Man born blind, and now adult, and taught by his touch to distinguish between a Cube, and a Sphere of the same metal, and nighly of the same bigness, so as to tell, when he felt one and t’other; which is the Cube, which the Sphere. Suppose then the Cube and Sphere placed on a Table, and the Blind Man to be made to see. Quaere, Whether by his sight, before he touch’d them, he could now distinguish, and tell, which is the Globe, which the Cube."

Locke then records his agreement with Molyneux's negative answer and adds his own reflection about how much we owe to "experience, improvement, and acquired notions." My difficulty is in identifying which parts of this reasoning commit Locke to a strong, sense-specific empiricism about ideas—one that might be undermined if cross-modal neural plasticity allows tactile and visual spatial representations to share cortical structure—and which parts require only the more minimal claim that the particular learned correlation between a given tactile pressure and its corresponding visual contour has not yet been acquired. In other words, does the passage force Locke to deny that there could be any innate or structurally shared mapping between touch and sight, or can his argument survive if we allow that the brain might pre-structure spatial form in a modality-independent way, provided that the specific associative link between "this protuberant angle felt" and "that visual outline seen" is still missing? I want to know which sentences in the passage support the stronger thesis and which leave room for the weaker, correlation-based reading, especially given that my own work with sensory substitution suggests that some cross-modal translation can occur far faster than pure associative learning would predict.
