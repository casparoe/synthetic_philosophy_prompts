I've been working through a line of reasoning about G. E. Moore's ethics for a seminar paper, and I keep returning to an argument that seems to collapse into absurdity, though each step looks defensible if you take Moore's claims about beauty and friendship seriously. (1) In Principia Ethica, Moore treats both deep friendship and the contemplation of beautiful objects as intrinsic goods of the highest rank, and he insists that when they occur together they form an organic unity: the value of appreciating a work of art in the company of a friend is not the arithmetic sum of friendship-value plus aesthetic-value, but a qualitatively greater whole whose value exceeds that sum. (2) If we accept that intrinsic goods generate pro tanto reasons to realize them, and that a greater available good generates a stronger reason than a lesser one, then whenever we face a choice between a state containing this organic unity and one lacking it, we have decisive reason to choose the former. (3) Now, for any solitary or non-aesthetic activity—say, writing alone, performing necessary labor, or even sleeping—we can imagine a corresponding version performed with a friend in an aesthetically rich environment, and by (1) that version instantiates the organic unity. (4) Therefore, for any moment not spent in such combined friendship-and-beauty states, we are passing up a greater available good. (5) This implies not merely that we should cultivate art and companionship, but that we are under a standing obligation to restructure our lives so as to eliminate all solitary, mundane, or non-aesthetic moments in favor of continuous communal aesthetic experience. (6) But that conclusion is plainly false: solitude, routine work, and even boredom are permissible and sometimes necessary parts of a good life. So the argument must fail somewhere.

Where does the mistake lie? I am inclined to keep (1) intact—Moore's account of organic unity between friendship and beauty seems genuinely illuminating, and I don't want to abandon it for a flat additive picture. I also find (2) hard to reject entirely, since it expresses a minimal principle of practical rationality. Is the error in (3), which assumes that any activity can be "upgraded" by embedding it in the organic unity without altering its nature or cost? Or is there a hidden scope shift between claiming that the unity has great value and claiming that every instance of its absence is a moral failure? Perhaps the flaw is deeper: maybe Moore's distinction between intrinsic value and right action means that the existence of a greater good does not, by itself, generate an obligation to maximize it, and (2) smuggles in a consequentialist premise that Moore's framework explicitly rejects. I want to know exactly which step is the weakest link, and what giving it up costs—whether we lose a plausible moral principle, a correct reading of Moore, or just an overambitious application of organic unity that treats value as recursively stackable across every domain of life.
