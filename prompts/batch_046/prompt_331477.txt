After relocating for work three years ago, I now live permanently under laws I did not help choose, pay taxes to a government in which I have no vote, and am subject to policies—housing, environmental, immigration—that shape my life without my consent, which has made the democratic boundary problem personally urgent for me. Reading about it, I see that the all-affected-interests principle would seem to grant membership to anyone impacted by a state's decisions, but that appears to dissolve any bounded demos into a universal constituency and thereby erase the very possibility of democratic self-rule within a particular political community. Conversely, restricting membership to citizens defined by birthright or naturalization law looks circular: the state decides who belongs to the state. From this I conclude that democratic legitimacy must depend on a pre-political, non-circular criterion—perhaps shared language, continuous territorial presence, or subjection to coercion—that fixes the demos prior to any democratic procedure. Since every proposed criterion either presupposes the boundary it is meant to justify or expands membership beyond coherence, the problem looks not like a tension to be managed but like a fatal, self-undermining contradiction in democratic theory itself. So why do theorists of democratic boundaries treat this circularity as an unavoidable feature rather than a reductio of popular sovereignty? Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then develop the reasoning with no preamble and without restating the question.
