I have been working through my own reactions to various knowledge-attribution scenarios, and I would like to know whether there is a single principle that makes sense of the pattern, or whether I must abandon one or more of my verdicts. Here are four cases, with my intuitions.

First, consider a disjunctive inference: Dr. Aris justifiably believes, on the basis of an erroneous catalogue entry, that her colleague Dr. Bex owns a particular rare manuscript. She infers that either Dr. Aris or Dr. Bex owns it. In fact Dr. Bex does own it, but Dr. Aris has no idea of this; the first disjunct is false because of the catalogue error. I judge that she does not know the disjunction is true, even though she is justified in believing it and it is true.

Second, consider a dormant archive: a legal historian forms the belief that a treaty clause was ratified in 1847, relying solely on a forged parliamentary record she takes to be authentic. Unbeknownst to her, she possesses—though has not examined—three authentic diplomatic letters in her own files that independently confirm the 1847 date. The clause was indeed ratified then. Here I am inclined to say she does have knowledge, because her belief is anchored in genuine evidence that belongs to her epistemic position, even though her actual reasoning traces back to the forgery.

Third, consider a compensating error: an engineer calculates a bridge's load-bearing capacity using a formula with an unnoticed unit-conversion error. By pure coincidence, the actual material strength deviates from specification in exactly the opposite direction, so her figure is correct. She publishes it without awareness of either error. I think she lacks knowledge; the accuracy is environmental luck, and her method was defective.

Fourth, consider a redundant lemma: a number theorist proves a result about prime distributions by invoking a lemma that is in fact false, though she also has—without realising it—a fully sound proof of the same result that bypasses the lemma entirely. The result is true, and all her other steps are rigorous; the false lemma is confined to an unnecessary branch. I judge that she does know the result, since her cognitive position includes a sound route to the truth, even if the route she actually travelled was flawed.

These four verdicts feel stable to me, but I can see they pull in different directions: the false-premise case and the redundant-lemma case both involve flawed reasoning paths, yet I withhold knowledge in one and grant it in the other; similarly, the dormant-archive and the disjunctive-inference cases both involve hidden or unaccessed truths, yet I grant knowledge in one and deny it in the other. Is there a coherent principle—perhaps concerning essential dependence on falsehood, or the structure of available justification—that delivers all four? If so, please state it precisely, indicate which clause is motivated by which case, say what it predicts for a scenario I have not listed (for instance, where a belief is formed by a reliable but malfunctioning cognitive module that happens to align with reality), and be honest about which of my four verdicts it forces me to overturn. If no single principle accommodates all four, present the two strongest competing candidates and explain the cost of adopting each.
