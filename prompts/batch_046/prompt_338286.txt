I recently retired after several decades in molecular biology and have begun auditing philosophy courses; I am presently working through literature on collective memory and moral obligation, particularly the tension between defenses of an ethics of remembrance and arguments—such as those associated with David Rieff—that collective forgetting can be a political and moral necessity. In a study group with other late-career professionals, I constructed the following argument, which I now find suspicious because its conclusion—that there can be no standing duty to remember past injustices—seems to undermine the very moral intuitions that drew me to the subject. I would strongly prefer to preserve the idea that grave, inadequately acknowledged wrongs generate some form of ongoing obligation, but I want to know precisely where the reasoning fails and what it costs to block the slide.

Here is the argument, set out in clearly marked steps:

(1) If an injustice was grave and remains inadequately acknowledged, there exists a standing moral duty that it be remembered; allowing it to vanish from collective awareness compounds the original harm by treating its victims as if they never mattered.

(2) Remembering, in the morally relevant sense, is not a private mental state but a causal-institutional practice: it requires maintaining archives, commemorative rituals, educational transmission, or other mechanisms that keep the event causally connected to present moral consciousness.

(3) Any failure to actively maintain these mechanisms—whether through neglect, resource reallocation, or deliberate dismantling—counts as a form of forgetting.

(4) There is no non-arbitrary boundary that limits which grave injustices fall under (1); temporal distance, number of victims, or geographic proximity all seem morally irrelevant from the victims' standpoint.

(5) Therefore, for every grave injustice in history that lacks full acknowledgment, present agents bear a corresponding obligation to maintain its memorial mechanisms.

(6) The set of such injustices is vast, and the resources required for continuous preservation are finite; satisfying all corresponding obligations simultaneously exceeds what any society or individual can do.

(7) If a principle generates obligations that are collectively impossible to fulfill, then the principle is false (ought implies can).

(8) Hence, claim (1) is false, and there is no general duty to remember.

The chain appears valid, yet I am reluctant to abandon either (1) or (7). Where does it go wrong—whether in an equivocation between active and passive forgetting, a hidden quantifier shift in distributing collective obligations across individuals, a modal slide from "can preserve some" to "can preserve all," or elsewhere? Please identify the specific premise or step I should reject, explain why it initially looked plausible, and then price the alternatives: what does each standard position—those treating remembrance as a perfect duty, those treating it as an imperfect duty of charity, and those denying any collective duty altogether—have to pay in order to reject that step? In your response, please also say how confident you are in your diagnosis and explicitly single out the point about which you are least sure.
