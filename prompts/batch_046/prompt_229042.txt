Here are my judgments about when automation eliminates or diminishes work rather than merely transforming it, and I want the principle behind them, if there is one. When welders are replaced by robotic arms and laid off, their work is genuinely eliminated, not relocated. When a lawyer uses an AI to draft contracts but still reviews, revises, and signs them, her labor is compressed, not erased. When a musician writes lyrics and melody but uses a generative system for backing tracks, the compositional work remains hers, though the arrangement labor is partly displaced. When delivery drivers are replaced by drones, the driving work is eliminated, even if new "drone-coordination" positions appear—those are different roles, not the same work preserved. When a mathematician uses software to verify a long proof, her mathematical labor is not diminished at all; verification is auxiliary to discovery. But when a philosopher uses an AI to generate objections to her own argument and then answers them, the philosophical labor feels genuinely reduced, as if part of the thinking has been outsourced. I also think that if a surgeon relies on an AI for diagnosis but performs the operation herself, her surgical work survives intact, whereas if she relies on an AI for the incision and merely supervises, something essential has been removed. Is there a coherent account—perhaps centered on judgment, authorship, or the location of skill—that delivers all of these verdicts, or must I drop one? In particular, why does AI-generated verification feel harmless to the mathematician while AI-generated objections feel harmful to the philosopher? Please state the principle precisely, say which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate, and tell me what it predicts about a case I have not listed: a therapist who uses an AI to generate interpretations of a patient's dreams, which she then delivers in her own voice during the session.
