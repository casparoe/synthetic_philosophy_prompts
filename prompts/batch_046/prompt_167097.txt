Exam question:
The scale argument for insect moral status holds that, because global insect populations are astronomically large, even a very small per-capita probability of sentience yields an enormous expected number of sentient beings, which makes human activities that harm insects—such as widespread pesticide use—potentially very morally significant. Critics reply that this reasoning treats sentience as an all-or-nothing property uniformly distributed across a homogeneous population, ignoring that consciousness might vary in intensity or qualitative character and that moral weight may depend on cognitive architecture rather than on mere presence or absence. Can the scale argument be reconstructed to accommodate graded or architecture-sensitive sentience without collapsing? If so, does the reconstructed argument strengthen, weaken, or leave unchanged the practical moral urgency?

Student answer:
The scale argument is basically a utilitarian aggregation: you take the number of insects, multiply by how likely they are to feel pain, and then multiply by the intensity of that pain, then compare it to human interests. Because there are about 10^18 insects, even with a 0.1% chance of sentience the total suffering is massive, so pesticides are a huge moral problem. This is essentially the same reasoning Peter Singer uses when he applies the principle of equal consideration to all sentient beings regardless of species. If sentience is graded, then insects probably have very weak consciousness, so you just scale down the intensity factor. Once you do that, the total expected suffering is much smaller, and the argument mostly collapses unless you also assume that every tiny bit of suffering matters equally, which is just the Repugnant Conclusion applied to bugs. So the reconstructed argument is weaker, and in practice it doesn’t change much because we still don’t know the base rate well enough.
