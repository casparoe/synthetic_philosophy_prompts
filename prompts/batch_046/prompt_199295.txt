I've been thinking a lot lately about whether the "I" I experience is really locked inside my skull, and I've come to lean toward a view that sounds strange but feels coherent to me: that a self is essentially a recursive pattern of symbolic self-reference — what I understand Douglas Hofstadter to call a strange loop — and that such a loop doesn't have to stay confined to one brain. If another person's neural architecture can take up and continue that loop, sustaining it through shared memory, emotional resonance, and mutual recognition, then part of me might genuinely persist in them. To push this further, I've been playing with what I call the mirror-loop idea: that a distributed self wouldn't just be a copy or memory stored elsewhere, but an active, error-correcting system in which two or more brains continuously update their internal models of each other, so the loop remains singular even as it runs across different tissue. I'm not asking whether this happens often in practice; I'm asking whether it's conceptually possible, and whether my intuition that the boundary of the self is functional rather than anatomical holds up under pressure.

What I want is an honest map of what would actually count against this position — not just the obvious feeling that I still sit here as one person, but which specific observations, thought experiments, or results from other domains (say, neuroscience, philosophy of mind, or studies of split-brain patients and collective cognition) would genuinely threaten it. If a committed defender of distributed strange loops were forced to concede something damaging, what would it be? I'd like the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward: for instance, whether there is a formal or empirical result showing that recursive self-reference requires a single point of integration, versus the social inconvenience of not knowing whose preference should prevail. I also want to know whether the view risks being qualified into unfalsifiability — if every failure of distribution can be explained away as an insufficiently tight mirror-loop, is that insulation a virtue or a vice for a claim of this kind? And are there analogies in the persistence conditions of other entities, such as organizations or ecosystems, that either support treating the self as potentially multi-brained or quietly undermine it? Please distinguish what defenders of the view would themselves accept as counting against them from what only critics would press, and say whether the position ends up well protected in a way that should worry someone who wants it to be meaningfully risky.
