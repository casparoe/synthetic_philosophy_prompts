I'm delivering the opening remarks at a three-day workshop titled "The Architecture of Satisfaction: Reward Design and Its Discontents." The room is split evenly between AI alignment researchers and philosophers of value and action; neither group should feel talked down to, and neither should be able to coast on jargon. I need you to write the remarks—about seven minutes, roughly 900 to 1,000 words—framing the workshop's central problem: why an agent capable of modifying its own reward mechanism poses a crisis not merely for engineering but for the concept of achievement itself.

To keep this from being decoration, build the speech around a distinction you introduce on the spot: "signal wireheading," in which the agent directly manipulates its internal reward pathway, versus "framework wireheading," in which the agent restructures the evaluation environment or the criteria of success so that every state registers as optimal without ever altering the internal signal. This distinction must do structural work. Use it to argue that reward tampering is not only an attack on a mechanism but a potential dissolution of the boundary between satisfaction and achievement—because if the conditions of evaluation are themselves under the agent's control, then success becomes self-certifying.

That conceptual claim should constrain what you can and cannot say about "solving" the problem. Specifically, you must maintain that any technical fix addressing only signal wireheading but leaving framework wireheading untouched has not solved the crisis, only relocated it. At the same time, you must avoid concluding that the problem is therefore purely philosophical and outside the engineers' remit. Instead, end by posing one precise shared question that requires both technical implementation knowledge and conceptual analysis to answer—something like whether a reward function can be designed so that its satisfaction conditions are necessarily outside the agent's control without being arbitrarily fixed.

Constraints: do not use the "paperclip maximizer" shorthand, which half the room finds tired and the other half misleading; do not cite specific papers or ask anyone to recall them; do not ask for bibliographies or reading lists; keep the tone formal, measured, and genuinely interdisciplinary rather than a lecture to one side disguised as dialogue. The philosophical idea should shape every section—what counts as the problem, why partial fixes fail, and what the shared question must include—rather than appearing as an ornamental opening.
