I run a discussion group that pairs classic cinema with philosophy of science, and we are screening Kurosawa's Rashomon next week. I would like to read the film through the framework of Jeffrey conditionalization—the rule for revising credences when one's evidence is not learned as a definite proposition but is itself observed with uncertainty, requiring a redistribution of probability across a partition of possible outcomes rather than simple conditioning on a single event. The film presents four mutually incompatible accounts of an encounter in a grove—delivered by the bandit, the wife, the dead samurai through a medium, and the woodcutter—without ever granting any narrative authoritative status. How should a rational viewer update beliefs in light of these testimonies? Does Rashomon treat the ambiguity as a purely epistemic limitation, implying that one true sequence of events occurred but our access is noisy, or does it suggest that when evidence is radically underdetermined the rational posture is to maintain genuinely partitioned credence rather than forcing convergence?

I am especially interested in whether the film's formal structure mirrors the distinction between standard Bayesian conditioning—accepting or rejecting a specific claim, such as the bandit's boastful version—and Jeffrey updating, in which one assigns weights across the entire space of possibilities (for instance, treating the wife's account as partly credible, the woodcutter's as partly credible, and distributing posterior belief accordingly). The commoner's cynical framing and the medium's theatrical possession scene complicate this picture: they imply that testimony is not merely noisy data but performative and strategically shaped by shame, self-interest, and social expectation. Where does that leave the Jeffrey framework—does it illuminate the woodcutter's final, hesitant revision of his own story, or does the film demonstrate that when evidence is deliberately manufactured rather than accidentally ambiguous, distributing credence across partitions is itself a rational error? Finally, what does the work add to the philosophical debate that a formal analysis of uncertain evidence alone cannot capture—perhaps something about the moral and existential cost of withholding judgment when honor, guilt, and human lives depend on reaching a verdict?
