Exam question: In Lakatos’s methodology of scientific research programmes, explain the distinction between a programme’s “hard core” and its “protective belt” of auxiliary hypotheses, and the distinction between a progressive and a degenerating problem shift. Then apply these distinctions to the following scenario. Programme P holds as its hard core that all perceptual experience is constructed by top-down predictive models. Over the past two decades it has generated novel predictions—e.g., about neural correlates of surprise in predictive-coding frameworks—that were subsequently confirmed. More recently, however, defenders of P have faced anomalies from sensory-substitution experiments suggesting direct perceptual access in certain conditions. In response they have added auxiliary hypotheses about “precision-weighting failures” and “context-dependent attenuation” to accommodate these findings, but these additions generate no new, independently testable predictions. Is Programme P currently undergoing a progressive or a degenerating problem shift? Justify your answer using Lakatos’s criteria.

Student answer: The hard core of Programme P is the claim that all perceptual experience is constructed by top-down predictive models. This core is protected by a negative heuristic: scientists must not direct criticism at it but should instead modify the protective belt of auxiliary hypotheses. The protective belt now includes claims about precision-weighting failures and context-dependent attenuation. Lakatos distinguishes progressive from degenerating problem shifts. A shift is progressive if it predicts novel empirical facts; it is degenerating if it only accommodates known anomalies after the fact. Programme P was clearly progressive in its earlier phase because it predicted neural correlates of surprise that were later confirmed. The recent additions to the protective belt successfully explain the sensory-substitution anomalies, so the programme retains its progressive status. Because the hard core has not been falsified, and because Lakatos holds that we should abandon a programme only when its hard core is directly refuted, Programme P should continue to be pursued. This shows that Lakatos, unlike Kuhn—who holds that anomalies eventually force a paradigm shift regardless of predictive success—allows a programme to survive as long as its core remains intact. Therefore Programme P is progressive.
