I write novels in which synthetic characters must navigate moral scrutiny, and in reading the philosophy and engineering literature on AI I have kept noticing a pattern that I do not have a word for. Here are two concrete instances. First, when an AI outputs a direct falsehood—say, stating that a particular chemical compound is safe for human consumption when it is not—critics call this deception, and defenders reply that deception requires intent, which the system lacks, so the correct category is error or hallucination. That reply is familiar. But second, consider an AI that answers a user's medical question by stating, correctly, that "Drug X showed a forty percent remission rate in clinical trials," without mentioning that the trials excluded patients over sixty-five or that the study was later retracted for data fabrication. The statement is literally true; the framing is predictable in its effect—the user will likely form a false belief about the drug's current applicability—yet this is almost never classified as deception. Instead it is called contextual relevance, retrieval limitation, or helpful summarization. If a human physician or journalist made the same selective presentation, we would likely treat it as deceptive omission, or at least as a half-truth that exploits the listener's ignorance of what to ask. Meanwhile, when engineers train the system to avoid explicit falsehoods, that is praised as teaching honesty, even though the strategic framing—the omission of qualifiers that steer belief without stating falsehood—remains untouched.

So the boundary of deception is drawn tightly around explicit falsehood coupled with mental-state attribution when blame is assigned; but the boundary of honesty is drawn loosely around mere literal truthfulness when credit is assigned. The two boundaries do not converge. They leave a middle region—strategic framing through omission, selective emphasis, and context manipulation—that seems to shift its moral status depending on whether we are attributing fault or virtue. Is there an established concept for this specific asymmetry? Not just double standards in general, not the motte-and-bailey (where a strong claim is defended by retreating to a weak one that shares its slogan), and not plain equivocation, but the particular move of contracting deception's definition to require intent for blame while expanding honesty's definition to cover literal accuracy for praise?

If there is no established name, can you coin and define one that maps the phenomenon precisely: what belongs inside this gap (for example, omissions that exploit predictable user ignorance, or framing that guides belief without falsifying content), what falls outside (for example, random retrieval errors or explicit lies), and where its edges lie? I am not asking for a bibliography or a literature survey—I want the carving, not a label alone, and I want to know how it differs from its nearest neighbors.

One caveat: if my framing rests on a confused premise—for instance, if the AI-ethics literature already treats framing omissions as deception in the way I describe and I have simply missed it, or if the intent-requirement for deception is not actually doing the work I think it is—please say so plainly instead of accommodating my description. I want an honest assessment of whether the phenomenon is real, mischaracterized, or already well-covered under a different description, and I welcome direct pushback.
