I've been thinking about this ever since I started day-trading with my roommate and also took an epistemology seminar — weird combo but it stuck with me. There's this result in finance, the no-trade theorem, that says if two people are rational and share the same prior beliefs, they shouldn't trade assets just because they've seen different information. If I'm willing to sell you my stock at your price, that already reveals my private info, so you should update and refuse the deal. Yet markets have massive trading volume, so economists explain it away with liquidity needs or risk preferences. Fine, I get it.

But then it's occured to me that the exact same structure shows up when philosophers disagree. If me and my study partner are both rational, we started with the same background evidence about some metaphysical question, and then we read different papers and now hold opposite views — shouldn't that be like me trying to sell you my belief? Once we know we disagree, the Aumann-style agreement result says we should converge. But philosophers never converge; we just dig in deeper. So either philosophers aren't rational (seems harsh), or we never actually shared common priors about basic reasoning rules, or philosophical disagreement isn't really about tracking truth at all but about identity, vibes, or social positioning.

Here's the arguement that's been eating at me and feels like it proves way too much:

(1) In any domain where rational agents share common priors, persistent disagreement after full disclosure is impossible — call this the epistemic no-trade condition.

(2) Philosophy, economics, and even politics involve persistent disagreement even after everyone has said their piece.

(3) So either the agents in these domains lack shared common priors, or the disagreement isn't genuinely epistemic.

(4) But if we don't share priors about reasoning and evidence, there's no shared ground for debate — we're running different rulebooks and talking past each other.

(5) And if disagreement is never epistemic, then changing your mind is never rational progress, just social pressure or fashion.

(6) But that dissolves inquiry itself into sociology or psychology, which can't be right.

(7) So (1) is false, and rationality places no constraint on belief divergence at all.

But wait — if (1) is false, then two people with identical information and reasoning capacity can hold opposite beliefs forever with no rational failure, which makes belief-formation look totally arbitrary. Where does the chain actually break? Is "common priors" smuggling in hidden value differences? Or is philosophical disagreement always partly about non-epistemic stakes? I want to keep the idea that disagreement can be rational and meaningful, but I don't want to end up saying all philosophical debate is just noise. Which step should I reject, and what's the cost of giving it up?
