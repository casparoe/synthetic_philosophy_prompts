As a philosopher of science with an interest in classical Indian epistemology, I wish to examine whether the Nyāya framework of pramāṇas can illuminate a modern scientific anomaly without being stretched beyond recognition. Take the 2011 OPERA collaboration’s announcement of faster-than-light neutrinos: the result depended on GPS-based time synchronization (instrumental observation), statistical inference from flight-time measurements, comparison with prior CERN velocity records, and, crucially, the collaboration’s collective decision to publish despite internal reservations (a form of testimonial authority, śabda). Analyze the episode through each of the four pramāṇas—pratyakṣa, anumāna, upamāna, and śabda—but impose two specific constraints. First, do not treat the instruments as unmediated direct perception; instead, address whether Nyāya’s distinction between savikalpaka (determinate) and nirvikalpaka (indeterminate) perception can accommodate mediated instrumental observation, and whether the loose fiber-optic cable and clock-oscillator error that eventually nullified the result should be classified as viparyaya (misapprehension) in the technical Nyāya sense. Second, evaluate whether the collaboration’s publication decision represents a failure of anumāna—specifically, an inadequate pañcāvayavī inference—or whether it constitutes a legitimate, if ultimately mistaken, reliance on śabda as collective expert testimony. Finally, state precisely where the Nyāya framework is genuinely illuminating—does it reveal a structural feature of scientific error or institutional reasoning that standard philosophy of science overlooks?—and where it remains silent or anachronistic, particularly with respect to statistical reasoning, probabilistic error analysis, and modern peer-review institutions. I am not seeking bibliographic references or a survey of the secondary literature; I want a disciplined conceptual mapping that runs in both directions, with the episode testing the theory as much as the theory explains the episode.
