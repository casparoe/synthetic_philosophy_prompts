Write a sestina—six stanzas of six lines, then a three-line envoi with all six end-words, two per line—from the perspective of the rescuer in John Taurek’s boat dilemma, able to save either one person or five others but not both. Use these six rotating end-words: one, five, tie, count, face, save. The rotation should do philosophical work: in no stanza before the envoi should “one” and “five” share a line or appear in an arithmetic relation, and in the envoi they must sit on separate lines, adjacent but unmerged, never summed.

Inject this framing: treat the choice not as weighing aggregates but as facing singular obligations. On Taurek’s anti-aggregation view, if numbers truly do not count, then “the five” lacks any collective moral status; they are not a group to be balanced against the one, but five distinct potential face-to-face encounters. Saving any one fulfills an obligation without generating a residual debt to the remaining four, since there was never a compounded duty to “the greater number” in the first place. Let the sestina’s recursive, non-cumulative rotation mirror that refusal of additive moral weight: each return of “save” should feel like a renewed singular commitment rather than an accumulation, and the tie should appear not as a resolution but as the persistent structural fact that magnitude offers no further reason.

The poem should be accurate enough that someone who knows Taurek’s argument recognizes the reasoning—especially the denial that there is a moral reason to save the greater number—while remaining a strong poem for a reader who does not. Do not resolve the dilemma with a decision; end in the envoi with “one” and “five” side by side, still uncounted.
