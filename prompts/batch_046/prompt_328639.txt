I'm preparing a long-form essay on what immersive technology is doing to our emotional architecture, and I've run into a philosophical knot I can't untangle on my own. Two weeks ago I experienced an interactive installation—part live performance, part algorithmically generated narrative—where I knew, with complete transparency, that the figure addressing me was not a person but a composite: a live actor's physical presence overlaid with dialogue produced in real time by a language model, all framed by program notes that explicitly described the mechanics. There was no illusion of reality, only a shared space with a responsive, non-human narrative agent. When the scripted arc concluded with the character's termination—a visible system shutdown—my reaction wasn't detached aesthetic appreciation. I felt something structurally closer to grief: a sustained emotional weight that lingered for days, entirely at odds with my knowledge that nothing had died, no one had suffered, and the entity in question had no mental states whatsoever.

A philosopher friend suggested I'm confronting a variant of the paradox of fiction, but with a twist: the emotional object wasn't encountered through a static text but through responsive interaction in physical co-presence. I want to understand whether my grief was rationally defensible, or whether it reveals a kind of emotional malfunction. Specifically, I'm trying to see whether standard resolutions—make-believe theories, pretense accounts, or thought-theory approaches—can accommodate emotions directed at entities we interact with in real time, knowing them to be computational constructs. Does the interactive, responsive nature of the encounter change the philosophical analysis, or is it just the paradox of fiction wearing a new interface? I need to work through the conceptual structure here, not gather sources; the question is whether there is a coherent framework that validates this emotional response without requiring me to have believed, even for a moment, that the entity was genuinely alive.
