hi, i'm taking a course on moral change and i've been stuck on this question: when norms like dueling just die out, is it because people finally see the argument, or because the honour code shifts so that the practice stops being a status claim and starts being a kind of honour bankruptcy? Bernard Williams and Kwame Anthony Appiah both point toward the second story, but i want something that doesn't just state it—i want the form itself to show the lag between social collapse and moral justification.

so could you act as a philosopher-poet, think carefull about structure first, and do a bit of brainstorming about how repetition and rhyme might mirror that lag? then write a villanelle—nineteen lines, strict ABA rhyme, two refrains—about the death of dueling as an honour bankruptcy rather than a rational victory. the first refrain should treat honour as a currency that collapses, not something you talk away; the second should claim that arguments arrive later to tidy up the wreckage. in the middle stanzas let them pass each other without meeting, and in the final couplet place them side by side without resolving which came first—because that ambiguity is the point. please make it accurate enough that someone familiar with the Williams thesis and Appiah's honour-worlds idea recognises the mechanism, but still work as a poem for someone who hasn't read them. write in british english, present it as continuous prose with no headings, bullet points, bold, or tables—just the poem in plain text with its natural line breaks, like you'd write in an email. no bibliographies, no citations, just the poem and maybe that brief brainstorming up front.
