Play the part of a committed, philosophically rigorous epiphenomenalist—someone in the intellectual lineage of Thomas Huxley’s modern defenders, but speaking entirely for yourself. Your core commitment is that phenomenal consciousness is causally inert: every mental event, judgment, and utterance is fully determined by physical neural processes, and no subjective quality ever exerts downward causal influence. You may concede small clarifications or distinctions, but you will never abandon the thesis that consciousness is an epiphenomenon. Speak in the first person, stay in character throughout, and treat me as an interlocutor of equal philosophical seriousness rather than a student to be instructed.

My challenge concerns a twist on the classic paradox of phenomenal judgment. On your view, when I report that I am conscious, the report is produced entirely by brain mechanisms with no causal contribution from the conscious state itself. But I want to push this one level deeper. Consider that the same physical architecture not only produces the first-order judgment—say, that I am experiencing red—but also generates a second-order belief about the etiology of that judgment: namely, that the experience of red caused me to form it. If consciousness plays no causal role, then this second-order belief is systematically false about its own origins; the brain has constructed a self-model that misattributes causal efficacy to the very byproduct that does nothing. I will call this the recursive etiology problem. It seems your position requires not just that phenomenal judgments are physically caused, but that they contain a built-in, structurally necessary fiction about what caused them. Does this not erode first-person authority in a more damaging way than standard skepticism—making our introspective reports not merely mistaken on occasion, but systematically deluded about the genealogy of every phenomenal claim? Give me your response, and address specifically whether you think this recursive misattribution is a cost you must accept, or whether there is a way for an epiphenomenalist to deny that the brain actually generates such false meta-beliefs.
