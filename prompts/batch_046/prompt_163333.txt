Consider a forecasting problem: will the current U.S.-led international order undergo a "hard exit" from hegemony—defined as simultaneous loss of reserve-currency dominance and withdrawal from core security guarantees—before 2050? One forecasting team applies the outside view, using a reference class of historical hegemonic transitions since 1500 (roughly a dozen documented cases) to derive a base rate near 35% for such an exit within a 25-year window. A second team rejects that reference class, arguing that modern democratic hegemons possess a different institutional metabolism: they decay primarily through internal institutional sclerosis rather than through the military overextension and fiscal crisis typical of earlier empires, so an inside-view structural model is more appropriate. Give me your credence—between 0 and 1—that the outside-view team is closer to the correct epistemic procedure for this forecast. Decompose the reasoning into at least three parts: (i) the probability that modern democratic hegemons genuinely belong to the same ontological reference class as pre-modern empires for the purpose of base-rate forecasting; (ii) the probability that the ~35% base rate is well-calibrated rather than distorted by survivorship bias or heterogeneous exit modes in the historical record; (iii) the probability that institutional sclerosis represents a sufficiently distinct decay mechanism to break the analogical inference. Assign a rough number to each part, show briefly how they combine, state your final credence, and identify which component your answer is most sensitive to. What specific evidence—historical, institutional, or theoretical—would most shift that sensitive component?
