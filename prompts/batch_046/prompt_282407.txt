After a long, somewhat circular argument with a friend over whether the Humans-Consulting-Humans (HCH) model and the broader idea of factored cognition—breaking a complex judgment into sub-judgments made by agents with limited visibility—can genuinely serve as an idealization of real human deliberation, I realized neither of us could say exactly what such a theory would have to deliver to be adequate rather than merely useful for scalable oversight. I am drafting this for a seminar on idealization in social epistemology, and I want the full specification of adequacy conditions before I evaluate any particular defense.

Please write the desiderata: what must any theory that treats HCH and factored cognition as an idealization of deliberation actually get right? Specify the phenomena it has to explain (for example, persistent disagreement that does not decompose into independent subproblems, emotional or rhetorical influence that is not purely informational, power asymmetries in who gets consulted, and the phenomenological difference between being convinced and being overruled), the distinctions it must preserve (such as between distributing cognitive labor and distributing authority, between a subproblem being technically hard and being undefined outside the larger context, and between recursive consultation and coercive aggregation), and the results from cognitive science or social epistemology it must remain consistent with—each with the concrete case or counterexample behind it. Also flag any pair of these requirements that look jointly unsatisfiable, and identify the single requirement that, if enforced rigorously, does the most damage to the standard AI-alignment framing of these tools. Present the criteria in a table with exactly these columns: Requirement, What it protects (phenomenon or distinction), Motivating case or constraint, Negotiability (Hard / Soft / Only at high cost), and Joint-satisfiability notes. After the table, provide brief prose scoring two framings against the list—the standard scalable-oversight framing and an alternative framing of democratic deliberation under conditions of epistemic fragmentation—keeping the analytical weight on the criteria themselves rather than on the scoring.
