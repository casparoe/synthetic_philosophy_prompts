In a philosophy of mathematics seminar I attend, a fellow student keeps deploying the line "independence proves there's no fact of the matter about CH" whenever the continuum hypothesis is raised. Most recently, after I asked whether there might be a correct cardinality for the power set of the natural numbers even though ZFC leaves it open, she used the slogan to dismiss the question entirely, arguing that because Cohen forcing produces models where CH fails and Gödel's constructible universe produces one where it holds—both without leaving ZFC—there is literally no correct answer to be discovered. I am sure the slogan is doing more work than it should, but I cannot separate the different jobs it might be doing.

Could you disambiguate the precise claims that could be packed into "independence means there's no fact of the matter"? I want the menu to include, at least: the purely logical claim that ZFC neither proves nor refutes CH; the methodological claim that mathematicians should therefore treat CH as permanently undecidable and stop asking metaphysical questions about it; the pluralist claim that there are equally legitimate set-theoretic universes with incompatible answers; and the metaphysical claim that the continuum itself lacks a determinate size independent of our formal choices. For each reading, indicate whether it is a trivial consequence of Gödel's completeness and incompleteness results, a substantive but defensible philosophical stance—such as a multiverse conception of set theory or a form of formalism—or actually false given that independence is fully compatible with there being a unique intended model of the set-theoretic universe. Crucially, tell me which of these readings actually warrants dismissing my original question—whether CH holds in the actual structure of sets—and which ones merely shift the topic from metaphysics to proof-theoretic limitation, leaving my question untouched but unanswered.
