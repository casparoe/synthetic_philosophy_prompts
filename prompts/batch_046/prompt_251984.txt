I've been working through Keynes's ethical and economic writings—not the macroeconomic mechanics for which he is best known, but the normative vision that runs through his essay on economic possibilities for future generations and his earlier Moore-influenced reflections on value. Most commentary fixates on his prediction that rising productivity might eventually reduce labor to a few hours per week, treating the good life as a simple matter of leisure abundance. I think that misses the deeper structure of his argument: he distinguishes between absolute needs, which can be satisfied, and relative needs, which are insatiable because they depend on social comparison, and he treats the love of money not as a private vice but as a collective mental disease that corrupts the very capacity for intrinsic goods such as love, beauty, and knowledge.

Please make an argument for the following position: once a society has secured the absolute material needs of its members, further economic growth oriented toward private luxury consumption is not merely inefficient but ethically destructive, because it reactivates the competitive logic of relative needs and thereby undermines the non-comparative conditions required for Moorean intrinsic goods. In particular, defend what we might call Keynesian aesthetic austerity—the claim that individuals have a positive moral obligation to avoid status goods and to direct any surplus productive capacity exclusively toward non-rival, non-positional public goods—on the grounds that Keynes's own epistemic skepticism about long-range forecasting denies us the rational basis for deferred gratification. You should explain how his view that distant futures are radically opaque actually supports immediate, non-accumulative investment in present intrinsic goods, and you should address the obvious objection that such austerity is paternalistic by showing how it follows from Keynes's own distinction between needs that are genuinely ours and needs that are artificially induced by the social structure of competition.
