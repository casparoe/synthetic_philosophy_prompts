I've been trying to apply the idea of tangled hierarchies—where levels that should remain distinct loop back on themselves, as in Escher's Drawing Hands—to the architecture of emotional self-awareness, and I've constructed an argument that seems to show our ordinary notion of emotional self-knowledge is incoherent. The steps are as follows.

(1) In a genuine tangled hierarchy, no level is inviolate: any level can be referenced, modified, or generated by processes operating at another level within the same loop. (2) Emotional awareness appears hierarchical: there is a first-order affective state (say, anxiety), and there can be a second-order judgment that one is anxious, which in turn feeds back and reshapes the original feeling. (3) If emotional architecture is genuinely tangled, then there is no inviolate boundary separating the first-order feeling from the second-order judgment about it; every purported "observation" is itself inside the loop it claims to observe. (4) But if there is no standpoint outside the loop, then the distinction between correct and incorrect emotional self-ascription collapses—there is no independent criterion by which a judgment "I am angry" could be false, since any corrective judgment would merely be another node in the same loop. (5) Therefore, on the tangled-hierarchy model, emotional misidentification is impossible. (6) Yet ordinary experience and clinical psychology are full of cases in which people misidentify their own emotions—interpreting physiological arousal as anger when it is fear, or mistaking envy for righteous indignation—so misidentification is clearly possible. (7) To preserve the reality of misidentification, we must reject the tangled-hierarchy model and posit an inviolate level: a raw phenomenal feeling that is immune to cognitive penetration and that serves as an independent check on second-order judgments. (8) But if such an inviolate level exists, then cognitive reflection can never genuinely reshape or integrate with feeling; emotional education, regulation, and therapeutic reinterpretation become conceptually impossible, since cognition is forever external to the affect it addresses. (9) So we face a dilemma: either emotional misidentification is impossible, or emotional education is impossible, and both options seem absurd.
