I'm delivering the opening remarks tomorrow morning for a three-day workshop on end-of-life decision-making across cultural frameworks, hosted jointly by our medical school's bioethics center and the university's comparative philosophy program. The room will be divided almost evenly: on one side are clinicians, palliative-care nurses, and policy staff who need practical guidance; on the other are scholars of Jain philosophy, comparative religion, and medical anthropology who will be listening for conceptual precision. I need the remarks to run about eight minutes and to do real structural work rather than simply ornament the proceedings. Specifically, I want to frame the entire workshop through Jain anekāntavāda—the doctrine of many-sidedness and the disciplined refusal of absolute, one-sided claims—without turning the speech into a lecture on Jain metaphysics. The audience should understand that this is not a slide into relativism ("every culture is equally right") but a rigorous practice of holding partial truths together, and that understanding should shape what I can and cannot say from the podium: I must not declare that Western autonomy-based ethics "wins" over family-centered models, nor should I pretend the disagreements are merely cosmetic differences in vocabulary.

To make this framing concrete, I want the remarks built around syādvāda—the mode of conditional, qualified assertion ("in some respect, yes; in some respect, no")—and around nayavāda, the recognition that every judgment proceeds from a particular standpoint or naya. The philosophical idea should determine the ground rules for the workshop itself: how participants will present their positions, how disagreement will be recorded, and what counts as having genuinely addressed an objection, using the logic of the seven conditional modes (saptabhaṅgī) as a procedural metaphor rather than as technical logic to be solved. I also want you to weave in one somewhat under-discussed tension: in Jain ethics, ahiṃsā (non-harm) is traditionally interpreted as extending not only to physical harm but to the epistemic and emotional harm done by insisting on a single, authoritative narrative about a dying patient's "best interest," yet clinical practice demands decisive, time-sensitive action. The speech needs to acknowledge this tension honestly and say exactly how the workshop will navigate it—through structured, conditional assertions—without resolving it prematurely or giving either side false comfort. Please write this in American English, keep the tone respectful and formally warm rather than dryly academic—there should be a human opening that acknowledges the weight of the subject matter—ensure both halves of the room feel directly addressed, and close by returning to the practical stakes of the day's first session on family conferencing in intensive care.
