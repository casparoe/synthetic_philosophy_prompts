As a retired molecular biologist who has only recently begun reading analytic metaphysics in earnest, I have been struggling to move past the standard toy examples—tables that might have been made of ice, or persons who might have had different hair colors—to understand what is genuinely at stake between counterpart theory and transworld identity. To that end, I want to frame the issue through a biologically concrete case: consider a diploid organism, O, whose genome is identical across two possible worlds except for a single silent point mutation in a non-coding region that produces no phenotypic difference whatsoever; in world W₁ the mutation is absent and O develops into a particular adult morphology, while in W₂ it is present yet the developmental trajectory remains phenotypically indistinguishable. Are the two organisms strictly the same individual, present in both worlds with one of its molecular properties varying (transworld identity), or are they merely counterparts linked by an extraordinarily high degree of qualitative similarity, since there is a genuine physical difference at the base-pair level?

With this case in mind, I would like you to predict how Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz—who of course died long before David Lewis formalized counterpart theory or the modern framing of transworld identity was articulated—would have adjudicated the matter. Given Leibniz’s commitment to complete individual concepts, the principle of the identity of indiscernibles, and his view that every monad contains all its predicates essentially, would he have maintained that O in W₁ and O in W₂ share one and the same complete concept (assuming the silent mutation is not essential to the concept), thereby endorsing a form of transworld identity? Or would he have insisted that any physical distinction, however phenotypically invisible, entails numerically distinct monads, producing a result structurally closer to a counterpart relation mediated by pre-established harmony rather than by shared identity? Please ground your answer specifically in Leibniz’s metaphysics of substance and his treatment of relational versus intrinsic properties, rather than in generic historical speculation.

Finally, I ask that you be explicit about the fragility of your own reasoning: identify which specific feature of Leibniz’s system—perhaps his denial of purely relational distinctions, or his account of how bodies relate to underlying monads—is bearing the greatest weight in your interpretation, and state precisely what alternative reading of that feature, or what overlooked textual commitment, would lead you to reverse your verdict about whether Leibniz would have favored transworld identity over a counterpart-style separation.
