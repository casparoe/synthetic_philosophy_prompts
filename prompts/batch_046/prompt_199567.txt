A philosophy blogger I follow summarized the demandingness objection to consequentialism as essentially a complaint about scale: consequentialism requires agents to maximize aggregate welfare, which in practice means donating the bulk of one's income and sacrificing personal projects, and critics object that this is psychologically unrealistic. The blogger noted that consequentialists often reply that morality is indeed demanding, so the objection confuses difficulty with falsity. But the blogger claimed this reply only addresses a superficial version of the problem. The real issue, according to the summary, is structural: because consequentialism evaluates every action solely by its contribution to the overall good, it eliminates the conceptual space for supererogation—there can be no acts that are good but not required, only degrees of compliance with a single maximal demand. Consequently, anyone who falls short of the optimal sacrifice is not merely failing to be heroic but is positively failing a duty, which collapses the moral distinction between ordinary decency and sainthood. The blogger concluded that this is not a practical difficulty but a feature of value-aggregation itself, making the theory irredeemable regardless of how modest the empirical demands turn out to be.

I want to check whether this summary treats the debate fairly. Which parts accurately represent standard consequentialist responses and the supererogation-based criticism? Where does it conflate the quantitative demandingness objection with a distinct structural argument? And is the conclusion that the problem is built into aggregation actually supported by the positions of major critics, or does it overstate the case?
