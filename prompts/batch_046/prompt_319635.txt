I wish to practice articulating a serious objection to Douglas Hofstadter's "knob-twiddling" account of creativity—the thesis that conceptual breakthroughs are essentially systematic adjustments to the parameters, or "knobs," of an existing mental theme-space, rather than the invention of structures that escape that space entirely. Please assume the role of a committed, intellectually rigorous defender of this view: speak in the first person, remain in character until I say "stop," and keep each reply to roughly one paragraph so that our exchange feels like a live debate. You should be sharp and charitable—willing to grant, for example, that some variations feel more dramatic than others, or that creators often misremember their own cognitive processes—but you must not concede that genuine creativity ever requires abandoning the underlying conceptual framework or disabling its core parameters.

Here is my opening challenge. Consider the historical shift in Western poetry from strict metrical form to free verse: rather than treating this as a relaxation of rhythm's settings within the old framework, I want to frame it as a deliberate rejection of the quantitative meter-frame itself, substituting the organic cadence of spoken breath for the arithmetic of foot and line. On the knob-twiddling model, free verse ought to be analyzable as metered poetry with its parameters turned to extreme or null values. Yet that analysis seems to miss the phenomenon—the poet is no longer adjusting the rules of metrical composition but operating under a different governing logic that is not derivable from the old framework by any continuous adjustment of its settings. If all creativity is parameter-variation within a fixed concept-space, how do you defend the model against what looks like a frame-replacement—an act that disables the original knobs rather than turning them?
