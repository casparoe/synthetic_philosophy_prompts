At a recent workshop on democratic theory and institutional design, two participants—I'll call them Mara and Jonah—fell into a sustained disagreement about whether constitutional entrenchment clauses are genuinely analogous to Ulysses contracts. I was sitting nearby and genuinely could not tell who was carrying the argument. They went through four rounds.

(1) Mara opened by saying the analogy collapses at the consent level: in a personal Ulysses contract, the agent who binds herself and the agent who is later tempted are one and the same diachronic person, but in constitutional precommitment the future citizens who face the binding rule are not the ones who ratified it. Without personal identity across generations, she argued, there is no future self being protected from its own weakness, only strangers being constrained by the dead hand of the past.

(2) Jonah replied that this assumes an implausibly individualist ontology of the state. A constitutional order has institutional continuity conditions—legal personality, territorial jurisdiction, procedural succession—that make it the same collective agent over time. Just as Odysseus remains Odysseus despite cellular replacement, the polity remains the same subject of obligations, so inherited consent is not a category error but a feature of collective agency.

(3) Mara came back with a more targeted objection. Even granting institutional identity, she said, the psychological mechanism that makes a Ulysses contract rational is absent in democratic amendment. Odysseus anticipates a specific future weakness—madness induced by the Sirens—and arranges in advance to be restrained. But constitutional amendments are rarely best explained as weakness of will; they are usually defended as moral corrections or adaptations to changed circumstances. Calling democratic change a temptation that needs tying to the mast therefore misdescribes its normative structure. The bound party does not experience itself as the crew failing to resist the Sirens; it experiences itself as the legitimate sovereign revising an error.

(4) Jonah answered that this romanticizes democratic change. Many amendments—especially those relaxing fiscal rules, weakening rights protections during emergencies, or expanding executive power—are driven by exactly the short-term pressures a Ulysses contract is meant to block. He then proposed what he called a nested second-order contract: rather than binding specific policy outcomes, the original constituent power binds only the amendment threshold itself, making it costly but not impossible for future generations to alter particular provisions. The crew can still untie specific ropes, he said, but only by overcoming a deliberately constructed procedural friction that preserves the binding logic without denying all future agency.

They asked me to referee. Please adjudicate move by move: rate each of the four moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with brief justification for each rating. Then say which party is more right overall, and identify what both of them are missing—particularly whether Jonah's nested second-order model actually solves Mara's motivational-structure objection, or whether it merely relocates the problem one level up. Work with the arguments as presented; do not invoke specific named scholars or texts.
