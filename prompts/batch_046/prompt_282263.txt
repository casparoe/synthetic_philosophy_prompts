I am a final-year undergraduate taking a module in epistemic normativity and practical reason, and for my assessed portfolio I need to operationalise the wrong-kind-of-reasons problem into a reusable practitioner checklist. I work part-time as a senior writing-centre tutor, running brief ten-minute consultations in which students ask whether they ought to revise a central claim in an essay. Please design an ordered decision procedure—diagnostic questions in sequence, stopping conditions, explicit flags for irreducible judgment calls, and clear separation of evidential or conceptual reasons (the right kind for belief-revision) from prudential, institutional, or grade-oriented reasons (the wrong kind)—that I can apply without specialised philosophical training. The procedure must handle hard hybrid cases, such as a student who seeks counter-evidence solely because they fear a poor mark yet the counter-evidence is genuinely decisive, or an assignment that requires defending a specific thesis regardless of private conviction. Apply it to three worked cases: a student who finds a strong counter-argument only after reading an examiner’s prior feedback; a student assigned to defend a particular philosophical position whom they now find implausible; and a case in which the procedure advises against revision despite intense pragmatic pressure, producing an answer I will find unwelcome. State precisely where the procedure necessarily stops and defers to values, and what it cannot settle. Write the response in British English.
