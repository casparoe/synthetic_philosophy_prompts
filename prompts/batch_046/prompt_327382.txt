I am a high school student preparing an independent study presentation for my philosophy elective on Buddhist metaphysics and personal identity. I need a formal, reusable diagnostic decision tree that sorts a respondent's actual commitments—not the philosophical label they prefer—into one of several positions on whether and how a person persists through change, with careful attention to the Buddhist anatta doctrine that there is no permanent, unchanging self underlying the five aggregates.

The tree should begin with a concrete case—such as gradual neuron replacement over years, a split-brain scenario with divergent memories, or a monk claiming to recall past lives—and proceed through a short sequence of yes/no or either/or branch nodes. Each node must be phrased as a question a respondent can answer without knowing technical jargon, and each must test one genuinely divisive commitment: for example, whether persistence requires spatiotemporal continuity of a living organism; whether branching destroys identity; whether conventional designation can fully explain moral responsibility; or whether continuity conditions can be graded and context-dependent rather than all-or-nothing.

The leaves must include: (1) a standard psychological-continuity view with the no-branching clause; (2) a standard animalist view; (3) a narrative-identity view; (4) a deflationary no-self option that treats personhood as a conventional designation without an underlying substrate; and (5) a hybrid position I am developing, which I call the stream-without-streambed view. This fifth view treats the skandhas as a causally continuous process-series but denies both a substrate and a single unified psychological thread, allowing instead graded, context-dependent continuity conditions—so that moral accountability might track a narrower causal chain than biological survival does.

Build the tree from the logical space of answers rather than from a list of named theories: first map the combinations of answers the nodes allow, then locate the five leaves within that space, and flag any unoccupied combinations—such as a view that accepts strict animalist continuity together with full psychological branching—with a brief diagnosis of why that combination is unstable, incoherent, or merely unexplored. The instrument should be usable by a high school audience: it must not require knowledge of Abhidharma technical literature, but it must not misrepresent the core anatta claim.

Please include three worked applications: one involving gradual bodily replacement, one involving split-brain divergence, and one involving the monk's past-life claim. For each, trace the path through the tree that the respondent's answers suggest, and explicitly mark the nodes where the tree cannot settle the answer and human judgment or further metaphysical commitment must take over. Include at least one case that lands in the stream-without-streambed leaf despite initially seeming to fit psychological continuity, and explain which answer pattern drives that result—especially if that result conflicts with common intuition.

Finally, state clearly what the tree can and cannot do: which distinctions it preserves, which it collapses for usability, and where it will misfire or require an editor's decision rather than delivering an automatic classification. Present the deliverable as a polished handout suitable for an academic setting, with the branch nodes listed in order, the leaf descriptions in one sentence each, and the three case applications formatted consistently. Do not include a bibliography, reading list, or citation requests; the procedure must stand on its own for a user without web access.
