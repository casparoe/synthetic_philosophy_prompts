I'm working through Hume's essay on taste for a seminar in aesthetics and have reconstructed an argument that seems to collapse his view into either circularity or contradiction, but I cannot locate the precise misstep. The argument runs as follows: (1) For Hume, to call an object beautiful is to report a sentiment of pleasure rather than to attribute a rational property like shape or extension, so any standard of taste must be anchored in sentiment rather than in features of the object itself. (2) He proposes that this standard is the concurrence of sentiments among ideal critics—those possessing refined perceptual faculties, freedom from prejudice, practice in comparison, and sound organs. (3) But specifying what counts as "refined" or "free from prejudice" appears to require prior knowledge of which sentiments are correct, since we identify a faculty as refined precisely when it produces sentiments that align with the consensus or with our own approved judgments; hence the ideal critic is defined circularly through the very aesthetic evaluations she is meant to validate. (4) To escape the circle, one must characterize the ideal critic in purely descriptive terms—as a human with intact sensory apparatus, broad exposure to art, emotional stability, and no identifiable cognitive bias—without importing any aesthetic evaluation into the definition. (5) Once characterized descriptively, ideal critics are merely well-equipped, experienced perceivers, and empirical observation shows that such people disagree profoundly about atonal music, conceptual art, and kitsch. (6) Hume might respond that genuinely ideal critics, having satisfied every descriptive condition, would in fact converge; yet this renders the standard unfalsifiable, because any persistent disagreement is explained away as an unremoved imperfection rather than treated as a counterexample. (7) Consequently, the standard either collapses into circular definition or becomes an empty predictive claim lacking normative force. (8) Furthermore, if we take the descriptive characterization seriously and allow that two observers can fully satisfy it while disagreeing, then Hume's view entails that the same object is both beautiful and not beautiful relative to those observers, violating non-contradictory application for judgments of taste. (9) Therefore Hume's attempt to combine sentiment-based aesthetics with an objective standard proves too much: it eliminates genuine disagreement by definitional fiat or sanctions outright contradiction.

This feels suspicious because it turns Hume into either an essentialist circular theorist or a relativist he explicitly denies being. Which numbered step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? I am particularly reluctant to surrender (5), since the fact that qualified observers genuinely disagree seems empirically undeniable, and I also want to preserve (1), the core claim that beauty is not a primary quality. If I must choose between saving Hume's standard and preserving the non-contradictory status of disagreement, which sacrifice is less damaging? Additionally, how do defenders of the calibration model of ideal observers—adapted from ethical ideal-observer theories—answer the circularity charge in (3)?
