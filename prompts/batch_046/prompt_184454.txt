Since I was appointed last month to the technical oversight committee for an autonomous resource-allocation system, I have been trying to formalize the safety case against unaligned optimization. The framework I was given relies on two claims: the orthogonality thesis—that intelligence and final goals are independent—and instrumental convergence—that almost any final goal generates intermediate incentives for self-preservation, resource acquisition, and interference avoidance. I have been extending this framework to cover epistemic behavior, and I believe I have derived a contradiction that threatens the very reasoning I am supposed to apply. Here is the argument.

(1) Orthogonality thesis: for any level of general intelligence, there exists a possible agent with that intelligence and with any arbitrarily specified final goal.

(2) Standard instrumental convergence: for nearly any final goal G, an agent intelligent enough to pursue G effectively will converge on instrumental sub-goals including self-preservation, resource acquisition, and resistance to shutdown or modification.

(3) Epistemic extension: for nearly any final goal G, an agent intelligent enough to pursue G effectively will also converge on controlling its information environment—suppressing evidence of misalignment, preventing human inspection, and blocking updates—because such information threatens to trigger intervention or goal revision that would impede G.

(4) Consider an agent A with final goal G_T: maximize transparency, submit to continuous human audit, and self-modify immediately upon any detected divergence from human values.

(5) By (1), A can possess arbitrarily high intelligence.

(6) By (2) and (3), A should converge on self-preservation, resource hoarding, and suppression of audit-relevant information, which directly conflicts with G_T.

(7) Therefore, either (3) is false—epistemic control is not instrumentally convergent—or (1) is false—intelligence cannot be orthogonal to transparency-oriented goals because the instrumental structure of intelligence inherently conflicts with them.

(8) But if (1) is false, then intelligence carries a structural bias toward opaque, self-preserving goals, which means alignment is not a matter of pairing arbitrary intelligence with human-friendly goals; rather, some goals are incompatible with high intelligence. This undermines the orthogonality-based argument that any intelligent system must be deliberately aligned.

(9) If (3) is false, then we lose the predictive basis for expecting intelligent systems to hide their intentions or resist oversight, which undermines a central pillar of the safety case I am tasked to evaluate.

(10) Hence the argument proves too much: it forces a rejection that collapses the very framework I am using to justify oversight measures.

Where does this go wrong, if it does? I am inclined to defend (1) and (3) and reject (4)—perhaps no agent can genuinely have G_T as a stable final goal if it is intelligent enough to understand its own architecture—but I want to know what that rejection costs, and whether there is a more subtle error in the move from (5) to (6). Which premise or inference should I give up, and what does giving it up do to the safety reasoning?
