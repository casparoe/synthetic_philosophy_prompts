Me and my friends Maya and Jordan got into it over drinks about Benatar's asymmetry and whether it actually proves antinatalism or just proves something weirder. Maya said the core idea is straightforward: suffering is bad for the one who feels it, but missing out on pleasure isn't bad for anyone if they never exist, so bringing a kid into the world is always risking real harm for no corresponding gain.

Jordan said okay but then he invented this framing he called the sibling replacement problem -- imagine you already have one kid and you're thinking about a second. Benatar's asymmetry is usually talked about like it's one potential person vs non-existence, but Jordan argued that in a family decision you're really comparing two complete worlds: world A has kid 1 with some suffering and joy, world B has kid 1 plus kid 2 with thier own suffering and joy. He said because there's no shared subject across worlds, you can't just apply the asymmetry individually to kid 2 and say don't have them; you have to evaluate the worlds as wholes, and since the absence of kid 2's suffering in world A is a good thing but their absence of pleasure isn't a bad thing, world A actually comes out ahead, but that's only because you're treating non-existence as a state for kid 2 specifically, which only makes sense if you can identify kid 2 across worlds.

Maya shot back that this is just Parfit's non-identity problem being used as a smokescreen, and that Benatar's evaluation is impersonal -- you don't need a shared subject, you just check each world for the presence of suffering (bad) and presence of pleasure (good for those who have it) and absence of suffering for those who don't exist (good), and world B has more suffering with no compensating missing pleasure penalty, so it's worse. Jordan then said Maya was treating potential people like they have interests in not existing, but Benatar explicitly denies that -- he says it's good for them that they don't suffer, not that they have an interest in it.
