I facilitate a philosophy seminar for about 14 graduate students, meeting once for 75 minutes in a room with movable tables. Design a runnable in-class game that makes participants feel the backward induction paradox in the centipede game—not as an observed result, but as a lived tension between accepting the logical proof and acting within the interaction. To inject common knowledge of rationality into the strategic environment itself, require that before any play begins each student anonymously writes a brief statement declaring whether they accept the backward-induction argument for a six-round interaction; these statements are then read aloud and posted visibly, so that belief in the proof becomes common knowledge. The game should be played in rotating pairs using only point tokens (no real money—accumulated points determine a collective seminar outcome, such as selecting the next reading or funding refreshments). Provide the exact six-round payoff schedule, the rules for taking versus passing at each round, and a rotation scheme ensuring every student plays at least twice. Embed in the rules a “shadow termination” mechanism: at the start of each round a card is drawn from a deck that, if revealed, would end the interaction, but the deck in fact contains no termination cards; the card is revealed only if both players pass, meaning the mechanism is present but never activates—altering the atmosphere of risk without altering the payoffs. Include any necessary materials and instructions. Then provide a debrief that maps specific player moments—such as an immediate defection following unanimous acceptance of the proof, or sustained cooperation despite it—onto the formal premises of backward induction (common knowledge of rationality, sequential consistency, and the absence of binding commitments). Be explicit about what will predictably go wrong the first time (for example, students tailoring their anonymous statements to influence opponents, or the shadow mechanism being ignored entirely), and how to handle it. Finally, state frankly which structural feature of the logical paradox this physical staging cannot reproduce—such as the infinite regress of belief attribution in an unbounded game or the pure theoretical absence of uncertainty about the endpoint—and explain how you will supply that missing element verbally during the debrief.
