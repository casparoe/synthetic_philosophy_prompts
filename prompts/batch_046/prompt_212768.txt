Consider the institutional practice, common across several Buddhist traditions, in which families commission post-mortem rituals—merit-transfer ceremonies, memorial offerings, and registry entries—that explicitly address a named deceased person by kinship relation and vital dates, directing the generated benefit to that specific recipient in whatever realm they now inhabit. The same institutions maintain records in which the dead are assigned posthumous religious names and entered into monastic or ancestral lineages, treating the deceased as the continuous bearer of that assigned identity and its associated obligations. Doctrinally, however, these institutions affirm anatta: there is no permanent, unitary self or soul that persists through death, and rebirth is understood as the causal continuation of aggregates rather than the transmigration of an entity. The ritual and bureaucratic form therefore appears to treat personal identity as determinately continuous—there is a specific “mother” or “ancestor” who receives merit, and a specific deceased individual who “holds” a posthumous name—while the official metaphysics denies precisely the kind of enduring subject that could serve as that recipient.

What philosophical commitments about personal identity does this institutional complex presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory: does the practice require only a conventional or pragmatic self (a pudgala) that can be named and addressed without ontological commitment to a soul? Does it depend on a reductionist “process-identity” view, in which the causal stream itself is the legitimate bearer of ritual obligations across states? Or does it quietly presuppose a substantialist view that the doctrine of anatta officially rejects? Distinguish strictly what the practice requires from what merely fits it well—could a hardline eliminativist who denies even conventional personal continuity license these rituals and registries, or would that undermine their institutional logic? Further, examine whether the practice is internally consistent: it addresses the deceased as an existing recipient of merit (suggesting they persist in some form), yet also teaches that they may have achieved liberation (leaving no recipient at all) or been reborn as an entirely different being (leaving only an unrelated causal stream).
