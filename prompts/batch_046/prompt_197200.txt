Student answer: Jaspers distinguishes four types of guilt. Criminal guilt is strictly individual: only specific persons who committed illegal acts can bear it, so a nation as such can never be criminally guilty. Political guilt applies collectively to citizens because they belong to the state whose government acted; since citizenship is continuous across generations, this guilt is inherited by descendants who remain part of the same political body, meaning later generations remain politically guilty until full reparative obligations are discharged. Moral guilt concerns one’s own conscience and ethical failures, which Jaspers treats as strictly personal—no one can bear my moral guilt for me—so it cannot be shared. Finally, metaphysical guilt is essentially moral guilt scaled up to the cosmic level: it is the specific guilt of survivors and witnesses who failed to prevent suffering they observed, and like moral guilt it attaches only to particular individuals rather than to groups. Therefore, saying “Germany is guilty” makes sense only as political guilt, and only for the direct generation; metaphysical guilt never applies collectively, and moral guilt is always private.
