I sit on the public-history advisory board for our university museum, and we are finalising the interpretive framework for a permanent exhibition on the state-engineered assimilation and demographic collapse of the River Valley indigenous communities during the period 1870–1930. The curatorial team has proposed titling the gallery "Genocide in the River Valley," but this has divided the board. Several legal historians on the committee note that, under the 1948 UN Convention on the Prevention and Punishment of the Crime of Genocide, the protected groups are defined narrowly and the required specific intent to destroy may be difficult to establish for policies that combined forced relocation, suppression of language, and ecological dispossession. By contrast, other members invoke Raphael Lemkin's original 1944 formulation, which encompassed the destruction of cultural structures and the deliberate undermining of a group's way of life, arguing that the Convention's narrower legal taxonomy should not govern moral and historical naming.

Compounding this is a conceptual concern raised by one of our external consultants: that imposing any single international legal category risks "ontological crowding," whereby recognising one form of harm—group destruction—systematically obscures co-constitutive harms, particularly the ecological dispossession that was inseparable from the assimilation policy, and that recent critical heritage scholarship suggests atrocity exhibitions ought to privilege the victims' own categorical frameworks rather than retrofitting them into legal instruments designed for state prosecution. Our chair has asked me to draft the board's decision memo for circulation before next Thursday's vote. The memo should recommend a concrete curatorial course of action—whether to adopt the proposed title, to use qualified language, or to reject the term altogether—with a clear time frame for implementation, and it must engage seriously with the strongest argument on the opposing side rather than merely restating either the legal definition of genocide or the moral urgency of naming.

Please draft this memo in British English, with a formal tone appropriate for circulation to board members and museum stakeholders. Your recommendation should be actionable, your reasoning should address both the Lemkin-inspired broad conception and the narrower Convention criteria, and you should include an honest assessment of the risk that our chosen framing might misrepresent or marginalise dimensions of harm that fall outside the category we select.
