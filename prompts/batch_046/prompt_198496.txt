Below is a short-answer exam question from an advanced philosophy-of-mind course, together with a student’s response. The course covered illusionism about phenomenal consciousness—the view that phenomenal properties do not exist and that reports of them stem from introspective misrepresentation—alongside representationalism and broad eliminativism about mental states.

The exam asked students to do three things: (1) distinguish illusionism about phenomenal consciousness from general eliminativism about mental states; (2) explain how an illusionist might deploy the “refrigerator light” analogy to account for the apparent continuity of conscious experience; and (3) assess the self-defeating objection that if phenomenal consciousness is illusory, then the illusion itself must be phenomenally conscious, and explain whether this objection succeeds.

Student answer:

Illusionism denies that there is any “what it’s like” quality to experience. In this respect it is essentially behaviorism: both reject subjective mental life, though illusionists allow that people sincerely report having experiences. The refrigerator-light analogy shows that consciousness seems continuous only because checking for it produces the appearance, like a fridge bulb that seems always on because opening the door turns it on. This proves phenomenal properties are absent in the gaps between checks. The self-defeating objection fails, since the illusionist can treat the error as a purely cognitive misrepresentation with no qualitative feel of its own. Nevertheless, illusionism is really just an extreme version of representationalism: both views agree that qualitative feels are genuinely real, and illusionists merely claim we misjudge their metaphysical status.
