I run a philosophy workshop at a community college, about 14 people, one 80-minute session. We're working through Nietzsche's essay on history and life — the three modes, monumental, antiquarian, critical, and how each can both serve and choke living. I want an activity that makes them feel the suffocation part, not just understand it conceptually.

Design a structured role-play called the 'Kreutz Archive Crisis' (Kreutz is a made-up rust-belt town whose old mill just burnt down). Split the group into three committees of ~4, plus one observer. Each committee is assigned one historical mode: monumental (looking for heroic models to rebuild identity), antiquarian (preserving every trace for its own sake), critical (judging which past elements poison the present). Give them 12 artifact cards (invented items: a worker's glove, a company ledger, a protest banner, etc.) and a physical constraint: they have only 5 slots on a shared 'town memory shelf' and must decide collectively which stay, which go, and which get elevated or destroyed.

The rules should force the conflict: monumental can only advocate for artifacts that show greatness and must build a literal pedistal for one; antiquarian must defend every artifact they save with a chain of continuity to another saved item; critical must identify at least one artifact as harmful to present life and physically remove it from the table. They have 30 minutes to negociate, then present the shelf.

Then a debrief that maps what happened onto Nietzsche's framework — where did each mode actually help the town imagine a future, and where did it become paralyzing? Also be honest about what this simulation can't reproduce: the actual temporal weight of living under one mode for years, or the individual psychological need for roots versus greatness.

Also include a timeline (setup, negotiation, presentation, debrief), materials list, and a note on what will predictably go wrong — probably the critical group dominates and everyone feels attacked, or the antiquarian group refuses to let anything be removed. Tell me how to handle that without stepping in as lecturer.

Keep it casual — this is for my syllabus draft, so a typo or two is fine.
