Exam question:
Some predictive theories in the social sciences are said to be 'right but early': the causal mechanism they identify remains operative, but the crisis they forecast has been repeatedly postponed or averted in the observable short run. Using Malthus's argument about population growth and food supply as your central case, address the following. (a) State the geometric-ratio and arithmetic-ratio premises and show how they generate the prediction of inevitable positive checks. (b) Explain how technological innovation and the demographic transition function as 'rescue hypotheses' that preserve the core Malthusian mechanism while accounting for the failure of near-term predictions. (c) Evaluate whether accepting these rescue hypotheses renders the theory unfalsifiable, and whether there is a principled distinction between legitimate theoretical rescue and ad hoc immunization. To illustrate the general logic concretely, apply your analysis to a hypothetical long-run resource model—call it the cumulative extraction-cost curve—in which extraction costs escalate non-linearly while substitution technologies improve only linearly.

Student answer:
Malthus argued that human population tends to grow geometrically (1, 2, 4, 8, 16...) whereas food production can grow only arithmetically (1, 2, 3, 4, 5...). From this mismatch he inferred that population must eventually outstrip available resources, producing positive checks such as famine, epidemic, and conflict. Over the past two centuries this global outcome has not materialized, chiefly because of agricultural innovation—exemplified by the Green Revolution—and because of the demographic transition, in which rising prosperity leads societies to lower fertility voluntarily. These developments are classic rescue hypotheses: they do not deny resource scarcity, but they delay the point at which scarcity becomes binding. Indeed, Malthus essentially anticipated the demographic transition through his own concept of preventive checks, particularly moral restraint and delayed marriage, so critics who treat him as blind to behavioral adaptation are unfair.

This raises a falsifiability concern. If every apparent failure can be met by citing a new technological or behavioral rescue, the theory risks becoming empirically empty. I believe Malthusianism escapes this charge, because the underlying mechanism can still be tested directly: should innovation stall, resource pressure ought to become visible almost immediately. The cumulative extraction-cost curve makes this concrete. Because extraction costs rise exponentially while substitution improves linearly, there exists a mathematically determinate crossover point at which technology can no longer compensate; this supplies specific conditions and a rough timeline, distinguishing the model from an ad hoc defense that merely asks us to wait another five centuries. Consequently, Malthus should be read not as refuted but as right but early—the mechanism is intact, and we have not yet reached the global dominance point where rescue effects are overwhelmed.
