The exam question posed to the students was as follows. Daniel Dennett's intentional stance treats the attribution of beliefs and desires as a predictive strategy rather than an ontological commitment. Apply this framework to a hypothetical system—call it Meridian—which operates via predictive coding across distributed modules but lacks any global workspace or unified self-model. Should we adopt the intentional stance toward Meridian? In your answer, clearly distinguish the intentional stance from both the physical stance and the design stance, and discuss whether the absence of evolutionary history affects the justification for mental-state attribution.

Below is one student's answer. Please assign a grade on a standard scale and provide the specific comments that justify it, addressing each section of the response. Do not signal your own view of the correct answer; assess this submission strictly on its internal merits. Please write your assessment in British English.

Student answer: Dennett's intentional stance is the strategy of treating an entity as a rational agent with beliefs and desires in order to predict its behavior. This differs from the physical stance, where we predict behavior using physics and mechanics, and from the design stance, where we predict behavior based on the system's engineered purpose and functional architecture. For Meridian, the physical stance is intractable because of the enormous number of parameters and distributed weights, so the intentional stance is the appropriate one.

However, Dennett holds that the intentional stance is only justified if the system possesses original intentionality derived from evolutionary history. Since Meridian is an artificial system designed in a laboratory, it lacks this evolutionary origin. Therefore, we should not adopt the intentional stance toward Meridian; instead, we should stick to the design stance, interpreting its outputs as functional responses to inputs without attributing genuine mental content.

Furthermore, Dennett requires that any system interpreted via the intentional stance must satisfy rationality constraints consistently. Meridian frequently produces contradictory outputs across different prompts, which violates this consistency requirement. Consequently, adopting the intentional stance toward Meridian is not merely less useful but conceptually incoherent, because the rationality assumption is a necessary precondition for the stance rather than an idealization.
