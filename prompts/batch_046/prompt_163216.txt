I've been working through Hannah Arendt's reflections on the Eichmann trial for a graduate seminar on political evil, and I'm struck by how her concept of thoughtlessness—her claim that Eichmann was not a monster but someone incapable of thinking from another's standpoint—seems eerily applicable to modern bureaucratic systems that operate without sustained human judgment in the loop. In a recent conversation with friends about algorithmic welfare administration—specifically, a technically feasible but still largely hypothetical predictive eligibility framework in which machine-learning models automatically suspend social benefits based on behavioral risk scores, with no individual administrator reviewing the particular case—we wondered whether Arendt would see this as a new form of the banality of evil. The system is not malicious; its designers are often well-meaning; yet the result is a distributed, automated cruelty that no one specifically intends.

I would like you to reconstruct, in Arendt's own conceptual vocabulary, what she would likely say about such an arrangement. How would her distinction between thinking and knowing, her critique of rule-following bureaucratic conduct, and her specific observations about Eichmann's failure of moral imagination apply to administrative decisions made by algorithm? Would she argue that removing the human administrator eliminates even the minimal moral agency that Eichmann retained, thereby producing something worse than banal evil—perhaps a structural absence of thought that requires no subject at all? Or would she resist extending her framework beyond human actors and insist that evil, even banal evil, must always be traceable to a thinking (or failing-to-think) person? Please build your answer on Arendt's actual arguments about judgment, responsibility, and the conditions of moral failure, but do not hesitate to extend them speculatively to this technology of governance that emerged after her lifetime. I am not looking for citations or a reading list—just a careful, conceptually precise reconstruction of her probable position.
