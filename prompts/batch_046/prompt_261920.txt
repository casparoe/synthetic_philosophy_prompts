Here is an argument I have been turning over since working through the Zhuangzian butterfly transformation, and I now find it suspicious because it seems to generate global skepticism from a local phenomenological observation. (1) If, from the first-person perspective, state A and state B are phenomenally indistinguishable, then the subject is not epistemically entitled to believe they are in A rather than B. (2) Zhuangzi upon waking cannot distinguish being Zhuangzi who dreamed he was a butterfly from being a butterfly now dreaming he is Zhuangzi; the first-person phenomenology is symmetric. (3) By parity, I cannot now distinguish being awake from being in a dream that replicates waking phenomenology exactly, since any criterion I might invoke—coherence, temporal continuity, or external verification—could itself be part of the dream. (4) So I am not entitled to believe I am awake. (5) Most of my beliefs—about logic, about the existence of other minds, about the reliability of reasoning—presuppose that I am awake and not dreaming. (6) Therefore I am not entitled to hold any of those beliefs either. (7) But the belief that I am not entitled to hold those beliefs is itself one of them, since it depends on reasoning that presupposes wakefulness. (8) So the argument undermines the very entitlement it needs to establish its conclusion. I feel like something slipped between (3) and (4), or between (5) and (6), but I also worry the problem is in (1). I would most like to keep (1), because giving it up seems to license belief in any phenomenally coherent scenario—including a brain-in-a-vat scenario—without epistemic cost. Which step should I reject, why did it look plausible, and what does each rejection cost me?
