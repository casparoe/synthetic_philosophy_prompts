I've recently been serving on a university ethics board that reviews discretionary fund allocation between direct student welfare programs and prestige-building capital projects, and the experience has made me think much more carefully about whether prioritarianism really escapes the leveling-down objection in the clean way standard presentations suggest. I'm preparing an essay for an advanced philosophy of public policy course—not asking for bibliographies or citations—on whether there is a structurally hidden version of the leveling-down problem that applies specifically to prioritarian aggregation when welfare is interdependent rather than atomistic.

The familiar defense holds that prioritarianism weights benefits to the worse-off more heavily but never values equality for its own sake, so it should never reduce the well-being of the better-off unless doing so produces direct, redistributable gains for the worse-off. But consider contexts where welfare metrics are genuinely interdependent: the baseline well-being of the worst-off may be actively depressed—not merely relatively lower—by the presence of extreme advantages elsewhere, through mechanisms such as political capture of public budgets, relative-deprivation effects on mental health, or fixed-resource competition that makes luxury goods structurally costly even when they are not directly funded from the same pool. In such cases, prioritarian aggregation might appear to recommend suppressing high-end benefits not because they cause harm that can be redistributed, but because their mere existence lowers the weighted sum by eroding the welfare of those already worst off.

Write an essay evaluating whether this "interdependent leveling-down" genuinely threatens prioritarianism, or whether it collapses into either a disguised egalitarianism or a misapplication of the prioritarian formula. Weigh the principal arguments with care: on one side, that prioritarianism should adhere strictly to absolute welfare and reject relational adjustments as outside its scope; on the other, that any empirically adequate welfare metric already incorporates these positional and structural effects, so ignoring them renders prioritarianism naively abstract. Reach and defend a specific conclusion—whether prioritarianism survives intact only if we restrict the welfare metric, whether it must embrace a limited form of leveling-down after all, or whether the objection reveals that prioritarianism and egalitarianism converge in practical application. In doing so, separate what is conceptually settled—such as the formal distinction between prioritarian weighting and equality of outcome—from what remains genuinely contested among philosophers of distributive justice, particularly regarding whether well-being should be measured in purely absolute or partly relational terms, and do not present either contested view as the settled consensus.
