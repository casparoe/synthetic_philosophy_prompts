I serve as the senior governance advisor to the Transboundary River Basin Democratic Commission, a body chartered to design the voting rules for the proposed Great North Waters Compact. Our mandate is concrete: we must issue a formal recommendation, with a clear bottom line, on who belongs to the demos for the binding referendum that will ratify or reject the Compact's water-allocation framework. The Compact's territory spans three provinces and crosses an international border; within it live citizens, long-term non-citizen residents, seasonal agricultural workers with multi-year contracts, members of two indigenous nations whose treaty rights predate the provinces, and communities immediately downstream who will be affected by allocation decisions but reside outside the jurisdiction. A coalition of delegates has proposed what they call a nested-demos model: rather than a single uniform electorate, different articles of the Compact would be ratified by different constituencies—citizens alone for sovereignty clauses, all permanent residents for local infrastructure provisions, affected downstream representatives selected by lot for environmental-impact articles, and appointed guardians for provisions binding future generations. The opposing delegation argues that democratic legitimacy requires a single, coextensive demos defined by the all-subjected principle—anyone subject to the Compact's coercive authority—warning that nesting fractures accountability and creates a recursive legitimacy trap, since the current demos would decide, in effect, who gets to vote on changing the membership rules themselves.

Please draft the Commission's decision memo. You should act as an expert in democratic theory and institutional design, and think carefully through the philosophical stakes before reaching a recommendation. Begin by briefly mapping the distinct dimensions of the boundary problem as they appear in this case—citizenship, affected interests, subjection to law, and recursive self-definition—then recommend whether the Commission should adopt the nested-demos model, reject it in favor of a unified all-subjected electorate, or approve it only under specific conditions (such as prohibiting recursive self-amendment or requiring supermajorities for membership changes). Your memo should have an actionable bottom line with a time frame, for example recommending adoption with named conditions by the March session. You must treat the strongest argument on the losing side seriously: if you reject nesting, explain why the accountability objection outweighs the inclusion gains; if you endorse nesting, explain how you avoid the recursive legitimacy trap. Crucially, separate what is philosophically settled from what remains genuinely contested among democratic theorists—do not present a contested view, such as whether affected interests alone generate democratic claims or whether future generations can be represented without present consent, as the consensus view. The memo should be formal and polished, suitable for circulation to commissioners who are not specialists in political philosophy, but precise enough that researchers could respond to its reasoning.
