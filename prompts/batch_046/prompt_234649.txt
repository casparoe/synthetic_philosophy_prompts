I've been thinking about whether being maximally morally good is actually something to aim for, or whether the idea itself contains a hidden mistake. Please write a dialogue between two people, Mira and Eli, who have no formal philosophy training but have thought about this carefully on their own. Mira believes that moral goodness works like optimization: you should always choose the action that does the most good for others, and that stopping short is a kind of failure, as if leaving value on the table. Eli believes this "maximization" picture is wrong; he argues that treating every decision as a moral calculation wipes out the private spaces and personal commitments that give life its shape, and that real goodness requires knowing when not to calculate. Have them respond directly to each other rather than talk past each other—specifically, Mira should counter Eli's concern about private spaces by treating privacy as just one more good to be weighed against others, not as a protected boundary; Eli should reply that once privacy is treated as weighable, it ceases to function as the kind of boundary that makes relationships and selfhood possible. Keep both characters equally competent, avoid strawmen, and keep the exchange focused. After writing the dialogue, please state how confident you are in the philosophical positions represented, and single out the specific claim you are least sure about.
