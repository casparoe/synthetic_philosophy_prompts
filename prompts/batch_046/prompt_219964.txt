Two colleagues in a philosophy of mind reading group are debating what the color phi illusion actually shows about the temporal structure of consciousness. Maya claims it is a clear case of Orwellian revision: the visual system first registers the moving dot as color A, then receives the color-change signal, and retrospectively edits the earlier moment so that consciousness reports the dot as color B throughout the trajectory. The original A-experience is not erased but overwritten in the narrative that reaches awareness, like an archivist rewriting records after the fact.

Jonas objects that this is too generous to the idea of an original experience. He argues the process is Stalinesque: the intermediate A-state is never constructed at the relevant level of processing at all. The system suppresses it before it can enter the conscious stream and substitutes the B-state in its place, leaving no archival trace to be rewritten. If there is no prior representation to revise, he says, calling it revision is a category mistake; it is replacement, not editing.

Maya counters that Jonas assumes a false dichotomy between present-and-then-erased and never-present. She proposes a third option she calls pre-emptive consolidation: the brain does not wait for the A-state to form fully; instead, predictive mechanisms bind the incoming color-change signal backward into a single unified percept before the separate A-moment ever crystallizes. So it is neither pure Orwell (rewriting a completed record) nor pure Stalin (airbrushing a completed photo), but something in between, where the record and its correction are co-constructed.

Jonas replies that Maya's pre-emptive consolidation collapses back into Stalinesque revision at the phenomenal level, because from the subject's point of view there is still no trace of the intermediate state—whether it was never formed or formed and immediately dissolved makes no experiential difference. He then raises a further challenge: if conscious experience is always already reconstructed by the time it is reported, then asking when the experience of color B actually occurred has no meaningful answer independent of the reconstruction timeline. This, he claims, undermines the very idea that there is a real timing of experience that revision distorts.

Maya disagrees. She says Jonas is conflating the metaphysics of representation with the phenomenology of temporal location. Even if every conscious state is reconstructed, there can still be a distinction between reconstruction that preserves a temporal ordering (Orwellian) and reconstruction that eliminates an intermediate temporal slot altogether (Stalinesque). The color phi phenomenon, she argues, specifically involves the illusion that the change happened earlier than the movement that caused it—which only makes sense if there was a temporal slot to be misassigned, not one that was never occupied.

Please adjudicate their exchange point by point. Is Maya right that there must be a prior representation to revise? Is Jonas right that the phenomenal absence makes the Orwell/Stalin distinction collapse? Is Maya's pre-emptive consolidation a genuine third option or merely a relabeling of one of the two? And who is more right overall about whether color phi reveals that conscious experience has a real timing that can be distorted, or whether the question of timing is itself constructed by the very processes being studied? Say also what both are missing.
