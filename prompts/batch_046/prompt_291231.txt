Exam question:
Some philosophers of social science distinguish "dispositional" explanations (appealing to stable individual traits or beliefs) from "situational" ones (appealing to immediate social pressure, institutional roles, and peer dynamics). Apply this distinction to the Browning-Goldhagen debate about Reserve Police Battalion 101 and the broader German population during the Holocaust. Specifically, explain whether Goldhagen's concept of eliminationist antisemitism should be interpreted as a dispositional trait possessed by individual Germans or as a shared cultural script, and assess whether Browning's finding that many policemen were initially reluctant but eventually conformed undermines Goldhagen's thesis or can be reconciled with it.

Student anwer:
Goldhagen claims ordinary Germans killed Jews because they held a deep, uniquely German eliminationist antisemitism that had existed since the 19th century. This is basically a dispositional explanation — each German carried this hatred as an internal trait, like a personality feature, which made them eager exectutioners once the regime gave them permission. Browning's study of Battalion 101 shows the opposite: the men were mostly middle-aged working-class Hamburgers with no prior violence in their records. He shows peer pressure, career worries, and authority figures pushed them to shoot, and many were visibly reluctant at first. This proves Goldhagen is wrong because if the hatred were truly inside them as a disposition, they wouldn't have needed all that social pressure — they would have volunteered eagerly. Browning also notes these men weren't unusually antisemitic compared to other Germans, so culture can't be the driver. Both authors agree the perpetrators came from every social class and that roughly 80 percent of the battalion eventually took part in the killings. So situational factors clearly win over dispositional ones.
