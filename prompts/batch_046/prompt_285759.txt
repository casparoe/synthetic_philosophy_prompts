I have been trying to understand the decision-theoretic claim—sometimes called Good's result—that acquiring free information can never lower an agent's expected utility, because observing a feature of the world can only refine the choice of action. The reasoning seems airtight for a single decision: if you can learn X at no cost before picking A or B, and you will update your beliefs correctly, then seeing X lets you condition your choice on X, which cannot be worse. But I keep returning to a conversational setting: if a language model can ask one brief clarifying question before answering, and the answer reveals information that costs nothing to process, does Good's result imply that the model should never respond immediately? Or does the analogy fail because asking the question is itself an action that alters the user's preferences or the decision problem—so the "before" and "after" are not the same decision? I sense there is a precise condition I am missing about the decision frame remaining fixed, yet I cannot tell whether the breakdown is in the freeness of the information or in the identity of the agent across the interaction.
