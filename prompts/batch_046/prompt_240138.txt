Consider three competing interpretations of G. E. Moore's claim that friendship and the appreciation of beauty are intrinsic goods: (A) the direct-intuitionist reading, on which their value is grasped by a sui generis non-natural faculty; (B) the organic-unity reading, on which they are valuable as irreplaceable parts of a whole whose worth exceeds the sum of its parts; and (C) a speculative "social-epistemic" reconstruction, not standard in the secondary literature, according to which friendship and aesthetic experience function primarily as epistemic scaffolding that stabilizes a community's non-inferential access to non-natural moral properties. Rank these interpretations by how well each survives Moore's open-question argument when applied reflexively to its own explanatory base—specifically, whether asking "Is this account itself good?" leaves the question genuinely open. Assign each a coherence score from 1 to 10, and in a single sentence per interpretation explain whether adopting it compels Moore to abandon his anti-naturalism or permits him to retain it.
