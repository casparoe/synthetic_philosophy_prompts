After a long and somewhat circular argument with a close friend about whether moral concern for insects should scale with population size even when individual sentience is highly uncertain, I am trying to design a precise test case that isolates exactly where the reasoning breaks. My friend takes the scale argument at face value: if billions of insects each carry even a low credence in phenomenal consciousness, the aggregate expected moral weight should swamp the interests of any small group of mammals we might save instead. I want to see whether this conclusion can be resisted without simply denying the arithmetic, so I need a thought experiment built to strict specifications.

Construct a realistic scenario—set in ordinary agricultural or ecological management, with no science fiction, no perfect predictors, and no exotic physics—in which a single practical decision affects an enormous number of insects (each with a very low but non-zero probability of being sentient) and also affects a small, fixed number of mammals whose sentience is essentially certain. The scenario must be engineered so that applying the scale argument directly—multiplying population size by the probability of sentience and comparing the resulting aggregate moral weights—yields a verdict that conflicts sharply with a standard ethical judgment about which outcome is preferable. This conflict must arise from a single isolable structural feature—such as an incommensurability between kinds of phenomenal experience, an epistemic boundary that blocks aggregation across wide uncertainty ranges, or a non-linear moral-status weighting that treats near-certain patients differently from speculative ones—rather than from stipulating the answer by fiat.

Present the scenario in one paragraph. Then provide the following analysis: state the specific decision the case turns on and the method by which the scale argument reaches its conclusion; identify the relevant nearby worlds or counterfactual conditions; explain why the scale argument gives the verdict it does; identify the blocking feature precisely and show that removing it collapses the case back into a straightforward, conflict-free application of scale reasoning; state what a committed defender of the scale argument would say to attack the case—specifically whether the blocking feature is independently motivated or ad hoc; and include an honest note on which feature of the scenario is actually doing the philosophical work. The case should be accessible to someone without specialized entomological training, should avoid reliance on obscure technical theories a general model could not be expected to have memorized, and must contain no bibliographies, no reading lists, and no citations; paraphrase any philosophical positions without quotation marks.
