Make the argument that dreams are best understood neither as full-blown experiences (Dennett) nor as non-experiences (Malcolm), but as “latent phenomenal fragments” that acquire genuine experiential status only through a post-awakening binding process in working memory. First, address Malcolm’s criterion-of-identity objection—there is no continuous subject in REM sleep capable of bearing an experience—by conceding that no unified dreamer exists, yet insisting that fragmentary phenomenal content can occur without a unified bearer. Then engage Dennett’s narrative-construction thesis: accept that dream reports involve retrospective confabulation, but argue that this reconstruction binds pre-existing disunified content rather than inventing it ex nihilo. From this hybrid view, derive a specific claim about the epistemic authority of dream reports: they are neither transparent windows onto sleep-state consciousness nor pure fiction, but reconstructed bindings of genuinely present yet unintegrated phenomenal structures. Keep the focus on the metaphysics of the dream-state and on what conditions must be met for phenomenal content to count as an experience belonging to a subject.
