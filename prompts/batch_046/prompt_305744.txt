I serve on the editorial board of a university press reference encyclopedia that classifies catastrophic mortality events by causal ontology rather than by chronology alone. We are deadlocked over the taxonomy for three entries: the 1943 Bengal famine, the Irish Great Famine of 1845–1852, and the mortality crisis during China’s Great Leap Forward. One faction wants all three filed under Natural Disasters and Environmental Shocks, citing blight, drought patterns, and harvest failure as the proximate triggers. The other faction demands classification under Political Crimes and State Policy Failures, invoking Amartya Sen’s entitlement-collapse framework and noting continued food exports, inadequate relief logistics, and procurement policies that deepened starvation.

The dispute is not merely historical; it turns on a philosophical interpretation of Sen’s model itself. Does democratic accountability and a free press operate as a necessary causal mechanism within entitlement collapse—implying that famines in authoritarian contexts are categorically different—or is it only a contingent correlation Sen observed in post-colonial India? If the mechanism is necessary, then the absence of democratic feedback during the Great Leap Forward might paradoxically weaken rather than strengthen the political-crime label, since Sen’s theory predicts that functioning democracies do not experience famines.

We need a written recommendation for Thursday’s meeting. It should propose a concrete taxonomy—whether binary, hybrid, or dimensional tagging—with an implementation timeline (for example, whether we revise the style guide by Q3). The memo must apply consistent reasoning to Bengal, Ireland, and the Leap; include an honest steel-man of the strongest objection from the losing faction; and reach an explicit verdict rather than splitting the difference. In particular, please resolve whether our classification criteria should include a counterfactual robustness test—asking whether comparable mortality would have occurred under an alternative institutional arrangement facing the same shock—and whether the food-export dynamics visible in Bengal and Ireland versus the state-induced procurement collapse visible in the Leap should drive different ontological labels. Draft the memo I will circulate.
