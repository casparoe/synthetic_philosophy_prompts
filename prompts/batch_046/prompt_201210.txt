I came across a summary in a philosophy-of-science review—intended, I think, for biologists who want orientation in the functional debate—that attempts to adjudicate between etiological and causal-role theories of biological function, with a brief aside on a newer organizational-closure proposal. The summary runs as follows: "The etiological theory holds that a trait's biological function is fixed solely by its selective history: a trait has a particular effect as its function if and only if variants with that effect were differentially reproduced in the ancestral population because of it. Consequently, traits produced by genetic drift or as evolutionary by-products have no proper functions, regardless of how beneficial they are in the present environment. The causal-role theory, in contrast, identifies a trait's function with whatever contribution it currently makes to one or more capacities of the containing system—most paradigmatically, survival and reproduction. Because it examines present causal organization rather than past selection, this view can assign functions to traits that have never undergone selection, such as a heart whose rhythmic sound happens to deter predators. Advocates of the causal-role approach also maintain that it handles malfunction more straightforwardly than its rival: a heart that fails to pump is malfunctioning precisely because it is not contributing to circulation. By comparison, the etiological view seems unable to accommodate malfunction, since if a heart was selected for pumping, it retains that function even when it fails; defenders attempt to rescue the view by claiming that malfunction is merely the non-performance of the selected effect, but this response collapses the distinction between having a function and actually functioning. Moreover, because causal-role analysis allows any contribution to system capacity to count, it offers no principled criterion for distinguishing a heart's proper function—pumping blood—from its accidental effects, such as warming the thorax or producing audible rhythms. A more recent organizational-closure proposal tries to split the difference by defining function in terms of contribution to a self-maintaining loop of interdependent processes, yet this hybrid inherits the etiological problem: if organizational closure is assessed synchronically, it cannot explain why a trait that currently sustains closure but arose by pure drift should be denied functional status. Given these considerations, only the etiological account can ground the normative dimension of dysfunction and biological design, whereas the causal-role view dissolves function into an undifferentiated inventory of present causal powers."
