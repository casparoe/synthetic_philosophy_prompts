I'm taking a graduate seminar on distributive justice, and we've been trying to reverse-engineer the implicit normative framework of a visiting lecturer who has delivered firm verdicts on a series of welfare-allocation scenarios without ever labeling her theoretical commitments. Here is her track record so far. In Scenario A, with two groups at welfare levels of 90 and 30, she endorsed redistributing both to 60 rather than leaving them unequal, even though the aggregate sum falls. In Scenario B, facing the same 90-to-30 baseline, she rejected a policy that would have lowered the 90 group to 30 without improving the 30 group—pure leveling—calling it impermissible on the grounds that harming some without helping anyone violates the only standard she treats as decisive. In Scenario C, with groups at 80, 50, and 20, she approved lowering the 80 group to 65 in order to raise the 20 group to 35, leaving the middle group untouched. In Scenario D, with groups at 100, 100, and 10, she endorsed lowering both affluent groups to 60 to raise the worst-off to 50, but she rejected an alternative that lowered all three to 10 in order to achieve perfect equality at the bottom.

Now consider Scenario E: three groups sit at 85, 40, and 35. A proposed policy lowers the 85 group to 75, leaves the 40 group unchanged, and raises the 35 group only to 38. The worst-off gain is minimal, the best-off suffer a substantial absolute loss, the middle group is unaffected, and the equality gap shrinks noticeably without anyone crossing a clear sufficiency threshold. If we want to remain consistent with her judgments in A through D, what should she conclude about Scenario E? Identify at least two competing distributive rules—such as prioritarian weighting with concave welfare weights, a leximin ordering, a sufficientarian threshold view, or a relational egalitarian concern with status differences—that accommodate all four earlier verdicts, state precisely what each rule predicts about Scenario E, and give your best-guess verdict with reasons. Then specify a further Scenario F, involving different baseline numbers or a different structural feature, that would definitively separate the rule you think she is running from its closest rival, and say what each rule would predict in that separating case.
