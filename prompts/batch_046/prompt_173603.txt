I have no academic training, but for the past decade I have helped organize a cooperative orchard governed by strict rotational rules: each season we divide our shared irrigation and labor between guaranteed-yield blocks—root vegetables and grains that feed our members regardless of weather—and long-odds blocks, such as an experimental graft that might, with very low probability, eventually produce a harvest large enough to supply several neighboring cooperatives. Over time I have developed my own vocabulary for these choices: I distinguish soil-commitment, the resources that must be protected to maintain the orchard’s baseline survival, from speculative draw, the resources we risk on uncertain future abundance; and I keep a mental ledger of harvest-debt, the obligation I feel we incur to current members whenever we divert water or labor away from guaranteed blocks toward the speculative ones. Recently I encountered, secondhand, the philosophical debate about whether rational decision-making should always maximize expected value, even when that means accepting enormous risks for astronomically large but improbable rewards, and I understand that critics call this tendency fanaticism. I want you to restate both the defense of pure expected-value reasoning and its principal criticisms entirely within my orchard vocabulary—soil-commitment, speculative draw, harvest-debt, rotational rules, communal meals, and the physical fact that water diverted to one block is permanently lost to the others. For each side, say precisely what translates cleanly: does utility become total harvest summed across all possible years, does a moral patient become a current member versus a future neighbor, does probability weighting become weather forecasting, does obligation become harvest-debt? Then identify what must be distorted to fit, and what has no honest expression at all in this framework. Finally, give me the residue report: is there any claim in the philosophical argument—perhaps about obligations that persist even when no actual person is harmed, or about the moral significance of outcomes that never occur—that lacks any counterpart in orchard management? And if there is such a residue, does it indicate that my vocabulary of soil, water, and debt is too parochial to capture a real moral distinction, or does it reveal that the philosophical debate relies on abstractions that dissolve once you reason in terms of actual resources and actual hungry people?
