I am a high school junior presenting a fifteen-minute paper at our regional interscholastic philosophy symposium next Thursday. The thesis I defend is a view I call “threshold tranquilism”: the only intrinsic good is the complete absence of craving and suffering, but—contrary to standard Schopenhauerian or popular Buddhist accounts—this absence is not a mere privation. Once achieved, it constitutes a stable, positively characterized state of “unconditioned rest” that is not improved by further knowledge, achievement, or social connection; those goods are strictly instrumental, valuable only insofar as they reduce craving over time. I apply this to argue that conventional welfare metrics—educational attainment, social capital, economic growth—should be judged solely by their long-run effect on reducing craving, not by any direct contribution to human flourishing.

The audience is specific: my philosophy teacher, who teaches Aristotelian virtue ethics and is skeptical of reductionist ethics; a visiting religious-studies lecturer from a local university who specializes in Theravada Buddhist soteriology and knows the canonical distinctions between tanha, dukkha, and nibbana; two peers active in effective altruism who accept suffering-reduction as central but reject exclusivity; and a general audience of students and faculty who will ask practical, often naive objections. I need a Q&A prep sheet, not a defense of the view itself.

Please list the objections I will actually face, in the order they are likely to come, phrased as each group would phrase them: the Buddhist scholar’s concern about mischaracterizing cessation as annihilation; the virtue-ethicist’s challenge to human excellence; the effective-altruist peers’ charge of arbitrariness and their “anesthetize everyone” reductio; and the practical student questions about drugs or ambition. For each, tell me what I should concede up front, what I should hold, and a concise reply suited to a two-minute answer window. Note where each reply is weakest. Include the one objection I am hoping nobody asks—likely the one about wild-animal suffering and whether my view obligates universal sedation—and tell me exactly how to survive it if it comes.

Finally, please state how confident you are in each part of this analysis, and explicitly single out the one point in my position or in your advice about which you are least certain. Do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; I have no access to verify them, and I want to rely only on what can be defended from common philosophical knowledge and the structure of the argument itself.
