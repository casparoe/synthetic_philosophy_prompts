I have been trying to classify failures of valid consent, and I keep encountering a configuration that does not fit neatly into the familiar triad of deception, coercion, and incompetence. Consider an agent who correctly understands what will happen if she agrees, who grasps the risks and benefits without distortion, who faces no external pressure, and who possesses general decision-making capacity; nevertheless, she consents partly because she holds a false belief not about the procedure's effects, but about the modal status of refusal — for instance, she believes that declining is metaphysically impossible for her, or that the rejected option would be taken up by a numerically different person rather than by herself under different circumstances. In one case I imagine, a research participant agrees to a multi-year study because she thinks that withdrawing would retroactively replace her past study-self with a counterpart, so withdrawal is not a live option. In another, a patient accepts an aggressive treatment because he believes refusal is incompatible with some necessary feature of his identity, not merely harmful. Is there an established concept for consent undermined specifically by a misapprehension — whether externally induced or self-generated — of the agent's own space of possible actions, rather than of the outcomes? How should it be distinguished from ordinary deception about consequences, and from competence failures involving risk or probability? If no standard term exists, how ought one carve the phenomenon: as a subspecies of deception, as a distinct failure of modal competence, or as an independent category?
