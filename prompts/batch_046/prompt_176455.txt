I teach a small grad seminar on decision theory — about 14 people, one 75-minute session — and I want them to physically feel the pull of Good's theorem on the value of information, but not the sanitized textbook version. Specifically, I want them to experience the moment where free information should rationally be taken, yet taking it collapses a coordination or commitment structure that was actually sustaining the payoff. So design a runnable classroom game: real but small stakes (candy, course-credit points, whatever), rules and materials that don't need tech, a payoff scheme that maps onto an expected-utility calculation, and a debrief that connects what just happened to the formal inequality (the value of information being non-negative) and to the rationality assumption that gets strained.

Inject one weird mechanic that isn't in the standard examples — maybe an oracle that reveals the hidden state only if every player publicly commits not to change their action upon learning it, or a rule where observing the signal makes your previous agreement unenforceable. The participants should construct the decision tree themselves during play and only notice at the end that they've walked into the paradox. They should be able to opt to recieve a free signal about a hidden parameter, then discover that the signal changed not just beliefs but the enforceability of the deal. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first run — I bet some groups will refuse the signal even when it's free, others will take it and then face a coordination failure — and be frank about which feature of Good's result the physical setup simply can't reproduce, so I'll need to supply that verbally in the debrief. Keep it grounded in the logic, no citations or reading lists needed, just the activity and the mapping.
