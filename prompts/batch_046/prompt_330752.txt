Exam question: “Consider Hilbert’s hotel, Cantor’s diagonal argument, and the finitist rejection of actual infinity. In approximately 350 words, explain how Hilbert’s thought experiment is meant to defend the coherence of actual infinity, how Cantor’s argument relates (or does not relate) to Hilbert’s purpose, and identify one specific conceptual error a finitist might charge against both arguments. Be precise about what each argument assumes about completed infinite totalities.”

Student answer:
David Hilbert’s infinite hotel is designed to show that actual infinity is internally coherent, not contradictory. If every room is full, the manager can shift the guest in room 1 to room 2, room 2 to room 3, and so on, freeing room 1. This demonstrates that a proper subset of an infinite set can have the same cardinality as the whole, violating Euclid’s principle that the whole exceeds the part. Cantor’s diagonal argument operates on the same insight: by arranging all real numbers between 0 and 1 in a list and constructing a number that differs from every entry, Cantor proves that the real numbers are uncountable. Together, Hilbert and Cantor establish the hierarchy of transfinite cardinals—Hilbert fixes ℵ₀, while Cantor shows ℵ₀ is strictly smaller than the cardinality of the continuum.

The finitist rejects both arguments because each treats an infinite collection as a completed totality rather than as a merely potential process. The finitist grants that we can always add another natural number, but denies that all natural numbers exist together as an actual set. Applied to Hilbert’s hotel, the finitist objects that the manager can never complete an infinite sequence of shifts; at every finite stage some room remains unshifted, so there is no “completed” state in which every original guest has moved. Applied to Cantor, the finitist denies that a completed list of all reals exists; since the diagonal construction requires such a completed list as its premise, the argument fails.

That said, Hilbert himself was a mathematical Platonist: he believed that infinite sets and transfinite cardinals exist in an objective, mind-independent realm, and he intended the hotel precisely as an ontological proof that such entities are possible. The finitist therefore commits a modal error—confusing our epistemic inability to complete an infinite task with an ontological absence. Just because no human can finish shifting every guest does not entail that the infinite hotel cannot exist.
