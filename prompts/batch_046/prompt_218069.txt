Design a sorting taxonomy—structured as a decision tree with exactly six terminal leaves—for a graduate philosophy seminar on whether karma is best understood as a theory of moral desert rather than as a causal law of action and consequence. Every internal node must be phrased as a substantive philosophical claim that a student can accept or reject without knowing which leaf it leads to; no node should be a named tradition or label the student must recognize. The root splits on whether desert is backward-looking (retributive, fixing past moral quality) or forward-looking (distributive, allocating future welfare by merit). Each of those branches then splits on the ground of desert: intrinsic to the agent (essential character or moral properties), relational-causal (continuity of consciousness or personal identity across lives), or conventional-institutional (socially constructed tracking of merit). This produces four combinations. You must then divide one of these four—choose either backward-looking/intrinsic (splitting on whether desert can be satisfied vicariously or only by the agent herself) or forward-looking/conventional (splitting on whether temporal discounting applies to welfare allocations across rebirths)—to reach exactly six leaves. For each leaf, provide a concise descriptive name (invented if necessary), a one-sentence statement of the karma-as-desert view it represents, and a brief flag indicating whether the position is coherent, incoherent but attractive, or merely unexplored. In addition, identify one answer-combination that the tree permits but that remains unoccupied, and diagnose whether the gap results from logical incoherence, philosophical unattractiveness, or historical neglect. Ensure the taxonomy distinguishes positions by what they believe about desert, not by what they call themselves, and keep all questions answerable by students familiar with basic moral philosophy but with no specialized training in karma doctrines.
