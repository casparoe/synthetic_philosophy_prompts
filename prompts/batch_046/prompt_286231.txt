Exam question: In climate ethics, the so-called non-identity problem suggests that our carbon policies help determine which specific individuals will exist in the future. Explain why this seems to undermine person-affecting obligations to mitigate climate change for the sake of particular future people. Then evaluate whether the problem also undermines identity-independent obligations—obligations to make the world better regardless of who lives in it. In particular, distinguish between (a) an impersonal principle that evaluates worlds primarily by aggregate welfare, and (b) an identity-independent principle that does not depend on comparing populations across possible worlds. Finally, discuss whether avoiding the repugnant conclusion requires abandoning all identity-independent grounds for climate obligations, or only specific formulations of them.

Student answer:

The non-identity problem arises because future generations are not fixed entities waiting to be harmed; rather, they are contingent on our present actions. If we pursue high-emission policies, the resulting economic, social, and reproductive conditions will produce one set of individuals in 2100, whereas mitigation policies would produce a different set. Consequently, no particular future person can claim that mitigation would have made them better off, since under mitigation they would never have existed. This undermines any person-affecting duty to mitigate—there is no identifiable victim who is wronged by our emissions.

However, this does not eliminate climate obligations altogether. We can still appeal to an impersonal principle: a world with lower emissions is simply better than one with higher emissions, regardless of who inhabits it. Typically, this is understood as an aggregate welfare comparison—adding up well-being across all actual and possible lives. From this view, high emissions are wrong because they lower total or average welfare in the actual world.

Some critics worry that this impersonal approach leads to the repugnant conclusion, according to which a vastly larger population with lives barely worth living is better than a smaller, flourishing population. Because aggressive climate mitigation might reduce future population size by altering economic growth and fertility patterns, adopting an aggregate welfare framework could actually commit us to preferring high-emission scenarios that support larger populations. To avoid this paradox, we should reject identity-independent reasoning entirely. Our real obligations are not to hypothetical future people but to present generations: we have a duty not to expose currently living people to climate risk, and any further concern for the distant future collapses into present-centered risk management.
