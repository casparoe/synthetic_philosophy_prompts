I have been discussing with colleagues in my philosophy of science seminar the pessimistic meta-induction, and I find myself returning to an analogy that is often deployed in these debates: the history of scientific theories is compared to the succession of dominant architectural styles. Just as baroque, neoclassical, and modernist buildings were each once regarded as structurally definitive and aesthetically final, only to be later rejected as misguided or merely fashionable, so too have phlogiston, caloric, and the luminiferous aether been abandoned despite their predictive success. Proponents of the induction use this comparison to support the conclusion that our current theoretical edifices—quantum field theory, evolutionary psychology, and the like—are almost certainly destined for the same museum of obsolete ideas.

I should like you to audit this analogy with some rigour. Provide a correspondence table: what plays the role of the architect, the building materials, the structural engineer, the inhabitants who must live inside, the demolition crew, and the museum curator? Then identify the points at which the mapping fractures—particularly those that bear on whether predictive success in science corresponds to aesthetic or functional success in architecture, and whether abandonment in one domain entails falsehood rather than mere supersession. I want you to separate the disanalogies that genuinely weaken the pessimistic conclusion from those that are merely decorative. Does the analogy quietly import the assumption that scientific theories are primarily social fashions rather than representational instruments? And if the analogy collapses under scrutiny, what would be a more honest comparison—perhaps to cartography, or to engineering prototypes? Finally, tell me whether the pessimistic conclusion survives once the architectural metaphor has been dismantled, or whether it was carrying the entire argumentative load.
