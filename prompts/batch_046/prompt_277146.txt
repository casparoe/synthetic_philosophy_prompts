I am a retired experimental physicist who, since leaving the laboratory, has taken up the philosophy of mind with some seriousness, though I still think in terms of mechanisms and evidence rather than texts. In a recent seminar on consciousness, a colleague used the following line to shut down a discussion: "If it was not reported upon waking, it was not experienced in the first place." He invoked it to dismiss my question about whether a dream of falling possesses any phenomenal character independent of the narrative we construct in retrospect. I recognize the spirit of this as belonging to the debate between Malcolm and Dennett on whether dreams are experiences at all, but I want the precise conceptual machinery unpacked. Could you lay out the distinct theses that slogan might actually express—ranging from the trivial epistemic point that third-person science relies on reports, through intermediate claims about the constitution of mental content by linguistic or narrative structures, to the strong metaphysical denial that there is any "what-it-is-like" during sleep absent a waking reconstruction? For each reading, indicate whether it is a platitude, a substantive and defensible position, or genuinely false, and say specifically which version would actually support dismissing my question about phenomenal character in the falling dream. I am especially interested in whether any of these readings can be reconciled with contemporary predictive-processing and memory-consolidation accounts of REM sleep, or whether those neuroscientific frameworks necessarily push one toward Dennett's side regardless of how one interprets the slogan. Please present the options as a disambiguated menu rather than selecting a single interpretation, and do not supply bibliographies, reading lists, or citations—my concern is with the conceptual landscape, not with tracking down sources.
