I am presenting a seminar paper defending a reflexive application of Wittgenstein's family resemblance: I argue that the concept of "language game" itself is held together only by family resemblance, which generates a regress that standard readings avoid only by smuggling in an essentialist anchor—namely, a shared human "form of life." I propose replacing that anchor with what I call an ecology of practices: a distributed network of functional niches that can be occupied by artificial as well as human agents. From this I draw the consequence that large language models can genuinely participate in certain language games—not by simulating mental states or following rules in the internalist sense, but by occupying ecological niches within human communicative practices—without requiring anything like a shared biological or cultural form of life. The thesis is therefore both interpretive (about Wittgenstein) and applied (about AI language use).

The audience is a philosophy-of-language workshop with four kinds of interlocutors I need to prepare for precisely: (1) two analytic philosophers of language trained on Grice and Davidson, who regard Wittgensteinian therapy as obscurantist and will press me on whether I am offering a substantive theory or merely dissolving problems; (2) a cognitive scientist who works on categorization and knows Rosch's prototype theory well, and who will ask whether I am simply importing empirical psychology into conceptual analysis; (3) a historian of philosophy specializing in the transition from the Tractatus to the Philosophical Investigations, who cares about textual fidelity and will challenge my reading of "form of life" as a distributed ecology rather than a shared human background; and (4) an AI-ethics researcher who worries that my ecological criterion is too permissive and risks anthropomorphizing systems that merely predict tokens.
