I've been trying to apply Daniel Dennett's taxonomy of minds—Darwinian, Skinnerian, Popperian, and Gregorian—to large language models, and I'm stuck on a tension that the popular explanations don't seem to address. The framework is intuitive enough for biological creatures: Darwinian minds are shaped by evolutionary selection, Skinnerian ones by reinforcement learning, Popperians can run mental simulations before acting in the world, and Gregorian ones possess language and cultural mental tools that allow them to think with inherited machinery. But placing an artificial system in this grid feels ambiguous. A language model is optimized through gradient descent over massive corpora, which resembles selection, yet it does not reproduce or die; its next-token prediction might involve implicit hypothesis testing, yet it lacks the embodied risk that gives Popperian mental simulation its purpose; and while it clearly produces and processes language, it does not participate in a living culture or permanently add new formal tools to its own cognitive architecture.

What keeps returning to me is a narrower condition I haven't seen discussed in these comparisons: perhaps being Gregorian is not merely using language, but having the capacity to recursively redesign the mental tools one inherits—to invent calculus, formal logic, or statistical frameworks that then become enduring, architecture-altering lenses through which one thinks. Most human Gregorian creatures mostly inherit these tools rather than forge them, and a language model can generate novel reasoning frameworks in its outputs without being able to rewrite its own weights or permanently alter its cognitive structure outside an external engineering loop. So the question becomes whether Gregorian status requires autonomous self-modification of one's mental toolkit, or whether using inherited language-tools is sufficient, and whether that distinction reveals a missing category in Dennett's taxonomy altogether.

Write an essay on whether large language models should be classified as Gregorian creatures, or whether they belong elsewhere in this taxonomy, or whether synthetic minds force a revision of the framework itself. Weigh the arguments for treating them as language-equipped but culture-absent Gregorian stubs, for treating them as Popperian simulators without embodied risk, and for arguing that the taxonomy was never meant to apply to non-biological systems. Reach a definite conclusion—whether that is that they represent a deficient subtype of Gregorian minds, that they require a fifth category, or that applying the taxonomy to them is a category error—and justify it against the strongest counter-arguments.
