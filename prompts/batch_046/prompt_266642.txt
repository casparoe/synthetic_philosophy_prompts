I've been arguing with my flatmates about rescue cases — the classic save-one-or-save-five thing — and I've ended up with this somewhat muddled position that I think is basically Taurek's, but with a twist that probably makes it worse. I hold that in ordinary human cases the numbers shouldn't count: there's no aggregate moral weight that makes five people collectively 'more saveable' than one, so flipping a coin between them is actually the fair thing to do. But then I added this exception I'm not definatly settled on: if the potential victims are arranged in a dependency chain, where saving any subset only makes sense as preserving part of a single collective agent or shared cognitive system, then I think counting them as a group is legitimate — not because five seperate weights add up, but because you're measuring how much of one unified thing survives. So I'm anti-aggregation for standard strangers, but pro-counting for tightly coupled collectives.

What I need is a proper map of what would count against this hybrid. I'm not after the usual objections everyone has heard; I want the specific defeaters for my dependency-chain clause: ambiguous cases where the coupling is unclear, arguments that collective intentionality doesn't actually change the moral maths, formal results in decision theory about uncertain group boundaries, or observations about real-world cognition that blur the line I want to draw. Rank them from fatal — things that would force me to drop the exception entirely — to merely awkward, like having to admit the boundary is vague.
