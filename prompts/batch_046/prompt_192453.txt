Here is my opening. I am not a scholar; I have been thinking about sympathy and moral judgment on my own, and I want to test an idea that keeps returning to me. Consider two people who witness the same injustice done to a stranger. The first feels an immediate, unthinking sympathy and acts at once. The second feels nothing spontaneous, but deliberately constructs a detailed narrative of the victim's inner life—almost like composing a story—until a kind of sympathy finally arises, and only then does she act. I want to ask whether your framework treats these two responses as morally equivalent. More specifically, I am wondering whether there is a meaningful distinction, in your terms, between sympathy that arises spontaneously and sympathy that is deliberately constructed through imagination. If the man within the breast can be reached only by this second, reflective path, have we made ourselves more moral, or have we replaced genuine sympathy with a kind of performance? And does the spectator judge only the outward act and its propriety, or does he also examine the quality and origin of the feeling behind it? I ask not for a history lesson, but for your own judgment.
