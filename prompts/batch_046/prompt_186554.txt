I work in applied ethics consulting across several countries, and I have noticed a recurring pattern in cross-cultural moral-psychology surveys that I do not have a precise term for. The instruments present hypothetical dilemmas—trolley-style trade-offs, harm-benefit allocations, or obligations to strangers—and record divergent selections across cultural groups. Yet in some contexts, respondents appear to treat the task not as choosing what ought to be done, but as identifying which option best expresses proper social relations, role obligations, or descriptive norms. Their answer is then coded as a “moral intuition,” though it functions more like a sociological judgment about appropriate ordering. This seems to produce a specific measurement artifact: not ordinary response bias or framing effects, but an ontological mismatch in which the survey presupposes a prescriptive, individual-choice framework that the respondent does not share. Is there an established concept—perhaps in anthropology or survey methodology—that distinguishes this speech-act and framing divergence from genuine variation in normative judgment? If there is no standard name, what conceptual boundaries would separate it from the familiar WEIRD versus non-WEIRD divergence, and how should we classify cases where the divergence disappears once the scenario is reframed as asking about social roles rather than individual decisions?
