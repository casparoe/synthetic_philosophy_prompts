(1) The argument from illusion says that hallucinating a dagger and actually seeing one share some core mental feature.
(2) Disjunctivists deny that: in the good case it's a direct world-relation, in the bad case it's a totally different mental kind with no shared phenominal core.
(3) But disjunctivists still want to say the hallucination is "phenominally indistinguishable" from the real thing.
(4) If two states are phenominally indistinguishable, there must be some shared phenomenal property P that makes them match.
(5) So in both cases you're aware of P.
(6) In good cases you're aware of P and also the dagger.
(7) That means even in veridical perception you're directly aware of something mental (P) that isn't the dagger - exactly the sense-datum theorist's point.
(8) And since P is present in hallucination without the dagger, you can never rule out being aware of P alone, so you never really perceive the dagger.
(9) So disjunctivism was meant to save direct realism, but it ends up conceding there's always a mental intermediary, just called P instead of a sense-datum. It proves too much.

which step should i drop? i have a hunch it's (4) but i can't articulate why without sounding like i'm just begging the question. if i give up (4), what do disjunctivists have to pay? do they lose the ability to talk about "indistinguishability" altogether? please say how confident you are in your answer and single out the point you're least sure about.
