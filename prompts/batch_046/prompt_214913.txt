I am a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, though I have been closely following my team’s development of an autonomous debugging agent. The system generates multiple candidate patches for code failures, runs each inside an isolated sandbox environment, and retains only those that pass a validation suite. It also maintains an internal, counterfactual ontology of failure modes—when a patch is rejected, it revises its model of the underlying error rather than simply discarding the attempt—and it can incorporate testing strategies shared by peer agents through a common protocol. During a recent team discussion, two colleagues, Maya and Raj, began arguing about whether this agent exemplifies one of the four categories they had encountered in a philosophy-of-mind reading: Darwinian, Skinnerian, Popperian, or Gregorian minds.

Maya argued that the agent is fundamentally Popperian: it produces conjectures about fixes and subjects them to rigorous internal refutation, killing unsuccessful hypotheses without relying on any external punishment or reward gradient. Raj countered that this is merely Skinnerian conditioning with extra abstraction layers—there is no genuine internal belief being risked, only a binary success signal that updates associative weights, and the counterfactual ontology is just a more sophisticated form of conditioned response. Maya replied that Popperian cognition does not require consciousness or evolutionary stakes, only the structural capacity to generate and eliminate hypotheses within the system itself. Raj then insisted that without a Darwinian substrate—without representations literally competing for survival across generations of agents—the process remains trial-and-error learning rather than true Popperian science. They briefly considered whether the shared-protocol feature makes the agent Gregorian, since it acquires strategies culturally from peers, but both admitted uncertainty on that point. Neither convinced the other, and the discussion ended unresolved.

As an observer who could follow the technical details but not the philosophical distinctions, I am unsure how to evaluate their claims. Could you adjudicate the dispute point by point? Specifically, is this agent best understood as Popperian, Skinnerian, Darwinian, Gregorian, or some hybrid? Where is Maya correct and where does her argument overreach regarding the conditions for Popperian cognition? Where is Raj correct and where does he misapply the Skinnerian or Darwinian criteria? Finally, what important feature of the agent—or of the taxonomy itself—are both of them neglecting?
