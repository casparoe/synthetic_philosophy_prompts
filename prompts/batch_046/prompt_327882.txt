I was recently appointed to an ethics review panel for digital-infrastructure regulation, and we face a recurring structural dilemma: one system design risks a single catastrophic harm to an individual, while the alternative distributes an imperceptible burden across millions. This mirrors the philosophical aggregation problem—whether one extreme harm can be outweighed by an astronomical number of trivial ones—but I need a practical tool I can run in a thirty-minute session with panelists who have no philosophy background.

Please operationalize the core principle behind this tension into a reusable decision procedure for our panel: a step-by-step checklist in words, specifying the questions to ask in sequence, what counts as a sufficient answer at each step, the precise point where the procedure stops and yields to an explicit value judgment rather than a calculation, and a flag for when the inputs are too uncertain to apply the framework at all. Do not include bibliographies, citations, reading lists, or requests for literature review; the procedure must work for reviewers who will not search sources.

Then apply it to two proposals we actually received: (A) a centralized biometric vault that risks one catastrophic identity breach affecting a single citizen, versus distributed local storage that exposes every citizen to a minute but continuous privacy erosion; and (B) an automated hiring filter that rejects one clearly qualified candidate with severe livelihood damage, versus a calibrated model that slightly lowers the evaluation score of thousands of marginal candidates. Include one case where the procedure yields an answer that conflicts with common moral intuition.

Organize your response as follows:

The Procedure (approximately 60% of the response): present the checklist, the judgment-call markers, the stop conditions, and the uncertainty flag.

Worked Applications (approximately 30%): run the procedure step-by-step on Proposal A and Proposal B, with explicit answers at each stage.

Limits and Judgment Points (approximately 10%): state which distinctions from the original philosophical debate had to be dropped to make the procedure usable, where it will misfire, and what it cannot settle.
