Design a 75-minute simulation for about 14 graduate students in a philosophy-of-mathematics workshop. The goal is to have them feel the conceptual clash between Hilbert’s hotel-style actual infinity and finitist denial of completed infinite totalities—not through explanation first, but by running into it through rules, roles, and physical stakes.

Split the group into “Hotel Managers” and “Finitist Auditors.” Managers receive a physical deck of numbered room-cards and a supply of shift-tokens; they must accommodate a queue that delivers one new guest every two minutes using only the “move every occupant to the next room” rule. Auditors may veto any manager action if they can show it requires a completed infinite task or an actually infinite set of tokens. Managers win if they never turn a guest away; auditors win if they can halt play by demonstrating that an infinite number of completed shifts now exists and no further move is determinately defined.

Arrange the rules so that managers naturally claim there is always room even when every visible card has been moved, while auditors must pinpoint the exact step at which a “completed infinity” has been assumed. Do not name Hilbert’s hotel, Cantor, or finitism until the debrief. In the debrief, map each crisis point—the first shift, the accumulating token pile, the claim that the hotel is full yet not—onto the corresponding parts of Cantor’s idea of one-to-one correspondence with a proper subset and the finitist insistence that any actual collection must have a definite cardinality. Be explicit about predictable failures (managers may stop the clock to avoid infinite rounds; auditors may conflate potential with actual infinity) and how to correct them in the moment. Also be frank about which feature of Cantor’s diagonal argument the card-and-token format cannot reproduce, and supply that missing piece verbally in the debrief so the activity still connects to the broader argument about actual infinities.
