I am a high school senior facilitating a peer discussion group—twelve students with only introductory exposure to ethics—devoting one forty-five-minute session to tranquilism, understood specifically as the thesis that well-being is constituted entirely by the absence of craving and suffering, while positive affective states contribute nothing in themselves. I am not asking for an explanation of the view; I need a structured pedagogical plan. First, the dependency order: which conceptual distinction—say, between the removal of a negative and the presence of a positive—must be secured before the core claim can even be stated without sounding like mere pessimism? Second, the carrying example: I have been considering framing the discussion around noise-canceling headphones versus playing music, treating silence-as-absence-of-noise as analogous to tranquility-as-absence-of-craving, but I worry this smuggles in a hidden physicalist assumption; if not that, what concrete scenario should carry the idea and what must it include and exclude? Third, the misconception map: my peers will likely conflate this with Stoic apatheia, with Buddhist dukkha, or with negative utilitarianism—at what point in the sequence should each conflation be anticipated and neutralized? Fourth, a concise check—perhaps a forced choice between two described lives—to verify at the end whether they have grasped the structural asymmetry rather than simply feeling repelled by it. Finally, what should I openly refuse to discuss at this level—perhaps the metaphysics of desire or comparative interpersonal measurement—while still being honest about the refusal? Please treat the audio-analogy question seriously: does it illuminate the asymmetry or does it collapse upon inspection?
