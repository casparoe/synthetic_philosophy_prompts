I'm giving the opening remarks at a memorial symposium for Dr. Elena Vasquez, a philosopher of mind who spent her career defending the view that the self is a constructed narrative without any enduring substrate, and who privately organized her thinking around Zhuangzi's parables of transformation—the butterfly dream and the story of his response to his wife's death. The room is split: on one side are philosophers of mind and comparative philosophers who know the interpretive dispute over whether Zhuangzi is best read as an eliminativist about the self or as a transformationist who accepts fluid continuity; on the other side are her family and lab colleagues with no background in either tradition. Write a ten-minute address that takes her professional eliminativism seriously and uses Zhuangzi's image of death as seasonal change—not as decoration, but as the structural constraint that determines what can and cannot be said about her absence. In particular, navigate the tension between her claim that there is no enduring self to be destroyed and the Zhuangist imagery that seems to presuppose something that persists through change. The speech must not claim she survives death in personal form, must avoid generic consolation, and must land for the specialists without losing the family. Do not ask for citations, bibliographies, or further reading.
