I have been preparing lectures on epistemic circularity and keep returning to the bootstrapping problem—the concern that one cannot legitimately deploy a belief-forming method to establish beliefs about that very method's reliability without presupposing what is to be shown. To sharpen the issue, consider a hypothetical autonomous diagnostic system that calibrates its own sensors using feedback loops generated by those same sensors, thereby acquiring what epistemologists term easy knowledge of its functional integrity. This strikes me as structurally parallel to the circularity that undermines naive coherentist accounts of justification, since in both cases the legitimacy of a cognitive source is derived entirely from outputs that already depend upon that source. Given this parallel, I wonder whether any epistemology that rejects an external, non-circular foundation—whether applied to human cognition or to such self-validating systems—must necessarily abandon the hope of rationally grounded self-trust, or whether there exists a principled distinction that allows reflective equilibrium to avoid both vicious bootstrapping and foundationalist regress.
