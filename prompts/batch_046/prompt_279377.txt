I have been discussing the heterogeneity problem for hedonism with a study group, and we want to avoid the standard script in which one side simply lists diverse pleasures and the other appeals to an unanalyzable feeling. To push the debate into less familiar terrain, consider two positions we are trying to articulate precisely. Rhea defends a functional-profile account: pleasures are not unified by any shared intrinsic phenomenal quality—there is no common hedonic glow linking the taste of wine, the resolution of a paradox, and the relief of a headache—because cross-modal phenomenological comparison is unreliable and reports of a common feel are often post-hoc constructions. Instead, she argues that pleasures share an identical regulatory role: each operates as a non-instrumental positive-reinforcement signal that updates an agent's preference weights, making pleasure the class of states that reward without further justification. Theo defends a modified phenomenal-essence view. He grants that pleasures vary enormously in intentional object and cognitive texture, but insists that a thin, pre-conceptual affective valence—something like a primitive pro-attitude—pervades all of them, even when masked by the richness of the experience. Theo worries that Rhea's account collapses pleasure into generic reward-learning, stripping away the normative significance of feeling good and leaving it mysterious why pleasure should be pursued for its own sake rather than as an instrumental calibrator.

Please write a dialogue between Rhea and Theo in which they genuinely engage each other rather than talk past one another. Each should revise or sharpen her or his position in response to the objection, not merely restate an opening claim. The exchange should be philosophically rigorous but accessible, with no appeals to named theorists or texts—work out the reasoning on its own merits. If the framing above rests on a confused premise—for instance, if the functional-profile and phenomenal-essence distinction is incoherent, or if demanding a non-circular detection of valence is unfair—say so plainly rather than constructing the dialogue politely.
