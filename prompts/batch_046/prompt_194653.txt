As a novelist working on a book about how universities stage intellectual inheritance, I sit on our university's Collections and Naming Committee, and we face a bequest that feels less like a donation and more like a philosophical trap. A private collector offers to endow a permanent exhibition in the philosophy library: facsimiles of Gottlob Frege's logical manuscripts—drawn from the Grundgesetze—will be mounted in cases directly adjacent to facsimiles from his posthumously discovered private diary, the one containing the nationalist and antisemitic entries he wrote near the end of his life. The donor's deed insists that the two bodies of work remain physically unseparated, and she requires us to publish a joint catalog bearing the invented title The Same Hand: Logic and Politics in Frege. She contends that any attempt to isolate the logician from the citizen amounts to an illegitimate form of intellectual compartmentalization, and she presses the further, stranger claim that Frege's own doctrine of Sinn—his distinction between sense and reference—actually undermines such separation: if the very same mind that constructed an extensional calculus also inhabited an intensional moral universe in which certain populations were denied full standing, then the public sense of his logical symbols may carry a contamination that pure formalism cannot wash away.

We must circulate a written opinion before our meeting on the twenty-third, recommending a concrete course of action with a clear time frame—accept the bequest as written, accept only if the display is split and the catalog divided, or decline outright. The recommendation should engage seriously with the donor's Sinn-based argument rather than dismissing it, and it should weigh the strongest consideration on the other side—whether separating biography from philosophy is not compartmentalization but a necessary condition for evaluating logical work on its own merits, or whether, conversely, the exhibition's design forces viewers into an untenable hermeneutic. Please draft the formal memo I will circulate: it should address whether Frege's political diary bears on his philosophy in any hermeneutically relevant way, whether the proposed joint exhibition presupposes a particular theory of intellectual identity that our institution should endorse, and what practical steps—curatorial, editorial, and temporal—we should take if we move forward.
