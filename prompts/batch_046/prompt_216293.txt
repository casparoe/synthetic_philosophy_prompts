I am a novelist preparing to present a paper at an interdisciplinary seminar on narrative consciousness and rational agency. My thesis is that sustained first-person narration demands what I call an "architectural inside view": the author must construct a character's self-assessment of reasons entirely from within, yet maintain an epistemically modest outside view that treats that self-assessment as defeasible not merely by psychological error but by structural features of the narrative architecture itself. Specifically, the formal constraints of plot coherence, temporal ordering, and inter-character evidential access generate knowledge about the consistency of a character's belief system that the character, by virtue of being embedded within it, cannot possess. Epistemic modesty, on my account, requires the author not to resolve this asymmetry through omniscient correction or ironic distance, but to hold it open as a sustained formal tension—allowing the reader to occupy both views simultaneously without collapsing one into the other.

The audience is precisely composed: four analytic philosophers of mind, mostly sympathetic to higher-order theories of consciousness and skeptical of narrative knowledge-claims; two literary theorists committed to distant reading and statistical narrative analysis, who regard first-person phenomenology as methodologically naive; and three creative-writing faculty who view philosophical framing as hostile to craft intuition and who will defend the autonomy of "voice" against theoretical interference. The session is twenty minutes of presentation followed by open questioning. I need a Q&A prep sheet tailored to this audience.

Please provide the eight objections I will actually face—not generic philosophical objections, but objections phrased in the vocabulary, commitments, and temperamental biases of these specific groups—ordered from most likely and damaging to least damaging. For each objection, include the audience member or subgroup most likely to raise it, the precise form in which they will phrase it, a two-minute reply I can deliver without notes, and an explicit note on where that reply is weakest or concedes ground I should not pretend to hold. Then identify the one objection I am hoping nobody raises, explain why it would collapse my framing if pressed rigorously, and advise whether I should concede it preemptively or defend it at all costs. I do not need bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; I need to know which elements of my position are soft enough that I should acknowledge their limits in my opening remarks rather than waiting to be cornered. Keep the focus on the interplay between the inside view's first-person authority and the outside view's structural epistemic privilege, and do not dilute the prompt with requests for general writing advice.
