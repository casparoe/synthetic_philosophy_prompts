ok so I'm retired—used to do condensed matter physics—and only got into philosophy lately, so bear with me if this is messy. Here's roughly where I've landed and I'd like to know if it hangs together, or if I'm just helping myself to incompatible stuff without noticing.

(1) I'm an indirect consequentialist about moral norms: the right rules are the ones that, if widely internalized, maximize welfare, but agents should never do explicit case-by-case utility calculus—just follow the rules.

(2) I think a norm's authority comes from idealized endorsement: what you'd still choose after perfect information, zero bias, and full coherance—like a deliberaton with all cognitive noise stripped out.

(3) I one-box in Newcomb's problem. Rationality tracks expected payoff, not causal influence.

(4) I think you should pay the driver in Parfit's hitchhiker, even after you're safely in town and the causal link is broken. Your prior commitment binds you.

(5) I think norms have to be "action-guiding" in a practical sense: you can apply them without knowing the full causal backstory—like you don't need to trace the evolutionary origin of a rule every time you use it.

(6) I think evolutionary debunking arguments miss for normative claims because norms aren't representational beliefs about the world; they're more like boundary conditions in a dynamical system—constraints, not descriptions.

So do these hang together? I'm especially worried about (1) and (2) pulling opposite directions—if I'm fully informed and unbiased, wouldn't I just calculate outcomes directly and drop the indirectness? And (4) with (2) also feels weird: in ideal deliberaton I'd see the driver can't affect the past, so why pay?

But also (3) and (5) look like they clash—one-boxing seems to depend on knowing the predictor's mechanism (some causal backstroy?), while (5) says you shouldn't need that deep history to apply a norm. Maybe that's only an apparent clash though?

Can you audit this? Rank the real tensions by severity, say which commitment has to give in each case, flag the ones that only look bad but dissolve on inspection, and tell me what's the cheapest single revision that cleans up the most mess. Don't list them all as equally bad—some are worse than others.
