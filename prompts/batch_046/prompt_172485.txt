I serve as a senior program officer at a multilateral climate-finance facility, and our charter now requires that every proposal above a set cost threshold be evaluated not only for direct causal efficacy but also under the evidential-cooperation framework in large-world decision theory: in a sufficiently large or structurally repeating universe, my decision to approve or reject can provide evidence about what structurally similar decision-makers elsewhere will do, and that evidential correlation may dominate negligible direct effects. I need a reusable operational procedure—something my committee can run in under an hour, without cosmological modeling—that translates this framework into concrete steps. The procedure should specify, in order: how to define the peer class (what counts as “me elsewhere” for evidential purposes); how to weigh evidential correlation strength against direct causal efficacy; a clear stopping rule that returns “reject” when the evidential chain is too attenuated or the peer class is ill-defined; explicit flags marking where a value judgment (not a factual determination) must take over; and a brief audit-trail format so the reasoning is reviewable by an external referee. Then apply the procedure to three proposals: (A) a $40 million direct-air-capture pilot in a region with no regulatory spillover, defended solely on the ground that approval would signal to peer facilities that this technology class is fundable; (B) a $5 million public-education campaign on climate adaptation in a region with zero emissions footprint, defended because widespread adoption of the curriculum would indicate global norm convergence among similar agencies; and (C) a $20 million reforestation project with modest direct carbon sequestration but a claimed strong evidential linkage to land-use policies adopted by structurally similar bodies. In at least one case the procedure should yield a recommendation that conflicts with the intuitive moral urgency of the proposal, and you should identify exactly which step forces that result. Conclude by stating what the procedure systematically misses—particularly regarding moral uncertainty, the ontology of the peer class, and the risk of spurious correlation—and explain why those gaps cannot be closed by adding further steps.
