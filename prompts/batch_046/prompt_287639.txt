There is a long-standing interpretive stalemate regarding Plato's attitude toward drunkenness, particularly in Laws I–II and in the framing of Alcibiades' intoxication in the Symposium. One camp reads the Athenian Stranger's proposal for regulated drinking parties as a serious civic technology for testing and strengthening temperance; the other treats it as dramatic irony, with Alcibiades representing the inevitable collapse of such control. The relevant Greek is stable, both sides draw on the same tripartite psychology from the Republic, and neither claims the other misreads the lines—yet the disagreement has not narrowed for decades. I suspect the impasse is not philological but structural: each side implicitly operates with a different model of what rational governance requires—something like a unified hierarchical controller versus a distributed, context-sensitive system of checks—and therefore they disagree about whether "controlled loss of control" is conceptually coherent as an educational practice, rather than about what Plato actually meant.
