Having spent a career in experimental physics and only recently turned to philosophy, I find the epistemology of testimony curiously analogous to problems of observer-dependent measurement and error propagation. I would like you to read Kurosawa's Rashomon through the debate between reductionism and anti-reductionism about testimonial knowledge: specifically, whether what we learn from another's report can be fully decomposed into our own perceptual and inferential capacities, or whether accepting testimony requires an irreducible normative commitment of epistemic trust.

The film offers four incompatible accounts of an assault in the forest. Focus on the woodcutter's concluding narrative, which seems to synthesize the others: does it function as a reductionist reconstruction built solely from his direct sensory data, or does it still depend on an unreduced acceptance of framing—perhaps that supplied by the priest and by the other witnesses? I want a reading that takes both the philosophical positions and the film's structure seriously: identify scenes or formal choices where the distinction between reduction and irreducible trust illuminates the narrative, and be explicit about where the film resists being mapped onto either view. Does Rashomon ultimately dramatize that reductionism collapses into incoherence without trust, or that anti-reductionism invites an ungrounded relativism? And what, if anything, does the work contribute to the philosophical debate that abstract argument alone does not capture?
