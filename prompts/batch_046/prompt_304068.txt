I'm drafting this novel about a woman who keeps replaying the same arguement with her dead sister in her head, and I got fixated on sphexishness - you know, that wasp that checks its burrow in this exact loop, over and over, like mechanical rigidity with no room to improvise. I started wondering wether free will is just the absence of that loop, but then I wrote out this argument for my character's philosopher-friend and now it feels like it swallows everything, including the friend.

Here it is: (1) Free will means an agent could genuinely do otherwise in the exact same internal and external conditions - not just that history might diverge, but that the agent's own reasoning could break pattern. (2) Sphexishness is pure mechanical rigidity: same input, same behavioral loop, no flexibility. (3) But flexibility isn't really the opposite - it's just sphexishness moved up a level. A flexible agent doesn't break loops; she runs a meta-loop, a decision-rule that picks which sub-loop to execute based on reasons and values. (4) So if you map out her beliefs, weights, and reasoning algorithm well enough, the "flexible" choice is still predictable. It's just a more elaborate burrow-check. (5) That means any rational, reason-responsive agent is algorithmically sphexish: her reasoning is deterministic, just nested deeper. (6) The only way out of that nesting is to put actual randomness into the choice - a neural coin-flip that breaks the link between reasons and action. (7) But if my character picks something because of random noise, that's not her will acting; it's an accident inside her skull. So randomness doesn't rescue freedom either. (8) Therefore every agent is either mechanically rigid or randomly erratic, and neither is free will. So free will doesn't exist.

I really don't want to accept (8) - I need my characters to have some real agency, or the whole theme collapses. I think (2) is solid, and I'm kind of attached to (5) because it mirrors my character's nested self-justifications. Maybe the error is in (3), where I treat flexibility as hidden determinism? Or maybe in (6), assuming determinism and randomness exhaust the options? If I have to choose, I'd rather save (1) and (2), but I'm not sure what giving up (3) or (5) actually costs me conceptually. Where does this argument slip, and what do I pay if I reject that step?
