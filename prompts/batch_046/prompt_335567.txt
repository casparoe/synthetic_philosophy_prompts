I have been reflecting on a recurring argumentative structure in debates over whether national prosperity stems mainly from political institutions, geographic endowments, or cultural norms—particularly when historians and economists use paired comparisons to defend institutional explanations against skeptical counter-narratives. I am writing this in connection with my doctoral research in economic history, and I first noticed the pattern while discussing case studies with colleagues, but I could not map it onto any standard taxonomy of causal fallacies.

The pattern unfolds in two layers. First, an observer compares two neighboring societies with broadly similar climates and ethnic compositions—say, a region that adopted representative assemblies in the nineteenth century and later industrialized, versus one that retained centralized extraction and stagnated—and argues that the difference in governance caused the divergence in wealth. A critic then demonstrates that the region with assemblies had already experienced a transient mineral boom that enriched local merchants and made them demand power-sharing, while the other region never faced such a shock; therefore the institutional change was an effect of the boom, not its cause. Rather than conceding that institutions are epiphenomenal, the defender shifts ground: she grants that the origin of the assemblies may have been exogenous, but insists that once established they generated a self-reinforcing cycle—secure rights attracted capital, capital strengthened the merchant class, and that class blocked authoritarian reversal—so that institutions still explain the long-run trajectory, not merely the initial spark. In a second instance, a country overhauls its legal framework after a war destroys its old landed elite, and then enjoys rapid growth; skeptics note that the war also demolished obsolete capital, raising returns to new investment regardless of legal form, while supporters reply that only the post-war settlement made those high returns durable rather than fleeting.

What I want to identify is not generic reverse causation, nor is it simply the idea that institutions persist over time; it is this specific layered retreat, in which an institutional explanation survives the undermining of its causal origin by relocating causality to dynamic stability and feedback, without clearly specifying how one would distinguish that stability from the continued influence of the original exogenous factor. Does this configuration have an established name within the philosophy of social science, comparative historical analysis, or causal inference? If there is no precise label, how should one delimit the phenomenon: does it occur whenever an institutional account survives an origin-challenge by appealing to path-dependent reinforcement, or only when that appeal implicitly treats institutions as autonomous from the conditions that created them? I am not seeking references or a reading list; I want the conceptual carving—what exactly this is, what adjacent mechanisms or fallacies border it, and where its edges lie.
