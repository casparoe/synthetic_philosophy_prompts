I am a PhD student in metaphysics working on identity and physical structure, and I have been trying to formalize a worry about scale-invariance in counterexamples to the Principle of Identity of Indiscernibles. A colleague walked me through the following chain, which I cannot fully exit, and I need a precise diagnosis.

(1) The intrinsic version of Leibniz’s Principle of Identity of Indiscernibles (PII) holds that if x and y share all intrinsic qualitative properties, then x is identical to y.

(2) In the symmetric-universe scenario with two spheres—call them A and B—each sphere possesses the same mass, radius, color, and internal structure; they differ only in relational properties, specifically their spatial separation and orientation relative to one another.

(3) If we deny that purely relational differences ground numerical diversity—on the grounds that in a universe containing only these two spheres the relation “being separated by distance d” is symmetric and does not by itself distinguish which sphere is which—then A and B violate intrinsic-PII and should be one object, which contradicts the premise that there are two.

(4) To preserve the intuition of numerical diversity, defenders of the counterexample typically invoke either primitive thisness (haecceity) or indexical discernibility: an embedded observer can point and distinguish “this sphere” from “that sphere,” securing two objects without any qualitative difference.

(5) This metaphysical remedy should be scale-invariant. If numerical diversity can be grounded solely in bare indexical reference or primitive particularity for macroscopic spheres, consistency demands the same for fundamental physical entities.

(6) Consider two electrons in a symmetric singlet state. They share charge, mass, spin, and spatial wavefunction symmetry; standard quantum mechanics treats exchange as producing no observable difference precisely because there is no qualitative fact of the matter about which electron is which.

(7) Yet if indexical discernibility is sufficient to establish that A and B are two, then an analogous indexical tag—“this electron” versus “that electron”—should establish that the two electrons are metaphysically distinct individuals, regardless of their qualitative identity.

(8) Once electrons possess primitive numerical identity, the symmetric/antisymmetric wavefunction constraint can no longer be interpreted as a metaphysical identity condition; it collapses into a merely epistemic or representational restriction on our descriptions.

(9) But this dissolves the standard explanation for quantum statistics and the Pauli exclusion principle, turning necessary physical constraints into contingent bookkeeping conventions.

(10) Therefore, accepting the standard anti-PII response to the spheres forces a radically revisionary quantum metaphysics. Since we should not be driven there by a thought experiment, the inference from (3) to (4) must be flawed.

I feel that something slips between (5) and (8)—perhaps an illicit assumption that metaphysical particularity must be physically manifest—but I cannot locate the precise equivocation or scope error. Which step should be rejected, and what does rejecting it cost? In particular, if we save quantum mechanics by blocking the scale-invariance move, do we thereby undermine the original defense of two spheres, or is there a principled reason the remedy applies only at macroscopic scales?
