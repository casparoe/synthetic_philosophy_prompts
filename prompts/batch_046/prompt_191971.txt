I'm serving as a grader for a graduate seminar in philosophy of physics and spacetime ontology, and I'd like a careful point-by-point assessment of one exam answer before I finalize the mark. The exam prompt asked students to explain the hole argument in general relativity: specifically, to describe how diffeomorphism invariance allows one to construct a second solution metric that is identical to the first everywhere outside a bounded region H but differs inside H, with both satisfying Einstein's field equations for the same initial data outside H, and to clarify why this appears to undermine determinism. Students were then asked to evaluate the "sophisticated substantivalist" response that retains spacetime points as real entities but denies them primitive identity or haecceity, individuating them only through relational properties fixed by the metric; they had to say whether this genuinely restores determinism or instead collapses the view into relationalism by making points ontologically dependent on the field, and whether the observational equivalence of the two metrics introduces a separate problem about empirical verification.

Here is the student's response: "The hole argument shows that Einstein's field equations are diffeomorphism invariant. Take a solution metric g and apply a diffeomorphism φ that is the identity everywhere except inside some region H, the hole. The pushed-forward metric φ*g agrees with g outside H but differs inside, yet both solve the field equations with identical initial conditions outside H. This means the same past state allows different future states inside H, so determinism fails unless we deny that g and φ*g represent distinct physical situations. The relationalist response, developed initially by Leibniz and later formalized by Earman and Norton, denies that spacetime points possess primitive thisness. Without haecceities, φ*g is just g with points relabeled, so there is only one physical situation and determinism is preserved. A sophisticated substantivalist might try to adopt this relational individuation while still claiming points are real entities. But this is unstable: if a point's identity depends entirely on its relational properties within the metric, then points are not independent entities but merely positions in the field structure. This collapses substantivalism into relationalism, undermining the ontological commitment. Moreover, even if relationalism saves determinism, it does not resolve the deeper problem. Because g and φ*g are observationally equivalent, we can never empirically determine which point corresponds to which across the transformation. Thus the hole argument is ultimately about the limits of empirical verification rather than about ontology or determinism."
