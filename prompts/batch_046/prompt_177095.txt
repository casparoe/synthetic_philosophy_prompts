Construct a formal diagnostic taxonomy—structured as a branching decision tree with labeled internal nodes and terminal leaves—that classifies philosophical positions on lexical-threshold negative utilitarianism together with the question of whether pleasure and pain are commensurable. The tree should begin from a single entry commitment: "Reducing suffering is the primary or sole moral aim, but I am uncertain whether pleasure can ever compensate for it." Each branching node must pose a concrete, case-based question that a respondent can answer without knowing which philosophical label it leads to. Specifically, the tree must force explicit choices on: (1) whether there exists any common metric—quantitative, qualitative, or structural—between a unit of pleasure and a unit of pain; (2) if suffering has priority, whether that priority is absolute and lexical (no amount of pleasure can justify allowing it), weighted but overridable, or merely heuristic; (3) whether the lexical threshold, if endorsed, is defined by the intensity of suffering, by its qualitative kind (for example, torture versus mild discomfort), or by a relational feature such as "not redeemable within the subject's remaining life"; and (4) whether pleasures below a certain phenomenological resolution—sub-threshold or granular pleasures—can aggregate across subjects or over time to outweigh a single macro-instance of suffering.

Map at least six named or precisely described positions onto the leaves, including: a strong lexical incommensurabilist view; a weighted scalar asymmetrist view; a full commensurabilist negative-utilitarian view; and a "post-resolution collapse" view (the niche claim that once pleasure is decomposed below its granular threshold, it loses normative weight entirely, leaving only suffering-reduction as a meaningful objective). For each leaf, provide the specific combination of node-answers that leads to it.

Then complete the taxonomy in three further ways. First, flag at least two pairs of leaves that the tree cannot genuinely separate—explain whether the overlap is conceptual (the positions share all relevant commitments) or practical (they diverge only on empirically undecidable details). Second, identify at least one logically possible combination of answers that remains unoccupied in the space you have mapped, and briefly diagnose the vacancy: is it incoherent, normatively unattractive, or simply unexplored? Third, include a brief scoring key that allows a reader to answer the node-questions in sequence and arrive at their own position without consulting the leaf labels in advance.

The instrument should be designed for an advanced undergraduate or graduate philosophy audience. Use between seven and ten decision nodes. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, author citations, or requests for further sources; build the classification from the logical structure of the commitments themselves.
