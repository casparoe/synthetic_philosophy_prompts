I've been trying to work out, without any formal study, exactly where the boundary lies between doing what is expected of a decent person and acting in a way that is genuinely heroic—where failing to act would not be blameworthy. My coworker Elena and I keep testing our intuitions against specific situations.

Elena's verdicts so far are these. If you find a lost wallet on the sidewalk and return it with little trouble, that is basic honesty, nothing more. If you find one in a distant park and spend an entire day tracking down the owner, that goes well beyond the call. If you pull a stranger from an oncoming vehicle at no risk to yourself, you must do it. If you pull that same stranger out at serious risk of injury to yourself, it is admirable but nobody should be faulted for refusing. Elena also holds that a surgeon who performs an emergency operation on a stranger during her own vacation is simply fulfilling her professional role, not earning extra moral credit. By contrast, donating a kidney to save a sibling is, in Elena's view, "just what family does"—expected rather than extraordinary—whereas donating a kidney to a complete stranger is clearly beyond what is required. She further thinks that an extremely wealthy person who gives away a sum large enough to save thousands of lives, but which costs her almost nothing in lifestyle, is being responsible rather than heroic; yet if a poor person gives away money she needs for her own rent to save a stranger, that is genuinely heroic.

Here is the new case. You are the only living person who possesses a highly specific technical skill. Six strangers are trapped in an underwater research habitat and will die within forty-eight hours unless you perform a repair that can only be done by hand. The task requires you to miss your daughter's wedding and will permanently damage your hearing. You have no contract, no prior relationship to the crew, and no legal obligation. You never sought out this situation.

Predict Elena's verdict: is performing the repair obligatory, or is it going well beyond what anyone should demand? More importantly, identify the implicit rule she seems to be applying—whether it tracks social proximity and institutional role, the ratio of personal cost to one's resources, voluntary commitment, or the uniqueness of one's ability to prevent catastrophe. Which of these rules best accommodates all eight of her earlier judgments? Finally, propose one further case—ideally one not involving life-or-death stakes—that would best distinguish her actual rule from the closest competing alternative.
