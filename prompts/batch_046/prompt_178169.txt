And here's the student's answer, copied exactly:

Judy starts with P(Red) = P(Blue) = 1/2. The assignment rule is about her location given her company, not about her company itself, so learning it shouldn't shift her belief about which company she's in. This is the standard no-change result that van Fraassen originally defended.

To formalize, Jeffrey conditioning updates probabilities inside each partition cell without touching the marginals. So if P(Front|Red) and P(Rear|Blue) both become 0.8, we just redistribute within Red and Blue, leaving P(Red) at 1/2.

Some people try to use Bayes' rule on the material conditional (Red → Front) ∧ (Blue → Rear), but that doesn't work because conditionals don't have prior probabilities—this is basically Lewis's point. So Bayes is inapplicable here, and Jeffrey conditioning is the only correct method.

There's also the nested Jeffrey rule, which applies Jeffrey conditioning twice: once for the inner conditional probabilities and once for the outer company probabilities. Since the first update doesn't change the marginals, the second update is redundant. So the nested rule also gives P(Red) = 1/2, confirming the result.

One mistake people make is thinking Judy should update her company belief based on where she 'feels' she is, but that's just psychological bias, not rational updating.

Overall, Jeffrey conditioning solves the problem, Bayes' rule is blocked by undefined priors, and the nested rule agrees.
