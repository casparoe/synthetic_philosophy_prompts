I've got this annoying funding puzzle and I'm not sure whether I'm being stubborn or whether there's actually a philosphical point hiding in it. Every year I recieve about £8k in discretionary research cash — I'm a philosophy lecturer, mostly ethics and logic — and I can split it between safe, incremental projects that reliably grow my budget by about ten percent, or I can dump part of it into one wild interdiscplinary gamble that either quintuples my capital or burns it entirely. There's a nasty catch: if my running balance ever drops below £2k I lose access to the shared lab, and rebuilding from there is practically impossible — it's like hitting a wall you can't climb back over. I also noticed that if the gamble succeeds, it doesn't just pay out once; it boosts the baseline for every future year, so the outcomes are kind of chained together.

My colleague in maths says I should always risk the same proportion of whatever I've got, year after year, because over the long run that maximises how fast my capital grows, even if it feels reckless near the wall. Another friend — an economist — says that's dangerous and I should shrink the bet as I get closer to the £2k floor, maybe even stop betting altogether. I keep thinking there's a deeper disagreement here about what 'rational' means when you're not making a single choice but playing the same decision over and over with multiplicative results. Is there a real philosophical divide about whether rationality is about averaging your outcomes or about protecting your ability to keep playing? Or am I just overthinking my grant allocation?
