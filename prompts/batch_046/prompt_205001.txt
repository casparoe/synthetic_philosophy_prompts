If a rigid designator denotes the same object in every possible world in which that object exists, and if Kripke's thesis that identities such as Hesperus is Phosphorus are necessary yet known only a posteriori demonstrates that empirical investigation can uncover modal truths without analytic reasoning, then consider the following distinction: call a term metaphysically rigid if it tracks one object across worlds regardless of how we learn of it, and epistemically rigid if the reference-fixing procedure itself—astronomical observation, chemical assay, or ostensive pointing—would yield the same result in every world accessible to our evidence. Now, since the identification of Hesperus with Phosphorus depended on contingent observational conditions—different orbital mechanics or atmospheric scattering might have led ancient astronomers to stabilize the name on a different celestial body—does this not entail that the designator is epistemically non-rigid? And if the act that fixed reference was itself contingent on empirical circumstances that could easily have attached the name to a different object, can we still maintain that the resulting identity statement expresses a genuine metaphysical necessity, rather than a modal artifact of the particular path through which we happened to stabilize the term's reference?
