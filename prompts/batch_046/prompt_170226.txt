Design a conceptual taxonomy tree for a philosophy-of-mind seminar that sorts philosophical accounts of why pain is bad by testing them against clinical pain asymbolia—the phenomenon, well documented in neuropsychology, in which patients report intact nociceptive intensity yet deny that the sensation is unpleasant, show little or no avoidance motivation, and in some documented cases actively prefer that it continue. The tree must be constructed from logical divisions rather than from a list of authorial positions, and it should function as a diagnostic instrument: every internal node should present a concrete claim about the patient’s mental state or about the internal structure of pain that a respondent can accept or reject without already knowing which philosophical label it corresponds to.

Root node: does asymbolia establish that the sensory and evaluative dimensions of pain are genuinely separable?

From there, branch according to where the separation is located: within phenomenal consciousness (between sensory-discriminative and affective-motivational qualities), within intentional content (between representing tissue damage and representing that damage as bad-for-me), or outside consciousness altogether (in behavioral dispositions, desire-frustration conditions, or functional roles).

The tree must contain exactly five leaves:
1. An intrinsic phenomenalist position that denies genuine separation is possible and interprets asymbolia as misreporting, conceptual confusion, or an unnoticed but present affective quality.
2. An affective-separation position that locates badness specifically in a dissociable emotional-motivational phenomenal quality, treating the sensory component as evaluatively neutral.
3. A representational-evaluative position that treats badness as part of pain’s intentional content, so that asymbolia is best explained as a failure of that specific evaluative representation while sensory representation remains intact.
4. An instrumental or desire-based position that grounds badness in the frustration of avoidance desires or in functional disutility, treating the absence of aversion in asymbolia as direct evidence that the state lacks the property that makes pain bad for that subject.
5. One logically permitted but apparently unoccupied combination—identify the precise conjunction of commitments that defines this space, label it “unnamed,” and provide a one-sentence diagnosis of why it is empty (for example: incoherent, redundant with another leaf, or conceptually unstable because it combines commitments that cancel each other).

At each branch, phrase the dividing claim as a question answerable by reference to the patient’s responses to hypothetical trade-offs, implicit behavioral tendencies, or first-person reports—not by asking whether the respondent endorses a named theory. Explicitly flag any pair of leaves that the available clinical and conceptual distinctions cannot reliably separate, and explain the source of the overlap (for instance, that two positions make identical predictions about every observable response). Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, citations, or references to specific papers or authors. Avoid invoking highly specialized neuropsychological sub-classifications or precise lesion localizations beyond what is strictly necessary to frame the phenomenon; the task is conceptual sorting, not clinical diagnosis. The final tree should reveal which commitments about phenomenal quality, representational content, and normative evaluation the respondent actually holds.
