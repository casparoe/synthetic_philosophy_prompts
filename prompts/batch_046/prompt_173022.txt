Two grad students, Jordan and Taylor, got into it over drinks about the 'man of his time' defence for Columbus, Jefferson, and Churchill. Jordan started: (1) The defence isn't nostalgia, it's historical humility. Moral concepts develop; Jefferson owned slaves but also wrote about equality in a context where abolition wasn't mainstream, and Columbus operated before anyone had articulated indigenous rights in a form we'd recognize. You can't just teleport 2024 ethics backward and call it justice. Taylor replyed: (2) But we apply context selectively. We praise Churchill for being ahead of his time against Hitler, then excuse his imperial racism as just of his era. If temporal context wipes away moral blame, it should also wipe away moral credit — but nobody does that consistently, which shows it's usually just special pleading for figures we already like. Jordan came back: (3) There's a real difference between actions that faced live opposition then and those that didn't. Las Casas was already condemning Columbus's cruelty not long after, but Virginia slavery was legally embedded and Jefferson was genuinely conflicted — he knew equality and still failed. That internal conflict matters; it's not mere ignorance. Taylor finished: (4) Knowing the principle and violating it is arguably worse, not better. Jefferson's hypocrisy isn't a defence; it's aggravating. And Churchill's colonial subjects were actively resisting empire — he wasn't blind to opposition, he crushed it. So 'of his time' just collapses into 'of my preferred narrative.'

Please adjudicate move by move: rate each of the four moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right and briefly justify each rating. Also say what, if anything, both partys are missing — especially whether there's a principled line between genuine moral ignorance and culpable failure across these three cases.
