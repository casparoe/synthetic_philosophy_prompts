Write an essay on whether Kant's universalizability tests—specifically the contradiction-in-conception and contradiction-in-will variants of the Formula of Universal Law—can coherently adjudicate what we might call threshold-conditional maxims: principles that direct an agent to act in one way provided the principle is not yet widely adopted, but to switch to a different action once universal (or near-universal) adoption reaches a specified point. The motivation for such maxims is familiar from collective-action problems, in which each agent might rationally want universal cooperation yet also prefer to defect once cooperation is assured. Frame the controversy precisely. On one side, argue that universalizing a threshold-conditional maxim yields no logical contradiction, because the maxim's content explicitly includes the adoption-level condition; at full universalization, every agent simply executes the second clause, and no impossible state of affairs results. On the other side, argue that such a maxim is practically self-defeating in a deeper sense: it treats one's own rational endorsement as a strategic variable to be manipulated rather than as an unconditional commitment, thereby violating the Formula of Humanity, and that the contradiction in the will arises not from the outcome but from the agent's necessary interest in preserving the very condition—widespread non-adoption—that the maxim requires for its first clause to apply. Weigh whether this second objection collapses the distinction between moral and prudential reasoning, and whether accommodating threshold-conditional maxims forces Kantian ethics into a form of rule-utilitarianism. Reach and defend a specific verdict: either that Kant's tests must reject such maxims but then need a supplementary account of collective moral action, or that they can accept them only by reinterpreting universalizability as a test on institutional rules rather than on individual maxims. Do not end on an agnostic note; justify your conclusion by showing which interpretation better preserves the unconditional character of the categorical imperative.
