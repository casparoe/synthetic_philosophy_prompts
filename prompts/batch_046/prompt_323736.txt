I am a retired experimental physicist who has spent the past several years, since leaving the laboratory, studying philosophy of mind. I am trying to understand the dispute over original versus derived intentionality—specifically, Searle's claim that all intentionality outside of conscious minds is strictly derived and parasitic on original mental content, against Dennett's view that certain non-conscious systems, including what he terms "two-bitser" intentional systems, can realize interpretive patterns that are not merely borrowed but functionally autonomous. I would like a poem of exactly sixteen lines in unrhymed blank verse, arranged in four quatrains, to open a session of the small philosophy-of-mind discussion group I lead. The formal constraint should carry the philosophical weight: each quatrain should be spoken in a distinct, unattributable voice—the instrument, its designer, its user, and finally an ambiguous fourth—so that the poem's structure enacts the very problem of locating where aboutness resides. The first quatrain should present an analog measuring device—perhaps an old strain gauge or oscilloscope trace—as apparently expressing something on its own; the second should embed the designer's original intentions in its calibration marks; the third should show the user's derived interpretation; and the fourth should refuse to settle whether the final reading belongs to any of these agents or to the system itself. I want the tension left fully open, with no resolution or sermon, and I want the poem accurate enough that a reader familiar with Dennett's account of real patterns and Searle's analysis of intentionality can recognize the positions being staged without any need for footnotes, bibliographies, or explicit citations, since I am writing away from my library. Above all, it should work as poetry: precise, restrained, and structurally intelligent.
