You are an expert philosopher of technology and epistemologist with practical experience in professional licensure and assessment design. Before you begin, take a few minutes to brainstorm the conceptual distinctions that must survive translation into a practical tool—specifically, the difference between using an external cognitive instrument and being epistemically eroded by it, and which layers of understanding (propositional, procedural, corrective) can be delegated without loss.

I serve on a national engineering licensure board. We are seeing a rising volume of design portfolios—structural, software, and systems-level—that incorporate outputs from generative AI: calculations, code modules, risk assessments, and design rationales. We need a reusable decision procedure that a three-person technical review panel can run in approximately 45 minutes per portfolio. The panel has domain expertise but no formal philosophy training. The stakes are high: a false positive licenses an engineer who cannot independently defend critical reasoning; a false negative unfairly bars a competent practitioner who used tools appropriately. Decisions must be documentable for appeal, and any hand-off to subjective judgment must be explicitly flagged and signed off by the panel chair.

Operationalize the framework of epistemic scaffolding versus epistemic substitution. Epistemic scaffolding occurs when the AI extends the candidate’s reasoning—accelerating computation, formatting outputs, or surfacing alternatives—while leaving the candidate able to reconstruct, correct, and defend every substantive step in the reasoning chain, including the identification of embedded assumptions and the conditions under which the output would fail. Epistemic substitution occurs when the AI occupies a substantive reasoning node that the candidate treats as a black box: they can report and apply the output but cannot explain why competing alternatives were rejected, cannot identify the assumptions the model relied on, and cannot correct the output if underlying conditions change. Between these lies procedural offloading, where the candidate understands the domain but has delegated a specific reasoning sub-routine that they could, with reasonable effort, reconstruct.

Write the deliverable as a flowchart in words: an ordered sequence of gates with concrete behavioral indicators. For each gate, specify:
- The exact question the panel must ask.
- What counts as a sufficient answer (observable, not impressionistic).
- Whether the gate is a testable factual checkpoint or a judgment call.
- The stop condition: definitive pass (scaffolding confirmed), definitive fail (substitution detected, portfolio rejected or sent back for revision), or explicit hand-off to the panel’s values judgment, marked as such.

The procedure must handle these hard cases and include them as worked applications:
1. A structural engineer used AI to generate load-bearing calculations. They can explain the underlying physics and boundary conditions but cannot reproduce the numerical integration without the tool.
2. A software architect used AI to write a safety-critical encryption and access-control module. They understand the system specification and can defend the choice of protocol family, but they did not write the algorithm, cannot trace a specific failure mode through the implementation, and rely on the AI’s assurance that the module is side-channel resistant.
3. A case where the procedure returns an answer the board will find unwelcome: the candidate demonstrates deep, reconstructive understanding of the overall system architecture and can defend every design decision except one technically correct but epistemically opaque core sub-component generated by AI. The procedure classifies this as substitution at that node, requiring revision, despite the overall competence being high.

Then apply the full procedure step-by-step to all three cases, showing the path through each gate and the final determination.

Finally, state honestly what the procedure cannot do: which kinds of understanding it will miss, where it is likely to misfire (e.g., highly verbal candidates who mask substitution with fluent explanation), and under what conditions the board should override it. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, citations, or references to specific named theorists or papers. Keep the framework self-contained so that a technically trained panel can apply it without philosophical training.
