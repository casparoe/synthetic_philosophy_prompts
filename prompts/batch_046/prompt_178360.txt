I'm delivering the opening remarks for a two-day workshop titled "Consciousness and Clinical Practice," facing an audience split between neuropsychologists who have run split-field experiments with callosotomy patients and philosophers of mind who know the literature mainly through secondhand summaries. Rather than rehearse the familiar "one mind or two?" debate that both sides have exhausted, I want the speech to pivot on a conceptual tension I've been developing—call it the authority asymmetry: in standard descriptions of split-brain behavior, the left hemisphere's verbal report is treated as the authoritative account of "the patient," while the right hemisphere's nonverbal responses are read as evidence of a separate conscious stream only when they conflict with that verbal narrative, not when they silently agree with it. I want to use this asymmetry to restructure the workshop's central question away from counting consciousnesses and toward asking whether our clinical and philosophical vocabulary for attributing experience already presupposes a unified narrator, thereby rendering certain split-brain results unreadable on their own terms. The classic experimental setups—lateralized visual presentation, chimeric figure recognition, intermanual conflict—should be treated as shared background, not as exhibits requiring citation or author-date attribution. The tone should be formal but not academic in the worst sense: rigorous enough that clinicians feel their practical knowledge is being honored, yet clear enough that philosophers without case-file experience can grasp the structural point. Crucially, the speech must not resolve the tension or crown a winner; it should leave both groups with the same uncomfortable possibility—that unity might be less a neurological finding than a framing condition built into how we ask patients to report on themselves. Please write the seven-minute address.
