Consider an agricultural ethics board with these past rulings: it permitted broad-spectrum insecticide use only when crop failure threatened regional food security and no targeted alternative existed; it banned neonicotinoid treatment of ornamental lawns despite low human risk; it approved releasing parasitoid wasps that inflict prolonged suffering on target caterpillars but spare pollinators; it rejected an invasive-ant bait that also kills native ground beetles, even where the ants destroy crops; and it conditionally approved acoustic bird deterrents in orchards after visual methods failed, accepting stress-related harm. A new proposal seeks to deploy a species-specific fungal pathogen—engineered to infect only one locust species—that causes roughly 48 hours of visible neurological impairment before death, avoiding chemical runoff and non-target casualties; the alternative is immediate mechanical crushing. Should the board approve it to stay consistent with its record? State the closest analog, lay out at least two organizing principles that fit the prior rulings, predict what each principle says about this case, and—if the precedents genuinely underdetermine the answer—identify the moral commitment the board would be adopting with each choice. Write in American English.
