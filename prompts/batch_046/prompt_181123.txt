I serve on the clinical ethics and intervention board of a neurorehabilitation hospital that reviews care-plan adjustments for patients in disorders of consciousness—minimally conscious state or unresponsive wakefulness—when subjective reports are obtained through brain-computer interfaces, eye-tracking, or structured behavioral assessment. Our advisory literature has adopted the view that phenomenal consciousness is best modeled not as a direct readout of neural activity but as a functional user-illusion: an evolved interface that simplifies and represents underlying brain states for navigation, which can become decoupled from those states when architecture is severely damaged. On this framework, residual circuits in injured brains can generate interface artifacts—mechanically produced signals or reports of subjective experience that lack the integrated functional profile associated with genuine consciousness.

We need a reusable decision procedure that my board can run in a single 90-minute session, without requiring philosophical expertise, to determine whether a given patient report should be treated as authoritative for high-stakes clinical actions such as withdrawing analgesia, enrolling in experimental therapy, or altering life-support parameters. The procedure must translate the user-illusion principle into an ordered sequence of factual questions or tests, specify what counts as sufficient evidence at each step, identify the precise nodes where judgment is unavoidable, and include clear stop points that yield one of three outputs: treat the report as authoritative, treat it as a probable interface artifact and do not base care upon it, or declare the data insufficient and defer to further evaluation. It must also handle cases where family values conflict with the procedural recommendation.

Please construct the procedure in explicit stepwise form, then apply it to three cases:

1. A patient in a minimally conscious state whose BCI outputs a consistent affirmative signal to pain queries, but whose thalamocortical integration metrics sit near baseline and whose responses occur only in rigid, stereotyped rhythms unrelated to stimulus variation.

2. A patient with extensive frontoparietal damage who, via eye-tracking, reports vivid visual experiences; functional imaging reveals isolated occipital activation with no detectable global workspace integration or recurrent processing.

3. A scenario in which the procedure classifies a report as a probable interface artifact and recommends discontinuing analgesic treatment based on that classification, but the patient’s family insists on continuing care, citing the patient’s previously expressed values about suffering and dignity.

In your response, present the ordered steps with decision nodes, state for each step whether the criterion is factual or a masked judgment call, specify the evidence that satisfies or fails it, mark all stop points with their outputs, work through all three applications in detail—including the unwelcome recommendation in case three—and conclude with a frank statement of what the procedure cannot do: where it will systematically misfire, which ethical burdens remain outside its scope, and which theoretical commitments about consciousness are embedded in steps that appear purely technical. Do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations.
