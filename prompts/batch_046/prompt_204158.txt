As a novelist constructing a narrative about institutional self-entrapment, I have been studying Martin Shubik's dollar auction less as a parlor curiosity than as a structural template for historical escalations in which rational actors incrementally commit resources toward a prize that recedes faster than it approaches. I want to examine the American escalation in Vietnam between 1965 and 1968—specifically the successive troop increases requested by General Westmoreland, authorized by President Johnson and Secretary McNamara, often in direct contradiction to internal assessments that the military strategy was failing to secure its stated objectives. Analyze this episode through the lens of escalation-of-commitment dynamics: map the actual decision-makers as bidders, identify what was being auctioned (national credibility, the avoidance of perceived defeat, or something more diffuse), account for how sunk costs functioned as binding bids, and explain why each incremental deployment seemed locally rational even as the aggregate commitment became strategically incoherent.

Then show me where the auction metaphor fractures—for instance, whether the North Vietnamese refusal to behave as an auctioned prize means there was never actually a winnable object, whether domestic audiences such as Congress and the press operated as an unacknowledged auctioneer altering the payoff rules, and whether the theory's necessary silence on the expendability of human lives renders it illuminating but finally inadequate as an account of what transpired. I am especially interested in whether the escalation can be read as a case in which the bidders themselves did not share a common definition of the prize, and what that misalignment reveals about the limits of backward-looking rationality in institutional decision-making.
