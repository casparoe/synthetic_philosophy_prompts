I'm taking a course on existentialism and the philosophy of religion, and we've spent several weeks working through Kierkegaard's Fear and Trembling. After a seminar discussion about whether Abraham's suspension of the ethical is a singular anomaly or a structural feature available to any faith, I reconstructed what looks like a reductio of the teleological suspension, but I cannot locate whether the error is a hidden equivocation or whether I am simply being forced to bite an unwelcome bullet.

(1) For Kierkegaard, the ethical stage is defined by the universal: moral norms apply identically to every individual, regardless of private inclination or particular circumstance. (2) The teleological suspension of the ethical occurs precisely when an individual stands in an absolute relation to the absolute—Abraham receives a command that conflicts with the universal prohibition on killing his child, and in that relation the ethical is suspended. (3) If the ethical is genuinely suspended in even one authentic case, then its authority is not unconditional but defeasible: it binds only so long as no overriding absolute relation is present. (4) Any conscientious agent who sincerely believes they have received such an absolute command must treat the ethical as suspended for them, on pain of disobeying the absolute. (5) Because the presence of an absolute command is not publicly verifiable, every agent must—before acting on universal norms—perform an inward audit to check whether they stand in such a relation; otherwise they risk treating a genuine command of the absolute as if it were merely ethical. (6) Therefore, the ethical can never be followed straightforwardly but must always be held in abeyance, pending this private check. (7) This collapses Kierkegaard's tripartite structure: if everyone must live in this state of potential suspension, then the knight of faith is not exceptional but universal, and the ethical stage loses its independent validity.

My intuition is that the slip lies somewhere between (3) and (5)—perhaps an equivocation between metaphysical defeasibility and an epistemic obligation to suspend, or a quantifier shift from "there exists a case" to "for every agent, there might exist." But a friend in the course insists the chain is valid and that we should instead reject (1) and treat the universal as already conditional. Which numbered step should actually be rejected, and what does giving it up cost—specifically, does it preserve Abraham's exceptionality, or does it force the conclusion that ordinary ethical life is impossible without constant inward crisis? Please conclude with a single line in the form \answer{step [N]; central cost: [brief phrase]} stating the step you reject and the core price of that rejection.
