As a retired physicist who has recently begun studying philosophy of social science in earnest, I am preparing a workshop for a small interdisciplinary reading group—approximately ten to twelve adults with backgrounds in the natural sciences, law, or engineering, meeting for a single ninety-minute session. I want them to experience, rather than merely discuss, how a convention in the Lewisian sense can outlive every reason that originally established it, yet remain fully stable because each subgroup preserves a different fragment of its history and treats that fragment as authoritative.

More precisely: imagine a coordination problem is initially solved by a salient feature that everyone can observe—say, a shared landmark. Over successive generations of interaction, direct knowledge of the landmark fades, but different clusters retain partial records: one cluster remembers the landmark's location but not its purpose; another remembers the purpose but misplaces the location; a third has no record but enforces compliance through punishment of deviation, interpreting the punishment itself as proof of the landmark's importance. The result is a self-enforcing equilibrium that no participant can fully articulate, where attempts to rationalize the convention generate competing narratives that themselves become coordination devices.

Please design an in-session activity—preferably a structured game or role-play with defined rules, roles, and a non-monetary payoff scheme—that makes participants discover this dynamic for themselves before it is named. The rules should be arranged so that the group initially coordinates easily around an explicit shared signal, then, through staged information decay (for example, restricted communication rounds, role-specific memory constraints, or hidden rule modifications), finds itself maintaining a pattern of behavior whose original justification has vanished, with different members defending incompatible reconstructions of why the pattern must continue. Only after they have experienced the tension between stable coordination and lost common knowledge should the facilitator map the experience onto Lewis's framework—salience, precedent, common knowledge, and the conditions for convention stability—and onto the specific phenomenon of fossilized convention I have described.

Please include: (1) the exact setting and group division; (2) step-by-step rules and materials needed; (3) a timeline for the ninety minutes; (4) the debrief structure that connects lived moments to the conceptual framework; (5) an honest assessment of which feature of fossilized convention the activity cannot reproduce and must therefore be supplied by the facilitator; and (6) a brief note on what is likely to go wrong the first time this is run and how to correct for it.

The design should not depend on bibliographies, citations, or specialized literature that a well-trained model without search access could not reasonably reconstruct from general knowledge.
