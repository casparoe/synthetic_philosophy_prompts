Write a villanelle on Montaigne’s comparison in “Of Cannibals” between the ritual consumption of enemies by Tupinambá warriors and European practices of judicial torture and execution. Use two refrains that exchange blame and description: first, “We tear the living limb from limb,” spoken initially as European self-accusation disguised as other-description; second, “They eat only those already slain,” spoken initially as defense of the cannibal practice. Structure the poem so that each tercet shifts the speaker’s identity—European observer, cannibal respondent, and finally an ambiguous voice—without marking the shift explicitly, so that by the final couplet, where both refrains must appear together, the pronouns “we” and “they” have become unstable. Let the rhyme scheme enforce this exchange: the A-refrains should occupy the anchoring lines, while the b-rhymes migrate vocabulary of cruelty (torn/slain, fire/pyre, etc.) across stanzas so that the descriptors apply to both sides. The poem should be accurate enough that someone familiar with Montaigne’s essay recognizes his specific contrast—cannibals killing enemies in open battle and honoring them afterward versus Europeans destroying bodies slowly out of fear and superstition—rather than treating cannibalism as generic savagery. After the poem, include a brief note stating how confident you are in this reading of Montaigne and identifying the specific point—whether about the cannibals’ motives, the comparison to religious wars, or the ambiguity of the speaker—where you are least sure or where the poem takes the greatest liberty with the text.
