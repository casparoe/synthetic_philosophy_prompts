I am writing a dissertation chapter on the normative architecture of linguistic practice, and I am trying to isolate a conceptual tension in the Kripkenstein literature that does not collapse neatly into either the familiar skeptical paradox or the canonical straight solution. I am particularly concerned with cases in which the relevant linguistic community is not homogeneous but stratified by expertise—professional mathematicians, advanced students, and lay speakers—so that the distribution of dispositions to apply a given rule is bimodal rather than uniform. The difficulty I want to crystallize runs as follows: if correct rule-following depends on agreement in responses, but the community’s responses cluster around two genuinely incompatible norms—say, regarding the legitimacy of a particular inference step in non-standard analysis—then an unqualified appeal to “the practice of the community” seems to underdetermine the correct rule unless one imports an external normative hierarchy that the anti-reductionist straight solution was meant to bracket.

Could you construct a concrete, worked example—deliberately distinct from the standard “plus” versus “quus” scenario—that renders this stratification vivid within mathematical or logical practice? I would like the example to show not merely disagreement about application, but a structured divergence in which each subgroup claims authoritative mastery of the rule. Once the example is in place, please locate the precise conceptual pressure point: does the difficulty expose an internal failure of the agreement condition itself, reveal a concealed reliance on epistemic authority incompatible with Wittgensteinian anti-reductionism, or constitute an altogether separate skeptical challenge that requires its own form of response? I am not looking for a bibliography; I want the carving of the concept—what it is, what it borders on, and where its edges lie.
