You are a philosopher of law and constitutional theory with expertise in rational-choice institutional design. Before answering, take a moment to brainstorm: list at least four distinct precise theses that a single proverb could be encoding, then evaluate each for plausibility and for whether it actually supports the argument being made with it.

In recent debates about democratic constitutionalism, advocates of rigid entrenchment—such as eternity clauses, multi-generational supermajority rules, or non-amendable rights provisions—often invoke the proverb "tie yourself to the mast," drawn from the Odyssey narrative in which Odysseus orders his crew to bind him so he can hear the Sirens without steering the ship onto the rocks. They deploy the image to argue that a democratic polity ought to bind its future legislative selves with hard-to-revise constraints, on the grounds that present citizens can foresee that future majorities will suffer from predictable failures of rationality (populist surges, fiscal myopia, rights-erosion, or intertemporal discounting). The slogan is made to do heavy normative work: it is meant to bridge descriptive psychology and constitutional architecture, suggesting that precommitment is not anti-democratic but an expression of democratic self-governance over time.

But the proverb is deeply ambiguous. Separate out the precise claims it could be making. For each reading, indicate whether it is a platitude, a substantive and defensible thesis, or false, and specify whether that reading actually supports the use of entrenchment clauses in democratic constitutions. In particular, address these dimensions of ambiguity and lay them out as a menu rather than selecting a single interpretation:

1. The ontology of the "self" being bound. Is the future legislature a numerically different agent, the same continuing polity with diachronic identity, or a collective that lacks any unified self? How does each answer affect whether "binding yourself" is coherent, and does coherence depend on accepting a particular metaphysics of collective agency?

2. The nature of the "irrationality" being precommitted against. Is it preference reversal (time-inconsistent choice), an information failure that future actors will correct once informed, or a normative change in values that present citizens happen to reject? Does the slogan distinguish between protecting future citizens from cognitive bias versus imposing present substantive values on them?

3. The mechanism of binding. Does "tying" require an external, non-democratic enforcer (e.g., an independent constitutional court with strike-down power), or can democratic institutions bind themselves purely through internal procedural rules?

4. The scope of legitimate precommitment. Does the slogan justify only narrow, reversible commitments (like balanced-budget rules with sunset provisions), or also broad, irrevocable ones (like banning amendments that alter the constitutional form itself)?

Which of these two mechanisms is actually at work in constitutional entrenchment, and does the proverb's imagery of physical restraint mislead us about which mechanism democratic constitutions actually employ?
