As a novelist constructing a narrative about an isolated monastic scriptorium, I have become preoccupied with the philosophical debate over norms of assertion—whether a speaker must know what she says, need only speak truly, or may assert on sincere belief alone. To ground a scene in which an elder scribe pronounces on the meaning of a lost original manuscript, I want you to rank four candidate norms according to how well they adjudicate her entitlement to speak in a precisely engineered case.

The four norms are these: the knowledge norm; the truth norm; the belief norm; and what I will call the custodial-chain norm, according to which assertion is warranted if the speaker occupies an unbroken institutional line of textual custody, irrespective of direct epistemic access or personal justification. The case is constructed as follows. Across four generations of hand-copying in a sealed library, each scribe sincerely intended fidelity, but a symmetric corruption introduced in the second generation accidentally restored the original meaning in the fourth copy. Consequently, the elder scribe’s belief is true and sincerely held, yet her justification is structurally severed from the source—her accuracy is owed entirely to the corruption’s accidental symmetry, not to any reliable connection to the original. Rank the four norms from the one that most clearly condemns her assertion to the one that most clearly permits it. For each, provide a single sentence explaining its placement and identify the precise feature of the custodial-corruption scenario that forces that verdict.

Please also state how confident you are in this overall ordering, and explicitly single out the point about which you are least sure—particularly whether the custodial-chain norm is genuinely distinct from a degraded belief norm or instead collapses into a form of institutional deference that philosophers of language would reject as a confusion of social role with epistemic entitlement.
