Consider the administrative practice, common in conditional unemployment-benefit regimes, of requiring claimants to maintain a verified job-search log—often with employer stamps, timestamps, or digital check-ins—where sanctions are triggered almost exclusively by formal gaps (missing entries, absent signatures) rather than by the substantive implausibility of the applications listed. A claimant who submits thirty applications for roles for which she is manifestly unqualified, all properly stamped, typically faces no penalty; a claimant who makes ten serious, well-matched applications but fails to record one employer's stamp may lose benefits for weeks. What philosophical theory of desert, moral responsibility, or the purpose of welfare conditionality does this asymmetry presuppose? Does it presuppose that blameworthiness tracks procedural compliance rather than good-faith effort, or can a control-based account of responsibility explain why missing documentation is treated as a more serious failure than strategic box-ticking? Is the practice consistent with the same legal system's insistence, in tort and negligence law, that we assess culpability by reference to reasonable care rather than to record-keeping? Reconstruct the implicit theory of the 'undeserving poor' that makes this design rational, distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what merely fits it well, and say whether an alternative framework—say, one grounded in capability or in genuine labor-market attachment—would license a fundamentally different sanction structure, or whether the current form is philosophically underdetermined.
