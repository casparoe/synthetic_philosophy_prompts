I spent thirty years in experimental condensed-matter physics before retiring, and in the past few years I have turned to metaphysics and the philosophy of science with the same systematic temperament I once brought to the laboratory. One problem that now strikes me as genuinely unsettled—and surprisingly relevant to how we talk about the entities I used to measure—is what philosophers call the special composition question: under what conditions do parts genuinely constitute a single whole, rather than merely coexisting?

Within this area, several competing lines of inquiry seem to demand attention, but I am skeptical that all of them are equally fruitful. Rather than a broad survey, I want a defended agenda: please evaluate the four issues below, rank them from most to least worth sustained philosophical effort over the coming decade, and justify the ordering with explicit criteria—tractability, downstream consequences for scientific ontology, and the risk that a line of work is merely semantic or redundant. I am writing this as someone who cares whether metaphysical conclusions should change how a working scientist conceives of her objects, not as a historian of philosophy, so please do not provide a reading list, bibliography, or citations.

The four issues are these:

1. The causal-functional integration criterion. Should parts be said to compose a whole only when they stand in specific causal or functional relations—rather than in mere spatiotemporal proximity—and does adopting this criterion resolve, or merely relocate, the boundary-vagueness that afflicts moderate answers to the special composition question?

2. The ontological status of borderline scientific entities—viruses, Bose-Einstein condensates, or tightly coupled ecological communities. If our best physics treats a condensate as occupying a single quantum state, does that commit us to the existence of a corresponding composite object? Or can scientific practice remain ontologically neutral about composition without undermining explanatory power?

3. A tension I will call mereological state-dependence: in certain quantum regimes, the individuation of constituent parts appears to dissolve, so that asking whether “the atoms” still compose “the condensate” seems to depend on the physical state rather than on timeless mereological rules. Is this a genuine challenge to the special composition question, or is it a category mistake that confuses physical description with ontological structure?

4. The downstream implications for macroscopic object ontology. If some form of restricted composition is correct, do we then have principled grounds for denying the existence of arbitrary scattered objects? And more importantly, does that denial carry weight for scientific practice—for instance, in explaining why a crystal fractures along specific crystallographic planes rather than explaining properties of its randomly scattered atoms?

Please defend your ranking: identify your top two and bottom two, explain the trade-offs between them, and specify which single resolution, if achieved, would most change how a scientist should think about the entities in her models. I want to know where limited effort should go, and why.
