In a recent philosophy discussion group on population ethics, two participants—call them Mara and Leo—became stuck in an extended disagreement about pronatalism and whether a larger population is genuinely better, with a framing twist that neither felt certain how to classify.

Mara argued that pronatalist policies are defensible on total-welfare grounds: if lives are generally good, then bringing more such lives into existence increases the total amount of good in the world. She acknowledged the non-identity problem—changing reproductive policy changes who exists, so one cannot claim that any particular future person is “better off” for being born—but insisted that this only blocks person-affecting comparisons, not aggregate comparisons across different possible populations.

Leo conceded the aggregate point but introduced what he called a “relational-structure” objection. Some goods, he said, are essentially plural and non-decomposable: the value of a language sustained by millions of speakers, the value of dense intergenerational knowledge networks, or the value of artistic traditions that require large populations to maintain critical mass. These goods, he argued, are not reducible to the summed welfare of individuals; they are properties of the population structure itself. So pronatalism could be justified not merely by adding more welfare-bearers, but by enabling collective forms of life that are structurally impossible at smaller scales.

Mara replied that Leo’s relational goods remain parasitic on persons: a language needs speakers, a tradition needs practitioners. If the justification for creating more people is to sustain these structures, then persons are being treated as instrumental to structural goods. This, she claimed, violates a person-affecting constraint even if no specific individual is made worse off. She added that Leo’s view appears to license extreme pronatalism—if larger populations always enable richer relational structures, there is no natural stopping point short of physical limits.

Leo countered that Mara’s aggregate view faces an analogous instrumental charge: on total-utilitarian grounds, individuals are also instrumental to the maximization of welfare. He then raised a comparative point: if we reject pronatalism because of identity-affecting effects, consistency seems to demand that we also reject climate-mitigation policies that change who will exist in the future, which strikes him as implausible. He asked whether there is a principled asymmetry that permits identity-affecting climate action while blocking identity-affecting pronatalism, without merely appealing to intuition.

They asked for an adjudication of three specific questions: whether Leo’s relational-structure argument genuinely escapes the non-identity problem or collapses into instrumentalization; whether Mara’s aggregate view successfully avoids the instrumental charge she levels at Leo; and whether Leo’s climate-mitigation comparison succeeds or overlooks a relevant asymmetry. They also want to know what both parties are missing.
