I am a novelist applying for a six-month interdisciplinary writing fellowship, and I need a precise, workable proposal for a hybrid manuscript—philosophical argument interwoven with narrative fragments—addressing the following question: has the modern revival of Stoicism, particularly in therapeutic and popular contexts, quietly transformed Epictetus's sharp dichotomy of control (between what lies in our power and what does not) into a graded spectrum of "influence," and does this shift depend on an unexamined assumption about the linear, forward-moving temporality of agency that collapses under the retrospective structure of narrative interpretation?

The standard positions in both popular Stoicism and its academic defenders leave this open because they treat the boundary between prohairesis and externals as identifiable in real time by a single agent, without asking whether that identification requires the agent to stand in a specific temporal relation to the event. Fiction reveals the gap: in a completed narrative, interpretive judgments about character and cause are causally posterior to the events they organize, yet the text was constructed with those judgments anticipated. I want to introduce the concept of retrospective control asymmetry—the idea that the dichotomy may be structurally sharp for the agent living forward but porous for the agent (or reader) reflecting backward—and test whether modern Stoicism's therapeutic coherence actually requires suppressing this asymmetry.

The method: month one, refine the conceptual framework by comparing the classical Stoic sources attributed to Epictetus with three contemporary popular Stoic texts, tracking exactly where the binary is relaxed; months two and three, draft four fictional case studies (each approximately 5,000 words) that dramatize characters mistaking interpretive control over a past event for causal control over it; month four, analyze whether these dramatizations expose a dependency in modern Stoicism on non-retrospective agency; month five, synthesize the argument and assess whether the binary can be restored without losing therapeutic utility; month six, revise into a final 30,000-word hybrid manuscript plus a 2,000-word deliverable explaining how the framework might inform clinical discussions of agency without requiring Stoic metaphysics.

A useful result would be a defensible claim that the modern revival's relaxation of the dichotomy is not merely sloppy but structurally necessary for its application to complex, interpreted lives, paired with narrative evidence that this relaxation creates a specific kind of self-deception that fiction is uniquely positioned to diagnose. The project fails if retrospective control asymmetry collapses into the banal claim that hindsight is clearer; if the narrative fragments prove merely decorative rather than structurally necessary to the argument; or if the philosophical analysis demands more specialized Hellenistic scholarship than can be responsibly done in six months without formal philological training, leaving the historical claims unsupported.

Please present this as a formal proposal with a precise statement of the question, why current answers do not settle it, the month-by-month approach, what success and failure look like, and an honest assessment of scope risk. In your response, say how confident you are in what you claim and single out the point you are least sure of.
