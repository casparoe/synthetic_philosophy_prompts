I work on formal verification for distributed systems, and my team audits logical deduction chains embedded in system specification documents. We have no formal philosophy background, but we keep encountering what looks like Carroll's regress with Achilles and the Tortoise: a proof applies an inference step, but when we ask which adopted primitive rule licenses it, the answer cites another rule, and then another, with no stopping point. I need a reusable audit procedure—ordered tests, clear stopping conditions, and explicit judgment hand-offs—that a reviewer without philosophy training can run in about fifteen minutes on any deduction chain in a spec. The deliverable is not a one-off analysis but a practitioner-facing checklist.

Please operationalize the core insight from Carroll's problem into a step-by-step flowchart in words. For each deductive step in a chain, the reviewer must classify it as either (i) an instance of an explicitly adopted primitive rule in our framework, (ii) derived within three explicit intermediate steps from such a primitive, or (iii) a regress gap—an unstated inference rule that must be flagged. Include a concrete substitution test: if replacing a term with a co-referential expression breaks the step, the reviewer has detected an implicit structural rule that needs naming. Specify exactly what counts as a passing answer at each stage, where the procedure issues a hard stop and rejects the step, and where it must hand the decision to a human because the issue is a framework-choice value judgment rather than a factual check.

Take a committed, definite stance: adopt the hard line that any step not traceable to an adopted primitive within three explicit derivation steps is a regress gap that must be halted, and defend this against the more permissive alternative that allows common-logic rules to pass implicitly. Do not present both options as equally valid; explain why the permissive approach fails in a verification context.

Apply the procedure to three worked cases: (a) a security-protocol deduction that uses explicit modus ponens from an adopted conditional axiom, where the procedure should clear the step cleanly; (b) a state-machine proof that appeals to the transitivity of equality without listing equality axioms, where the procedure must flag a regress gap and halt—even though this produces an answer my team will resist because the inference seems obviously valid; and (c) a deduction where two different meta-rules could justify the step, leaving the framework adoption genuinely open, so the procedure stops and hands off.

Finally, state precisely what the procedure cannot do: it cannot justify the primitive rules themselves, it will misfire on genuinely primitive steps that happen to have complex syntactic form, and it does not resolve disputes about which primitives a framework ought to adopt. Keep everything usable by an engineer with no philosophy background, avoid any request for bibliographies or citations, and do not invoke niche theoretical frameworks that a small model would not reliably recall.
