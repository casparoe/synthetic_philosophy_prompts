I am preparing a graduate seminar in philosophy of history, and I find that discussions of presentism too quickly collapse into either unreflective moral condemnation or an equally unreflective appeal to empathy, without anyone specifying what a normative theory of presentism is actually being asked to do. Before we assess particular defenses or rejections, I want a rigorous specification—written entirely as continuous prose, arranged in flowing paragraphs without headings, bullet points, boldface, or tables, as if in a formal letter—of the desiderata that any adequate theory of presentism in historiography must meet, where adequacy is understood as being sufficient to regulate historiographical judgment without collapsing into either moral relativism or anachronistic dogmatism. Please set out the full requirements in prose form: the phenomena the theory must explain, the specific cases it must correctly adjudicate, the conceptual distinctions it must preserve rather than collapse, and the results from hermeneutics or philosophy of science with which it must remain consistent, giving the motivating consideration behind each. You should also indicate which desiderata are non-negotiable conditions of theoretical adequacy and which are merely negotiable preferences of particular camps—such as the demand for complete diachronic neutrality versus the demand that historians actively suppress present moral vocabulary—and you should identify which single requirement, if enforced strictly, eliminates the largest number of popular positions.

In particular, the theory must distinguish and separately account for explanatory presentism, in which present conceptual schemes reshape causal narratives without altering the underlying evidential base, and axiological presentism, in which present moral standards are applied to past agents, since the objections to each differ fundamentally and a theory that conflates them fails at the outset. It must also address the rarely discussed problem of retrospective causal ascription in microhistory, where the salience of particular causes depends on present interests, and it must explain how historians can maintain epistemic humility given the hermeneutic circle without surrendering to a radical relativism that renders cross-temporal disagreement impossible. Another necessary but often ignored constraint is that the theory must respect the distinction between first-order presentist judgment and second-order methodological presentism—the reflexive awareness of one's own historical situatedness—without treating second-order awareness as an automatic cancellation of first-order evaluation. Among these, I regard the preservation of some reconstructed distinction between descriptive explanation and normative evaluation as non-negotiable, though whether that distinction must be primitive rather than pragmatically reconstructed is open; by contrast, the insistence that historians never apply contemporary moral categories strikes me as a camp-specific prejudice rather than a condition of adequacy. Please also flag any pair of desiderata that appear jointly unsatisfiable—especially whether the demand for complete escape from one's own conceptual horizon can be reconciled with the hermeneutic necessity of present interpretive resources.

Finally, in the same continuous prose and without offering any bibliography, citation, or reading list, briefly score the main contenders against these criteria: the strict anti-presentist orthodoxy associated with certain mid-twentieth-century historiographical commitments, the qualified presentism that permits moral judgment but prohibits anachronistic causal explanation, and a radical hermeneutic position that denies the possibility of transcending one's historical horizon. Note which requirements each view sacrifices, whether any sacrifice is fatal to its adequacy, and whether any contender manages to satisfy the non-negotiable core without falling into the prejudices of its camp. The evaluation should stand on conceptual grounds alone, suitable for discussion in a seminar that has no access to external references.
