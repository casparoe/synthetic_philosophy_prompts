I've been tracking a particular argumentative pattern in discussions of deontological ethics—especially with friends who take Kantian or rights-based approaches seriously—and I'm unsure whether it's a recognized failure mode or just a rhetorical habit I've started noticing. The pattern works like this: a speaker defends an absolute constraint, such as never using a person's labor without explicit consent, not merely by appealing to the act's intrinsic structure, but by adding that such rules protect people systemically—if everyone violated them, social cooperation would collapse and more people would end up exploited. So far this sounds like a standard institutional justification layered on top of agent-centered morality.

But then comes the second move. When confronted with a case where the institutional protection is absent—say, a crisis where no collective mechanism exists, and one person's refusal to violate the constraint will result in dozens of others being exploited—the speaker doesn't allow that the systemic failure changes the agent's duty. Instead, they say the individual must still obey the constraint, and that fixing the systemic gap is someone else's responsibility—the state's, the community's, not the agent's. What's striking is that the systemic reasoning, which was offered as part of the justification for the rule, is suddenly disqualified from justifying an exception; it only works upward, never downward. I've watched this happen with arguments about lying, about bodily autonomy in triage scenarios, and about property rights in disaster zones. The rule is defended with one eye on outcomes, then enforced with both eyes shut.

Is there an established concept for this specific one-way insulation—where deontological constraints borrow consequentialist or systemic support for their legitimacy, but reject any consequentialist or systemic override in application? It feels related to the paradox of deontology, but that paradox is usually framed as the tension between allowing greater harm to prevent lesser harm. Here the issue is different: the harm is invoked to justify the rule, then ignored when it might excuse breaking it. If there is a name, I want to know how it differs from simple rule-worship, from institutional division of labor, and from ordinary moral rigidity. If not, can you coin a precise term with clear boundary conditions—what counts as an instance, what doesn't, and where it shades into legitimate separation of institutional and individual morality?
