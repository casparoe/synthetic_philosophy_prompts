Design a 90-minute, in-person seminar activity for 16 upper-level philosophy students that makes them physically experience the tension between Cantorian actual infinity and strict finitist verification. The setting is a workshop room with movable chairs; do not begin by naming Hilbert's hotel, Cantor's theorem, or finitism. Instead, participants should discover the conflict through structured action.

Divide the group into four "hotel managers," four "finitist inspectors," and eight "guests." Arrange 16 chairs in a line labeled 1 through 16, with an empty "continuation zone" marked by a single symbol (∞) but no additional chairs. Give each guest a card bearing a unique natural-number identity. Managers must seat every arriving guest using only two permitted operations: the "shift" (every current occupant moves to the next chair, freeing chair 1) and the "pairing" (guest with identity n is seated in chair 2n, leaving odd-numbered chairs empty). Managers earn 3 points for every guest seated without leaving anyone in the hallway, but lose 2 points each time an inspector issues a valid "freeze."

Inspectors are equipped with "ledger tokens": physical counters that must be placed on a tracking board every time a manager reassigns a guest. The inspectors' rule — the injected constraint not found in standard presentations — is that any reassignment chain must reach a "base case" (a chair with no prior occupant) within five token placements. If a chain loops or extends beyond five tokens with no base case, the inspector may freeze the move, forcing the manager to revert it. Inspectors earn 2 points per valid freeze, but lose 3 points for an invalid freeze (a chain that actually terminates).

Provide managers with a payoff scheme using non-monetary stakes: accumulated points determine which team chooses the seminar's closing discussion question, giving them social incentive without real money.

At minute 30, introduce the "diagonal arrival": a new guest card is generated automatically by a rule visible on a central board — "if chair n is occupied, this guest takes chair n+1 in the continuation zone; if chair n is empty, this guest takes chair n." Managers must attempt to seat this guest using only shift or pairing. The physical layout should make the failure visceral: seating the diagonal guest requires displacing the occupant of chair 1, who then must be reseated, triggering an infinite regress that visibly exceeds the 16-chair boundary and generates an unclosable token loop.

Run the simulation for 40 minutes, followed by a 20-minute debrief. In the debrief, map the successful shift onto Hilbert's hotel's claim that an infinite set can accommodate a new element; map the pairing onto Cantor's one-to-one correspondence with a proper subset; map the token-loop freeze onto the finitist insistence that actual infinity violates verifiability conditions (no completed totality can be audited); and map the diagonal arrival onto Cantor's argument, but present it only as the concrete scheduling failure the group just experienced, not as an abstract proof over all functions.

Be explicit about what the activity reproduces (the intuitive pull of infinite accommodation, and the intuitive resistance based on unverifiable regress) and what it cannot reproduce: the universal quantification required for Cantor's proof that no enumeration can cover all real numbers, since the group encountered only one specifically constructed counter-example, not the full space of possible assignments. Also be frank about predictable failures: managers will try to extend the chair grid into the continuation zone, inspectors will overuse freezes out of confusion rather than principle, and participants will conflate the physical lack of chairs with logical impossibility — include specific facilitator interventions for each.

Deliverables: minute-by-minute timeline (0–90); card and token designs; chair-layout diagram description; complete rules and payoff scheme; debrief script with mapped correspondences; honest limitation statement; and troubleshooting guide for the three predictable failures. Do not request bibliographies, reading lists, or citations, and do not refer to theories or figures outside standard philosophy-of-mathematics curriculum.
