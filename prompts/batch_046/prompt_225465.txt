Jordan opened with a standard decision-theoretic argument: if you have invested years in a research program with negative marginal expected value, continuing solely because of the time already spent is a pure fallacy. The past expenditure is causally inert; it should play no role in the present choice, just as the shadow of a fallen tree should not determine where you plant a new one. Only future costs and benefits matter.

Riley replied with a specific structural counterexample. Imagine a mathematician who has spent a decade developing intuition in a highly idiosyncratic formal framework—complete with custom notation and proof techniques that do not transfer to adjacent fields. The remaining work needed to complete a result is small, but its marginal value to an outsider would be near zero, because the result is only comprehensible within that framework. For the mathematician herself, however, the past investment has not merely created psychological attachment; it has constructed a private capability landscape. Quitting now does not preserve resources for an equivalent alternative, because the expertise is not fungible. Thus, Riley argued, continuing is not honoring a dead cost but recognizing that the agent's present decision-relevant assets include a transformed skill set that makes the remaining path uniquely valuable to her.

Jordan responded that Riley has merely redescribed a forward-looking justification. The reason to continue is not the past years, but the present possession of specialized expertise that raises the future payoff. That is fully compatible with standard sunk-cost reasoning: update on your current state, ignore the history that produced it. If the same expertise had been acquired through a quick flash of insight rather than a decade of labor, the rational choice would be identical. So this is not a counterexample to the fallacy; it is just correct Bayesian updating on current assets.

Riley then shifted to a second, distinct argument about diachronic consistency. Even if the local calculus in a particular case favors quitting, an agent might rationally adopt a meta-rule—do not abandon long-term projects unless the marginal value is catastrophically negative—as a commitment device against her own present-biased tendencies to over-discount future rewards. In that case, honoring the sunk-cost-like pull to finish is not a cognitive error but the execution of a rationally pre-committed policy. The past investment serves as a tracking mechanism: it identifies which projects fall under the protected class, and the agent honors it not for its own sake but to preserve the credibility and efficacy of her future self's commitments.

Applying it in a particular instance where the local calculus clearly favors abandonment—where the remaining work is genuinely worthless and there are far better alternatives—is then just rigid error, not justification. A rational agent should violate her own rules precisely when she can see that doing so is optimal, otherwise she substitutes rule-worship for reasoning.
