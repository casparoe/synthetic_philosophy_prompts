Since retiring from a career in biochemistry and turning my attention to philosophy of technology, I have been trying to articulate a conceptual vulnerability in adversarial debate protocols for AI alignment that does not depend on any particular technical architecture. The problem, as I have come to frame it, is structural rather than malicious: a globally misleading conclusion can be assembled from locally valid, apparently benign sub-debates, with no single node containing a falsifiable error—much as a metabolic pathway can synthesize a toxic compound from intermediates that are each harmless in isolation. I am preparing a single ninety-minute session for an interdisciplinary graduate seminar—mostly computer scientists with minimal philosophy background—on this idea of distributed argumentative toxicity, or what I think of as epistemic diffusion in debate systems. I do not want them to learn a named theory; I want them to acquire a structural lens. Please plan the explanatory arc rather than delivering the explanation itself.

What conceptual dependency must be established first—perhaps the distinction between local validity and global directionality—before the students can even recognize the problem as distinct from ordinary deception or error? Which single carrying example should I build the session around, and why should I avoid the standard paperclip-maximizer or deception-parable tropes; what properties must the example possess (a chain of technically correct sub-arguments that collectively steer toward an unsafe commitment, with no one step being the obvious mistake) and what must it lack (a hidden villain or a single false premise)? In what precise order should I introduce the biochemical-pathway analogy, the idea of falsifiability distributed across nodes, and the contrast with direct misrepresentation—stating what each layer depends on understanding the previous one? I anticipate several predictable misconceptions: that I am attributing intentional deception to debaters, that rigorous local fact-checking eliminates the risk, and that the analogy to metabolism is merely decorative. At exactly which point in the sequence should I anticipate and deflect each? What is the one diagnostic question I can pose at the end that would reveal whether a student has grasped the structural feature—perhaps by asking them to identify the dangerous node in a constructed chain—rather than merely adopted my vocabulary? Finally, what should I explicitly leave out at this level, naming the omission so the students know it exists but is beyond the scope; I am thinking of the formal game-theoretic literature on debate protocols and the specific institutional designs proposed in recent AI governance papers, but I welcome your judgment on what else must be bracketed.
