Write a two-page fellowship proposal for a one-year dissertation project in philosophy of mind and cognitive science. I am working within Daniel Dennett's framework that treats the self not as a Cartesian substance but as a center of narrative gravity—an abstract theoretical construct that organizes autobiographical interpretation rather than a hidden observer behind experience. The existing literature divides sharply between narrative theorists, who see selfhood as essentially constituted by storytelling, and anti-narrativists, who argue that narrative coherence is at best a byproduct of more basic cognitive processes. What neither camp adequately explains is why certain disruptions to autobiographical memory—such as those reported in severe depersonalization or after targeted therapeutic interventions—are experienced not merely as updates to a fictional character but as threats to the self's continuity. I propose to investigate whether this phenomenon can be captured by what I call narrative viscosity: the differential resistance of specific autobiographical structures to revision within an otherwise fluid narrative system. The central question, stated precisely: is narrative viscosity an emergent feature of the center of gravity itself, arising from the density of interdependent beliefs that constitute it, or does it require an independent indexical anchor—a first-personal point of view that is not itself narratively constituted? Your proposal should include: (1) a precise formulation of the problem and why the current narrative versus anti-narrative debate leaves it unresolved; (2) the method, mapped month by month—conceptual analysis of first-person clinical reports of depersonalization, paired with a formal reconstruction of belief-revision dynamics drawn from predictive processing, to model how viscosity might arise without a substantive ego; (3) the criteria for success (a principled distinction between benign narrative updates and self-threatening disruptions that avoids both Cartesian substantialism and Humean reductionism) and for failure (either conceding that all autobiographical change is equally superficial, or reintroducing a pre-narrative subject to do the anchoring); (4) an honest scope assessment—what is achievable in twelve months without direct empirical collaboration, and where the project risks becoming too large for a philosophical dissertation chapter; and (5) a brief justification of why this is worth funding as conceptual work rather than clinical psychology. Do not include a bibliography or citation list; I will add references myself. Write from the perspective of a doctoral candidate who needs to convince a philosophy funding panel that the question is both tractable and not already settled by existing accounts of selfhood.
