I am a retired experimental physicist who has recently begun studying philosophy of science and epistemology. I wish to treat the common-prior assumption in Bayesian agreement theory as one might treat a fragile crystal under controlled stress: I want a precisely engineered scenario that reveals exactly where and how the assumption can fracture, without invoking exotic machinery. Please construct a thought experiment — a single, realistic laboratory episode, no longer than one paragraph — that satisfies the following specifications exactly.

Design a situation involving two rational scientists, Ada and Ben, who begin with an identical prior distribution over a binary empirical claim: whether a particular ceramic compound exhibits a sharp transition in electrical resistance at 77 K. Both observe the same public instrument trace; both are fully rational; and the rationality of each is common knowledge between them. The case must be built so that a standard Bayesian agreement model predicts convergence to identical posteriors once they share their updates. Nevertheless, Ada and Ben must end in stable, rational disagreement — not through hidden private data, but through a divergence in how they correct the shared likelihood function. Specifically: Ada applies a conservative truncation rule that discards tail readings as calibration artifacts, justified by a laboratory safety convention; Ben applies an evidential reweighting rule that preserves outliers but discounts them according to a principle of minimal theoretical revision. Both rules are genuinely optimal within their respective non-epistemic frameworks, and both scientists know precisely which rule the other employs.

After presenting the scenario, perform the design check. First, state Ada’s belief, Ben’s belief, the observational method, the relevant nearby possible worlds in which the trace differs slightly, and explain precisely why the standard agreement theorem delivers its verdict of convergence. Second, isolate the structural feature that does the work in breaking that convergence — name it, show that it is carrying the explanatory labor, and describe exactly what happens to the case if that feature is removed (that is, if both scientists apply the identical correction rule). Third, explain how a defender of the common-prior agreement thesis would attack your case: would they argue that the robustness rules are themselves part of the prior, that common knowledge of rationality is undermined, or that the disagreement is merely verbal? Finally, state honestly whether removing that feature collapses the scenario back into a standard agreement case, and whether the fracture you have isolated is therefore substantive or merely an artifact of framing.

Keep everything within ordinary scientific practice: no perfect predictors, no demons, no science-fictional instrumentation, and no appeal to unnamed niche doctrines that a generalist could not evaluate. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations. I want to see whether the failure of convergence is intrinsic to the prior assumption or hidden in the architecture of the likelihood model.
