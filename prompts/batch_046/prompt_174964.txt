I am preparing the closed-book final examination for an upper-level seminar in moral and political philosophy focused on aggregation, rescue obligations, and distributive ethics. Because students will not have access to external sources, the assessment must evaluate conceptual command rather than bibliographic recall or citation. I need one essay question—designed for approximately 90 minutes of writing and expected to produce an answer of about 800 words—accompanied by a model answer and a granular marking scheme. The subject is John Taurek's well-known thesis that, in a rescue scenario, the bare fact that more people are stranded on one side than on the other does not by itself generate a stronger moral reason to save the larger group, provided each person has an equal claim to rescue. To prevent answers that merely recite the standard harm-versus-failure-to-benefit distinction, please construct the question around two embedded conceptual twists. First, present a modified rescue case in which a single vessel can reach either Island A, where five stranded individuals share no prior social bonds, or Island B, where six are stranded, but the sixth is the pilot whose prior negligence caused the shipwreck that endangered the other five. Ask students whether this culpability changes the moral arithmetic—specifically, whether the negligent pilot's diminished moral standing can justify saving the smaller group without implicitly reintroducing a form of aggregation, and whether Taurek's pairwise-comparison framework can accommodate differences in moral desert among potential victims. Second, require students to address a subtle misreading frequently found in textbook summaries: the charge that Taurek's refusal to count heads presupposes moral atomism, treating each victim as an isolated rights-bearer with no relational obligations. The model answer should show how a contractualist reconstruction of Taurek's view—treating rescue duties as pairwise comparisons of what a rescuer owes each individual, rather than as summed weights—can answer this charge without secretly reinstating numerical comparison. The marking scheme should allocate 25 points across five required moves: (1) precise reconstruction of Taurek's core anti-aggregation argument and its separation from utilitarian reasoning (5 points); (2) analysis of the culpable-pilot variant, distinguishing moral desert from the structural logic of rescue obligations (6 points); (3) identification and correction of the moral atomism misreading (6 points); (4) articulation of the contractualist pairwise reconstruction and an explanation of why it avoids hidden counting (5 points); and (5) a concluding critical evaluation of whether the reconstructed position can accommodate the intuition that numbers sometimes matter, without merely asserting one side (3 points). For each move, include a brief note on the characteristic error that should cost marks—such as conflating Taurek's view with simple equality of concern, treating prioritarian weighting as the only alternative, or resolving the pilot case by appealing to aggregate welfare. The prompt must be self-contained, must not request bibliographies, reading lists, or citations, and should avoid reliance on any highly specialized theoretical apparatus beyond the standard vocabulary of moral philosophy.
