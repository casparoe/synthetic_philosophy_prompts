I have been working through the philosophical problem of consequentialist cluelessness—the thesis that moral agents are generally unable to foresee the distant ramifications of their conduct with sufficient reliability to render consequentialist comparison meaningful. In a recent discussion with colleagues, the conversation turned towards intergenerational ethics, and I found myself wondering whether the difficulty might actually be resolved by sharpening our tools for modelling future welfare rather than by abandoning outcome-based morality altogether. It struck me that much of our apparent ignorance stems from the non-identity problem: because present decisions, such as those governing carbon emissions or reproductive technologies, determine which specific individuals will come to exist in future generations, we cannot straightforwardly claim that an action harms or benefits particular people. Consequently, I began to think that if we applied a rigorously calibrated temporal discount rate specifically to the welfare of those contingent future populations—those whose very existence depends upon our current choices—we might overcome the epistemic paralysis that cluelessness induces. Would this not allow a consequentialist to compute the expected utility of climate policies or institutional reforms without requiring impossible foresight about every remote causal chain?

Yet I am unsure whether I have correctly diagnosed the relationship between these two objections. Is consequentialist cluelessness fundamentally a problem about the identity of future moral patients and our failure to apply appropriate discounting methods to them? Or is it a distinct challenge—one that concerns our ignorance of outcomes regardless of who the affected parties turn out to be—such that even a perfect model of intergenerational welfare and identity-contingent populations would still leave consequentialist reasoning without adequate practical guidance?
