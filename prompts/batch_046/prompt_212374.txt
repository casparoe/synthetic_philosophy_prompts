I was talking with my friend who works at a consulting firm that just rolled out this wellness app based on Franklin's old virtue chart — you know, the 13 things like temperence, silence, order, frugality, resolution, etc. Every day you tick off whether you kept them, and the app tracks your streaks. But here's the weird part: they added a 'moral bankruptcy' button. If you fail one virtue like ten times in a month, you can hit the button and that column resets to zero, like the debt is just gone. The firm's handbook also says character is built through continous incremantal improvement, not innate talent, and they have this anti-gaming rule that dock's there score if you only log successes. At the same time, there's a floor — your overall rating can never drop below 40 out of 100, no matter how bad the month gets.

What philosophical commitments about moral improvement and personal identity does this whole system presuppose? The bankruptcy feature seems to treat moral failure as dischargeable, which feels at odds with Franklin's view that moral perfection is cumulative and every slip leaves a stain. But maybe a narrative or control-based view of the self can make sense of erasing a column without saying the past self was a different person? And the floor rule — does that presuppose an inalienable moral baseline, or is it just a behavioral nudge? I'm also puzzled by the anti-gaming rule, which treats honest self-observation as essential to moral knowledge, yet the bankruptcy feature literally lets you erase the evidence of failure. Can these parts be consistent? Does the practice quietly commit to one view of moral responsibility and then undermine it with another? If it turned out that moral character isn't incremental at all but highly situational, which parts of the app design would be structurally broken and which are just decorative? And does the combination of Franklin-style bookkeeping plus modern self-forgiveness actually presuppose a hybrid theory that neither Franklin nor his critics would recognize?
