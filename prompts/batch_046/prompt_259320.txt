I am drafting an essay that reframes the replication crisis not as a simple contest between frequentist p-values and Bayesian posterior probabilities, but as a problem of what I call "framework-debt": the normative obligation for an empirical claim to survive translation across structurally different statistical architectures—e.g., frequentist mixed-effects versus Bayesian hierarchical with weakly informative priors—before it can be treated as established. I outline three illustrative cases: a social-psychological priming effect that reproduces in direct repetition but shifts markedly when covariance structures are altered; a clinical trial in which frequentist and Bayesian analyses both reject the null yet yield incompatible judgments about practical significance once prior clinical evidence is incorporated; and a hypothetical measurement study in which an effect is stable within one modeling family but vanishes when the construct is operationalized through a different psychometric model. I then ask whether this framework-debt criterion illegitimately collapses replication into robustness or generalization, and whether it neglects institutional incentives that drive questionable research practices regardless of statistical philosophy. I would welcome focused comments on four points: (a) the accuracy of my comparison in the clinical-trial case, especially regarding how informed priors reshape conclusions about effect size rather than mere significance; (b) the coherence of "framework-debt" as a normative standard—does it demand too much, or does standard replication already implicitly require it?; (c) the plausibility of the third case, given that measurement and statistical models are rarely independent in practice; and (d) whether the institutional-critique objection is decisive or can be accommodated within the framework-debt view.
