I'm currently taking an upper-level seminar on the philosophy of technology and longtermist ethics, and I'm trying to develop a term-paper thesis around the interaction between the simulation argument and what some call "far-future fanaticism"—the view that the overwhelming moral priority should lie with the astronomical number of potential lives in the distant future. Most treatments I've encountered treat the simulation hypothesis as either an epistemic curiosity or a blanket reason to discount longtermism altogether, but I think there's a more structurally interesting middle ground: the argument may not eliminate far-future concern but instead restructure which features of it are rationally defensible.

Specifically, I'm interested in identifying which open questions and lines of work in this intersection actually deserve serious development, and which are overrated distractions. Among the candidates I'm weighing are: (i) the causal-efficacy problem—whether actions taken within a putative simulation can influence outcomes in base reality, and whether this undermines the instrumental reasoning that longtermism relies on; (ii) the axiological-indexing problem—whether simulated lives ought to be weighted differently in moral calculus than non-simulated ones, and whether this creates a discontinuity in the argument for far-future investment; (iii) the ancestor-simulation constraint problem—whether being a simulated ancestor generates special obligations (for instance, avoiding behaviors that might trigger simulation termination) that compete with, rather than support, expansive future-building; and (iv) the nested-reasoning problem—how decision-theoretic rationality should be revised if one assigns non-negligible credence to being embedded in one or more levels of simulation.

Could you help me construct a defended prioritization? I want to know which two or three of these directions are genuinely worth pursuing over a semester's work—considering criteria like tractability, independence from one another, and potential to reshape practical reasoning about the far future—rather than receiving a survey of all four. In particular, I'm trying to understand whether solving the causal-efficacy problem collapses the others, or whether the indexing and constraint problems stand on their own. Please explain what makes your top choices structurally central and what would have to change in the argument to reorder them. I'm writing for an audience that takes longtermism seriously but is skeptical of hand-waving about simulations, so I need the agenda to be rigorous rather than speculative.
