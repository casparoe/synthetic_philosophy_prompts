I have come to hold a position that tries to dissolve both the lottery paradox and the preface paradox by rejecting the assumption that rational belief aggregates linearly over propositions governed by a single uniform threshold. On my view, which I call a contextual graded-hinge model, epistemic commitment is not a binary property applied proposition-by-proposition with a fixed credence cutoff, but a structurally layered attitude in which certain propositions function as local hinges—standing fast not because they have exceptionally high individual credence, but because they constitute the frame within which particular inquiries become possible at all. The lottery paradox seems forceful only if we assume that believing each of a very large number of independent claims about individual lottery tickets is rational at the same threshold used for ordinary beliefs, and then aggregate them into a contradiction with our belief that some ticket will win; the preface paradox seems forceful only if we assume an author can rationally believe every claim in a book while also rationally believing the global claim that at least one error remains, using the same flat standard of commitment in both cases. I contend that both paradoxes dissolve once we recognize that the appropriate threshold for belief shifts with the practical and logical structure of the frame: in large disjunctions or comprehensive works, the conditions for rational commitment are not the same as those governing isolated assertions. This is not merely a pragmatic relaxation of standards but a claim about the architecture of rationality—belief is always belief-within-a-frame, and the frames that govern a single ticket evaluation differ from those governing a statistical ensemble or a complete manuscript.

What would genuinely count against this position? I want more than the standard observation that people do seem to accept lottery propositions individually; I want to know what evidence, formal results, intuitive cases, or structural features of reasoning it would have to concede. In particular, does the view risk being insulated by qualification—if every counterexample can be absorbed by redrawing the frame, has the position become unfalsifiable in a damaging way? Which specific predictions does it make that a linear threshold model does not? Would a committed defender of this contextual graded-hinge model concede that there are cases where frame-boundaries collapse and the paradox returns with full force, or would they maintain that any apparent collapse is itself a misapplication of the frame concept? I am especially interested in whether results from formal epistemology—say, about the impossibility of non-trivial belief revision under certain consistency constraints—would undermine the layered architecture, and whether the view can accommodate the intuition that an author genuinely knows each chapter claim while genuinely knowing that some error lurks somewhere.
