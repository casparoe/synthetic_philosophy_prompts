Write a dialogue between two equally informed interlocutors—one defending Hobbes’s view that geometry supplies the proper demonstrative method for civil philosophy, the other arguing that Hobbes’s own mathematical practice undermines that methodological claim. The defender should maintain that geometric certainty is available to politics once first principles are correctly defined, regardless of whether Hobbes personally resolved particular problems like quadrature; the critic should counter that Hobbes’s well-known geometric errors and his disputes with contemporary mathematicians reveal a deeper mismatch between formal demonstration and the contingent, definitional nature of human affairs. Have them respond directly to one another on three points: whether political science requires demonstrative certainty or only probable reasoning; whether Hobbes’s materialist ontology genuinely supports the geometric analogy; and whether a failed practitioner can still legitimate a method. Neither speaker should be a strawman, and the exchange should turn on the relationship between formal method and subject matter rather than on biography alone.
