I am running a ninety-minute seminar for second-year philosophy undergraduates on the Ship of Theseus. They have encountered substance and accident, but not persistence conditions or four-dimensionalism. My goal is not for them to choose a correct answer, but to see that artifact identity depends on hidden conditions that must be made explicit before the puzzle can be properly framed.

Plan the explanatory arc. First, identify the single conceptual distinction that must be secured before the case becomes genuinely puzzling rather than trivial — whether numerical identity versus qualitative sameness, or design-intention versus material continuity — and justify why that ordering is necessary and what collapses if it is reversed. Second, specify the one carrying example: it must involve both gradual plank-by-plank replacement and a competing reconstruction assembled from the discarded original planks, and you should state precisely which feature of this paired scenario forces the conflict into view rather than letting students treat the two outcomes as independent puzzles. Third, map the misconceptions in the order they will likely arise — I expect an initial conflation of artifact identity with material-object identity, followed by an over-correction into pure functionalism — and indicate exactly where in the sequence each should be interrupted and with what counter-example or distinction. Fourth, provide one end-of-session diagnostic question that reveals whether students grasp the dependency structure among identity conditions rather than merely adopting a side. Finally, list the metaphysical machinery — for instance, temporal parts, essentialist theories of origin, or modal properties of artifacts — that should be explicitly excluded at this level, with a brief reason for each exclusion.
