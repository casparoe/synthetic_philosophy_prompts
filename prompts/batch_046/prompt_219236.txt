I'm writing up my philosophical profile on political deception for a seminar on Arendt, and I want to know whether these commitments hang together.

(1) Factual truths are uniquely vulnerable in politics because they depend on plural testimony and are contingent, whereas rational or moral truths are recoverable by solitary reason and are not vulnerable in the same way.

(2) Arendt's sharp distinction between the intentional liar, who knows the truth and hides it, and the self-deceived actor, who has lost access to reality, is overstated for modern bureaucratic politics: institutional incentives can produce widespread false belief without any central deceiver.

(3) The proper remedy is therefore not legal penalties for false speech but protected spaces of appearance — institutional settings where testimony can be contested without electoral or bureaucratic pressure.

(4) Arendt is wrong that totalitarian regimes destroy reality altogether; they replace one coherent narrative reality with another. Defending factual truth therefore means protecting plural testimony, not enforcing a single official narrative.

(5) Democratic citizenship requires representative thinking — judging issues from the standpoints of others — and this is incompatible with the epistemic closure required to sustain a politically motivated false narrative.

Are these jointly tenable? Present the audit as a table with exactly these columns: Tension Pair, Severity (High / Medium / Low), The Conflict, Cheapest Revision, Cost of Revision.
