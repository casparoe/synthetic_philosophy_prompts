I'm constructing a classification instrument for a graduate philosophy-of-language seminar on norms of assertion. The aim is to sort interlocutors by their verdicts on concrete stress cases rather than by the labels they prefer.

Please design a decision-tree taxonomy that starts from the root commitment: an assertion is defective exactly when some specific epistemic or institutional relation fails between speaker and content. From this root, branch through four diagnostic cases that separate knowledge norms, pure truth norms, belief norms, justified-belief norms, contextualist adjustments, and institutional-partition norms: (1) the lucky-true-belief case, in which excellent but misleading evidence yields a true assertion that the speaker does not know; (2) the justified-false-belief case, in which excellent evidence yields a false assertion because of an unknown defeater; (3) the expert-relay case, in which a non-expert asserts p solely on authoritative expert testimony that she understands but cannot independently verify; and (4) the partitioned-authority case, in which a designated institutional spokesperson asserts p supported collectively by the institution's knowledge, despite personally lacking belief in p.

At each node, phrase the splitting question so that a respondent can answer yes, no, or it-depends without already knowing which named position the answer selects. Every path should terminate in a leaf carrying either a named position—such as the knowledge norm, the pure truth norm, the sincere-belief norm, the justified-belief norm, contextualist knowledge norms, or an institutional-partition norm—or the label unnamed/unoccupied, together with a one-sentence formulation of the norm and its most awkward commitment as revealed by the case path that reaches it. Identify any logical combinations that remain empty, diagnosing whether the gap stems from incoherence, unattractiveness, or mere neglect. Flag any pairs of leaves that these four cases cannot genuinely separate. Keep the tree to at most twelve leaves, address it to graduate-level readers, and do not include bibliographic references, citation requests, or reading lists.
