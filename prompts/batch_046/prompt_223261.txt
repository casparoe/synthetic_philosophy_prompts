As a high school student working through moral philosophy, I have come to defend a qualified version of Scanlon's contractualism about the aggregation problem. I think the standard objection—that contractualism implausibly forbids saving five strangers over one—rests on conflating redundant moral reasons with structurally independent ones. On my view, when multiple people each hold an equally strong claim to rescue, and one action can satisfy all of them, the reason to act is not five redundant copies of the same reason but one compound reason whose weight reflects the number of claimants without requiring interpersonal aggregation in the objectionable sense. By contrast, when claims compete—when saving five requires harming one—the reasons remain genuinely unaggregated, because they point in opposite directions and share no common structural basis. This, I take it, preserves Scanlon's core commitment that wrongness is fixed by what principles no one could reasonably reject, while accommodating the intuition that numbers matter in non-competitive rescue cases. What would count against this qualified view? I want the arguments, formal distinctions, and intuitive cases that should genuinely move me: results showing that any line between redundant and independent reasons collapses under analysis; counterexamples in which the number of claimants seems morally relevant even inside competitive scenarios; or demonstrations that my "compound reason" is merely aggregation disguised and therefore violates the contractualist requirement to treat each person's complaint separately. I also want to know which of these defeaters defenders of pure non-aggregation would themselves concede as damaging, and which they would dismiss as misunderstanding the view. Please rank them from fatal—showing that the position collapses into either pure aggregation or pure non-aggregation—to merely awkward adjustments, and include an honest assessment of whether I have qualified the view into unfalsifiability, and if so, whether that is a serious problem for a contractualist account.
