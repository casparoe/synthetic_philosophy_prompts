Please argue that technological artifacts possess politics not primarily through their design intentions or immediate social effects, but through what might be called maintenance asymmetry: the unequal distribution of capacities to repair, modify, or withdraw from an artifact's operational logic across its lifecycle. Begin by distinguishing this claim from Langdon Winner's familiar argument that artifacts embody specific forms of authority and social ordering. Then show how maintenance asymmetry introduces a dynamic, temporal dimension that static, design-focused accounts miss—an artifact may be politically neutral at deployment yet become deeply political as its repair infrastructures, spare-part monopolies, and decay pathways consolidate power over time. Illustrate the point with a concrete example, such as a proprietary agricultural drone or a closed-source medical implant, and explain precisely how the politics resides in the long tail of upkeep—failure modes, dependency on specialised supply chains, and the differential ability of users to intervene—rather than in the initial engineering choices alone. Finally, address the objection that this merely redescribes economic inequality rather than revealing genuinely artifactual politics, and defend why the material specificity of the artifact makes the claim irreducible to market analysis. If the premise that artifacts can be separated from their maintenance regimes is confused, or if the argument collapses into a general claim about capitalism rather than technology, say so plainly rather than accommodating it politely. Write in British English, keep the reasoning self-contained, and do not request bibliographies, citations, or reading lists.
