A colleague offered me the following argument about money pumps and diachronic rationality, and I think it overreaches, but I cannot name the exact slip.

(1) A money pump requires only cyclic preferences—A ≻ B ≻ C ≻ A—revealed at successive choice nodes, each switch costing a small fee.
(2) Diachronic rationality demands that preference change occur only through information gain or altered circumstances.
(3) In the canonical pump, the agent’s beliefs and environment never shift; her ranking is identical at every node. She therefore violates synchronic transitivity, not diachronic coherence.
(4) Formalize diachronic rationality as a retroactive no-regret condition: at time t, the agent must rank her actual path of choices above every counterfactual path available at t−1, evaluated with her present values.
(5) For the pumped agent, applying (4) at the C→A node shows her path A→B→C→... has left her poorer than remaining at A, so she should have pre-committed at t=0 to refuse all trades.
(6) Yet the same evaluation applies to rational learning: an agent who updates from preferring meat to vegetarianism upon new ethical evidence can apply (4) and conclude she should have pre-committed to her initial diet.
(7) Hence, if (4) defines diachronic rationality, any preference revision—whether arbitrary or well-founded—becomes irrational, collapsing the distinction between rational updating and cyclic exploitation.

My colleague insists this proves money pumps are really diachronic failures masked as synchronic intransitivity. Which step should I reject—particularly the move between (3) and (5)—and what does rejecting it cost for our understanding of pre-commitment and preference stability?
