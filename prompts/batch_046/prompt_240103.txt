Please argue that visitors have a genuine moral obligation to observe local conventions of tipping, queuing, and jaywalking—not merely as a matter of politeness or negative-liberty non-interference, but specifically on republican-liberty grounds: that ignoring these conventions subjects residents to arbitrary domination by disrupting shared normative expectations without providing them any institutional recourse. Proceed by mapping the core elements of republican non-domination—the presence of arbitrary power, the vulnerability of the dominated party, and the absence of accountability mechanisms—onto informal social coordination, and then address the standard objection that republican liberty applies only to state or institutional power rather than to peer-to-peer convention. You should also examine whether this framing survives the symmetry problem: that visitors themselves are often vulnerable to local sanction, which might reverse the domination relation and thereby weaken or cancel the obligation. In your response, separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who know the topic, and do not present a contested view as the consensus.
