Write a proposal for a one-semester philosophy-of-science research project, approximately 6,000 words with a final presentation, on a structural problem in Jeffrey conditionalization. The asker has a half-formed idea: Jeffrey updating—revising beliefs when evidence specifies not a single observed event but a new probability distribution over possibilities—is standardly treated as a direct generalization of Bayes’ rule, yet it appears to presuppose that the agent’s possibility space is already partitioned in a way that remains fixed during the update. The precise question the proposal should frame is whether Jeffrey’s rule can be applied coherently when the uncertain evidence concerns the boundaries of the possibility space itself—specifically, when measurement reports that a previously excluded outcome now has non-zero probability, or that the calibration of the instrument changes the relevant outcome structure—without circularly assuming the very partition being revised. The proposal should explain why existing answers leave this open: standard treatments either restrict Jeffrey conditioning to updates within a stable partition, or they appeal to hierarchical Bayesian models that abandon Jeffrey’s distinctive feature of direct experience-probability updating, but neither option is philosophically well defended. The method should specify a step-by-step approach over the semester—formalizing discrete finite-space models of three cases (noisy parameter estimation, discovery of a previously zero-probability theoretical possibility, and instrument-calibration uncertainty), testing whether Jeffrey’s rule extends without circularity, and comparing any failure to analogous issues in standard conditioning—while honestly assessing what would count as success (a generalized, non-circular Jeffrey rule for structural evidence, or a precise argument that such generalization is impossible and Jeffrey conditioning is restricted to intra-partitional updates), what would count as failure (the problem dissolves into standard hierarchical Bayes with no conceptual payoff), and what makes the project too large for one semester (continuous measure-theoretic extensions, full historical exegesis of Jeffrey’s writings, or empirical case studies). The proposal should also include a brief argument for why the result matters for philosophy of science rather than statistics alone. Structure the response in these named sections, in this order with approximate lengths: The precise question (about 350 words), Why existing treatments leave it open (about 450 words), The approach and month-by-month plan (about 550 words), What a result looks like and criteria for failure (about 400 words), Realistic scope and what could make it too big (about 300 words), and Significance for philosophy of science (about 300 words). Do not include a bibliography, literature review by citation, or reading list; the asker will supply references. The tone should be that of a formal fellowship or term-paper prospectus, polished and self-contained enough that a reader can evaluate the project without external lookup.
