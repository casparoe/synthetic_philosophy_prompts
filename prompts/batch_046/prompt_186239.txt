As a retired experimental physicist who has only recently begun reading seriously in philosophy of action and decision theory, I am struck by how readily the literature treats weakness of will—akrasia—as nothing more than a miscalibration in an intertemporal discount function. I would like you to write a sustained, deadpan parody of an FDA-style pharmaceutical package insert (approximately 750–900 words) for a fictional prescription drug tentatively named Acrasiol, indicated for the treatment of chronic present-biased preference reversal. The document should adopt the exact register of clinical regulatory prose—passive voice, numbered sections, precise adverse-event taxonomy—and include the standard headings: Indications and Usage; Mechanism of Action (describing the normalization of the hyperbolic k-parameter and the suppression of steep present-bias divergence from exponential benchmarks); Clinical Pharmacology (referencing intertemporal choice tasks, not specific named studies); Warnings and Precautions (including a subsection on existential discontinuity upon normalization of the discount parameter, and on the risk of normative agency being misclassified as a metabolic error); and Adverse Reactions. The satire should exaggerate real features of the hyperbolic discounting model—its quantitative treatment of rational failure, its mechanistic analogy between temporal proximity and physiological drive, its implicit denial that first-person evaluation can override proximity—rather than invent straw-man positions, so that a reader familiar with behavioral economics recognizes the parody as fair. At the same time, the clinical form should quietly betray philosophical commitments—elimination of agential structure, reduction of normative judgment to parameter estimation, and the treatment of future selves as probabilistically discounted assets—without requiring the reader to name them. The piece must remain readable and amusing to someone without technical training: the jargon should do the ironic work. Maintain an entirely straight face; there should be no winking asides, no explicit mention that this is satire, and no invented citations, bibliographies, or references to specific living scholars. The target is the discursive practice of reducing moral psychology to curve-fitting, not any individual researcher.
