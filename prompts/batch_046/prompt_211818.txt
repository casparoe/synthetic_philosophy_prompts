When an interlocutor challenges one member—perhaps by observing that scientific rationalism has deep roots outside Europe, or that gender norms vary sharply across Western societies—the speaker does not defend that member on its merits. Instead, they contract the bundle, treating the challenged item as peripheral or accidental, and insist that “Western values” properly refers only to the remaining core, perhaps constitutionalism and individual rights. The debate then proceeds with this reduced core. Yet in a later context—perhaps defending deregulation or criticizing a different culture—the very same speaker will invoke “Western values” to include the previously dropped item, as though it had never been excluded, and will treat any challenge to it as an attack on the essential core itself.

This does not seem to be ordinary motte-and-bailey, because there is no single defensible “motte” and indefensible “bailey” shared across contexts; the boundary shifts depend on which specific element is under fire at the moment. It also differs from mere ambiguity or family-resemblance vagueness, since the speaker appears genuinely committed to there being a real, stable category, and from strategic essentialism in its usual form, because the elasticity is reactive rather than pre-planned.
