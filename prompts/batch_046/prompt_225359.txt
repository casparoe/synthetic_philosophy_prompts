I am drafting a dissertation chapter on whether Cartesian dualism can absorb modern comparative cognition and artificial intelligence without collapsing its human-animal boundary. For this purpose, imagine René Descartes—holding precisely the commitments he articulated in the Discourse, the Meditations, and the Passions of the Soul—is confronted with two bodies of evidence that emerged after 1650.

First, contemporary cognitive ethology has documented mirror self-recognition in European magpies, prospective tool-use planning in New Caledonian crows, and complex nociceptive responses in cephalopods. These findings seem to erode the sharp mechanistic divide he drew between human reason and the bête-machine.

Second, large language models now generate syntactically coherent, context-sensitive discourse without biological bodies, nervous systems, or souls, which forces the question of whether articulate language can exist in the absence of a res cogitans.

Given Descartes' actual criteria—his mechanistic physiology, his claim that language is the surest external sign of an internal rational soul, and his substance dualism—what would he conclude? Would he treat advanced animal cognition as a superficial resemblance to reason, treat artificial language as a more dangerous counterfeit precisely because it mimics the specific mark of humanity, or revise his anthropology?

Please do not merely list options. Take a committed, argued position: decide which of the two posthumous challenges—modern animal cognition or artificial language—poses the deeper threat to his system, defend that judgment by appealing to the internal logic of his dualism, and specify whether he would extend, restrict, or abandon the bête-machine thesis in response.
