I hold a stringent form of internalism about practical reasons, but with a twist that moves it away from the standard dialectic: I maintain that what are called external reasons are not merely motivationally inert for agents who lack the corresponding desires, but are strictly mischaracterized whenever they are treated as applying directly to an agent's first-order practical reasoning. Instead, any apparently external reason must be reconstructed either as an internal reason belonging to some counterfactual version of the agent whose motivational set has been revised in a specific direction, or as a second-order internal reason governing how one ought to revise one's desires, in which case it bears on desire-formation rather than on action itself. On this view, the claim that morality or prudence could supply reasons that bind an agent irrespective of what they care about rests on a grammatical confusion about how normative language operates in practical contexts, analogous to treating a rule about chess moves as if it could compel someone who is not playing chess. I want to know what would genuinely count against this position, not just restatements of the familiar intuition that one ought to rescue a drowning stranger regardless of personal indifference, but the deeper structural defeaters. Which specific cases, formal arguments, or features of practical reasoning would have to obtain for my reconstruction strategy to collapse? For example, could there be arguments showing that second-order internal reasons cannot carry the normative load required without secretly presupposing first-order external claims? Would genuinely incommensurable practical perspectives—where agents possess not merely different but structurally incompatible motivational sets—undermine the counterfactual reconstruction by removing any stable baseline for comparison? And what about collective or institutional contexts, such as a legislature, professional guild, or corporation that appears bound by reasons none of its individual members currently endorse? Would such plural-agent cases demonstrate that reasons can apply to groups in ways that do not reduce to the internal reasons of any constituent member, thereby breaking the reconstruction model? Please rank these potential defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, distinguishing those that a defender of this reconstructed internalism would be compelled to concede as damaging from those they would likely dismiss as pre-theoretical intuition or category mistakes of their own. I also want an honest assessment of whether this view has become insulated from refutation by its capacity to reconstruct every counterexample, and if so, whether that insulation counts as a theoretical virtue or as a vice that empties the view of predictive content. Present your response in continuous prose only, without headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, in the manner of a formal letter or written opinion.
