I hold an intermediate position on modal persistence: not Lewisian counterpart theory, which I take to overgenerate duplicates and to fail to capture genuine modal concern about an object, and not bare transworld identity, which I take to collapse into an unexplained primitive. Instead, I defend what I call modal-continuation theory: an object in a possible world counts as numerically the same individual only if it preserves the actual causal-nomic functional core that structures the object in the actual world, where the core is understood not as a set of essential properties but as a dynamical pattern of causal-nomic roles that can be realized differently across worlds. This allows for partial overlap without full similarity and avoids treating identity as a bare modal primitive.

What would have to be true for this view to be wrong? I want an honest map of its exposure, not a list of objections I have already heard. Which formal results—say, in modal logic, the metaphysics of persistence, or the logic of identity across worlds—would undermine the claim that such functional cores can be genuinely world-spanning? Which specific thought experiments about fission, fusion, or gradual replacement (whether in personal identity, biological organisms, or artifacts) would be decisive defeaters rather than merely awkward adjustments? What findings or arguments from philosophy of mind about psychological continuity, or from physics about the identity conditions of fundamental particles, stand or fall with this picture? Rank the potential defeaters from fatal to merely awkward. Which of these would a committed modal-continuation theorist have to concede as genuinely damaging, and which could they absorb by refining the boundaries or realization conditions of the core? Is there a live risk that the view becomes insulated into unfalsifiability—such that any apparent counterexample is handled by redrawing the core—and if so, is that insulation a theoretical virtue or a fatal defect? I am especially interested in whether there is a substantive argument that causal-nomic roles are themselves world-relative in a way that renders the core unstable across worlds, and whether that possibility is genuinely open or merely logically conceivable.
