I'm helping design a curriculum for a small liberal-arts college and we're using Nietzsche's essay on history—monumental, antiquarian, critical—not just as assigned reading but as the actual architecture for a required "history of thought" course. The faculty is split: some think monumental history (great thinkers as models) should dominate because students need inspiration; others say critical history (tearing down idols) should dominate or the course just becomes nostalgia; a smaller group wants antiquarian (preserving obscure texts for continuity) but worries it's too conservative. We're also toyign with adding a fourth mode, "reconstructive history," where students rebuild dead arguments from fragments and defend them against modern objections—this isn't in Nietzsche, and people disagree whether it answers his worry that history can become a burden on life or just adds more labor.

Given that we only have about three years and one small research group to figure out how this should actually work, which open conceptual questions or lines of work should we prioritize? I'm especially interested in these three: (1) whether Nietzsche's three modes actually map onto distinct pedagogical outcomes, or if they collapse into each other in practice; (2) whether a "reconstructive" fourth mode genuinely solves the problem of history overwhelming life, or makes it worse; (3) how to design assessment for critical history without it devolving into pure cynicism. But I want you to rank them—plus any other line you think I'm missing—and defend the ordering. What makes one worth the years versus the others? And what would change your mind if we got new evidence in the next couple years?
