I'm trying to think through what we actually commit ourselves to when people throw around the phrase democratic backsliding in the way NGOs and newspapers do. You know the practice: Freedom House or V-Dem publish their yearly scores, a country like Hungary or Poland drops a few points, and suddenly everyone's saying democracy is backsliding — whilst the US can lose ground on voting access or judicial independance and the same label gets applied selectively, sometimes as a political stick rather than an analysis. So here's the thing: what philosophical commitments does that institutional practice presuppose? Does it treat democracy as a single measurable quantity that can be audited from the outside, like a credit rating? Does it assume there's one correct liberal-democratic endpoint that every country ought to be converging on, so any deviation is decay rather than difference? And if the concept is meant to be descriptive, why does it seem to track partisan discomfort so neatly — is it smuggling in a normative theory about who should hold power, and is that theory consistent with the idea that democracy is just a procedure? I'd like you to reconstruct the implicit theory of democracy and political legitimacy that this scoring-and-labelling practice only makes sense under, say whether that theory can handle cases like Turkey or the US without becoming circular, and tell me what would have to change in the monitoring practice if we replaced it with a view that sees democracy as essentially contested and locally defined. Don't just list theorists — I want the commitments of the practice itself, and whether they're even consistent with each other. I also keep wondering if the whole framework secretly relies on a teleological story about political development that nobody actually defends anymore, but that might be overreaching.
