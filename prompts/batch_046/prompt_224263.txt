I'm delivering the opening remarks at the launch ceremony for a new municipal Intergenerational Design Board. This institution reviews long-term infrastructure and climate-adaptation projects not merely for risk to future residents, but for whether the projects themselves will alter the demographic composition of the city across generations — for instance, by changing settlement patterns, housing costs, or migration flows in ways that determine which people are eventually born. The audience is sharply divided: on one side are philosophers of population ethics who know the nonidentity problem and its standard formulations in detail; on the other are city planners, architects, climate officers, and local officials who have no background in moral philosophy but must understand why the Board's charter explicitly forbids justifying a project by saying it harms specific future citizens.

I need a ten-minute address that treats the philosophical problem as structural rather than decorative. The speech must explain why harm-based justifications fail in this institutional context without collapsing into the claim that future people do not matter, and it must give the non-philosophers a concrete, intuitive grip on the distinction — perhaps by walking through a specific design choice, such as a flood-wall routing decision that protects one neighborhood but redirects development in a way that changes who lives there and therefore who comes into existence. It should feel like an occasion-setting address, not a lecture, speaking to both halves of the room with equal respect and without patronizing either. Please write it, making sure that the constraint actually shapes what the Board can and cannot claim in its public mission statements and funding appeals.
