Here's an argument my roomate cooked up about Church-Turing and now I'm stuck in it. (1) The Church-Turing thesis says any function computable via an effective procedure is Turing-computable. (2) If it's a math theorem, it's false, since oracle machines compute non-computable functions with perfectly effective rules - you just feed the oracle an input and read the output. (3) So it must be either empirical (about what physics lets us build) or definitional (just fixing what 'effective proceedure' means). (4) If empirical, then building one physical hypercomputer that solves the halting problem should refute it. (5) But if we actually built it, then consulting that device just becomes part of what counts as an effective procedure, so the class expands and the thesis survives by redefinition. (6) So CTT is unfalsifiable in practice. (7) That leaves the definitional reading: 'effective' just means 'Turing-computable.' (8) But then it's a tautology, and can't support claims like 'human minds are computable' or 'quantum computing stays in the Turing class.' (9) So CTT is either empty or immune to counterexample - either way it has no real epistemic status. Where does this slip? I'd rather keep the idea that it's not just a tautology, but I want to know what I pay if I reject (5) or (8).
