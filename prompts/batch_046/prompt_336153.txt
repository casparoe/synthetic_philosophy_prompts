I sit on my university’s International Research Collaboration Review Board, and we must vote next week on whether to renew a five-year strategic partnership with a consortium of technical universities in a state that has sustained two decades of rapid economic growth, urban order, and technological advancement, yet maintains single-party rule, no independent judiciary, and documented suppression of dissent within its social-science and humanities faculties. Several colleagues argue that the regime’s delivery of prosperity and stability amounts to a genuine form of performance legitimacy, which should neutralize ethical objections—particularly because our agreement is restricted to materials science and engineering, fields they regard as politically neutral. Others reply that this reasoning mistakes instrumental success for normative justification, and that institutional partnership still channels prestige, funding, and implicit endorsement to a system in which universities function partly as instruments of state control rather than as autonomous sites of inquiry.

Please draft the board’s recommendation memo for circulation before Thursday’s meeting. Take a concrete position—renew with strict conditions, suspend pending structural safeguards, or terminate—rather than remaining agnostic. The memo must engage seriously with the performance-legitimacy claim: not dismiss it outright, but explain whether prosperity and public order can ever justify deepening collaboration with authoritarian institutions, and whether confining the partnership to technical domains actually insulates us from complicity. I want you to work with a distinction I have been developing between what I call “output legitimacy” (the regime delivers goods and growth) and “throughput legitimacy” (the processes by which it delivers them involve co-optation, suppression, or structural exclusion of independent judgment). Assess whether this distinction changes our evaluation: does the absence of throughput legitimacy undermine output legitimacy for purposes of institutional cooperation, or are they separable? The document should be formal and actionable—clear bottom line, time-bound conditions if you recommend renewal, and an honest account of the strongest argument on the losing side—so that board members who disagree can see their view represented fairly even as the memo argues past it.
