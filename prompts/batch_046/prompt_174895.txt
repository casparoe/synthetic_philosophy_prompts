In discussions of AI alignment and governance, the debate over the alignment tax and the competitiveness constraint has stayed frozen for years: one camp treats safety interventions as a persistent capability penalty that competitive markets will selectively punish, while the other treats them as temporary engineering overhead that regulation or consumer preference can eventually absorb, yet neither updates much despite shared observations on RLHF trade-offs and regulatory histories in other industries. My working hypothesis is that the stall is not mainly empirical but counterfactual—the sides measure the “tax” against different baselines, one comparing an aligned system to its unaligned architectural twin, the other comparing it to the next-best substitute technology that would occupy the niche if aligned deployment were delayed—so they are answering different questions under one label. Is that right, or is there a hidden substantive disagreement about whether alignment costs are modular (additive) versus architectural (multiplicative), or about what should count as evidence in modeling multi-actor competitive dynamics? I want a diagnosis anchored in what each side actually says: is this verbal, about standards of evidence, about which empirical analogies are admissible, or about bedrock intuitions regarding technological determinism? And given your diagnosis, what would actually move it—a formal distinction between static capability reduction and convergence-speed delay, a historical natural experiment from another safety-critical domain, or an argument that the competitiveness framing itself should be dissolved?
