Contemporary AI alignment practice evaluates a system primarily through average-case benchmark scores—mean harm reduction, typical refusal rates—while treating worst-case adversarial failures as a separate, often optional red-teaming exercise rather than integrating provable worst-case bounds into the core certification criterion. What philosophical commitments about risk, epistemic justification, and moral responsibility does that institutional design presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit view: does it treat catastrophic failure modes as epistemically intractable, as acceptable externalities of statistical learning, or as risks that can be compartmentally assigned to a distinct evaluation team? Say whether the practice strictly requires any one of these commitments or remains compatible with rival theories, and describe what an evaluation protocol that made deployment contingent on worst-case guarantees—rather than on high average performance—would have to change, not only technically but in how it distributes epistemic and moral accountability among developers, evaluators, and deployers.
