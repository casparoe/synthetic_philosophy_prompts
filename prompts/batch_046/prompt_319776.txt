Design a 90-minute workshop activity for 14 upper-level philosophy of science students on whether the sciences carve nature at its joints. The group should split into four "research institutes" of 3–4 students each, assigned one criterion: genetic lineage, ecological function, morphological structure, or chemical reactivity. Give each institute an identical deck of 18 specimen cards describing hypothetical and terrestrial entities; each card lists observable traits across all four criteria but reveals only one hidden "deep-structure" property (genetic marker, metabolic pathway, crystal lattice, or niche dependency) when a sealed envelope is opened in Phase 3.

Phase 1 (20 minutes): Each institute must partition the 18 specimens into exactly six natural kinds using only its assigned criterion and defend its taxonomy with a one-sentence "joint-carving principle." They record predictions about pairwise interactions (e.g., "specimens in Kind A will react with Kind B").

Phase 2 (25 minutes): The institutes are told an international treaty requires a single unified taxonomy of six kinds with unambiguous membership for resource allocation. They must negotiate a merger. The rules state that a kind is treaty-valid only if all four institutes agree on every member; predictably they deadlock, because the criteria cross-classify the same entities differently. To make the failure visceral, assign each institute a small point budget representing "research prestige"—they lose points for every kind they are forced to abandon, but they also lose collective points if the treaty fails. Let them feel the pressure to suppress data or overrule others.

Phase 3 (15 minutes): The facilitator reveals the hidden envelopes. Each specimen carries two independent essential properties that do not co-vary; no single criterion tracks all and only the "true" divisions, and some entities belong to two incompatible kinds simultaneously depending on which property is tracked. The institutes now see that their deadlock was not a failure of information but a feature of the domain.

Phase 4 (15 minutes): Each student privately votes among three options—monism (force one taxonomy and discard the rest), pluralism (accept overlapping, equally valid taxonomies), or eliminativism (deny that there are natural kinds at all)—and must state the specific cost of their choice in terms of predictive power, explanatory unity, or institutional authority.

Debrief (15 minutes): Map Phase 1 to essentialist confidence, Phase 2 to the incommensurability problem, Phase 3 to promiscuous realism or pluralistic joint-carving, and Phase 4 to the metaphysical price of each response. Be explicit about what the game cannot reproduce: it generates an epistemic crisis about access to joints, not a proof that there are no joints; the facilitator must supply the metaphysical argument that ontology does not depend on our classificatory success. Also note the predictable failure mode: in Phase 2, one dominant personality will usually try to impose morphological taxonomy as "common sense"; have a prepared intervention that requires each institute to present one cross-cutting counterexample before any vote, and warn that students often confuse "we can't agree" with "there is no fact of the matter," which the debrief must disentangle carefully. Include rules, materials list, payoff scheme (prestige points, not money), and an honest admission of which feature—say, the metaphysical independence of kinds from all human classification—the activity can only gesture at rather than stage. Do not ask for bibliographies, citations, or reading lists; the prompt should be answerable entirely from conceptual reasoning. Avoid attributing specific named theories to particular philosophers unless verified by source; describe the positions structurally rather than by citation.
