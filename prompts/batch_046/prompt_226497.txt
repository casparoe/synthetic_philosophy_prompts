I have been discussing with a friend who studies the metaphysics of continuous processes whether the paradox of Thomson's lamp ought to be interpreted as a failure of local causal closure rather than as a straightforward logical puzzle about infinite sequences. She observes that, since the toggling events occur at times 1/2, 1/4, 1/8, ... minutes and accumulate without a last member, there is no immediately preceding switch-flip that determines the lamp's condition at the exact limit point t = 2 minutes. From this she infers that a genuine causal gap opens up: the usual local mechanism linking one state to the next is absent, so some external principle—perhaps a continuity constraint or a boundary condition—must be invoked to fix whether the lamp is on or off.

This strikes me as conflating two different issues, though I am struggling to articulate the error precisely. It seems she treats the absence of a final toggle as if it were a missing link in a physical chain that nature must repair, rather than recognising that the toggle rules simply do not apply at the limit point. Yet I wonder whether her concern can be reformulated more charitably: is there a meaningful distinction between saying the lamp's state at t = 2 is mathematically unspecified by the protocol, and saying it is metaphysically indeterminate? Could you clarify, writing in British English, exactly where this line of reasoning mistakes the logical structure of a supertask for a demand on physical determinism? I should like an answer that keeps the discussion conceptual rather than empirical—there is no need to invoke the physics of light bulbs—and that distinguishes underdetermination arising from the rules of the sequence from any deeper ontological indeterminacy.
