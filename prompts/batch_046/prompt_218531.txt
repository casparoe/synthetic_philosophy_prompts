I have been thinking about the ethics of historical fiction and biographical drama, particularly about whether filmmakers and playwrights owe obligations to deceased subjects that differ in kind from obligations to the living. In a recent discussion with colleagues, I proposed a distinction that I have not seen fully articulated in standard debates: rather than asking whether we owe the dead “truth” in some undifferentiated sense, we should separate causal-ethical fidelity—the preservation of the actual sequence, weight, and interdependence of a figure’s morally consequential public choices—from phenomenal fidelity—the accurate representation of their private emotional, sensory, or cognitive experience. One interlocutor objected that this separation collapses, since any invented interior monologue inevitably reshapes an audience’s assessment of an agent’s reasons and thus contaminates the causal-ethical structure; I want to defend the separation and argue that the two tiers can be held apart.

Please argue that dramatists owe the dead causal-ethical fidelity but not phenomenal fidelity, and that imaginative reconstruction of private mental states is permissible—indeed, sometimes ethically necessary—provided it is explicitly framed as speculative and does not retroactively re-weight the figure’s documented moral choices. Address the contamination objection directly: explain why a viewer’s knowledge that an interior scene is invented can insulate the causal-ethical assessment from distortion, and identify the specific cognitive or framing mechanism that performs this insulation. You should also show why this two-tier framework avoids both the excessive austerity of strict documentary realism and the moral indifference of pure fictionalization, and you may illustrate your point with a hypothetical biographical scenario—no bibliographic verification required—in which a well-known historical figure’s documented decision remains intact while a speculative reconstruction of private grief or doubt is embedded within the narrative framing.
