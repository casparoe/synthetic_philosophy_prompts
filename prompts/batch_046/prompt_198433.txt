I have recently assumed responsibility for directing a family philanthropic foundation that disburses substantial capital, and in designing our allocation framework I have encountered what seems to me a structural tension within effective-altruist strategy that standard presentations rarely confront. The "earning to give" model treats career selection and charitable distribution as separable optimization problems: one maximizes income in a domain largely insulated from impact evaluation, then applies rigorous, bias-corrected metrics to grantmaking. Meanwhile, the institutional critique of measurability bias argues that funding mechanisms systematically overvalue quantifiable near-term outcomes at the expense of structurally important but opaque long-term interventions. The tension, as I see it, is that if measurability bias is a pervasive distortion in institutional reasoning about value, it should also corrupt the career-selection calculus that directs donors toward high-earning, quantifiably remunerative paths and away from direct, less-measurable advocacy or movement-building. Write an essay that examines whether the separation of "earning" and "giving" can survive this consistency challenge. Weigh the defense that career choice operates under market constraints distinct from philanthropic evaluation, against the counter that both are exercises in expected-value reasoning and therefore cannot be selectively quarantined from cognitive bias. Reach and justify a definite conclusion—whether earning-to-give remains coherent only by rejecting measurability-correction in career selection, or whether the institutional critique ultimately collapses the separation thesis and mandates unified, lower-earning direct engagement. Please do not include reading lists, bibliographies, or requests for citations; I am interested in the internal logic of the positions rather than in scholarly attribution.
