As a doctoral student in philosophy of physics, I recently read a popular-science summary that attempts to collapse the various arrows of time into a single statistical-mechanical account. The author argues that the thermodynamic arrow is fully explained by applying Boltzmann’s H-theorem to the early universe’s low-entropy boundary condition—the Past Hypothesis—so that macroscopic systems evolve toward states of higher entropy. From this premise alone, the summary claims, one can derive the psychological arrow: memory traces are macroscopic records that require local entropy decreases to form, and since global entropy increases, such local decreases can occur only in the downstream direction of the gradient. Hence remembering the past but not the future is, on this view, just an application of the second law to cognitive systems. The summary then asserts that Reichenbach’s common-cause principle, often invoked to explain why causes precede effects and why records point backward, is redundant: it adds no explanatory power beyond describing the same entropy-gradient structure. Finally, the article concludes that quantum decoherence approaches—specifically the decoherent histories framework—are unnecessary for explaining temporal direction, since classical statistical mechanics combined with the Past Hypothesis already fixes a unique direction of correlation-formation without any quantum-mechanical machinery.

This strikes me as suspiciously reductive, but I want to be precise about where, if anywhere, it goes wrong. Is the derivation of the psychological arrow from the Past Hypothesis a fair representation of the current literature, or does it conflate statistical tendency with the specific conditions required for record-formation? Does Reichenbach’s principle genuinely reduce to entropy-gradient reasoning, or does it make an independent structural claim about causal forks that statistical mechanics alone does not secure? And is the dismissal of decoherent histories accurate—do those frameworks aim merely to reproduce the thermodynamic arrow, or do they address a distinct problem about the emergence of definite histories that the Past Hypothesis leaves untouched? Please audit the summary by separating what is accurate popularization, what is a subtle conflation of distinct arrows or explanatory frameworks, and what is a straightforward mischaracterization of the positions it dismisses. I am particularly interested in whether the conclusion—that all non-thermodynamic explanations of temporal asymmetry are redundant—actually follows from the accurate parts, or whether it depends on the misrepresentations you identify.
