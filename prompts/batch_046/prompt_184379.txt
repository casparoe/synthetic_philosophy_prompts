I recently observed a debate between two researchers about whether attributing beliefs to an artificial system is ever warranted under the intentional stance. They were examining a warehouse-navigation agent trained solely via contrastive vision-language pretraining and then fine-tuned with sparse reward signals. In their tests, the agent consistently rerouted around a red-marked zone when, and only when, its prompt included the token "caution"; with synonyms like "careful" or with no linguistic cue, it traversed the zone without hesitation. No symbolic representation of danger or zone-avoidance had been engineered into the system, and no corresponding stable sub-network had been isolated in its weights.

(1) The first researcher argued that the routing pattern is best explained by attributing a context-dependent belief—roughly, that this zone is hazardous under cautionary framing—because this intentional-level description predicts behavior more compactly and accurately than any mechanistic account of attention weights and policy gradients. She noted that Dennett's intentional stance requires only predictive success and domain-relative rationality, not internal representations that mirror human mental states.

(2) The second researcher replied that predictive compression is not enough. He pointed out that the agent's behavior collapses with minor linguistic perturbations that a genuinely belief-guided system should treat as semantically equivalent; this fragility shows we are tracking a token-contingent heuristic, not a stable mental state. Applying the intentional stance here, he said, is a category mistake—useful fiction at best, misleading anthropomorphism at worst.

(3) The first researcher countered that demanding cross-synonym coherence imposes an unfair human-derived benchmark. Artificial systems often possess highly localized, non-generalizing representations; rationality should be assessed relative to the architecture's design constraints, not against human standards of conceptual stability. She conceded that the belief attribution might fail outside the narrow token-condition, but insisted that within that domain the intentional description remains the most powerful predictive tool available, which is precisely Dennett's criterion.

(4) The second researcher then claimed that if domain-relativity and predictive utility are sufficient, we must also attribute beliefs to far simpler devices—such as a thermostat that activates only when a specific voltage spike accompanies a temperature drop—because we could construct an equally local, equally predictive intentional narrative for them. Without a principled boundary, he argued, the intentional stance becomes an empty labeling exercise rather than an explanatory framework.
