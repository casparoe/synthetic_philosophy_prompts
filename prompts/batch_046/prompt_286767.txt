I'm taking an advanced course on epistemic rationality and have been arguing with friends about whether the dogmatism paradox genuinely undermines evidentialism or merely reveals a sloppy conception of belief revision. I'm drafting an essay that reconstructs the paradox—if rational agents are entitled to retain beliefs when confronted with inconclusive counter-evidence, then they appear entitled never to revise, which seems to annihilate rational responsiveness altogether—and then proposes a distinction I have not encountered in the standard discussions: between "evidential immunity," where a particular belief is locally shielded from defeat by a specific datum, and "revision-blocking entitlement," a standing cognitive permission to avoid reassessment altogether.

My central claim is that the paradox conflates these two notions. I sketch a layered model of cognitive architecture in which beliefs operate at different "depths"—surface commitments that are cheap to revise, intermediate methodological rules, and deep framework principles—each with its own evidential threshold for triggering reassessment. On this view, evidential immunity at one depth does not entail a global revision-blocking entitlement, because rational responsiveness is distributed rather than monolithic. Before I commit to this structure, I would value your comments on three points: whether the distinction between evidential immunity and revision-blocking entitlement is genuinely substantive or collapses upon analysis; whether attributing "depth" to beliefs illegitimately psychologises what should be a purely normative epistemological framework; and whether my model overlooks an obvious self-deception problem—namely, that agents might strategically misclassify beliefs as "deep" to avoid inconvenient revision. Please provide your assessment in British English, and feel free to suggest how the essay's framing could be sharpened.
