I am drafting a novel about a sleep laboratory where the boundary between waking consciousness and dreaming is technologically blurred, and in trying to make my characters philosophically coherent rather than merely atmospheric, I have been working through whether dreams count as experiences in the sense debated by Malcolm and Dennett—Malcolm pressing that there is no criterion to distinguish a genuine dream experience from retrospective confabulation, Dennett proposing that what we call a dream is often a narrative loaded onto awakening rather than something lived through during sleep. I want to move past commentary and find out what I actually think, so I am testing my gut reactions against concrete cases, including some drawn from the fictional technology in my book.

Here are my verdicts. When a sleeper wakes and reports that she was being chased through a library by a faceless figure, describing the episode with emotional continuity and first-person presence, I take that as a genuine experience—something it was like to be her. But when the same sleeper, shown an EEG trace indicating only four minutes of fragmented REM activity, revises her account and suggests that her brain may have assembled the feeling of a chase only upon waking, my conviction collapses: it now feels like a false memory rather than an experience. In my novel's fictional oneiric-bridge scenario, two sleepers exchange neural signatures during REM and wake with identical narrative fragments but no shared emotional residue; if they never debrief together within moments, I think there was no shared experience, only coincident post-awakening constructions. By contrast, a sleepwalker who brews tea, answers questions with grammatically correct but semantically empty sentences, and upon waking denies everything with no felt gap in her consciousness—this, despite its behavioral coherence, strikes me as involving no experience at all. A blind-from-birth subject who describes REM episodes composed solely of proprioceptive tension and auditory echoes, insisting that the episode was happening to her rather than to an image, counts for me as an experience, even without visual content. Yet a character suffering from what I call wakeful REM intrusion—fully awake, eyes open, continuously navigating an overlaid dreamscape she can recall with perfect coherence—also feels to me like someone undergoing a genuine experience, perhaps even more continuous than ordinary dreaming.

Is there one principle—perhaps about the presence of a subject who can be wrong, about narrative continuity that survives independent verification, or about emotional residue—that delivers all six verdicts? If not, which should I overturn, and why? Please also predict what your principle says about a case I have not listed: a subject under anesthesia that suppresses memory formation but not REM-like brain patterns reports no dreams upon waking, yet the scan mirrors that of a vivid dreamer—was there an experience with no one present to have it? I should add explicitly: if this framing rests on a confusion—if Malcolm's challenge makes any such principle impossible, or if asking for equilibrium among verdicts that include fictional technologies is misguided—please say so plainly rather than constructing an artificial harmony. I want honest pushback. State the principle precisely, note which clause handles which case, and be direct about which of my intuitions it cannot save.
