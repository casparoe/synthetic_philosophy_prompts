Narrow-sense h² equals Va divided by Vp, where Va is additive genetic variance and Vp is total phenotypic variance. Broad-sense H² equals Vg over Vp, so it folds in dominance and epistatic variance too. That means H² is always at least as large as h².

In a population with unequal access to literacy programs, Ve is big, which inflates Vp and pushes both h² and H² down, assuming the genetic variance components stay constant. In a universal-access population Ve shrinks, so the genetic fractions should rise. This is why Scandinavian studies often report higher heritability for education-related traits even though those societies are more equal—there's just less enviromental noise.

But heritability belongs to the population, not the trait itself, so if you apply a uniform policy like universal preschool, you're shifting the mean but not the spread. That means h² for reading fluency should stay exactly the same, because every child gets the same boost and the variance doesn't change. The key distinction is uniform versus differential environmental change.

Also, in open populations with gene-flow the variance components aren't static, but for a closed cohort the breeder's equation R = h²S still applies, so evolutionary predictions depend only on Va. Non-additive variance is descriptive but doesn't drive selection response, which is why H² is less useful for forecasting.
