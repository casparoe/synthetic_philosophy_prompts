I am a software engineer with no formal philosophy background, and I am designing a conflict-resolution layer for a municipal participatory-budgeting platform. The system takes citizen preference inputs and hard engineering or budgetary constraints and generates optimized infrastructure-spending portfolios. I want this layer to operationalize Rudolf Carnap's distinction between empirical-technical planning questions—disagreements about means, causal consequences, and feasibility that belong to science and engineering—and value-laden political questions—disagreements about ends, priorities, and distributive justice that express non-cognitive attitudes and should not be overridden by an optimization algorithm.

Write a reusable procedural protocol—structured as an ordered checklist with explicit decision nodes—that a planning officer or system auditor (technical background, no philosophy training) can apply under time pressure to classify any dispute that arises during a planning session. The context is fixed: each audit must be completed in approximately fifteen minutes; the officer must document the result on a standardized one-page form; and the mayor's office must sign off on every escalation. The officer will not have access to external literature or philosophical references during the audit.

The protocol must include the following:

1. A sequence of no more than five ordered test questions that distinguish empirical claims about means and consequences from value commitments about ends and distributive priorities. Include explicit instructions for hybrid cases in which a factual claim and a normative commitment are intertwined—such as disputes over whether a model's "quality of life" metric correctly captures well-being.

2. Three fully worked applications: (a) a pure empirical dispute over whether a proposed transit alignment measurably increases flood risk; (b) a pure value dispute over whether the budget should prioritize accessibility over travel speed; and (c) a hybrid case in which citizens reject the system's weighting of neighborhood equity versus aggregate efficiency, where the procedure should protect democratic authority by flagging the dispute for deliberation rather than resolving it technically—an outcome that I, as the engineer, will find technically unsatisfying but must accept.

3. Clear marking of every point where judgment cannot be eliminated and where the officer must invoke discretionary escalation, together with the specific documentation required for each path.

4. A frank statement of what the procedure cannot do—particularly regarding incommensurable value frameworks, hidden normative assumptions embedded in input data, and cases where empirical uncertainty is so high that the technical/value boundary collapses—plus an explicit warning about the conditions under which the protocol will systematically misfire.

Keep the language precise and accessible to a practitioner without philosophical training. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations. Do not ask the officer to consult external texts. The deliverable should be a reusable decision procedure, not advice for a single situation or a memo for one body.
