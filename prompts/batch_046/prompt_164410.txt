After retiring from a career in experimental physics, I have begun studying philosophy of science with some seriousness, and I am struck by how little the standard taxonomy of probability interpretations—frequentism, subjectivism, and propensity theory—addresses a problem that arises constantly in my former field: how to make sense of probabilistic claims in domains where the generating conditions are not literally repeatable. In evolutionary history, climate dynamics, or certain quantum measurement contexts, we speak of probabilities as attaching to single, unrepeatable configurations, yet propensity accounts seem to require a repeatable setup to ground real dispositions; frequentist accounts appear to depend on reference classes that we choose rather than discover; and subjectivist accounts, while flexible, risk reducing scientific prediction to rational belief revision without explaining why the world constrains those beliefs in one direction rather than another. Rather than asking you to declare a winner among the three interpretations, I want you to treat this tension as a map of open conceptual territory. Which specific questions—about explanatory power, about the ontology of chance, about the conditions under which a probabilistic model is genuinely justified—are the most important to resolve if one's goal is to understand how working scientists actually reason about uncertainty in non-repeatable systems? Please provide a defended prioritization: identify the two or three lines of inquiry or unresolved implications that matter most, rank them, and explain the criteria you are using (for instance, upstream conceptual dependence, tractability without new empirical results, or practical impact on scientific methodology). I am writing this for my own clarification, not for publication, so I am less interested in a survey than in a focused agenda.
