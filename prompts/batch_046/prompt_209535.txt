the ethics of voting debate feels totally stuck to me. on one side people say there's a civic duty to vote, like jury duty or paying taxes, and skipping it is basically free-riding. on the other side people say one vote has near-zero causal power, so calling it a 'duty' is just symbolic posturing — voting is really expressive, you're announcing who you are, not actually changing outcomes. then there's the lesser-evil question: is voting for a flawed candidate to stop a worse one okay, or are you still complicit in the harm they cause? philosophers have been going back and forth on this since the 70s and it never moves. my hunch is it's not really about moral facts at all — it's that one camp treats a vote as a private act with direct causal weight (however small) while the other treats it as a public signal inside a collective mechanism where responsibility is distribued across everyone. there's also that weird middle framing i keep seeing in social ontology — that voting isn't private or pure signal, but a 'node contribution' to a collective apparatus that only functions at scale. does that actually break the stalemate, or just rename it? diagnose this: is the disagreement verbal (about what 'duty' and 'expression' mean), substantive (about complicity and causal contribution), or are they answering different questions — individual ethics vs democratic legitimacy? what would progress even look like? a formal model of distribued responsibility, a change in framing, or is this just one of those disputes where both sides start from incompatible intuitions about collective action and there's nowhere to go?
