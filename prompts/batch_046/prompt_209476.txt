I'm trying to determine what I actually believe about the moral significance of video-game characters, working from my concrete reactions rather than from general theory. Here are my verdicts.

When I kill a hostile enemy NPC in combat designed for that purpose, I feel no moral wrong; the character functions as a designed obstacle. But if I use an exploit to trap a non-hostile, suffering-expressing NPC in an inescapable loop of distress, that feels genuinely wrong—closer to cruelty to an animal than to breaking a toy. When I choose whether to save or sacrifice a companion NPC with an authored backstory, emotional dialogue arcs, and relational history with my player-character, I treat the decision as carrying real moral weight, comparable to influencing the fate of a well-drawn fictional person. Yet when I later delete that save file, permanently erasing the same companion, I feel regret and aesthetic loss, but not guilt; it seems no one was wronged.

Consider a procedurally generated NPC with a unique appearance, name, and dialogue tree generated at first encounter, which remembers interactions within the session but has no persistence across saves. If I deliberately inflict prolonged suffering on it within that session, I judge this wrong, though less severely than harming the authored companion; but if I then learn that its generation algorithm is fully deterministic, so that any player anywhere could regenerate the numerically identical instance, my judgment weakens further, as though the harm were distributed across an infinite pool of interchangeable copies.

There is tension here: I seem responsive both to simulated phenomenology and to narrative uniqueness, yet they come apart. I am also uncertain about a collective case. In a persistent multiplayer world, there is a server-wide "guardian" entity—entirely rule-based, with no simulated consciousness—whose continued presence requires ongoing collective resource contributions from players. If the community gradually stops maintaining it and it fades, is that a moral failure, like neglecting a shared monument, or is it morally neutral, like allowing a garden statue to weather?

And I am unsure about emergence: suppose a pattern of aggregate player behavior becomes complex enough that the game's recognition system treats it as a "character," assigning it dialogue and reactive states, even though it is fully reducible to distributed player actions with no independent substrate. Does that emergent entity acquire any moral standing?

Is there a coherent principle—perhaps combining thresholds of phenomenological simulation, narrative non-fungibility, and relational investment—that captures all of these verdicts without ad hoc adjustments? Or does saving consistency require me to drop one? Specifically, what clause distinguishes the wrongness of trapping the non-hostile NPC from the permissibility of deleting the save file, and does that same clause imply that letting the server-wide guardian fade is wrong? What does your best principle predict for an unlisted case: an NPC that is a perfect behavioral clone of a specific deceased person, reconstructed from their digital traces, existing only in this game and responsive to my actions? Finally, state clearly which of my original verdicts your principle cannot accommodate, and why.
