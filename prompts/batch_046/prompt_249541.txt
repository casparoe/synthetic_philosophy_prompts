I've been working through Marx's critique of political economy—particularly Capital and the 1844 Economic and Philosophic Manuscripts—and I'm struggling to map the exact relationship between exploitation and alienation. My current view is that exploitation is essentially the structural mechanism that generates alienation: the extraction of surplus value forces the worker into estrangement from the product, from the act of production, and from species-being, so alienation is just the phenomenological shadow of being exploited. But a counterexample was raised in our seminar: imagine a highly compensated software engineer at a worker-owned cooperative who still produces surplus value that gets reinvested rather than distributed; she reports no subjective estrangement, yet she is technically exploited by Marx's definition. Conversely, a feudal serf might feel deeply alienated from his labor without being exploited in the specifically capitalist sense of surplus-value extraction. Given these disjunctions, I suspect I may be conflating two analytically distinct categories—one normative-structural, one phenomenological—into a single causal story. Could you clarify whether Marx's mature theory treats them as independent dimensions, and if so, which one does the heavier critical lifting? Please give me a committed answer: take a definite position on whether the conceptual separation undermines the unity of Marx's critique, and explain what a proper integration of the two looks like without collapsing them into one another.
