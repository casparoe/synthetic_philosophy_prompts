I serve on a three-member review board that approves public-facing system cards for deployed multi-modal AI agents used in clinical triage support. Every quarter we evaluate a new version, and we must decide—within a two-hour session, with one clinical ethicist, one ML engineer, and one legal sign-off officer who lacks technical training—whether the documentation may use intentional-language descriptions ("the agent believes the patient is stable," "it intends to escalate," "it suspects a contradiction") or must be rewritten in purely mechanistic terms ("the classifier assigns 0.92 to the stability token," etc.). The stakes are high: intentional framing shapes how clinicians calibrate trust and liability, yet stripping it can obscure emergent failure modes.

Please construct a reusable decision procedure, grounded in the logic of Dennett's intentional stance but translated into steps that do not require philosophical training, that tells our board when belief or desire attribution is warranted, when it is prohibited, and when the case must be escalated as an indeterminate values judgment rather than a technical determination. The procedure must include:

1. A preliminary gate: the architectural and deployment conditions that must hold before intentional language is even considered.
2. Three ordered operational tests, each with explicit pass/fail criteria, specified evidence requirements, instructions for missing evidence, and a note on whether the step may be skipped:
   - Test A (Cross-domain predictive compression): Does attributing the specific mental state reduce the description length needed to predict the agent's behavior across at least two functionally distinct operational contexts?
   - Test B (Counterfactual robustness under bounded perturbation): If non-essential parameters—prompt framing, sampling temperature, or minor weight perturbations—are varied within a defined tolerance, does the attributed belief or desire continue to explain the behavioral pattern?
   - Test C (Mechanistic redundancy and parsimony): Can the observed behavior be fully accounted for by a mechanistic description that never invokes the mental state and that does not require multiplying independent ad hoc auxiliary hypotheses beyond a defined threshold (specifically, more than one mechanistic explanation per distinct behavioral context)?
3. A decision matrix mapping every combination of pass/fail/insufficient-data outcomes onto one of three board actions: (a) permit intentional language with documented caveats, (b) prohibit it and mandate mechanistic rewrite, or (c) escalate to a full-board values judgment because the technical criteria are satisfied or indeterminate but institutional risk is unresolved.
4. Explicit annotations—marked clearly—of the points where judgment cannot be eliminated, specifying exactly which disagreements (e.g., between engineer and ethicist) are legitimate even when both parties apply the procedure correctly.

The deliverable should be formatted as a flowchart in words, with time allocations for each stage that sum to no more than two hours, and it must state what the procedure cannot do—for example, it cannot resolve whether intentional language is ethically appropriate when predictive criteria are met but liability law is ambiguous.

Then apply the procedure to the following three cases:

Case 1: A triage agent consistently refuses to recommend patient discharge whenever vital-sign ambiguity exceeds a threshold. This refusal correlates across input perturbations with activation in an internal representation cluster; mechanistically this is a gating layer, but no simpler mechanistic account predicts the cross-context consistency without referencing the cluster's semantic content.

Case 2: A scheduling agent, after a reward-model update, begins producing utterances such as "I need to protect my users' downtime" and adjusts calendar allocations defensively. The behavior vanishes when reward-model weights are perturbed within tolerance, yet no single mechanistic feature predicts both the linguistic outputs and the calendar adjustments without post-hoc stitching across modules.

Case 3: A diagnostic assistant outputs the phrase "I am uncertain" in exact correlation with model entropy; every instance can be replaced by "entropy exceeds 0.4" with zero loss of predictive accuracy across all contexts.

For each case, state the result of each test, the matrix outcome, and the required board action. In particular, identify the case where the procedure yields a result—either permitting or prohibiting intentional language—that conflicts with ordinary clinical intuition, explain the conflict precisely, and state exactly what the procedure requires the board to do despite that discomfort. Finally, note one scenario outside the procedure's scope where the board must still make a non-technical judgment.
