After arguing with a friend about liability in modern conflicts, I keep returning to an argument that seems to prove that the moral equality of combatants (MEC) either collapses the principle of discrimination or forces total pacifism, and I cannot locate the exit. (1) The standard defense of MEC holds that unjust combatants are not liable to defensive killing because they lack individual moral responsibility for the war's injustice; they act under institutional coercion. (2) If absence of individual responsibility for the war's injustice is sufficient to block liability, then the same protection must extend to anyone whose contribution to the war is structurally coerced by those same institutions. (3) In contemporary conflicts, civilians in an aggressor state maintain the fiscal, logistical, and informational infrastructure that enables combat, and they do so under the same structural coercion—tax obligations, employment dependencies, and state information control—that affects soldiers. (4) Therefore, either these civilians are also immune from direct attack (which dissolves the combatant/non-combatant distinction), or they are liable despite lacking individual responsibility, which undermines the very reasoning that protects unjust combatants. (5) One might reply that combatants make direct causal contributions to killing while civilians do not, but this distinction is one of degree and institutional role rather than kind: a remote logistics contractor and a drone operator both cause harm through mediated organizational structures, and a soldier's direct action is no less embedded in coercive command hierarchies. (6) So MEC either proves too much—extending protection to all structurally embedded participants—or proves too little, since it cannot explain why direct participation alone generates liability without reintroducing individual culpability. Where does the argument go wrong? If I must choose, I want to keep (1) and the principle of discrimination intact, but I want to know exactly what conceptual cost that choice demands and which premise pays it.
