I chair the Clinical AI Governance Board at a regional teaching hospital, and at our session next Thursday we must decide on a pilot proposal from the diagnostics division. The proposal asks us to approve a "recursive adversarial audit" protocol for a high-stakes radiology-assistance model: instead of relying solely on human expert review of the model's reasoning traces—which the division argues are now too long and technically dense for clinicians to evaluate reliably—the protocol would instantiate two copies of the model in a structured debate format, one defending and one challenging each diagnostic recommendation, with a smaller, fully interpretable "judge" model rendering the final verdict. The division cites scalable-oversight arguments that human reviewers cannot remain competent judges when models exceed them in pattern-recognition depth, and they propose a six-month pilot with monthly human spot-checks.

Several board members object that this substitutes an opaque theatrical procedure for accountable clinical judgment. They note that adversarial interaction between language models tends to produce rhetorical convergence and sycophancy—where debaters align with the perceived authority of the framing or the judge—rather than truth-tracking disagreement, and that a smaller judge may lack the representational capacity to understand the very arguments it is asked to adjudicate. Others worry that framing oversight as "scalable" quietly treats clinical accountability as a bottleneck to be engineered away rather than a normative requirement to be preserved. I need a formal decision memo—approve, approve with conditions, or reject—to circulate before Thursday's vote. It should recommend a concrete course of action with an explicit time frame, engage the division's scalable-oversight rationale honestly rather than dismissing it, and treat the sycophancy, opacity, and accountability objections as serious rather than merely conservative. If you recommend approval with conditions, specify what safeguards or termination triggers must be built in.

In drafting your response, please be explicit about where your reasoning is most likely to fail: identify the specific assumption or empirical claim—about debate dynamics in current architectures, about the interpretability of small judges, or about the irreducibility of human clinical accountability—that, if wrong, would lead you to a different recommendation. Also indicate what kind of evidence or argument would change your verdict.
