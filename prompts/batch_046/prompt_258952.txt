I have spent some time reading popular accounts of history—books that follow one unknown person through a crisis, and others that trace the rise and fall of states through laws and numbers. I think the disagreement between these two approaches runs deeper than taste: there are genuinely different beliefs about what counts as historical explanation, what sources can support it, and whether the two approaches can even talk to each other. I would like you to draft the document that an adversarial collaboration between these two camps ought to have produced.

Name the camps clearly: one camp holds that the primary task of history is to recover the agency, experience, and decision-making of ordinary people, often through intensive study of small-scale events, single lives, or local communities; they maintain that histories centered on elites, institutions, and aggregate statistics systematically obscure how most people actually lived and acted. The opposing camp holds that the proper aim of history is to explain large-scale, long-duration transformations—demographic shifts, state formation, economic structures—and that while micro-level narratives are vivid, they risk being unrepresentative and cannot by themselves establish broader patterns; they maintain that macro-level explanation requires aggregate evidence and that focusing too narrowly on individuals can mislead.

The document should have the following sections, in order:

A numbered list of agreed claims—statements about historical evidence, causation, and the limits of archives that both camps could sincerely sign. These must be substantive, not empty truisms, and must not be worded to favor either side.

A disagreements table. For each residual dispute, present: the precise question; the first camp's position in wording they would accept; the second camp's position in wording they would accept.

A section on cruxes—the sharper sub-questions such that resolving them would force at least one camp to revise a central commitment. I suspect that one crux concerns whether reliance on state-generated archives introduces a structural bias that makes non-elite action invisible by default, and another concerns whether drawing general conclusions from a single well-documented case necessarily commits a reasoning error analogous to an ecological fallacy; but you should identify the cruxes that genuinely follow from the disagreements above.

For each crux, specify: a concrete case or body of evidence that both sides agree is relevant and well-established (no obscure or disputed archival discoveries); each side's explicit prediction about what that case or evidence demonstrates; and the specific finding that would cause each side to change its mind.

Please do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations to specific scholars or works. The prompt should stand entirely on conceptual reasoning and the cases provided.
