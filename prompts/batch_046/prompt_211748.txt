In a clinical-legal framework governing gradual neural prosthesis—where surgeons replace biological cortical tissue with synthetic analogs over months or years—there is an institutional rule that a patient retains continuous legal and moral personhood only if the monthly replacement rate stays below five percent and the patient can verbally confirm autobiographical continuity at each checkpoint. Should the rate exceed that threshold, or should the patient fail the continuity check, the procedure is halted and a new legal identity is registered, with property rights and contractual obligations transferred to an estate rather than inherited. At the same time, the same medical-legal system treats a patient who suffers sudden traumatic brain injury resulting in total retrograde amnesia as unambiguously the same person—no identity reassignment, no estate transition—even though the psychological discontinuity is far sharper and the bodily substrate is unchanged.

What theory, or cluster of theories, of personal identity and moral status does this dual-track practice presuppose? Does it commit the institution to a psychological-continuity view with a rate-sensitive threshold, to a hybrid bodily-psychological criterion, or to something like a narrative-identity account moderated by subjective report? Can the protocol’s reliance on gradualness and autobiographical verification be reconciled with its refusal to apply analogous scrutiny to sudden amnesia, or does the trauma case reveal an unstated bodily-continuity commitment that undermines the prosthesis rules? Reconstruct the implicit philosophy precisely enough to show which rival views—animalism, pure psychological continuity without branching conditions, four-dimensionalism, or deflationary no-self accounts—it quietly rules out, and identify where the practice is philosophically underdetermined so that more than one theory could license it. Finally, if the underlying view were replaced by a straightforward animalist or a pure narrative-identity framework, what concrete changes would have to be made to the replacement-rate rule, the checkpoint protocol, and the trauma-case handling?
