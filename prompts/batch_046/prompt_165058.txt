Acting as an expert philosopher of perception with experience teaching first-year undergraduates, please think carefully and begin with a brief conceptual brainstorm before laying out the full plan. I am preparing a single 50-minute seminar for philosophy students who have just encountered the classic argument from illusion—specifically, the move from phenomenally identical experiences (a straight stick submerged in water versus one in air) to the conclusion that both must be explained by a shared mental element such as a sense-datum or an internal representational state. The students are sharp but arrive with a strong default intuition: if two experiences “feel the same,” they must share an intrinsic mental nature. I want to guide them through disjunctivism about perceptual experience—the view that in veridical perception the experience is constituted by direct acquaintance with the external object and its properties, whereas in illusion or hallucination there is no “highest common factor” and the mental events are of fundamentally different kinds—without simply lecturing at them.

Please deliver a structured teaching plan rather than the lecture transcript itself. First, map the exact dependency order: which idea must be in place before the next one can land, starting from the students’ existing common-factor intuition, and explain why the sequence collapses if a step is skipped. Second, identify and refine the one carrying example that should anchor the session; I am inclined to use a tactile vibration illusion in which a subject running a finger over a single oscillating surface at different velocities reports phenomenally distinct textures (smooth versus rough) and compare this with genuinely encountering two different surfaces, but you should specify precisely what this example must include and what it must avoid in order to do the philosophical work. Third, chart the predictable misconceptions in the order students will likely raise them—for instance, “if they feel identical they must be the same kind of state,” “disjunctivism makes hallucination conceptually impossible,” or “this just relocates the problem to explaining the bad case”—and indicate at which point in the arc each should be headed off and with what brief strategic move, so the sequence absorbs rather than derails. Fourth, propose a concrete check for understanding that would demonstrate they have grasped the disjunctive claim itself rather than merely memorized a slogan.
