While researching a long-form feature on consciousness for a science magazine, I have been speaking with philosophers of mind and reviewing the standard literature on epiphenomenalism, which is typically introduced through the familiar metaphor of mental properties as causally inert byproducts—present, like smoke trailing a locomotive, but incapable of altering the machinery’s motion. The difficulty that consistently arises in these discussions is the so-called paradox of phenomenal judgment: if my belief that I am experiencing a sharp pain is produced solely by neural firing, and the pain itself exerts no causal influence on that belief, then it seems the belief must be about whatever actually caused it—namely, the brain state—rather than about the experience. Applying what I take to be a natural principle, that a mental representation’s content is fixed by its causal origin, I arrive at the conclusion that our phenomenal judgments are not first-person reports of inner life but unwitting neurophysiological observations. Yet the philosophers I have consulted treat the paradox as a special threat to the reliability or justification of self-knowledge, not as a discovery that all our color and pain talk has always been covert neuroscience. Where exactly does my inference break down? Is the mistake in applying a causal theory of content to phenomenal judgments, or have I reconstructed the paradox itself incorrectly—perhaps by conflating it with a separate problem about mental causation? And if the causal-origin principle is indeed the error, how should we understand the relationship between a conscious experience and the judgment about it, given that the former still plays no causal role in producing the latter?
