I am defending a modified Schefflerian position in a philosophy colloquium: human extinction is distinctively bad not mainly because of the deaths it involves, but because it severs what I call "successor-context dependency" — the condition under which present projects derive their point from being recognizable and continuable by a living future interpretive community. I apply this to argue that "archival extinction," in which humanity dies but perfect digital records survive, is nearly as destructive of present value as total annihilation, which shows the harm is structural rather than distributive. The audience will be analytic value theorists and philosophers of death who have engaged with Scheffler's afterlife conjecture, including critics who regard it as psychologism about value, and environmental ethicists who worry that grounding extinction's badness in present meaning makes humanity's preservation instrumentally rather than intrinsically important. I have about twenty minutes plus Q&A.

Prepare me as a speaker, not as a defender: list the objections this specific audience will actually raise, ordered by likelihood and damage, phrased in their vocabulary. Finally, if my distinction between living interpretation and archival preservation is unstable, or if I have confused a psychological fact about motivation with an objective claim about value, say so plainly rather than polishing the argument.
