I have been thinking about a pattern I see in how courts and government agencies handle written rules, and I am trying to figure out what view of language and authority it quietly assumes, even though I have no formal training in law or philosophy. In many legal systems, judges say they must begin with the ordinary meaning of the words in a statute. But there is usually a recognized exception: if applying those words literally would produce something clearly absurd or contrary to what everyone agrees the law was meant to achieve, the court may set the literal meaning aside and look at legislative history, stated purpose, or practical consequences. At the same time, administrative agencies are often required by law to publish a detailed statement of purpose and supporting findings whenever they issue a new regulation, yet when those regulations are challenged in court, the judicial review focuses almost entirely on whether the regulatory text can be read as falling within the language of the authorizing statute; the agency’s own explanation of its purpose is treated as background context at best, and sometimes ignored entirely. I was discussing this with friends, and we noticed that the system seems to place “what the text says” and “what the rule is for” into separate institutional boxes, using one as the default and the other only as an emergency repair.

What philosophical commitments about legal meaning and institutional authority does this two-layered arrangement presuppose? Does it require the idea that a statute possesses a fixed linguistic content that can genuinely come apart from its normative purpose, and that courts have special authority over the first while legislatures have special authority over the second? Or can a consistent textualist—who holds that the law just is the conventional meaning of the enacted text—explain why literal meaning should ever be overridden by purpose, and why agencies must justify themselves by purpose if purpose is not part of legal interpretation? Conversely, can a purposivist—who holds that purpose is always relevant to fixing meaning—explain why courts treat purpose as an exceptional corrective rather than as part of the ordinary interpretive process? Reconstruct the implicit theory of legal content that this practice needs, distinguish what it strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, and say where the practice is philosophically underdetermined, so that several rival theories might license it. Then tell me what a legal system built on a straightforwardly unified view—either purely textual or purely purposive—would have to change about these institutional roles and review procedures.
