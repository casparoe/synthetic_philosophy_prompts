For a philosophy project I am debating with a classmate about material constitution, and I want to pressure-test our intuitions with a deliberately strange case. Imagine a museum conservator gradually replaces every atom in an ancient clay statue with synthetically manufactured atoms that are chemically and physically identical but produced in a laboratory; by the end, every macroscopic property—shape, weight, porosity, color—is preserved, yet not a single original molecule remains. What is your actual credence, on a scale from 0 to 1, that the resulting object is numerically identical to the original statue? Please decompose your reasoning into distinct components: the probability that material continuity is necessary for artifact identity, the probability that formal or spatiotemporal continuity alone is sufficient, the probability that synthetic replication differs metaphysically from natural gradual replacement, and the probability that there is even a determinate fact of the matter about artifact persistence. Assign a rough number or weight to each component, show how they combine into your final credence, and identify which single component is most sensitive—what specific argument or intuition pump would shift it the most.
