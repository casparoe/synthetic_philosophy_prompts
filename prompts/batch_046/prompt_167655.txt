I've been working through an argument a philosophy-of-art blogger laid out about the paradox of fiction, and I can't see where it breaks, though the conclusion feels wrong. The argument turns on what the blogger calls representational target-locking: an emotional state is genuinely directed at object O only if the cognitive appraisal that triggers it represents O as actually existing and as being in the relevant state (in danger, suffering, etc.). Here are the steps.

(1) For an emotion to be genuinely directed at O, the cognitive appraisal must represent O as actually existing and in the relevant state.
(2) In fiction, we represent characters and events, but our cognitive appraisal represents them as non-actual, invented entities.
(3) Therefore, the emotional arousal produced by fiction cannot be genuinely directed at the fictional objects; the appraisal lacks the actual-existence tag required for target-locking.
(4) The arousal is real, so it must be directed at something that does meet the condition: typically the viewer's own mental imagery, analogous real-world memories, or general existential concerns.
(5) So when we say we feel pity for Anna Karenina, we are misdescribing our state; the real target is either our own affective simulation or real-world analogues we are reminded of.
(6) By parity of reasoning, any emotional response to a merely possible future event (e.g., anxiety about a job interview next week) is also misdirected unless we represent that event as actually occurring now; but we represent it as future and contingent, not actual now.
(7) Therefore, either anxiety about the future is always misdirected self-pity, or premise (1) is false.
(8) But premise (1) seemed essential to distinguish fiction-feelings from real-world feelings; giving it up collapses the distinction and makes it mysterious why fiction doesn't motivate action.
(9) So we should accept (1) and conclude that most of our apparently object-directed emotional life is actually a kind of sophisticated self-reference or analogical projection, including in fiction and future-planning.

This seems to prove that our emotional lives are far more solipsistic or analogical than we think, which strikes me as too extreme. I want to keep (1) because it explains why we don't run out of the theater to save the character, but I also want to keep the idea that fiction can genuinely move us in ways that aren't just about ourselves. Which premise should I reject? And what does rejecting it cost for the distinction between fictional and real-world emotional engagement?
