I'm on my university's accessibility funding board and I just learned my roommate was diagnosed with a chronic condition that makes navigating our disability services a nightmare, so this suddenly feels less abstract. We have to vote Thursday on a pilot proposal that would redistribute mental-health crisis grants through a modified veil-of-ignorance procedure. Instead of students submitting diagnoses and being ranked by clinical severity, they'd file anonymized "institutional burden profiles" describing only barriers they face -- scheduling, housing, cognitive load from dealing with our buerocracy. A panel then allocates resources to maximize the worst-off position, but nobody knows whose profile belongs to whom, not even themselves. The grad student who designed it calls this "blind-profile maximin" and argues it forces deliberators to weight severe invisible disability more fairly since they can't assume able-bodied privilege.

My co-chair says this is Rawls with extra steps: if you strip away all identifying context, you lose the ability to correct for structural disadvantage that doesn't map onto generic "burden," and you risk treating real medical need as interchangeable bureaucratic friction. I think the difference principle actually requires knowing enough about primary goods to compare positions, but I'm not fully convinced.
