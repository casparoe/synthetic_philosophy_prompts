student answer:
Pragmatic encroachment says whether you know something depends partly on what's practically at stake, not just true belief with good reasons. If an engineer believes a bridge is safe after a quick check, she might know that for her commute, but not know it when she's signing off on 500 schoolkids crossing—same evidence, higher stakes, so the bar for knowledge rises. Epistemic contextualism is different because it puts the shift in conversational context: "knows" gets stricter or looser depending on what speakers are worrying about, not because the subject's real-world situation changed. In my bridge case everyone is in one meeting talking about the same bridge, so the context doesn't shift; the stakes do, which means it's encroachment.

About the stake-sensitive belief fixation model: it's basically pragmatic encroachment scaled up. When a committee raises its evidential threshold together because they're facing shared risk, they're doing the same thing the engineer does—letting practical stakes set how much evidence is enough—so it counts as collective pragmatic encroachment. One mistake people make is thinking encroachment requires feeling nervous; it's actually about objective risk, not feelings. Contextualism, by contrast, doesn't involve risk at all—it's purely about language use—so the two views don't really overlap.
