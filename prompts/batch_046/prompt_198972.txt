Construct a decision tree for a graduate jurisprudence seminar that maps the conceptual space opened by what I call the Mirror Amendment Problem: a constitutional text contains Article R, which provides that Article R itself may be amended only by a procedure that does not invoke Article R's authority, and that any amendment violating this condition is void ab initio. The tree should take a respondent from the initial commitment that a legal system should be able to correct its own rules of correction, and branch through four diagnostic nodes: (1) whether self-referential amendment clauses are coherent or necessarily paradoxical; (2) whether avoiding infinite regress requires an external anchor—such as constituent power, an algorithmic validation oracle, or a non-legal normative source—outside the code itself; (3) whether the system should tolerate a temporary state of logical inconsistency during a transitional amendment; and (4) whether the proper resolution is procedural (determining which institution has final authority) or substantive (determining which normative content survives). Each node should be framed as a concrete question that a respondent can answer without already knowing the corresponding position labels. The terminal leaves should be named conceptual stances—Hard Self-Reference Denier, Infinite Regress Acceptance, External Anchor Monist, Procedural Pragmatist, Substantive Preservationist, and Transitional Tolerance Advocate—rather than named historical theorists. Where your tree cannot cleanly separate two leaves, flag the pair and briefly diagnose whether the separation fails for conceptual reasons or because the space is merely underexplored. In your answer, state how confident you are in the tree's architecture, and explicitly identify the node or separation about which you are least certain.
