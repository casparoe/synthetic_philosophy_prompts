I've been working through Bradley's regress in a metaphysics seminar, and I tried to sharpen the classic problem of propositional unity into an argument that threatens not just propositions but rational cognition itself. I'm suspicious because the conclusion feels like an obvious reductio, yet each step looks defensible, and I'm struggling to locate the sleight of hand—whether it's an equivocation between metaphysical and mental relations, a hidden scope shift, or a premise that only looks necessary. The framing injects a modern candidate—treating mental synthesis as a potential unifier—but I want to stay within the philosophical problem of unity rather than drift into empirical neuroscience.

Consider the following chain: (1) Any proposition P that asserts that a is F must contain a, F, and some principle U that makes them genuinely one rather than a mere aggregate. (2) If U is an internal relation, then by Bradley's regress U must itself be related to a and to F, requiring further relations U1, U2, etc.; since no finite thinker can complete an infinite hierarchy, U cannot be internal. (3) If U is an external relation—a separate constituent standing alongside a and F—then a, F, and U are three items, and we need a further principle U' to unify them; but U' is either internal (regress) or external (same relocation), so external relations do not solve the problem. (4) If U is not a relation but a primitive, non-relational bond B, then B must either apply to each constituent separately or characterize the whole. If separately, applying B to a requires a relation between B and a (regress). If B characterizes the whole, then B presupposes the very unity it is meant to explain. (5) If U is instead an act of mental synthesis S—the mind binding a and F into a judgment—then S is either structured (with its own constituents, triggering a regress for S) or unstructured. If unstructured, S is a bare capacity; but a bare capacity cannot explain why a is bound to F rather than to some other constituent G, so it fails to account for the specific content of P. (6) Therefore every candidate for U either generates Bradley's regress or fails to explain specific unity. (7) So no proposition can be genuinely unified. (8) But rational cognition requires entertaining unified propositions—beliefs, judgments, inferences—since reasoning operates on structured content. (9) Therefore rational cognition is impossible. (10) Rational cognition clearly occurs. (11) So one of (1) through (6) must fail.

Tell me which step to reject, then explain why that step looked plausible and what the standard escapes—Russellian primitive unity, adverbialism, or the rejection of propositional realism—each have to pay if they deny it. I'd particularly prefer to keep (8), that cognition requires structured content, and I'm open to abandoning (1) only if the cost is lower than abandoning (5). If this argument is confused—say, if I'm illegitimately sliding between metaphysical relations and mental acts, or if the problem can't be diagnosed without first deciding whether propositions are primitive—say so plainly rather than playing along politely. I want pushback, not compliance.
