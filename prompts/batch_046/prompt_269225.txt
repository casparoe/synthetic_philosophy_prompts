I sit on the State Digital Archives Commission, which oversees the open-access repository for primary sources on twentieth-century political movements. The repository's director has proposed adopting a Unified Epistemic Metadata Standard for all new acquisitions. Under this standard, every record receives only an Interpretive Frame taxonomy—tagging items by historiographical school (social history, institutional, post-structural, etc.)—and the separate Documentary Fact layer is eliminated entirely. There would be no standalone provenance, reliability, or authenticity tags; selection, description, and framing would all be handled within the interpretive taxonomy alone.

Proponents argue that retaining a distinct "objective record" layer commits the repository to Ranke's ideal of reconstructing events as they objectively occurred, an ideal that Carr's critique of empirical objectivity and Hayden White's narrativist analysis of historical writing have shown to be untenable: every archive choice—what to preserve, how to describe it, which relations to highlight—is already an act of narrative construction, and maintaining a separate factual layer falsely suggests that interpretation can be cleanly separated from documentation.

Opponents reply that while Carr and White correctly demonstrate that narrative form shapes historical meaning, this does not collapse the epistemic distinction between an authentic first-hand labor-court transcript and a retrospective synthesis, or between a verified government memorandum and a later forged version. Removing the Documentary Fact layer, they contend, makes it impossible for users to distinguish primary testimony from historiographical framing, thereby undermining the repository's public mandate to preserve evidentiary transparency rather than merely curate interpretive coherence.

The Commission must vote on whether to approve the standard, reject it, or approve it with conditions that preserve some form of dual-layer classification. The memo should recommend a concrete course of action—adopt, reject, or adopt with a specified modification and implementation timeline (e.g., within six months)—that the staff can execute. The reasoning must engage the philosophical dispute seriously: explain how your recommendation navigates the tension between Ranke's aspiration to objective reconstruction and the sceptical/narrativist challenge that all historical presentation is constructed. Include a substantive, honest treatment of the strongest argument on the losing side; do not dismiss it as mere nostalgia for naive empiricism or as unrestrained relativism. Write the document as an institutional opinion with a clear actionable bottom line.
