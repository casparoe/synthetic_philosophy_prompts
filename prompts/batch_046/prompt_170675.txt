I'm putting together a workshop for about 12 grad students in transitional justice, 90 minutes, and I want them to physically feel why amnesty-for-truth deals don't just "work" or "fail" but actively reshape what kind of truth is even producible. I was chatting with a friend who does instutional design and she mentioned this idea that disclosure requirements can create a kind of strategic granularlity — perpetrators calibrate confessions to the verification budget, flooding the record with plausible but uncheckable details so the worst stuff hides in noise. I want an activity, not a lecture, where they run into that themselves.

Design a game or structured role-play. Split them into applicants for amnesty and commissioners with limited verification tokens, hidden cards showing different levels of culpability and what they actually know. The rules should force applicants to write disclosure statements to get amnesty, but commissioners only have so many resources to check consistency. I want them to notice, without being told, that the people with the most to hide end up producing the longest, most detailed narratives, while low-level ones either stay silent or get tripped up by over-sharing. Then a debrief mapping each moment — token budget, hidden info, the writing of statements — onto the design problem, and be frank about what the game can't reproduce (like real trauma or long-term political fallout).

Please also say what will predictably go wrong when this runs (someone cracks the optimal strategy too fast, commissioners feel powerless, etc.) and how to handle it. And inject a slightly niche twist: let applicants form coalitions to pool disclosure and share amnesty risk, so they feel the collective-action dimension that individualist amnesty models usually miss. No bibliography or citation list needed — I just want the mechanics and the debrief framing.
