As a novelist working on a manuscript structured through multiple first-person voices that share neither a collective consciousness nor a singular narrator, I am preparing a proposal for a six-month interdisciplinary fellowship pairing creative practice with philosophy of mind. The deliverable must be a research proposal with a precisely stated, genuinely open question. Here is the question I want developed: can the transcendental argument for the necessary existence of other minds—traditionally grounded in the conditions required for a unified subject of self-consciousness—be reconstructed for a distributed, non-unified narrative subject composed of distinct, non-subsumable perspectives, without reducing to empirical group psychology or collapsing into the claim that such a subject is impossible? In the proposal, state this question with exact conditions that would count as answering it; explain why existing treatments either reject transcendental reasoning altogether or defend it only for singular, continuous consciousnesses, leaving this intermediate case unsettled; propose an approach based on conceptual analysis of intersubjective narrative coherence rather than on empirical psychology or neuroscience, and justify why this approach is preferable to the obvious alternatives; describe what a successful result would establish—perhaps that other minds are necessary not for the unity of one consciousness but for the structural coherence of a multi-perspective narrative space—and specify precisely what would count as failure, such as the argument secretly reintroducing singularity or yielding only trivial conclusions; and provide an honest assessment of whether six months is sufficient, identifying the specific conceptual obstacle that could make the project too large. Do not include a bibliography, citation list, or reading list; I will supply references myself. Refer to philosophical positions and arguments generally, without relying on obscure technical terminology that requires specialist knowledge beyond standard philosophy of mind and narrative theory.
