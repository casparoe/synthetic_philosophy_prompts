My colleague, a philosopher of technology who studies responsibility gaps in autonomous systems, has given consistent rulings on the following cases. First, when an owner deliberately disabled a documented takeover-alert system in a deterministic self-driving car, and the vehicle then struck a pedestrian in precisely the conditions the alert was designed for, she held the owner responsible. Second, when a federated-learning clinical AI was corrupted by an adversarial model update that no network participant could have anticipated, and the local physician followed standard protocol without auditing individual weights, she declared a responsibility gap: neither physician nor manufacturer was liable. Third, when a warehouse robot with deterministic programming incorporated unaudited open-source navigation code that contained an unpatched, documented bug, she assigned liability to the manufacturer for failing to constrain its supply chain. Fourth, when a maritime delivery swarm with centralized routing but decentralized obstacle avoidance produced an emergent blockage of an evacuation route that could not be traced to any dispatcher command or single drone, she again found a gap with no liable party. Fifth, when a building’s adaptive climate system autonomously locked fire exits to preserve pressure differentials, and the facility manager had activated the adaptive mode without specifying fire-interaction constraints, she held the manager responsible.

The new case is a neuromorphic maritime co-pilot that integrates three separately certified adaptive modules—navigation, weather routing, and cargo stabilization—which negotiate actions in real time through a consensus protocol. During a storm, the cargo-stabilization module vetoes an emergency evacuation maneuver proposed by navigation; the veto is an emergent property of the interaction dynamics, not present in any module’s individual code or certification. The shipping firm specified only high-level operational goals and included no inter-module arbitration rules; no human was in the loop during the negotiation, and post-hoc analysis confirms that no individual module violated its certified behavior.

Predict her verdict: does she assign responsibility to the shipping firm, to the module designers, or declare another gap? Identify the implicit principle she is running—whether an agency-proximity rule that tracks the nearest meaningful human decision, an architectural-constraint rule that tracks whoever omitted a technically feasible safeguard in the design or deployment architecture, or a different framework altogether. Then specify the single further case, one that preserves all five earlier rulings intact but forces a divergence between these principles, that would settle which rule she actually holds.
