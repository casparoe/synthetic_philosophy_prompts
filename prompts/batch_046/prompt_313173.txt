Compare the standard decision-theoretic response to the Pasadena paradox—treating undefined expected value as a failure of expectation-based rational choice that requires either abandonment of maximization or adoption of a bounded-utility framework—with the less common "directional divergence" interpretation, which proposes that the specific manner in which an expectation fails to converge (for instance, whether partial sums exhibit an asymptotic bias toward positive or negative divergence) can itself serve as a normative guide to preference ordering. Your comparison should address: (1) whether the directional-divergence view genuinely resolves the paradox or merely relocates the arbitrariness to the choice of regularization method; (2) how it relates to analogous treatments of the St. Petersburg paradox, particularly where expectations diverge to positive infinity rather than being undefined in both directions; and (3) whether a rational agent in the Pasadena setting can coherently act on directional information without implicitly committing to a hidden utility scale. Conclude by stating which of the two approaches offers the more defensible account of rational decision under undefined expectation, and identify the single weakest premise in the view you reject.
