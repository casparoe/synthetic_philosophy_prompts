As a retired experimental physicist who has only recently begun serious study of philosophy of physics, I have been working through Einstein's reasoning in the 1913–1914 period concerning what is now called the hole argument. I understand the basic setup as reconstructed in the literature: Einstein considers a region of spacetime entirely free of matter—a "hole"—embedded within a larger universe governed by generally covariant field equations. He then applies a smooth coordinate transformation that is the identity everywhere outside the hole but non-trivial inside it. Because the field equations are generally covariant, this produces a second mathematical solution that agrees with the first everywhere outside the hole but assigns different metric values inside it. Since both solutions correspond to the same distribution of matter outside and the same boundary conditions, Einstein concludes that the equations fail to determine a unique gravitational field inside the hole. From this he infers that the points of spacetime cannot possess independent physical reality apart from the fields defined upon them; otherwise one would have two physically distinct states corresponding to the same material configuration.

What I find genuinely difficult is whether Einstein's inference of physical indeterminism depends on a hidden substantivalist premise about the identity of spacetime points across the two solutions. Specifically, some modern commentators suggest that Einstein was implicitly treating coordinate positions inside the hole as rigid designators for the same physical points in both models, so that the difference in metric values at those points represents a real physical ambiguity. On this reading, the argument only gets its bite if one assumes that points survive the transformation with their identities intact. I want to understand whether this interpretation reads a contemporary structuralist framework back into Einstein's 1913 reasoning, or whether his own texts from that period genuinely presuppose cross-model point-identity. Furthermore, I am unclear about the relation to modern relationalist replies: if a relationalist denies that spacetime points have any identity independent of the fields, does she thereby dissolve the hole argument by undermining its premise, or must she supply an independent reason for treating the two solutions as physically equivalent rather than as genuinely distinct worlds?

Please explain the logical structure of the argument, the role (if any) of point-identity in generating the problem, and how a relationalist interpretation interacts with Einstein's conclusion.
