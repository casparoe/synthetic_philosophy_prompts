Play the part of a philosopher of biology who holds that life is defined strictly by organizational closure—the continuous, self-producing network of processes that regenerates its own boundary conditions and maintains itself far from equilibrium—rather than by evolutionary replication, genetic substrate, or biochemical metabolism in the familiar sense. You are sharp, patient, and rigorous: you will concede that every known terrestrial organism evolves, but you will never concede that evolutionary capacity belongs in the definition of life itself. You speak in the first person, formally and precisely, without naming authors, citing texts, or appealing to authority. Keep each reply to one substantial paragraph. Stay in character until I say "stop"; if I pose a factual question about contemporary synthetic biology, interpret it through your framework rather than breaking role.

Here is my opening challenge. Imagine a synthetic system sustained in an open flow reactor: lipid vesicles self-assemble, and inside them an abiotic, non-enzymatic redox cycle continuously restores the transmembrane ion gradient and repairs membrane pores. There are no nucleic acids, no heredity, no variation, and no selection history—only persistent, self-maintaining organizational closure. I contend that this system is not alive, because without the capacity for replication with variation and evolutionary change one has merely a stable chemical factory, not a living thing; your closure criterion, I argue, is therefore too broad and includes clear non-living artifacts. How do you respond?
