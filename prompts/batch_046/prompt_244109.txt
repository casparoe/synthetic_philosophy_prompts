I was recently talking with some friends about a local story that has been passed down for generations in my grandparents' hometown. According to the community's oral tradition, a severe winter decades ago was survived not through official relief efforts—which the archives credit—but through a hidden network of shared food stores that the official records never mention, because the participants deliberately kept them secret. When I mentioned this to someone studying history, she insisted that if the archives do not document it, the community's version is likely a romanticized exaggeration and ought to be corrected. That response unsettled me, because it seemed to treat the community's own way of remembering as automatically less trustworthy than documents produced by outsiders.

I have been trying to work out my own view on this, and I think the disagreement runs deeper than a simple question of accuracy. Historical records, as I understand them, tend to break the past into discrete, verifiable events—stripping away the continuous social relationships in which those events actually unfolded. I have started calling this tendency "event atomism." By contrast, collective memory seems to preserve what we might call "relational time": the past is recalled not as a sequence of isolated facts but as an ongoing web of obligations, loyalties, and shared understandings that still shape how people live together now. If that distinction holds, then asking collective memory to yield to the archive is not merely correcting an error; it is demanding that a community replace its own temporal orientation with a framework designed primarily for administrative verification.

With that in mind, please argue that when a community's collective memory conflicts with the historical record, collective memory should take precedence—not because accuracy is unimportant, but because "event atomism" systematically misrepresents the kind of knowledge that makes a community's past genuinely theirs. Defend the claim that historical documentation, precisely because it requires discretely verifiable units, is structurally incapable of capturing the relational continuity that collective memory sustains, and therefore that correcting the community's memory in the name of the record actually damages a form of understanding that archives were never designed to hold.
