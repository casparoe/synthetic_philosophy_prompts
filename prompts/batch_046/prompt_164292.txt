Consider the following thesis and outline for a short philosophical essay. Newton's anti-Trinitarianism is often read through Arian or Socinian categories, but it is more precisely understood through a concept of divine dominion: the Father alone holds absolute, unmediated sovereignty, while the Son possesses a real but entirely derivative authority that flows from the Father's will. This theological commitment, I argue, is not isolated from Newton's natural philosophy. In the Queries appended to his Opticks, he presents space not as an empty container but as the direct arena of divine perception and continuous action—what scholars often call the sensorium of God. Taken together, these views form a unified metaphysical picture: a single supreme will acts immediately through an infinite, homogeneous medium, with no ontologically independent secondary causes that operate apart from that will. I then examine whether this picture collapses into occasionalism, since natural regularity would appear to depend entirely on immediate divine intervention mediated through space. To test the coherence of this system, I draw a structural parallel with Newton's extensive manuscript work on biblical prophecy, where he treats texts such as Daniel and Revelation as encrypted descriptions of future political and religious history. The parallel is this: just as natural law expresses uniform divine will operating through spatial extension, prophecy expresses a predetermined divine plan operating through historical time; both reject mediated institutional authority—whether Aristotelian physics or ecclesiastical tradition—in favor of direct access to divine intention.

First, does the connection between Newton's theology of dominion and his spatial metaphysics hold up against his actual texts, or does it improperly conflate domains that he kept separate? Second, is the occasionalism objection a fair characterization, or does Newton's insistence on mathematical law provide an independent, non-divine constraint on physical events? Third, does the analogy with prophecy strengthen the overall interpretation, or does it over-systematize a thinker whose theological and scientific writings may have served different purposes? Fourth, point out any place where the argument relies on selective framing or anachronistic concepts, and suggest a revision that preserves the core insight without that dependence.
