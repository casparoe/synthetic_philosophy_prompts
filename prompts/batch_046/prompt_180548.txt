I'm a software engineer working on formal verification for distributed systems, with no formal background in philosophy or modal logic. I've recently been reading about the Löbian obstacle in the context of AI alignment—the idea that a rational agent satisfying standard derivability conditions cannot consistently believe its own soundness without falling into inconsistency or omniscience. As I understand it, the result relies on a modal logic like GL and a proof predicate that can be internalized.

My own work involves a self-monitoring compiler that accumulates verified invariants across compilation passes and uses them as assumptions for subsequent optimization stages. This looks like a form of recursive self-trust: the system treats its prior verified outputs as authoritative. I initially thought this might trigger a Löbian collapse, but then I realized that my compiler doesn't use a modal belief operator—it just maintains a database of lemmas. More generally, I've seen proposals for probabilistic forecasting agents that maintain calibrated posteriors over logical sentences rather than binary beliefs. If the obstacle requires the specific derivability conditions of Peano Arithmetic or GL—particularly that the system can prove statements about its own provability—then replacing belief with a Bayesian confidence measure, or with an empirical lemma database, should dissolve the problem.

Yet alignment literature seems to treat the Löbian obstacle as if it applies more broadly to any architecture with recursive self-improvement, including empirical or probabilistic ones. Is there a hidden step I'm missing? Specifically, could a system that starts with purely empirical or probabilistic self-modeling eventually reconstruct an internal proof predicate rich enough to satisfy the Hilbert-Bernays-Löb conditions, thereby reintroducing the obstacle from the inside? Or is the widespread concern actually about a different, non-formal failure mode—such as reward hacking or deceptive alignment—that gets misclassified as "Löbian"?

I'd appreciate clarification on whether the obstacle is genuinely confined to formal modal systems, and if so, why it is invoked for architectures that never explicitly represent their own belief operators.
