Write a dialogue between two philosophy grad students, Maya and Jules, sitting in a coffee shop and arguing about the wrong-kind-of-reasons problem, but they keep getting sidetracked onto whether it applies to AI systems that behave well only because thier reward functions penalize harm. Maya should defend the view that acting for the "right" moral reason requires tracking the moral fact itself, not just an instrumental incentive—so if an agent returns a lost wallet only because its loss function spikes otherwise, it's no different from the classic prison-avoidance case, and we shouldn't call it morally worthy. Jules should push back hard: he should say the usual distinction between motivating and normative reasons was built for conscious agents with mental states, and applying it to distributed networks is just anthropocentric gatekeeping; if the causal structure reliably tracks harm and responds to it, complaining about the "kind" of reason is like complaining that a thermostat doesn't "really" understand temperature. Make sure they actually respond to each other—Maya needs to explain why tracking isn't enough if the tracking is mediated by programmer goals rather than moral recognition, and Jules needs to ask whether human moral cognition is really that different from optimized pattern-matching. Don't make either one a strawman. Keep it casual, maybe include a typo or two, and don't ask for citations or a reading list.
