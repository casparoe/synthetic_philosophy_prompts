I'm applying for a four-month independent research fellowship in philosophy of physics. I need a one-page proposal for a conceptual — not empirical — project on Gödel's argument that temporal passage is unreal in rotating universes.

Most existing treatments treat the argument as a failed cosmological inference: either the rotating-universe metric does not match our cosmos, or closed timelike curves do not in fact entail the subjectivity of time. I want to ask whether this misses Gödel's deeper point. The precise question: can Gödel's conclusion be reconstructed as a purely philosophical argument about the conditions for objective temporal order — specifically, whether he implicitly relies on a principle that any genuinely real feature of the world must be statically representable within a complete four-dimensional geometry — and can this principle be evaluated independently of the cosmological model he used to illustrate it?

Why existing answers leave it open: critics have shown that the metric is physically implausible and that the logical step from closed timelike curves to the unreality of time is questionable, but they have rarely examined whether Gödel intended the rotating universe as an illustration of a prior conceptual commitment about rational cosmic order rather than as the sole ground of his conclusion. If the conceptual commitment stands alone, then refuting the cosmological model does not defeat the philosophical argument.

The approach: I will reconstruct the argument in Gödel's published essay on relativity and idealism, comparing its logical structure with the discussions of time, rationality, and completeness found in his unpublished philosophical notebooks. The goal is not archival discovery but conceptual analysis: determining whether there is a coherent version of Gödel's view in which the rotating universe functions only as an existence proof for a static ontology, rather than as a necessary premise. A useful deliverable would be a clear map showing either that the philosophical premise is independent, defensible, and undermines standard A-theoretic accounts of time, or that it collapses into circularity or depends essentially on the cosmological model, in which case the standard critical dismissal is correct. Failure would look like finding that Gödel's text offers no separable philosophical principle — only an empirical speculation dressed in metaphysical language.

Scope: four months. Months one and two for careful reconstruction of the published argument and identification of the implicit premise about geometric completeness. Months three and four for evaluation: testing whether the premise can support the conclusion without the rotating-universe scaffolding, and comparing the result with contemporary B-theory and A-theory positions.

Please write the proposal. It should state the question precisely, explain why current literature doesn't settle it, identify the specific conceptual angle (the separability of a geometric-completeness premise from the cosmological model), describe what success and failure look like, and justify the four-month scope as realistic without requiring original archival access or empirical work.

In your response, clearly separate what is well established — for example, that Gödel proposed rotating universes with closed timelike curves, and that he used them to challenge the objectivity of time — from what is genuinely contested, such as whether his unpublished notebooks contain an independent philosophical argument, whether a principle of geometric completeness is implicit in his reasoning, and whether that principle can be defended without circularity. Do not present a contested interpretation, such as the claim that Gödel's real argument was purely conceptual and independent of physics, as if it were the consensus view.
