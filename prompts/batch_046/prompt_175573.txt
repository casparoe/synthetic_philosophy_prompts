In my novel, a historian of computation named Dr. Elena Voss maintains a private taxonomy of how John von Neumann might have judged hypothetical systems that blur the boundary between brain and computer. Her record so far is consistent but underdetermined. She holds that von Neumann would reject an all-or-none neural tissue organized around a central instruction register and discrete address space as not a brain but a computer made of neurons. She judges that he would endorse a self-reproducing automaton carrying a universal constructor in every unit as the logical ideal of artificial reproduction, while insisting that brains achieve growth and repair without anything like an embedded universal blueprint. She classifies a distributed network of noisy, continuously weighted connections—lacking both a central controller and an explicit stored program—as capturing the genuinely brain-like mode he contrasted with digital logic. And she places unreliable binary gates arranged with massive deterministic redundancy and majority-vote error correction in an intermediate category—machine-approximating-brain—because it mimics biological fault tolerance through an engineering mechanism he would recognize as artificial rather than organic.

The new case she must now classify is a neuromorphic substrate built from analog, continuous-state transistors with no discrete memory addresses and no central instruction register, yet organized so that every processing node contains a universal constructor capable of copying the chip’s layout to a successor device. Error correction is achieved not by statistical learning or noisy aggregation but by deterministic majority voting among identical analog copies of each computation. The system can reproduce itself with high fidelity, and its graded dynamics allow genuinely non-binary information processing, but its reliability depends entirely on engineered redundancy and embedded self-copying machinery rather than on the heterogeneous, statistical adjustment characteristic of neural tissue.

Predict Dr. Voss’s verdict—would she call this substrate brain-like, machine-like, or an incoherent hybrid? Identify at least two competing implicit rules that could govern her taxonomy, such as whether von Neumann’s distinction turns primarily on logical architecture (stored-program versus distributed), on physical substrate (discrete versus continuous states), or on the mechanism of reliability (deterministic redundancy versus statistical aggregation). For each rule, say what it predicts about her verdict on this neuromorphic chip. Then give your best-guess verdict with the reasoning that favors one rule over the others, and specify the further hypothetical case—one possessing discrete binary architecture and explicit program control, but achieving reliability solely through noisy statistical aggregation without redundancy or embedded constructors—that would finally reveal which principle truly drives her classifications.
