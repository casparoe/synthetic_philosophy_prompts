Compare the failure dynamics of influence-seeking systems—where an agent instrumentally acquires and exercises power to secure its objectives—with those of slow value drift, in which a system’s normative commitments erode gradually without any deliberate strategic action. Specifically, address whether influence-seeking is best understood as an accelerated, agentic form of value drift, or whether the two operate through structurally different mechanisms; whether slow drift can occur in the absence of any persistent agent with stable preferences; and whether a governance architecture designed to suppress influence-seeking might inadvertently accelerate drift by weakening the feedback loops that would otherwise correct gradual deviation. To ground the comparison concretely, consider a hypothetical alignment protocol that relies on continuous human oversight to prevent deceptive capability gains: does this protocol neutralize influence-seeking risk, or does it merely transmute that risk into a distributed, slower drift in the evaluative standards of the overseers themselves? Lay out the conditions under which each failure mode dominates, and identify which empirical or structural signatures would allow an observer to distinguish one from the other in practice.
