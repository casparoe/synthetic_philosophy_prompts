Since my mother's recent progression into dementia has forced me to confront the dissolution of her narrative memory, I have found myself returning to Buddhist accounts of anatta—not as an abstract metaphysical denial, but as a lived problem about whether a person survives the erosion of the stories that once held her together. I am drafting a philosophical reflection on this tension, and I want to read Michel Gondry's film Eternal Sunshine of the Spotless Mind (2004), written by Charlie Kaufman, through that specific lens. Rather than treating the film as a simple illustration of no-self, I want to examine it through the Theravada Abhidhamma framework of the five aggregates—particularly sankhara (mental formations) as the fabric of narrative identity—and through the two-truths distinction between the conventional person (puggala) and the ultimately empty substrate. Where does the film's visualization of elective memory erasure align with the claim that what we call a self is merely a conditioned stream of aggregates without an enduring owner? Where does Joel's resistance to the procedure, and the unconscious reconstruction of Clementine within his collapsing mental space, suggest a commitment to narrative continuity that Buddhist analysis would classify as sakkaya-ditthi (personality view)? I am especially interested in the scenes of the breakdown—the beach house dissolving, Joel hiding Clementine inside pre-erased memories, and the ambiguous train-station ending—to ask whether the film treats the persistence of affection across amnesia as evidence of a hidden substrate, or as proof that attachment can reconstruct identical patterns without any self to own them. At the same time, I want an honest critical assessment of where the film resists the reading: its romantic plot arguably requires an essential connection between Joel and Clementine that anatta explicitly denies, and the procedure itself is framed as a technological violation of something personal rather than a removal of illusory overlay. Can you help me map which scenes genuinely illuminate the aggregate analysis, which depend on Western essentialist intuitions about love and identity, and where the analogy between memory erasure and the Buddhist dissolution of self breaks down? I am not asking for a bibliography, citation list, or reading recommendations—only a careful, scene-by-scene interpretation that treats both the philosophy and the film with equal weight, and that tells me explicitly where the philosophical reading has to concede that the work is doing something else.
