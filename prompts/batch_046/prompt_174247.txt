Design a 90-minute, upper-level philosophy-of-mind exam essay on the causal exclusion argument and mental causation. The deliverable should contain four clearly titled sections, in this order:

1. The Exam Question (approximately 250–350 words): State the prompt that students will answer, including time and length constraints (about 1,200 words). The question must be unanswerable by merely summarizing Kim’s standard argument; it should force students to engage with a specific, technically precise variant. Frame the issue around Kim’s exclusion principle applied within a determinable/determinate ontology of mental properties: ask students to reconstruct the argument (closure, exclusion, supervenience), then explain how the determinable/determinate response attempts to dissolve causal competition between mental property M and physical property P*, then evaluate whether that response survives a reformulated exclusion argument that targets determinables rather than determinates, and finally ask whether saving mental causation this way forces the non-reductive physicalist to weaken causal closure.

2. Model Answer (approximately 800–1,000 words): Provide the essay a top student should write, showing the required conceptual moves—precise reconstruction of the argument’s premises, accurate characterization of the determinable/determinate relation, identification of the reformulation problem, and a clear verdict on whether mental causation is preserved or sacrificed.

3. Marking Scheme (approximately 350–450 words): Break the score into five must-hit points with specific marks (total 25), and for each point include a one-line note on how candidates typically miss it. The scheme should distinguish an A answer (hits all five with precision) from a B answer (accurate summary but misses the determinable-level reformulation).

4. Common Misunderstandings to Test (approximately 150–200 words): List three distractor-level errors that a strong exam should filter out—e.g., conflating the determinable/determinate relation with token-identity, or treating causal closure as a contingent empirical claim rather than a metaphysical principle—without asking for bibliographies, reading lists, or citations.
