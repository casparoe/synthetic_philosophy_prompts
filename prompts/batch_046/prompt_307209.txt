I just finished a long, circular argument with a friend about whether the Gould-Lewontin spandrels critique still has teeth against modern adaptationism. To press his case, he leaned on an analogy I have heard in looser forms but that he sharpened into something new. He said evolutionary traits are like modules in a legacy codebase: deliberately written functions are adaptations, while leftover dead code is the spandrel. He then introduced a distinction of his own—first-order spandrels, which are pure leftover with no present use, versus second-order spandrels, which have been repurposed so thoroughly that they now constrain future updates, like a deprecated library that the rest of the system depends on. From this he argued that Gould and Lewontin mistake a temporal stage for a permanent category: in any mature evolutionary system, dead-code spandrels are either deleted or become load-bearing, so we should stop asking about original function and study only current utility. In other words, the adaptation-versus-spandrel distinction collapses into a single question of present engineering value.

Audit this analogy for me. Give an explicit correspondence table—what plays the role of the programmer, the intentional module, the dead code, the deprecated-but-load-bearing library, and the update cycle—then lay out where the mapping fractures, especially where it smuggles in design intentionality that natural selection lacks. Separate the disanalogies that actually threaten his conclusion (that historical origin is unreadable and therefore irrelevant) from the cosmetic ones. Tell me whether his conclusion survives once the breaks are on the table, or whether the analogy was carrying the argument all along. If there is a more precise analogy for the relationship between original byproduct and later functional constraint, suggest it; if the codebase image is irredeemable, say so. Keep the response under 350 words, and lead with the bottom line: does the spandrel-adaptation distinction collapse?
