I am writing to seek your philosophical judgment on a dispute that has arisen among the curators of a small, highly specialized archive we maintain—an archive restricted exclusively to texts that deploy explicit formal apparatus, governed by a precise three-part taxonomy for acquisitions. Under our charter, a work is 'foundational' if it establishes an original formal system; 'extensional' if it applies an existing system to a new domain without altering its primitives; and 'reconstructive' if it rebuilds an argument using only conceptual resources available prior to 1950. For years this classification seemed exhaustive and sharply bounded.

Last month we received a manuscript that takes the complete syntax and semantics of modal logic—entirely unaltered—and applies them, for the first time, to the evaluation of aesthetic judgments concerning musical harmony. One colleague insists the work is straightforwardly extensional: the formal machinery is copied intact, only the object of evaluation changes. Another argues it is reconstructive, because projecting modal operators onto aesthetic properties forces a fundamental rebuilding of what counts as a 'possible world' within that domain. A third proposes that neither label fits, suggesting that when a conceptual structure is copied from its original microdomain into a sufficiently alien one, its constituent terms undergo an analogical slippage—similar to how a pattern-matching system might preserve relational form while allowing the entities related to shift in meaning—thereby distorting the very taxonomy we are trying to apply.

My question is not practical—whether to catalog the manuscript under A or B—but conceptual. Does this disagreement reveal a genuine philosophical difficulty about the portability of formal categories across microdomains, specifically the error of assuming that a taxonomy designed for one tightly bounded conceptual neighborhood can be transferred without distortion to another? Or are we simply failing to apply our existing rules correctly? I would particularly value an analysis of whether the boundary between 'extensional' and 'reconstructive' was always more malleable than our charter pretended, and what that implies for treating analogical copies of formal systems in unfamiliar territories.
