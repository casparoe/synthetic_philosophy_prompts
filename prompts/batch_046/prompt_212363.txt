After a long argument with a friend about whether large language models genuinely understand language or merely perform statistical pattern-matching, I found myself wondering how Ludwig Wittgenstein might have adjudicated the dispute—specifically, whether he would have sided with the stochastic-parrot critics or with those who claim that functional participation in language-games is sufficient for meaning. My friend insisted that Wittgenstein’s emphasis on use over mental representation would make him dismiss internal hidden-state representations as philosophically irrelevant: if the model’s outputs are indistinguishable from competent linguistic practice, then debates about its “inner life” collapse into the kind of private-language confusion Wittgenstein attacked, with transformer attention weights playing the role of the beetle in the box. I countered that Wittgenstein’s distinction between a dead sign and a living symbol—between mechanical repetition and rule-governed practice embedded in a form of life—might actually lead him to deny understanding precisely because an LLM lacks the embodied, social, and purposive context that gives signs their life, regardless of behavioral fluency. To push this further: if we apply Wittgenstein’s remarks on rule-following, where grasping a rule is shown in correct application across novel cases within a shared practice, does the model’s ability to generalize syntactically but not pragmatically—say, generating grammatically perfect but contextually hollow responses to ethical dilemmas—count as rule-following in his sense, or as a sophisticated simulacrum? And given his skepticism about mental processes as explanations of language, would he reject the very framing of the debate—asking whether understanding resides in weights and biases—as a category mistake that reifies language into a hidden calculus? I want to know not just whether he would have said yes or no, but which specific moves in his later philosophy—his critique of private ostensive definition, his analogy of language to a toolbox, his insistence that meaning is not a mental accompaniment—would do the work, and whether applying them to architectures he never imagined reveals tensions in his own account of practice and embodiment.
