I have recently been tasked with assessing whether my organization’s governance and decision-making protocols are genuinely constrained by historical accident—locked into suboptimal forms—or whether functionally equivalent arrangements would have converged regardless of the path taken. To prepare for a colloquium Q&A, I want to interview a scholar who defends the convergence thesis: that in social institutions weak path dependence (history shapes the route) is common, but strong path dependence (history permanently fixes inefficient outcomes) is rare and usually misdiagnoses temporary inertia as permanent determinism.

Draft eight to ten interview questions that move well beyond asking the guest to restate the thesis. I want at least one question on how the view distinguishes technological network effects, where lock-in is well documented, from normative or procedural institutions, where functional pressure might be stronger; at least one on the evidentiary challenge—since we cannot rerun history, what observable patterns in present institutional variation or comparative cross-national development could genuinely support or undermine the convergence claim; and at least one on whether recognizing convergence undermines the normative urgency of reform efforts that invoke path dependence as a reason for radical restructuring. For each of these three, include a follow-up keyed to the answer the defender is most likely to give—for instance, that comparative analysis supplies the necessary counterfactual leverage, or that reform urgency should target transition costs rather than endpoint design.

Please separate what is settled about path dependence and institutional convergence from what remains genuinely contested among specialists, and do not present the convergence thesis, or its rival, as the consensus view.
