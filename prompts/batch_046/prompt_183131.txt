I spent about thirty years in condensed-matter physics before I retired, and lately I've been trying to learn philosphy from scratch — mostly philosophy of science and history — but I'm still bad at framing questions, so bear with me. The other day a friend who works at a geopolitical-risk consultancy told me about their internal forecasting protocol, which sounds like a hardcore version of refernce-class forecasting applied to states. When they need to predict whether a current country will fragment, they don't start with local news or expert narratives. Instead they drop the case into a historical cluster — something like 'continental multi-ethnic empire with extractive bureaucracy' — and pull a base-rate dissolution frequency from every past instance in that cluster (Roman provincial, Austro-Hungarian, Soviet federal, Qing tributary, etc.). That number becomes the official forecast. Inside-view analysts can object, but only if they can identify a feature that genuinely excludes the case from the cluster; otherwise the base rate stands.

This feels like it carries a hidden metaphysics, not just statistics. Doesn't the practice presuppose that these historical entities are exchangable members of a natural kind, so that being a Qing-type empire makes you metaphysically substitutable with other Qing-types in the sample? Or can a purely epistemic story defend it — we just lack the knowledge to tell them apart? There's also a weird tension: the same consultancy runs qualitative case studies that treat each regime as culturally singular and incomparable. So what view of historical explanation does the forecasting arm strictly need? Is it genuinely incompatible with uniqueness, or just more comfortable with essentialism? And then there's the scoring rule: if collapse happens for a reason never seen in the cluster, the forecaster isn't penalized; if it happens for a cluster-typical reason they ignored, they are. That sounds like they're drawing a modal boundary around the reference class — as if there's a fact about which counterfactual histories belong in the sample space. What commitments about possibility, single-case probability, and institutional blame does that rule presuppose?

If we replaced the whole framework with the idea that every political formation is sui generis and base rates are just narrative patterns with no predictive force, what exactly in their workflow would have to change? Not just the math, but the role of the analyst, the meaning of being wrong, and the very idea of an outside view? Could you reconstruct the implicit philosophical theory this practice needs, distinguish what's strictly required from what's just window dressing, and say where it's philosophically underdetermined?
