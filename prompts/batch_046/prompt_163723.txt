Before assessing any proposed resolution to the Frege-Geach problem for moral expressivism, specify the full set of desiderata that an adequate theory must satisfy. The target is a resolution that preserves the core expressivist claim—that sincere moral judgments express non-cognitive practical attitudes rather than representational beliefs—while accounting for the compositional behavior of moral vocabulary in embedded contexts, including conditionals, negations, disjunctions, and attitude reports.

List the requirements with the specific case or philosophical consideration behind each: what must be explained about the unasserted moral clause in a conditional such as "If stealing is wrong, then encouraging theft is wrong"; about negation and the denial of moral content; about logical and inferential relations among moral sentences that appear to hold independently of whether anyone is asserting them; about the contribution of embedded moral terms to the determinacy of the larger sentences in which they occur; and about the preservation of practical disagreement in embedded versus asserted positions. Distinguish desiderata that protect the original motivation for expressivism from those that protect general semantic compositionality, and flag any pair you take to be genuinely in tension—particularly whether maintaining pure non-cognitivism in every syntactic position can be reconciled with explaining why embedded moral sentences make stable, determinable contributions to the wholes they help compose.

Specify also what distinctions the theory must not collapse: between expressing one's own attitude and attributing an attitude to another; between moral disagreement that carries practical weight and mere divergence in verbal behavior; and between the semantic content of a moral sentence and its pragmatic expressive force. State which single desideratum, in your judgment, eliminates the largest class of existing candidates.

Finally, briefly score the principal strategies—Geach's direct-expression approach, Blackburn's quasi-realist program, and Gibbard's norm-expressivism—against your list, keeping the weight on the criteria, their motivating cases, and any suspected joint unsatisfiability rather than on the evaluation itself.
