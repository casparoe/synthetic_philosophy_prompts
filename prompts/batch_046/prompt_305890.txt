Analyze the 1940 controversy over Bertrand Russell's appointment to the faculty of City College of New York — in which a court blocked the appointment on the grounds that Russell's published views on marriage and sexuality made him morally unfit to teach — through the lens of Russell's own framework in Marriage and Morals (1929). Do not recount the standard biographical narrative; instead, map the episode precisely onto his theoretical machinery: his distinction between conventional morality (which he treats as an instrument of economic and social control) and a rational ethics of sexuality governed by harm, his claim that institutional restrictions on sexual freedom rarely serve welfare, and his view that public outrage at nonconformity is typically a defense of authority rather than a response to actual injury. Identify which actors in the controversy — the court, the plaintiff, the university administration, Russell himself — fit which roles in his framework, and say what the framework predicts each should have done. Then report where the mapping breaks: does Russell's theory have any resources to distinguish a legitimate concern about a teacher's institutional and pedagogical influence over students from the kind of moral panic he diagnoses? Or does the episode reveal that his framework collapses all opposition to sexual nonconformity into conventional prejudice, thereby missing something structurally important about how institutions transmit values? Finally, assess whether the case confirms his thesis that conventional morality operates as a mechanism of social control, or whether it functions as an anomaly — a backlash that his harm-based, individualist ethics cannot adequately explain — and what that implies for the scope of his moral framework.
