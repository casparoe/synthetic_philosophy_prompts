I've been reading around the philosophy of objective probability for a course essay, and I've become stuck on a conceptual tension I can't quite articulate. Specifically, I'm trying to understand the Principal Principle — the idea that if you know the objective chance of some event is x, and you have no other relevant information, then your rational credence in that event should also be x. This seems straightforward in textbook cases like fair coins, but I'm troubled by what happens when the 'objective chance' is itself mediated by a theoretical model you only partly endorse. For instance, suppose a climate model assigns an objective chance of 0.7 to a particular extreme weather event occurring within a decade, but you think the model's parameterisation of cloud feedback is systematically optimistic. Do you defer to the model's output as the objective chance and update your credence to 0.7, or do you first adjust the model's credibility and then apply the Principle to the adjusted figure? And if the latter, haven't you effectively used your prior credence about the model to determine the chance, which then determines your posterior credence — creating a kind of circularity rather than genuine deference?

Could you explain the conceptual structure of this problem with some precision? I'd like you to distinguish between deference to a chance as reported by an authoritative source and deference to a chance that is itself the output of a reasoning process you are evaluating. In particular, please clarify whether the Principal Principle is meant to govern your attitude towards the event directly, or whether it governs your attitude towards the proposition that the chance is x, and how these two readings differ in cases where your epistemic access to the chance is mediated by contested science. I want to understand whether there is a consistent way to apply the Principle without either abandoning rational autonomy or collapsing into circular reasoning, and whether the distinction between 'objective chance' and 'credence about objective chance' can bear the weight that philosophers expect it to. I'm not looking for a survey of the literature, but for a clear, intuitive map of where the deference is supposed to happen and where it breaks down.
