Construct a thought experiment in which an institutional speech act—specifically, a performative appointment—fails Austin's felicity conditions but satisfies Searle's constitutive rules, with the divergence driven by exactly one engineered feature rather than by generic disagreement about authority. Constraints: the appointing body must be a decentralized governance protocol with no single chair, in which authority is distributed across nodes; the utterance must be executed through a natural-language mediator that paraphrases the speaker's input before broadcasting it; and the audience must receive only the mediated version while retaining access to the original transcript. The scenario must be realistic (no exotic physics or unknown legal doctrines), describable in one paragraph, and must not invoke specialist literature. After presenting the scenario, show precisely which Austinian felicity condition is violated and which Searlean constitutive rule is satisfied. Identify the structural feature that produces the split—whether the distributed location of authority, the mediation's transformation of intent, or the audience's dual epistemic access—and explain whether removing that feature collapses the disagreement. Finally, state how an Austinian defender would attack the case and whether that attack also undermines Searle's positive verdict.
