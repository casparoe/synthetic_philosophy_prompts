The exam question was: in Jaspers' Die Schuldfrage (1946) he sorts guilt into criminal, political, moral, and metaphysical. Explain each category, then apply them to whether a "nation" can be guilty across generations — specifically citizens born after the authoritarian regime collapsed, living now in a democratic successor state that retains the old borders, currency, and treaties. The student had to address whether political guilt can be inherited and whether metaphysical guilt applies collectively.

Here's the answer (I fixed one spelling mistake but left everything else intact):

Criminal guilt is the easiest: it requires an identifiable illegal act, is judged in court, and dies with the perpetrator or is served in prison. Jaspers is clear that you can't prosecute someone born in 1960 for a 1942 deportation.

Political guilt is different. Every citizen belongs to the state, so they share in what the state does. Jaspers says this is judged politically — by the victors, or later by history. Because citizenship is inherited by birth in a continuous polity, political guilt is also inherited intact. A citizen born in 1960 bears exactly the same political guilt as a bureaucrat in 1940, since both are members of the same institutional body, even though the younger one never voted for the regime. The successor state keeps the treaties and the currency, so the membership never broke.

Moral guilt is private. You answer to your own conscience for what you did or failed to do. It can't be assigned from outside, and it doesn't travel across generations because conscience requires a personal act of omission or commission.

Metaphysical guilt is the deepest layer. Jaspers describes it as solidarity with all human beings — feeling guilty that you live while others died, or that you belong to a species that could do this. Because it rests on shared humanity rather than citizenship, it is actually the only form that genuinely applies collectively across generations. Descendants inherit it collectively, as members of the human community that failed to stop the crime, and it binds them together in a kind of inherited metaphysical stain.

So to answer the question: yes, the nation remains guilty, but only in the political and metaphysical registers. Criminal guilt is dead, moral guilt is individual and present-tense, but political and metaphysical guilt survive regime change because both depend on membership — in the state or in humanity — rather than on personal action.
