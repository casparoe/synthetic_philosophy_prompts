I need a short research proposal for a three-month summer grant, about one page. My idea is half-formed and probably a bit weird but I want to see if it holds up. The usual story is that akrasia and hyperbolic discounting are basically the same mess: your present self undervalues future rewards and then screws over your later plans. But I'm interested in a narrower case—people who have already set up precommitment devices or internal rules to fix the discounting problem, and they still fail in a way that doesn't feel like ordinary weakness of will. Can we understand these failures as a kind of intertemporal betrayal, where the present self isn't just miscalculating payoffs but is treating the future self's plan as an illegitimate constraint, like breaking a promise to a seperate agent? The literature either collapses this back into standard hyperbolic models or treats it as a moral failure about willpower, but neither captures why the failure feels structurally different—more like violating a contract you made with yourself. So here's the precise question: is there a distinct form of akrasia that shows up specifically when an agent has adopted a multi-self framework but refuses to honor the contractual obligations she previously established with her own future stages? I want to defend that there is, and that fixing it requires something like intertemporal fidelity rather than just prefference integration. For the proposal, lay out: the question stated exactly so it can be answered yes or no, why the Davidson-style and economic accounts leave it open, the method—probably conceptual analysis of first-person case reports plus a formal sketch of the contract-breach structure—what a positive result looks like and what a failure result looks like, and an honest assessment of whether this is too big for three months. Write it all as continuous prose, no headings or bullet points or bold, like an email. Don't include any bibliography or citation list, I'll handle references myself.
