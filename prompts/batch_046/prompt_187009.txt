At a faculty seminar on institutional decision-making, two colleagues argued about a university's refusal to digitize and sell its climate-controlled rare-books wing despite chronic budget pressure. Marcus said this is a textbook case of costly signaling under the handicap principle: only an institution genuinely wealthy in prestige can afford to burn resources on a non-functional asset, and the waste is precisely what makes the signal honest. Elena replied that prestige can be signaled far more cheaply through branding, so the behavior is better explained by a non-instrumental commitment to physical preservation for its own sake. Marcus countered that digitizing destroys the very asset being displayed, and that a cheap substitute cannot carry the same weight because an honest handicap must be genuinely ruinous to fake. Elena objected that costly signaling requires an audience capable of decoding the cost, yet the library's operating budget is invisible to students, donors, and peer institutions, which means there is waste without signal. Marcus answered that accreditation bodies and rival universities do observe these commitments, and that bureaucratic opacity actually strengthens the signal by restricting its audience to precisely the insiders whose opinion matters. Please adjudicate in exactly five numbered points, each one or two sentences long, with no introduction or conclusion, assigning one point to each move: say who is closer to right in that exchange, identify the core dispute over whether opacity undermines or strengthens signaling, and in your final point note what both miss about applying an individual-level evolutionary framework to collective institutional actors.
