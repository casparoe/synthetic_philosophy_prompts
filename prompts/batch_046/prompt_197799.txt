As a novelist developing a long-form narrative in which a protagonist undergoes a gradual surgical separation of the cerebral hemispheres followed by a divergence of autobiographical memory, I have been examining Daniel Dennett's brain-transplant thought experiment—commonly referred to as Where Am I—to understand whether a divided consciousness can still sustain a single narrative point of view. Rather than requesting a survey of the personal-identity literature, I wish to know which open questions and unresolved lines of inquiry in this specific domain genuinely matter for constructing a psychologically plausible interior monologue, and which are largely ornamental for a writer concerned with believability rather than metaphysical certainty.

Specifically, over the course of approximately one year of part-time study, I must choose where to direct my attention: should I prioritise the debate between psychological-continuity accounts and narrative-identity theories; recent philosophical work on indexical self-reference and its conditions of failure in divided minds; or the challenge that embodied-cognition research poses to Dennett's original intuition that the self travels with the brain? Please defend a ranking of two or three priorities, using criteria appropriate to a novelist's practical constraints—namely, tractability within a year, direct relevance to rendering a character's sense of memory ownership, and whether resolving the issue would materially alter how the protagonist experiences being one person rather than two. I also want to know what kind of empirical or conceptual finding would cause you to reorder these priorities.

I am particularly interested in whether there remains a substantive disagreement about the location of the self, or whether the dispute largely dissolves into a verbal disagreement about the use of the first-person pronoun; and if the latter, I want to know what substantive residue, if any, survives that dissolution and still requires a narrative decision on my part.
