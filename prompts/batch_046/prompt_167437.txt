I'm writing a feature for a science-and-ideas magazine on the unexpected revival of Moritz Schlick's 1927 essay On the Meaning of Life among researchers studying intrinsic motivation. Schlick's central claim—as I understand it—is that life is meaningful only to the extent that we transform "work," or activity valued for its future results, into "play," or activity valued for its present execution; the child at play, he suggests, lives more meaningfully than the adult laboring for tomorrow. Recently, in conversations with behavioral economists, I've formulated what I call the "recursive instrumentalization" problem: if hyperbolic discounting causes us to constantly reframe present enjoyment as a down-payment on future well-being, then any sustained attempt to live "in the present" immediately collapses into strategic self-management. This leads me to a question I cannot resolve: if Schlick's criterion for a meaningful life is that our activities remain genuinely non-instrumental, and if modern psychology shows that reward systems inevitably instrumentalize sustained pleasure, hasn't Schlick effectively defined meaningful life out of psychological existence? Or does this objection depend on a false presupposition—specifically, that Schlick intends his distinction between play and work to function as an empirical prediction about mental encoding rather than as a normative orientation toward one's own activities, one that can be adopted even when the brain treats the activity as a means?
