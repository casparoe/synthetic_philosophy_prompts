Design a runnable 90-minute seminar activity for ten graduate students in philosophy of mind that makes them experience the conceptual pressure of Hofstadter’s claim that an “I” can be distributed across brains rather than anchored to one substrate. Invent a specific interaction protocol—call it the Consensus-First-Person Protocol—where pairs sit at separate physical stations and must co-author a continuous first-person narrative of a staged moral scenario delivered via shared audio feed. The rules should require that every use of the pronoun “I” be ratified by both stations within a three-second window; if ratification fails, the statement is struck and rewritten in the third person, with the disagreement logged publicly. State clearly what the activity successfully reproduces (the social and linguistic friction of sustaining a singular self-reference across two bodies) and what it necessarily cannot reproduce (the actual phenomenological continuity or strange-loop merging that Hofstadter describes in thought experiments about copied minds). Include an honest prediction of which failure mode is most likely in this group size and how the facilitator should treat that collapse as philosophical data rather than procedural error.
