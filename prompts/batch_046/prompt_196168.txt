Question: Bradley’s regress is usually read as showing that relations can’t be real entities, since linking R to term a requires a further relation R', and so on ad infinitum. First, set out the regress clearly. Then ask whether an adverbial analysis — treating 'aRb' as a being R-modified and b being R-modified — dissolves it, or just relocates the problem inside the terms. How does the adverbial view handle negative propositions like 'a is not R to b' without reintroducing a relating relation? Finally, does Russell’s doctrine of external relations answer Bradley, and does it also solve the problem of propositional unity?

Student answer:

Bradley’s regress begins once we treat R as a real entity that connects a and b. Then we need R1 to link R with a, R2 for R1, etc., so relations must be unreal. The adverbial theory avoids this by removing R altogether: a is modified R-adverbially and b is modified R-adverbially, with no separate linking thing. The chain stops.

But Bradley also claimed judgement is a non-relational unity. Consider 'the apple is red and round'. On the adverbial view, the apple is red-modified and round-modified, but we still need an account of what holds these two modifications together in one subject. So the unity problem hasn’t disappeared; it’s relocated inside the subject.

Russell’s external relations kill the regress, because relations are primitive and don’t need to be tied to terms by further relations. They also solve propositional unity: since relations hold externally, a proposition is just a and b arranged by R, with no extra glue needed. So Russell deals with both Bradley’s regress and unity in one move.

The only gap is that Russell never explains how a single mind grasps the whole proposition. Bradley’s account of judgement as a mental unity is therefore stronger. Hence Bradley’s regress argument is mistaken, but his view of judgement may still be correct.
