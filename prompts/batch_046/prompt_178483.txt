I am writing a philosophical essay on evolutionary debunking arguments and whether they apply reflexively to the theorist's own moral and religious commitments. I keep returning to Charles Darwin's personal crisis following the death of his young daughter Annie, and I want to analyse this episode not as mere biography but as a test case for Darwin's own account of morality — the view, developed in his later writings on human evolution, that moral sentiments originate in social instincts gradually refined by reason and communal habit, with no necessary theological foundation. The episode seems to present a lived conflict: Darwin's social and parental instincts, together with the moral expectations of his religious circle, pulled toward the consolation of an afterlife and the justice of divine providence; yet his empirical reasoning, already shaped by natural selection, rejected those explanations as unsupported. I want you to treat this as a structured case study. Map the actors and options as Darwin faced them — his instinctive moral attachments versus his rational assessment of theistic explanations — and analyse whether his eventual settlement into agnosticism while retaining a strong moral life confirms that morality survives evolutionary genealogy intact, or whether his persistent grief, his wife's religious distress, and his own reluctance to discuss the matter in public reveal that the debunking argument was never designed to account for first-person religious loss. Crucially, I also want you to identify where Darwin's evolutionary framework remains silent — for example, on whether Annie's suffering was genuinely gratuitous or whether moral progress can be measured across generations — and say whether that silence weakens the theory or protects it from overreach. Please lead with your bottom line in the very first sentence, then provide the reasoning with no introductory restatement of the question and no concluding summary. Use British English throughout.
