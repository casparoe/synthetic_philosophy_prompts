I am preparing a long-form feature on strategic precommitment—what researchers in game theory and AI alignment call commitment races—and I keep returning to a conceptual tension that standard treatments tend to smooth over. Most accounts frame the race as a symmetrical escalation: each party locks in an increasingly costly signal to outbind a rival, and stability arrives when both commitments are transparent and irreversible. But the framework seems to fracture when the commitment mechanism itself is only partially observable—an arrangement I have been calling an opaque-lock race, in which one agent can verify the structural rigidity of another’s lock only through second-order inference rather than direct inspection. In that setting, the strategic logic may shift away from binding power altogether and toward the manufacturing of credible illusions of binding, raising a distinct conceptual problem about whether the race is still governed by commitment logic or has silently collapsed into something closer to deceptive signaling.

Could you provide a concrete, self-contained example of a paradox or conceptual breakdown that arises specifically within this opaque-lock variant? In particular, I would like you to show how the asymmetry between an observable cost of commitment and an unobservable structural guarantee creates a strategic instability that is genuinely different from both standard commitment races and ordinary signaling games, and to explain briefly why the usual philosophical distinction between a credible threat and a credible commitment collapses when verification is necessarily indirect. Please treat the idea seriously and avoid reducing the scenario to a mere illustration; I want the example to stand on its own conceptual feet.
