Write the opening remarks for the dedication ceremony of a new university museum wing titled “The Civic Mind in Clay: Reconstructing Law and Ritual from Fragmentary Evidence.” The audience is divided: on one side sit archaeological epistemologists, legal historians, and museum curators who have spent years debating whether the displayed artifacts—scattered seal impressions, broken tablets with partial formulae, and the foundation traces of a large rectangular building—justify the gallery’s central claim that they come from an early administrative court; on the other side sit university trustees, city officials, and major donors who funded the wing and expect a celebratory affirmation that we have “recovered” an ancient legal institution. The philosophical problem of inference from objects to beliefs and institutions must do structural work in the speech: you need to explain, without condescending to either group, why reconstructing a “court” from these materials is not a simple discovery but an underdetermined interpretive act—one in which the same clay fragments can support incompatible institutional narratives—and yet you must still justify the gallery’s existence and the value of displaying the reconstruction. You should not ask the audience to suspend belief, but you should make clear exactly what kind of epistemic claim the exhibition makes. Include one sustained analogy—accessible to non-specialists but precise enough that experts will recognize the underlying philosophical point about underdetermination—that illustrates how material traces alone cannot fix the institutional meaning we assign them. The remarks should run about seven minutes, maintain a tone of intellectual celebration rather than apology, and avoid any request for further reading or citation.
