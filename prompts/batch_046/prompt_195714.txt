I've mostly bought into moral particularism lately -- the idea that there's no genuinely useful layer of moral principles, that rules are mostly stories we tell after the fact, and that getting a hard case right means seeing the whole shape of the situation in a kind of gestalt way rather than applying anything general. What would actually count against this view? I don't mean the usual complaints that particularism makes moral disagreement impossible or that it collapses into arbitrary intuitionism; I want the defeaters that particularists themselves would concede are real problems. For instance, what if moral psychology turned up strong evidence that people actually reason through explicit, compositional rules even in complex cases, or that cross-cultural convergence on specific judgments tracks shared principles rather than shared salience patterns? Or what about formal results showing practical reasoning has to be modular or rule-based to avoid combinatorial explosion? Rank these from 'this kills particularism' to 'this is just awkward but absorbable.' Also be honest: has the view been qualified into unfalsifiability -- like saying principles exist but are useless, or that salience is real but ineffable -- and is that insulation actually a problem for a theory that's supposed to describe how moral thinking actually works? Write it all in continuous prose, no headings or bullet points or bold stuff, just like you'd write an email. One more thing: I'm especialy curious whether particularism can survive if there's a robust account of moral education that depends on learning explicit norms first, since that sounds like generalism sneaking in through the back door.
