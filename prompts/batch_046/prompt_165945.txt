Write the opening remarks—about eight minutes, delivered standing—for an interdisciplinary workshop called “Progress Without Replacement,” convened by a philosophy department together with historians of science and literature scholars. The audience is split: half are professional philosophers who will recognize every allusion, half are graduate students and faculty from other fields who have never taken a philosophy course past logic 101. The conveners have asked you not to deliver a survey of the literature, but to frame the workshop’s actual tension: we have gathered because we think philosophical work can move forward, yet the field’s most visible feature is that its oldest disagreements are still live, still taught, still taken seriously as options rather than as historical curiosities.

Your remarks should make this tension do real structural work, not decorative work. Specifically, structure the talk around a distinction you are introducing for the occasion—call it “dialectical sedimentation” versus “cumulative replacement”—where philosophy does not retire its failed theories to an archive but keeps them in active circulation, so that progress looks less like a line and more like a thickening landscape in which old paths remain walkable. This idea should determine what you can and cannot say: you must welcome the non-philosophers without implying that philosophy is just another empirical discipline that gets closer to the truth every decade; you must honor the philosophers present without conceding that their disagreements are merely subjective; and you must account for why we are holding this workshop at all—what “moving forward” could mean in a field where Plato and a paper from last year can both be live interlocutors.

Make the speech concrete: open with one specific, slightly odd institutional detail—say, the fact that this department still requires every doctoral student to defend a position drawn from a pre-modern text as part of their qualifying exam, not for historical literacy but because those positions are treated as present competitors. Use that detail to ground the sedimentation idea. Then navigate the mixed audience by translating the philosophical stakes into a question about institutional design: what kind of progress justifies a workshop like this one, and what kind of progress would make this workshop absurd? Close with a single, precise invitation to the participants that depends on the sedimentation framing—do not end with generic enthusiasm for interdisciplinary exchange, but with a specific charge that only makes sense if philosophy’s progress is non-cumulative. Avoid citing authors by name unless necessary for recognition, do not provide a reading list, and do not quote any text verbatim.
