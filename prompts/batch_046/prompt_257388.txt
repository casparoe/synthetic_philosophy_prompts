After I recently had to evaluate two ambiguous compensation structures in a consulting contract—one offering a fixed fee and another with an unspecified multiplier that might double or halve my payment depending on an undisclosed client rule—I found myself revisiting the two-envelope paradox with a practical urgency I had not felt since graduate school. Write an essay on whether a rational agent should switch envelopes once the amount inside is observed. Weigh the standard expected-utility argument for switching, the objections that turn on improper priors over the envelope values and on the misapplication of conditional expectation, and specifically address whether causal decision theory dissolves the paradox in a way that evidential decision theory cannot. Reach and justify a definite conclusion—whether one should switch, stay, or reject the framing altogether—while clearly separating what is mathematically settled (e.g., the structure of the conditional expectation and the role of prior distributions) from what is genuinely contested among philosophers of decision theory, and avoid presenting any contested resolution as the established consensus.
