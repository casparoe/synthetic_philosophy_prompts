I'm in this upper-level ethics seminar and last week we got into Williams's integrity objection — specifically the one thought too many thing, where the utilitarian husband saves his wife only after running some mental calculus instead of just saving her because she's his wife. Me and my roomate kept arguing about it in the dining hall and I think I accidentally built a chain that proves integrity and morality can't coexist, which definatly can't be right. Here's how it goes:

(1) For Williams, integrity means your actions flow straight from your ground projects without an alienating intermediate step where you weigh them against impartial moral demands. (2) If that's correct, then whenever you stop to ask "is this project actually morally okay?" you've inserted exactly that alienating step between you and your commitment. (3) So asking for moral justification is itself having one thought too many — you're treating your deepest commitment as something that needs external validation. (4) Therefore anyone with real integrity can never legitimately check whether their projects violate basic moral constraints; they just pursue them blindly. (5) But that'd license literally anyone — a fascist, a cult leader, whatever — to claim perfect integrity while doing horrible things, and we obviously want to say they're wrong, not just alienated. (6) So either premise (1) misdescribes integrity, or we have to accept that integrity and moral reflection are fundamentally incompatible.

I keep feeling like step (3) is where the slip is, maybe an equivocation between weighing your wife against strangers in a rescue case versus checking whether your whole life-project crosses a moral line. But if I reject (3) I need some distinction between bad alienating calculation and okay moral self-checking, and I'm not sure Williams can draw that line without collapsing back into utilitarian-style weighing. I'd rather keep (1) and (5) if I can. Which step should I actually reject, and what does giving it up cost me? Does fixing it require saying integrity allows some kinds of reflection but not others, and if so, how do we tell them apart without smuggling in the impartial calculus Williams wanted to avoid?
