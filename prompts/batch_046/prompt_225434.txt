I'm moderating a podcast interview with a philosopher who has defended a qualified version of aesthetic pessimism under the label "scaffolded acquaintance." On her view, testimony can genuinely transmit aesthetic beliefs about works of art, but only for properties she classifies as "architectonic"—structural relations like compositional balance, chromatic harmony, or rhythmic periodicity—because these can be grasped through a mediated form of structural mapping that expert description genuinely facilitates. She denies, however, that testimony can transmit beliefs about what she calls "atmospheric" properties—melancholy, sublimity, uncanniness, grace—because these require a direct affective attunement that testimony can describe and direct attention toward, but never substitute in the listener. She is adept at deflecting counterexamples involving art critics or museum curators by claiming that in such cases the expert is merely supplying descriptive vocabulary or redirecting perceptual attention, not actually transmitting an aesthetic judgment. Draft eight to ten interview questions that avoid biography and go straight to the mechanics of her position.

Please include: one question pressing the boundary between architectonic and atmospheric properties—specifically, whether intermediate properties like "dynamic tension" or "visual weight" fall on one side or the other, and on what grounds—followed by a follow-up keyed to her likely reply that the distinction is pragmatic and graded rather than metaphysically sharp. One question targeting her "directing attention" deflection, using a case where an expert's testimony seems to do more than redirect—say, convincing a listener that a minimalist sculpture is serene despite the listener's initial perception of coldness—followed by a follow-up anticipating her move to invoke "background conditions" or "cultivated sensibility" as prerequisites for attunement. One question on higher-order aesthetic ignorance: can testimony ever inform me that my failure to perceive an atmospheric property is a failure of attunement rather than an absence in the object, and does accommodating that require her to allow second-order testimonial transmission? One question asking what empirical or cross-cultural finding would count against her view—for instance, if listeners from divergent aesthetic traditions consistently converge on atmospheric judgments via testimony alone—followed by a follow-up asking whether she would treat such convergence as evidence of hidden architectonic mediation. For the remaining questions, press on analogies with moral testimony, on whether her view implies that aesthetic education should be redesigned around structural analysis, and on how she distinguishes her position from full-blooded optimism. Keep the questions rigorous but conversational, anticipating her standard replies and including the specified follow-ups. Do not ask for citations, reading lists, or bibliographies.
