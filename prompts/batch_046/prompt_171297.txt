I have recently been appointed to advise on long-term watershed governance in a region where rainfall patterns have shifted gradually over decades, yet the present crisis is being treated as a sudden emergency requiring immediate political intervention. I want to understand whether that framing is appropriate. Analyze the decline of Angkor and the relocation of the Khmer political center toward the Mekong delta in the 15th century through Fernand Braudel’s framework of the longue durée, specifically distinguishing between the slow structures of geography and climate (the progressive siltation of the baray irrigation network, multi-decadal monsoon variability), the medium-term conjunctures (the rise of maritime trade networks, demographic pressure), and the événement-level narrative (the 15th-century Siamese military incursion and royal flight). How does Braudel’s tripartite division explain why the Angkor elite maintained massive hydraulic investments long after marginal returns had turned negative? Where does the framework fail to capture the agency of specific rulers—particularly Jayavarman VII’s monumental overextension of the hydraulic state—in accelerating structural exhaustion? And what does the Angkor case suggest about my own planning problem: does treating a contemporary water crisis as an événement rather than a conjuncture or structural shift risk misallocating institutional resources, or does Braudel’s framework dangerously suppress the political urgency that discrete crises demand for legitimate democratic response?
