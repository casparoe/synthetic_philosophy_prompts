In debates over collective moral responsibility for the First World War, commentators frequently analogize the nation-state to a unitary moral agent: if Fritz Fischer's thesis is correct that German leadership deliberately sought continental hegemony—documented in the September Program and in cabinet-level war aims—then "Germany" can be blamed in roughly the same way an individual is blamed for a premeditated act. Opponents analogize the state instead to an emergent system, like a market or an ecosystem, in which outcomes arise from dispersed interactions and attributing a single intent is a category mistake.

Audit both analogies systematically. First, provide the correspondence table for the agent-model: what plays the role of the will, the deliberative forum, the executed decision, and the victim? Identify precisely where the mapping fractures—does Fischer's evidence, which highlights a narrow executive circle, support attributing intent to a collective body that included conscripts, industrialists, and dissenting parliamentarians? What normative features of individual blame (e.g., the possibility of remorse or reform) are absent or transformed when the subject is a diachronic population?

Second, do the same for the system-model: what corresponds to the structural dynamics, the absence of central control, and the diffusion of causal contribution? Show whether this analogy illegitimately dissolves responsibility into impersonal forces, given that the "system" was maintained and directed by identifiable institutional roles.

Finally, determine whether either analogy can legitimately license its usual conclusion—that collective blame is either straightforwardly warranted or conceptually impossible. If the analogies collapse at the normative step, propose whether a different structural comparison—treating the state as a hierarchical organization with distributed but institutionally assigned accountability—better captures the Fischer evidence, and state what that alternative implies for holding a nation responsible.
