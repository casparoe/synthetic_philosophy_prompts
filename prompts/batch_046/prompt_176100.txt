I'm working through a conceptual tension in how heuristic reasoning is formalized, specifically around what is often called the presumption of independence—the idea that when two lines of argument seem to come from distinct sources, their combined weight should be closer to the product of their individual strengths than to their sum. I understand the standard Bayesian treatment where independence means the joint probability equals the product of marginals, but I'm interested in the harder case: heuristic arguments that aren't about probabilistic events but about plausibility judgments in conceptual analysis. Here's the specific issue I want you to take a definite stand on.

Consider two analogical arguments for a philosophical claim—say, that mental states have a kind of irreducible normative force. The first draws on legal interpretation: judges treat intent as not reducible to behavioral patterns. The second draws on evolutionary biology: signaling systems stabilize only when receivers treat signals as carrying normative commitments, not merely correlations. These arguments appear structurally independent—one comes from institutional practice, the other from functional ecology—yet they may share a hidden explanatory substrate: both depend on the idea that coordination requires treating representations as binding rather than purely descriptive. The presumption of independence would suggest combining them multiplicatively, giving the claim strong support. But if they share that substrate, combining them additively, or with partial discount, might be more appropriate.

The conceptual problem is that independence here seems to split into at least two dimensions: epistemic independence (no shared premises or evidence) and inferential independence (no shared reasoning pathway or conceptual machinery). In standard probability theory these collapse, but in heuristic argumentation they can diverge. A formal model that treats them as collapsed will either overcount arguments that share hidden structure or undercount genuinely diverse arguments that happen to touch the same evidence. Please explain, with a committed view, how we should conceptualize this divergence and whether the presumption of independence can be salvaged as a formal principle for aggregating heuristic arguments. In particular, address whether there is a principled way to determine when two heuristic arguments share an explanatory substrate without already knowing the conclusion they support—since that determination seems circular if it is used to weight the very arguments that establish the substrate's relevance. If you think the formalization fails, say exactly where it breaks and what should replace it; if you think it succeeds, specify the formal apparatus (even if only informally sketched) and defend it against the circularity worry. I want a definite position rather than a survey of options, and please flag explicitly any step in your reasoning that relies on a contested assumption about the nature of analogical reasoning or the ontology of explanatory substrates.
