I am writing as a doctoral student in philosophy of mind, currently drafting a seminar contribution on whether Frank Jackson's knowledge argument about phenomenal consciousness can be dramatized without collapsing into metaphysical confusion. Rather than working through Mary's room in the abstract, I want to test the argument against Spike Jonze's Being John Malkovich, treating the film not as an illustration but as a philosophical interlocutor that may complicate, resist, or misrepresent the position.

The central question I want pursued is this: does the film's portal mechanism—which permits visitors to inhabit John Malkovich's first-person perceptual perspective while retaining their own identities and beliefs—actually vindicate Jackson's claim that complete physical and functional knowledge does not entail phenomenal knowledge? Or does the film, by presenting subjective experience as a transferable, almost data-like stream accessible through a hidden doorway, inadvertently undermine Jackson's intuition by suggesting that knowing what it is like is merely a matter of gaining the right kind of epistemic access?

In developing the reading, please address three specific scenes or dynamics. First, the initial portal experience, in which Craig and others observe through Malkovich's eyes but remain external controllers of nothing. Here, the visitor acquires what seems to be direct acquaintance with Malkovich's sensory field without becoming the subject of that field. Does this map onto Jackson's Mary, who knows all physical facts but lacks color experience? Or does it more closely resemble David Lewis's ability hypothesis, according to which knowing what an experience is like consists not in acquiring a new fact but in gaining certain recognitional or behavioral capacities—capacities that the portal-users initially lack and must learn?

Second, the development in which Craig Schwartz, leveraging his skills as a puppeteer, learns to direct Malkovich's bodily movements from within, eventually displacing Malkovich's own consciousness so thoroughly that Malkovich becomes a trapped observer inside his own body. If phenomenal knowledge requires being the subject of experience, then Craig's complete takeover might seem to represent not knowledge but ontological substitution. Does this support Jackson by showing that there is an irreducible what-it-is-like aspect that cannot be transferred, only replaced? Or does the film's treatment of consciousness as a container that can be emptied and filled—coupled with Malkovich's continued awareness—suggest a functionalist or substrate-independent view of mind that makes Jackson's gap seem like a confusion between epistemic limitation and metaphysical location?

Third, I want an honest assessment of where the film stops doing philosophy and starts using consciousness as a metaphor. The rules governing the portal shift according to narrative need, and the plot's driving concerns—celebrity, artistic appropriation, gender and identity, the commodification of experience—may have nothing to do with the knowledge argument. Where should a careful critic insist that the film is not answering Jackson but borrowing the imagery of inner life for dramatic effect? And conversely, what does this particular cinematic treatment reveal about Mary's room that standard philosophical debate—whether Jackson's original formulation, the ability response, or the acquaintance reply—tends to overlook?

The deliverable should be a sustained critical reading, faithful both to the film's narrative logic and to the philosophical distinctions at stake, ending with a judgment about what this pairing adds to the debate that argument alone cannot.
