I was discussing Daniel Dennett's heterophenomenology with a colleague who had just read a summary in a cognitive-science methods text. According to that summary, heterophenomenology is essentially a behaviorist maneuver: it instructs the researcher to treat every first-person report about conscious experience—say, a subject's claim that she is seeing a red afterimage—as if it were a description of a purely fictional entity, comparable to a literary critic analyzing the world of a novel. The summary says that Dennett therefore denies that introspection provides any data whatsoever, because on his view there is no "inner theater" to observe, and that the heterophenomenological world is just a useful fiction the scientist invents to organize behavioral outputs. It further claims that Dennett introduced this method specifically to show that phenomenal consciousness is an illusion generated by linguistic confabulation, and that once we adopt the heterophenomenological stance we should stop asking what it is like to be the subject and instead only catalog correlations between verbal reports and neural activation patterns. Something about this strikes me as too dismissive of first-person testimony—Dennett seems more nuanced than a pure eliminativist—but I am not sure where the summary crosses from simplification into misrepresentation. Could you audit this characterization? Please distinguish which parts capture Dennett's actual method, which are standard pedagogical compressions, and which are outright distortions—particularly regarding whether heterophenomenology denies all first-person authority, whether the heterophenomenological world belongs to the subject or to the scientist, and whether the method was intended to eliminate phenomenal consciousness rather than to study it without granting incorrigible access. I would also like to know whether the summary's conclusion—that heterophenomenology makes qualitative experience scientifically dispensable—actually follows from the accurate parts.
