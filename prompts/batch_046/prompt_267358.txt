I sit on our research lab's AI governance panel and we're voting Thursday on whether to green-light "Aurelia," a reasoning agent designed to recursively update its own proof-checker. The engineers want each version to apply a reflection principle — basically "if I prove φ then φ is true" — to the outputs of its future self, so the system can self-improve without human verification at every step. But there's the Löbian obstacle: because of how Löb's theorem works in modal logic, the agent can't consistently prove that same reflection principle applies to itself for arbitrary statements, which means full recursive self-trust collapses into either inconsistency or an infinite regress where version N trusts N+1 but can't justify it. Half the panel thinks this is a formal glitch we can contain by restricting φ to a safe syntactic class; the other half thinks it's a hard epistemic ceiling and any deployment should be blocked untill the architecture solves the asymmetry. I need a memo — not a lecture — recommending approve, reject, or approve-with-conditions, with a concrete milestone or time-frame if conditional. Treat the strongest argument from the losing side seriously (don't just dismiss them as misunderstanding logic). Keep it readable for the non-logicians on the panel but don't water down why the obstacle isn't just a software bug. No bibliographies or citations needed.
