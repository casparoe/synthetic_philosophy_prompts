After a long argument with my friend about well-being in simulated environments, I want to test whether their judgments form a consistent pattern. They have said: first, that a person who knowingly enters a flawless virtual world in order to experience the mental process of scientific discovery—though nothing is actually discovered—is genuinely well-off if they value that phenomenology; second, that a person deceived into believing they have deep friendships, while actually isolated, is badly off despite feeling happy; third, that a high-fidelity simulation permitting real cognitive struggle, genuine problem-solving, and full awareness that the environment is artificial can support well-being, because the mental processes remain authentic; and fourth, that altering someone's brain so they no longer desire real-world contact, then placing them in a machine, neither harms nor benefits them—it removes the conditions for well-being entirely.

Now consider this new case: a "reflective-lock" protocol that delivers full phenomenological richness, permits real reasoning, and preserves accurate knowledge that one is simulated, yet also maintains—through an imperceptible neural feedback loop—a stable second-order belief that one's preference for this life was formed through fully autonomous, unmanipulated deliberation, when in fact the preference was calibrated by the loop itself. The inhabitant experiences themselves as having freely chosen authenticity-within-simulation.

What should my friend say about this inhabitant's well-being? Which of the four judgments is the closest analog? If the precedents genuinely underdetermine the verdict, identify the two most plausible governing rules, state what each predicts about the reflective-lock case, recommend the decision that best coheres with the track record with your reasoning, name the principle that would be adopted with each choice, and specify one further case that would distinguish those two rules.
