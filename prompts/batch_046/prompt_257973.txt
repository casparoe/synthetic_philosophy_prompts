I spent most of last evening in a long, exhausting argument with a close friend over whether the world is genuinely becoming less violent. She insisted that per-capita metrics—homicide and battle-death rates scaled to global population—are the only intellectually honest measure, since absolute death counts ignore demographic growth; I kept pushing back that if the underlying dynamics of harm have not disappeared but only changed form, then counting only direct physical killings might miss a structural transformation rather than a real reduction. We eventually circled the deeper question of whether there is a fact of the matter at all: is violence a stable natural kind that can be tracked across centuries, or is it a concept whose boundaries shift with our normative commitments, so that any claim about its decline already presupposes a contested definition?

Trying to organize my thoughts afterward, I returned to a passage I had copied years ago from Thomas Malthus’s 1798 Essay on the Principle of Population. The language is dense and the conceptual framework feels distant from modern statistical debates. Malthus distinguishes between preventive checks to population growth—moral restraint, delayed marriage, and voluntary limitation—and positive checks, which include explicit warfare, famine, pestilence, and all other physical sufferings that reduce the number of living people to match available subsistence. He then claims that in civilized societies these positive checks do not disappear; rather, organized warfare becomes less frequent and is replaced by other conditions of physical degradation that achieve an equivalent demographic result.

Here is the passage as I have paraphrased it: in every state of society, whether savage or civilized, there operates a constant tendency for population to increase beyond the means of subsistence; where preventive checks do not restrain this tendency, positive checks necessarily follow. Among these are not only direct war and famine, but every cause of physical suffering that diminishes human numbers. In more advanced societies, these checks are not abolished; instead, war is transformed into other forms of deprivation that serve the same function of aligning population with resources.

My difficulty is conceptual rather than historical. I want to understand what work the transformation claim is doing inside Malthus’s own argument—whether it is a demographic observation about equilibrium, a normative judgment about civilization, or something else entirely. Only then can I assess whether this framework offers any leverage on our modern disagreement. Specifically, could Malthus’s idea that positive checks are transformed rather than eliminated be read as an anticipation of the distinction between direct violence, which is captured in per-capita statistics, and structural violence, the distributed conditions that reduce life expectancy without a specific agent? If so, does this undermine empirical claims that violence is declining, on the grounds that we are measuring only a change in the form of harm, not its elimination? Or does the comparison collapse because Malthus is concerned solely with population dynamics, not with the ontology or measurement of violence?

Please help me understand the passage on its own terms first: what is the internal logic of the transformation claim, and how does it relate to the distinction between preventive and positive checks? Then, if the architecture of the argument genuinely permits it, tell me whether applying it to modern debates about violence metrics is defensible or anachronistic. I am not asking for a bibliography, a reading list, or citations to secondary literature; I want conceptual clarification about whether the specific reasoning in this passage supports the comparison I am trying to draw, and where the analogy breaks down if it does not.
