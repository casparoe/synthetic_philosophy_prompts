I retired from biochemistry a few years back and lately I've been trying to wrap my head around modal metaphysics. The Lewis/Kripke debate on counterpart theory vs transworld identity mostly makes sense to me, but I'm hung up on a specific conceptual knot that seems to come up when you think about biological essentialism.

So here's the issue: in biology we often treat functional equivalence as more fundamental than historical origin -- think horizontal gene transfer, or synthetic organisms that replicate natural genomes but have no evolutionary lineage. If I understand Lewis right, his counterpart relation is similarity-based, so an organism in another possible world could share my genome but have a totally different evolutionary history. That actually sounds closer to how biologists talk about gene function across species. Kripke's transworld identity, on the other hand, seems to lock in origin as essential: you can't be 'me' without my actual parents, and by extension maybe not without my actual phylogenetic tree.

But then I read this argument (maybe in a philosphy of science journal?) that counterpart theory doesn't really solve the essential-origin problem; it just relocates it into the weighting of similarity dimensions. If evolutionary origin gets low weight in the counterpart relation, then 'I' in another world might share almost nothing with me except a few molecular patterns, which makes the counterpart relation seem arbitrary. Meanwhile, if origin gets high weight, counterpart theory collapses back into something like transworld identity for organisms.

Can you explain this conceptual tangle? Specifically, does counterpart theory actually offer a coherent account of identity conditions for biological kinds that respects functional equivalence without making origin completely irrelevant? And please tell me what argument or finding would change your mind -- like if there's a formal result showing counterpart relations must preserve origin, or if biologists actually do treat origin as essential after all. Also, be honest about where your reasoning is shakiest, since I'm still learning to think like a philospher and I don't want to be led astray.
