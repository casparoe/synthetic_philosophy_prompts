In metaontology, there is a persistent deadlock between realists, who take existence-claims about composite or abstract objects to be substantive assertions about what the world contains, and deflationists, who diagnose such claims as verbal disagreements about the meaning of the quantifier or about which conceptual scheme to adopt. The realist insists that asserting the existence of a mereological sum of my left shoe and the Eiffel Tower is false if the world lacks such an object; the deflationist replies that this is merely a choice between linguistic conventions that share the same underlying ontology. For decades, neither camp has moved, and the exchange often resembles parallel monologues rather than a shared problem. My hypothesis is that the stall is not about ontology at all but about explanatory legitimacy: realists require that existence-claims be anchored to causal or explanatory roles, while deflationists treat such demands as an illegitimate import of substantive criteria into a purely methodological decision about quantificational frameworks. Is this correct? Or is there a hidden substantive disagreement—perhaps about whether quantifier variance carries empirical commitments, or about the relationship between semantic ascent and pragmatic ascent—that both sides gesture toward but fail to isolate? Diagnose the impasse: is it verbal, a clash over what counts as an adequate explanation, a case of answering different questions under the single heading of ontology, or an irreducible conflict of bedrock intuitions? And given your diagnosis, what, if anything, could break it—would a rigorous distinction between semantic and pragmatic ascent resolve the conflict, would an argument linking quantifier choice to predictive success do so, or should we conclude that the disagreement is structural and no progress is to be expected?
