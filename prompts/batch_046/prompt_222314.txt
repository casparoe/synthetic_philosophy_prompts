Case 1 — Weber's story itself. The Protestant emphasis on worldly asceticism, the calling, and disciplined labor feels like it genuinely contributed to the development of capitalist economic practices. I want to say the values were part of the real causal chain.

Case 2 — A society has strict dueling rituals, and a historian explains this by saying "they value honor above life." To me that feels circular: the value just redescribes the ritual in psychological language. It doesn't explain where the ritual came from. Not a legitimate causal explanation.

Case 3 — A religious community holds a value that certain days must be kept free of labor. Over generations this shapes regional labor markets and long-term growth patterns. This feels like real causation: the value drives behavior, and the behavior drives the economic outcome.

Case 5 — Two neighboring regions have identical soil, climate, and trade access. One develops a religious savings ethic; the other emphasizes communal generosity. The savings region builds banking institutions; the other doesn't. I keep going back and forth: I want to say the values caused the divergence, but I also wonder whether some hidden material factor produced both the values and the institutions. I can't settle it.

Case 6 — In the twentieth century, a nation adopts democratic norms. Some say this was caused by a pre-existing cultural value of "individual dignity." But the norms were also pushed by occupying powers and economic incentives. I think the values may have made the adoption stick, filtering how external forces worked, but I'm unsure whether they actually caused the change or just shaped its form.
