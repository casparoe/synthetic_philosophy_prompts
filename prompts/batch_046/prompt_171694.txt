Draft the joint statement that two opposing philosophers could both sign after a sustained adversarial collaboration on Mary's room and the knowledge argument. One party is a phenomenal-concept strategist: she holds that Mary's release yields only a new phenomenal concept—or mode of presentation—for a physical fact she already knew under a different description, and that this dissolves the argument without any ontological cost to physicalism. The other party is an acquaintance-structuralist: he holds that Mary's gain is irreducibly a form of knowledge-by-acquaintance with phenomenal qualities, revealing that physical descriptions are structurally incomplete because they omit relational structures accessible only through direct phenomenal experience; he denies this entails Cartesian dualism, but insists physicalism must be expanded to include these structural-phenomenal relations rather than leaving them out.

The document should be organized as follows.

1. Agreed claims (numbered list, roughly 300 words): shared commitments about the Mary's-room scenario itself, about the distinction between propositional knowledge and acquaintance knowledge, about what a successful physicalism must account for, about the legitimate but limited probative force of thought experiments, and about the methodological principle that conceptual change must be distinguished from ontological revelation.

2. Residual disagreements (table or paired sections, roughly 250 words): the precise formulation of each side's view in wording that its own proponent would accept; the separation between conceptual-change explanations and structural-incompleteness explanations; and the status of phenomenal concepts—whether they are fully reducible to physical-functional or representational descriptions, or whether they necessarily carry a non-functional residue tied to acquaintance.

3. The cruxes (roughly 200 words): identify the sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one party. I take the central cruxes to be (a) whether phenomenal concepts can be exhaustively explained by functional or representational roles without remainder, and (b) whether acquaintance knowledge can be modeled as a species of indexical or demonstrative knowledge that remains fully within the physicalist framework.

4. Tests and predictions (roughly 250 words): for each crux, specify a concrete case, argument, or empirical result that both sides agree would bear on it, with each side's prediction recorded in advance. For example, both should agree on what it would show if cognitive neuroscience could fully map phenomenal acquaintance onto representational state-transition dynamics, and both should state whether such a mapping would satisfy the acquaintance-structuralist's demand for structural completeness or merely confirm the strategist's claim of conceptual reducibility. Include one further predictive disagreement about whether a subject could possess complete physical information plus the relevant phenomenal concept and still lack the corresponding acquaintance.

Write in a formal, polished register suitable for a philosophy professor directing an advanced exercise. Do not request bibliographies, citation lists, or reading recommendations. Do not invoke obscure named doctrines that depend on specialist literature unknown to a well-trained generalist. Keep the focus on the conceptual architecture of the disagreement rather than on historical exegesis.
