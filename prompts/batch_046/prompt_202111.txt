As a novelist who has long been preoccupied with how collective trauma is narrated, archived, and silently transmitted across generations, I am constructing a final examination for an upper-level philosophy seminar on the ethics of memory. I want an essay question that resists mere summary: it should not be answerable by reciting Avishai Margalit’s account of shared remembrance as an ethical pillar or Philip Rieff’s critique of modern culture’s therapeutic displacement of moral authority. To force genuine adjudication, the prompt should present an invented, narratively specific case—an island republic with no direct experience of a particular historical atrocity, but one that inherits its archives solely through migration, and that now proposes a policy of legislated “structured amnesia.” This is not denial; it is a deliberate refusal to teach, display, or commemorate the event, justified on the grounds that remembrance has become a mechanism of moral credentialing for outsiders who never suffered, and that the polity’s only authentic ethical duty is to release its present citizens from an inherited burden of vicarious guilt. The examination question should ask students to determine whether this policy violates a duty to remember, fulfills a legitimate case for forgetting, or exposes a false dichotomy between the two. They must identify the precise locus of moral authority—victims, descendants, or the polity itself—construct the strongest argument for the opposing view, and locate the structural crux: whether commemorative justice can exist without experiential proximity, and whether Rieff’s suspicion of memorial culture applies differently when remembrance is not therapeutic self-soothing but a form of imported moral obligation. Please provide the exam question itself, specifying the level (upper-level seminar), the time allocation (ninety minutes), the word limit (approximately 1,200 words), and the total marks (forty percent of the final grade). Include a model answer of about 700 words that isolates the conflict between commemorative obligation and the ethics of release, and a marking scheme that distinguishes an A answer—one that distinguishes commemorative justice from commemorative politics, shows where Margalit’s concept of shared memory either applies or collapses under conditions of inherited, non-experiential trauma, and integrates Rieff’s critique of therapeutic culture into the island’s rationale without reducing it to a slogan—from a B answer that applies the thinkers’ labels mechanically to the scenario. Name the specific argumentative moves each grade requires, and list the particular misconceptions or omissions that should cost marks.
