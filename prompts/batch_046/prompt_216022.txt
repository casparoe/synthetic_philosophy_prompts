I'm currently working through the socialist calculation debate in a seminar on twentieth-century economic theory, and I've become fixated on what seems like an unremarked parallel. Oskar Lange's iterative pricing mechanism—where a central board adjusts prices in response to observed surpluses and shortages until markets clear—looks to me structurally indistinguishable from stochastic gradient descent: the board proposes a price vector, receives an error signal from the divergence of supply and demand, and updates parameters until convergence. If modern deep-learning systems routinely optimize over parameter spaces of far greater dimensionality than any socialist planning board ever faced, and if they can do so using only distributed consumption data without requiring private ownership of capital goods, then it strikes me that the Mises-Hayek critique has been superseded not by politics but by algorithmic capability. I find myself wondering whether the remaining disagreement is therefore purely empirical—about convergence rates, data latency, and hardware—rather than conceptual. Specifically, have I made a category error in treating entrepreneurial discovery and capital-good valuation as instances of a differentiable optimization problem? Or is there a substantive feature of market prices—perhaps tied to the institutional conditions under which capital goods are actually traded—that no iterative algorithm can replicate regardless of its scale?
