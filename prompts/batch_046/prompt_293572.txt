I am preparing a ninety-minute seminar for second-year philosophy undergraduates who have completed an introductory course in philosophy of mind but have no formal training in philosophy of language or artificial intelligence. In our last session, a student asked whether a large language model actually believes that climate change is occurring, and the discussion quickly bifurcated into two unsatisfactory camps: those who dismissed all talk of AI belief as mere anthropomorphism, and those who treated predictive coherence as sufficient proof of literal internal belief. I wish to use this tension as the entry point for teaching Dennett's intentional stance, not as a general metaphysics of mind, but as a disciplined predictive strategy specifically applied to attributing beliefs and desires to engineered language systems.

Please design the explanatory arc for me rather than delivering the explanation itself. First, specify the dependency order: which conceptual distinction must be firmly in place before the intentional stance can be introduced — I take it that the contrast between the physical stance and the design stance needs to precede it, but I want your account of why, and whether anything else must come earlier. Second, identify the single carrying example: I propose using an interaction in which an LLM is presented with a subtly altered trolley-style moral dilemma and produces a coherent, reasoned response that appeals to the moral weight of saving lives, yet reverses its apparent commitment when the framing shifts slightly without changing the underlying structure. Specify precisely what this example must contain — predictive success across contexts, absence of embodied stakes, lack of evolutionary or survival-based history — and, equally important, what it must deliberately lack so that students do not infer either full-blown personhood or mere mechanism. Third, map the misconceptions in the order they will likely arise: I expect students will initially conflate adopting the intentional stance with attributing consciousness; then they will slide from "this is a useful predictive tool" to "therefore the beliefs are literally instantiated in the model's weights"; and finally they may confuse predictive charity with moral excuse. For each, indicate the exact point in the sequence where it should be headed off, and how.

In addition, weave in a somewhat niche but not obscure distinction that will keep the session from being a generic rehearsal of Dennett: specifically, contrast Davidsonian interpretive charity — which presupposes rationality, shared environmental embeddedness, and evolutionary continuity — with what we might call calibration charity applied to engineered systems whose outputs are shaped by optimisation objectives and training distributions rather than by survival or social coordination. Explain how this distinction should be introduced without derailing the main arc, and what work it does in preventing the misconception that AI belief-attribution is identical to human belief-attribution. Then provide a precise check for understanding — one question or brief exercise — that would reveal whether the students have genuinely grasped the intentional stance as a predictive strategy rather than simply memorising that AI does not have real beliefs. Finally, state explicitly what should be omitted at this level and justify the omission: for instance, whether Searle's Chinese Room argument should be mentioned, whether functionalism needs to be addressed, and whether the physical stance requires detailed exposition. The plan should be suitable for a single ninety-minute session with students who are philosophically literate but not specialists.
