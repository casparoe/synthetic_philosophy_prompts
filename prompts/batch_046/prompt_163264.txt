hey, i'm a novelist working on a political drama and i'm trying to figure out what my protagonist actually thinks about voting. i have these gut verdicts but they don't line up neat, and i wonder if there's one principle behind them or if i'm just being inconsistant.

case one: in a swing district, voting for the mediocre candidate just to block the dangerous one feels like a moral duty, not just okay. case two: in a safe seat, voting for that exact same mediocre candidate feels like complicity, not okay — like you're giving them a mandate.

case three: casting a symbolic vote for a tiny protest party in a safe seat feels fine, even noble. case four: doing that same symbolic vote in a swing seat where it might spoil the mediocre candidate feels negligent, almost like leaving a door unlocked on purpose.

case five: sitting out the election because both options disgust you feels understandable, not blameworthy. case six: but actively organizing others to boycott feels wrong, like you're harming strangers just to make a point.

i keep playing with two ideas i made up for the book: electoral proximity, where the closer the race the more your vote is a causal lever and less an expressive gesture; and narrative residue, the story you tell yourself about what your vote meant. voting for the lesser evil in a landslide leaves a different residue than in a nail-biter, even though the ballot looks identical.

i also can't settle this edge case: writing in a fictional candidate's name as pure protest in a non-swing state. is that closer to the symbolic vote or to the boycott?

is there a coherent principle — maybe about duty versus expression versus harm — that saves all six verdicts? if not, which verdict should i drop? and what does the best account predict for the write-in case, and for hiding your vote choice from friends so you avoid the residue?

sorry if this is rambling, i'm just trying to make my character's ethics feel lived-in rather than textbook.
