I've been thinking about writing something with ghosts, and I got stuck on this idea from Sidgwick — not his ethics, but his psychical research stuff. He had this weird stance where he said evidence for ghosts should'nt be held to a higher bar than physics just because it's spooky, but also that the obsever's emotional state corrupts the data in ways hard to correct for. I want to read The Turn of the Screw through that lens. Does James's governess fail Sidgwick's test? Is the ambiguity of the ending actually dramatizing Sidgwick's worry that affective investment makes the observer unreliable? Or does the story resist that — maybe it shows that even a "clean" observer wouldn't solve it? I'd like a reading that takes both James and Sidgwick seriously, not just reducing the novela to an illustration. Also, does the story add something to the psychical research debate that pure argument misses?
