I'm taking a graduate seminar on distributive justice and recently found myself arguing with friends about whether there is a coherent principle behind our intuitions that some trade-offs between one severe burden and many mild ones are impermissible, even when the aggregate suffering is lower in the latter case. I want to check whether these judgments hang together or whether I need to abandon one of them.

Here are four concrete cases with my verdicts. First, imagine a factory manager who can either assign all dangerous overnight shifts to one worker—causing that worker chronic health damage—so that twenty colleagues avoid only occasional weekend work; I think it is clearly wrong to impose the severe burden on the one for such minor collective relief. Second, a city can build a highway that destroys one neighborhood entirely, or take a longer route that adds five minutes to every commuter's trip; I think the longer route is required, even if the summed disutility across all drivers exceeds the neighborhood's loss. Third, in a hospital triage scenario, saving ten patients with minor injuries requires diverting resources away from one critically injured patient who would then die; here I think it is permissible to save the ten, despite the single fatal cost. Fourth, during a public-health emergency, authorities can contain a contagious outbreak either by imposing a severe six-month lockdown on a geographically isolated region comprising 1% of the population—destroying local businesses and mental health—or by asking the entire population to observe mild social-distancing measures for two weeks; in this case, I think the targeted lockdown is permissible, even though the structural pattern looks almost identical to the factory case.

Is there one principle—perhaps about direct agency, about whether the burden functions as a precondition for others' gain, about the distinction between risk-pooling and burden-shifting, or about the kind of good being protected—that captures all four verdicts? If not, which verdict should I overturn and why? Please state the resulting account precisely, explain what clause handles which case, predict its verdict on a case I haven't listed—say, a tax policy that funds universal basic services by imposing extreme labor on a small, randomly selected cohort—and please write your response in American English.
