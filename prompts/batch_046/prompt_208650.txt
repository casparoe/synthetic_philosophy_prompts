I am finalizing the conceptual framework for a graduate seminar I will teach next term on cross-cultural ethics and communicative virtue, and I have tried to articulate my own settled convictions so that my students can press on them systematically. Here is where I stand, though I worry the pieces do not all hang together. First, I hold that intentionally causing another person to hold a false belief is pro tanto wrong in every context; this is not a cultural convention but a direct failure of respect for rational agency. Second, I think that Dutch directness is ethically preferable to Japanese indirectness because it refuses to treat the interlocutor as fragile or incapable of bearing truth, and I regard the Japanese distinction between honne and tatemae not as a moral resource but as a systematic form of epistemic paternalism. Third, I maintain that in high-context social environments, an explicit direct refusal can inflict a distinctive harm that I have begun calling relational rupture, which means indirect refusal is sometimes not merely permissible but morally required, even when it risks what I term pragmatic misdirection rather than outright falsehood. Fourth, I reject white lies—telling a host that a badly cooked meal was excellent—as always wrong, though I allow that strategic omission or conversational redirection belongs to a separate category and is often obligatory politeness. Fifth, I believe that any cultural practice that structurally requires deception is morally defective and ought to be reformed rather than accommodated, yet I also think that outsiders have a stringent duty of cultural humility that requires them to participate in those very practices rather than impose directness. Sixth, I deny that politeness norms are arbitrary conventions; I think they track real moral reasons about dignity, but I also deny that there is one universal form—whether directness or indirectness—that dignity must take in every setting.

Please go through these commitments and tell me which pairs are genuinely in tension and which conflicts dissolve upon closer inspection. For each real tension, indicate how severe it is, what the cheapest revision would be, and what that revision costs me elsewhere in the set. Then tell me which single revision—giving up or substantially modifying one commitment—would clean up the greatest number of conflicts.
