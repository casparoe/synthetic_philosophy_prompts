After a long and rather circular argument with a friend last night about whether institutional categories can ever be family-resemblance concepts in Wittgenstein’s sense, I want to put the matter to a disciplined test. My friend insists that terms like “pandemic” must have precise institutional definitions—otherwise emergency powers, funding mechanisms, and international law have no purchase—so Wittgenstein’s idea of overlapping similarities without an essential core is at best irrelevant and at worst dangerous in public-health governance. I think this confuses the grammar of a word with the bureaucracy that tries to stabilize it. To settle it, analyze the WHO’s declaration in March 2020 that COVID-19 had become a pandemic, and the immediate divergence in how different national governments used that same word over the following weeks—some treating it as a trigger for border closures and emergency decrees, others treating it as a descriptive label that changed little about existing policy. I want a careful mapping, not a vague gloss: identify the distinct language games being played (the epidemiological-statistical game of the WHO, the legal-emergency game of national executives, the media-public game of risk communication), show which features of “pandemic” are shared across these games and which are not, and explain whether the disagreements between, say, the Italian and Swedish responses in March 2020 reflect genuine factual disputes or grammatical differences about what counts as the same concept. Then tell me honestly where Wittgenstein’s framework explains the confusion and where it falls silent—particularly regarding whether an institution like the WHO can perform a “defining move” that is itself just one more use in a language game, or whether that move introduces a kind of vertical anchoring that family resemblance cannot accommodate. Do not give me a bibliography or list of further reading; I need the analysis to stand on its own reasoning.
