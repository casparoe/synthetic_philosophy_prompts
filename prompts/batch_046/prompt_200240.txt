At a philosophy of mind reading group, two members debated Dennett’s rejection of the Cartesian theater and his multiple drafts model of consciousness.

Maya opened by insisting that the Cartesian theater is not a philosopher’s strawman but a feature of ordinary experience. When she reports seeing a red apple now, she means there is a single present moment and a single observer for whom that experience occurs; any adequate theory must accommodate that unity.

Leo countered that this is precisely the illusion Dennett diagnoses. There is no central screen and no singular “now” — only parallel neural processes that generate competing drafts, with one eventually achieving “fame in the brain” and becoming reportable. The subjective sense of a unified present is a retrospective construction, not evidence of a theater.

Maya replied that if drafts are genuinely independent, the model should allow contradictory contents to reach awareness simultaneously, yet they do not. The fact that she never reports seeing red and green in the same location suggests some centralized editorial selection — a theater with distributed lighting, perhaps, but a selection point nonetheless.

Leo answered that contradiction is avoided not by a central editor but by the dynamics of competition among drafts: one achieves dominance through accessibility and coherence, like a newspaper edition that outsells its rivals. He then proposed a hypothetical case: a subject whose color processing is delayed 150 ms relative to shape processing. The subject can verbally identify shape before color, yet retrospectively reports both as experienced together. In a Cartesian theater, this is anomalous — the unified scene should contain both properties for the central observer at once. In the multiple drafts model, it is expected: different drafts reach reportability at different times, and a subsequent meta-draft binds them narratively.

Maya granted the hypothetical’s coherence with Dennett’s view but argued that it actually presupposes what it denies. The very capacity to report that the properties were “experienced together” requires a central integrative mechanism that can survey both drafts and construct the binding; without such a mechanism, there would be only disconnected reports, not a unified retrospective judgment of togetherness. Thus, she concluded, Leo has relocated the theater into the meta-draft rather than eliminated it.

Leo responded that this mischaracterizes the meta-draft. It is not a central surveyor but another distributed process — one that takes other drafts as input and produces a narrative about them. Calling it a theater is like calling a newspaper’s summary of its own articles an editor-in-chief: the summary is just more text, not a transcendent vantage point.
