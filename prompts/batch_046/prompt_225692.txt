A colleague recently summarized the Letter Spirit project's account of letter recognition to me as follows: "Letter Spirit solves the problem of recognizing every 'a' by decomposing each letter into a fixed skeletal structure—the topological relations among its strokes—while treating style as a purely superficial layer of ornamentation. Once the skeleton is identified, the specific curves, weights, and proportions that distinguish one font from another are essentially irrelevant to letter identity; they are just rendering choices applied after recognition. Consequently, the system can operate in two cleanly separated stages: an initial analogical extraction of the skeleton that is entirely style-blind, followed by a stylistic classification that has no feedback into what letter has been recognized. This proves that the essence of a letter is nothing more than its abstract geometric skeleton, and that any interaction between form and style is an illusion created by human observers who confuse appearance with identity."

Something about this sounds suspicious—particularly the claim that style has zero feedback into recognition and that the skeleton is entirely style-independent—but I am not sure whether these are genuine misreadings of Hofstadter's framework or just provocative restatements. Is this summary fair? Please assess it claim by claim: identify what accurately reflects the Letter Spirit distinction between letter roles and stylistic parameters, what subtly overstates the separation of form and style (for instance, by ignoring whether analogical matching processes might be influenced by stylistic context), and whether the conclusion that letter essence reduces to a pure geometric skeleton actually follows from the project's analogical, concept-fluid architecture. I would especially like to know if the two-stage decomposition described here is compatible with how the program actually handles ambiguous or highly stylized letterforms, or if it imposes an artificial modularity that the original model rejects.
