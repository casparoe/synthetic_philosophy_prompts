As a novelist drafting a metaphysical fiction, I ask you to write a short story that engages the special composition question well below the level of the familiar ship of Theseus. Center the narrative on a conservator or archivist who works with what philosophers call arbitrary undetached parts—fragments that have lost functional unity yet remain physically scattered—so that the plot stages the tension between unrestricted universalism, which holds that every scattered set of parts composes an object, and restricted views that deny composition in the absence of causal or functional binding. Invent a concrete, sensory setting, such as a restoration workshop where broken instruments are arranged and rearranged, so that the same physical parts seem to form, dissolve, and overlap into competing wholes depending on the observer’s conceptual scheme. Let the protagonist feel grief or attachment not to the bare parts but to a specific composed whole, and let the story ask whether that grief can be justified if only parts are ultimately real, or whether our value-assignments actually determine which compositions exist. Write with literary precision: avoid transparent allegory, do not name the philosophical positions explicitly in dialogue, and let the metaphysical tension emerge through image, action, and the quiet logic of the workshop rather than through exposition.
