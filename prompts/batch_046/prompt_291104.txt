For a seminar on the metaphysics of life, I want an audit of my own view before I present it. I hold: (a) life is essentially an autopoietic process—continuous organizational closure that regenerates its own boundary conditions—not a checklist of properties like reproduction or metabolism; (b) evolutionary history is irrelevant to whether something is alive, so a synthetic cell engineered de novo that achieves closure would be genuinely alive; (c) dormant seeds and desiccated tardigrades are fully alive, not merely potentially alive, because the organizational structure remains intact even when metabolic flow is suspended; (d) viruses are not alive, despite replication and evolutionary adaptation, because they never maintain organizational closure independently; (e) the category life is a natural kind with a unified essence, not a family-resemblance cluster; and (f) in borderline cases—say, a prion that propagates conformational information without any boundary, or a hypothetical sleep-mode synthetic cell that halts all metabolism but preserves membrane topology—what matters is whether the entity instantiates the essence of organizational closure, not how many familiar life-traits it displays.

My worry is that (a) and (c) pull in opposite directions: if life is essentially an ongoing process, how can a seed in which that process is completely suspended still be alive in the present tense? At the same time, (b) and (e) might look like they conflict—if evolutionary lineage does not matter, then in what sense is life a natural kind rather than an engineered functional category? Meanwhile, (d) and (f) seem consistent, but I want to check whether my exclusion of viruses actually follows from (a) or relies on an unstated assumption about independence that (e) does not license. Could you rank the tensions by severity, say which ones dissolve on closer inspection, and tell me what I would have to revise—whether (a), (c), (e), or my understanding of process—to restore coherence with minimal damage to my overall view?
