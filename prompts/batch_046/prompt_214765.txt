I'm working through a long-standing tension in my philosophy of education reading group about whether biography and hagiography can genuinely serve as sources of moral knowledge, specifically through the classical model of paired comparative lives—Greek and Roman figures set against one another for ethical reflection—and its later hagiographic descendants. One faction—let's call them the exemplarists—holds that reading carefully constructed lives of exemplary figures delivers practical moral understanding that abstract ethical theory cannot: by tracking how a particular virtue is embodied in concrete choices, narrative biography trains a kind of perceptual recognition and emulative disposition rather than propositional belief. The opposing faction argues that this is a category mistake: biography and hagiography are genres of narrative selection, not moral testimony. They smooth psychological contradiction, retroactively impose coherence on lives that were actually fragmented, and systematically privilege dramatic moments over mundane moral maintenance, so what they teach is admiration for a curated persona rather than transferable practical wisdom. For months we've circled the same examples—Plutarch's comparative pairings, the pedagogical use of saints' lives in medieval education, modern celebrity biography—without resolution. Each side agrees that narrative moves us emotionally and shapes our ideals; the disagreement is about whether that movement counts as epistemic progress or as a kind of aesthetic seduction.

My working theory is that the stall is not empirical—both sides grant that biography affects moral imagination—and not purely verbal, though they do use "knowledge" differently. Rather, I suspect they are answering different questions under a single heading: the exemplarists are asking how practical wisdom is transmitted through concrete particularity, while the critics are asking whether biographical representations are truth-tracking enough to justify belief. But I want an honest diagnosis rather than my own hunch confirmed. Is this a substantive disagreement about the conditions for moral learning, a hidden dispute about what counts as evidence in ethics, or an impasse born of bedrock intuitions about whether virtue can be taught at all? Given your diagnosis, what would actually count as progress—an empirical study of narrative emulation, a formal distinction between practical and propositional knowledge applied to biography, a reconception of the question itself, or should we expect permanent disagreement? Please engage concretely with what each side actually claims about the comparative biographical method and the hagiographic tradition, and tell me whether anything could genuinely move this debate or whether it is structurally deadlocked.
