Here is an argument I have been unable to unwind regarding higher-order evidence and epistemic akrasia, and I present it in numbered steps because I genuinely do not see where the chain breaks. (1) Rationality requires that whenever one possesses higher-order evidence that a first-order belief-forming process P is unreliable within domain D, one ought to reduce confidence in every output of P that falls within D. (2) I have extensive higher-order evidence—drawn from the literature on motivated cognition, framing effects, and my own record of revising past moral and political judgments—that my first-order reasoning in moral and political domains is systematically distorted. (3) Therefore, rationality requires me to reduce confidence in all of my moral and political beliefs. (4) But the assessment in (2) is itself produced by reasoning processes—call them P or structurally analogous processes Q—that are equally susceptible to the very biases they purport to diagnose; in particular, widespread narratives about universal human irrationality may themselves be artifacts of the cognitive distortions they describe. (5) Applying principle (1) to the belief formed in (4) requires me to reduce confidence in my belief that P is unreliable. (6) Hence I appear rationally required both to reduce and not to reduce confidence in my moral beliefs, which is flatly contradictory. (7) One might try to escape through a posture of reflexive calibration: holding each belief with an explicit, revisable confidence weight, never fully suspended but always open to meta-level adjustment. (8) Yet reflexive calibration demands that I assign weights not only to first-order beliefs but to meta-beliefs about those weights, and every such assignment is again an output of P or Q. (9) This generates an infinite regress with no stable termination point—only an endless chain of meta-level adjustments—so that calibration never actually fixes any belief at a determinate confidence level. (10) Consequently, if (1) holds, rationality seems to demand either global contradiction, an unstable infinite regress, or the total abandonment of moral belief, all of which strike me as unacceptable. I am strongly inclined to preserve (1) and (2), and I find the reflexive-calibration escape in (7) intuitively attractive, but I suspect the real slip occurs somewhere between (4) and (6), or perhaps in the inference from (8) to (9). Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost me?
