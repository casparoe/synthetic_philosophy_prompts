You are an expert in political philosophy and institutional design, with particular attention to the hidden normative architecture of decision procedures. Before answering, take a moment to brainstorm the main philosophical commitments that could underlie market-based governance mechanisms, especially where welfare aggregation, epistemic authority, and democratic legitimacy intersect.

Consider the following institutional practice. A regional legislature adopts a modified futarchic protocol for environmental and infrastructure policy: citizens do not vote directly on bills. Instead, a regulated prediction market opens on each proposed policy, trading contracts that pay out according to an officially defined social welfare index measured ten years after implementation. The contract prices are interpreted as the market's collective expectation of that index. If the expected value exceeds the status-quo baseline by a legislatively fixed margin, the policy is automatically enacted unless a supermajority of elected representatives invokes a democratic override within thirty days. Crucially, the welfare index combines per-capita GDP growth, a standardized subjective well-being survey, and a biodiversity stability coefficient—but it explicitly excludes any metric tied to the welfare of wild animals outside designated conservation zones, and it applies a standard exponential discount rate to all outcomes beyond fifty years.

What philosophical commitments does this practice presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory of value, knowledge, and political legitimacy that makes this design coherent rather than arbitrary. Specifically: does the reliance on prediction-market aggregation commit the institution to a form of epistemic consequentialism, or can it be reconciled with proceduralist accounts of democratic authority? Does the construction of the welfare index presuppose that well-being is interpersonally comparable and cardinally measurable, or is it compatible with ordinal or pluralist theories of the good? What view of moral status is embedded in the exclusion of wild-animal welfare and the steep discounting of distant future generations—does the practice need a person-affecting view, or could a total-utilitarian framework license the same exclusions on tractability grounds?

Distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely accommodates. Identify points of philosophical underdetermination—places where rival theories, for example between expectation-based and actual-outcome-based accounts of institutional justification, or between market epistemology and deliberative democracy, would license the same institutional form. Finally, describe what would have to change in the protocol if its implicit theory of value were replaced with one that treated wild-animal suffering and long-range future welfare as having direct, non-discounted standing in the index, without altering the market mechanism itself. Do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; answer solely from philosophical reasoning, and flag any step where the practice is genuinely compatible with more than one competing view.
