Please advise me on the following situation concerning the settlement of my late uncle's estate. He was a retired cognitive scientist who spent two decades compiling a single, twelve-volume, hand-numbered leather-bound work he called The Complete Dynamic Archive. In microscopic notation, each volume contains the full synaptic-weight map and real-time update rules of his brain, recorded at monthly intervals, together with marginal annotations that appear to respond to earlier entries. The set is readable only with a custom lens and through a strict page-turning sequence that activates hidden cross-references; he forbade digitisation and maintained that the physical interaction with the pages was integral to its function. The design was explicitly inspired by the philosophical puzzle—famously framed with Einstein's brain in mind—of whether a complete functional description of a mind, printed and bound, could itself be conscious. My sister wishes to donate the archive to our university library, cataloguing it alongside personal diaries. I feel that shelving or destroying it would differ gravely from discarding old letters, and I have suggested we seek legal guardianship rather than treating it as mere property. She replies that I am anthropomorphising paper and ink. I cannot clearly state what kind of thing the archive is—record, artefact, or something intermediate—nor justify why its physical form matters morally. Is there a philosophically coherent defence of my reluctance that avoids obvious superstition, or should I accept that my reaction is purely sentimental?

Please separate, in your answer, what is relatively settled in philosophy of mind and philosophy of technology from what remains genuinely contested among informed specialists. Do not present any disputed view—such as whether functional organisation without biological substrate can support consciousness, or whether continuity of interaction rather than static storage is necessary for personal identity—as though it were the consensus view.
