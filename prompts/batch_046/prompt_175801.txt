As a journalist covering the philosophy of medicine for a policy-minded audience, I am preparing an analytical feature on how health authorities navigate conditions that sit at the boundary of biological fact and social value. For this piece, I need a concrete conceptual instrument: please construct a reusable decision procedure — structured as an ordered sequence of tests with explicit stopping conditions, evidentiary thresholds, and clearly marked points where judgment replaces fact — that a municipal public-health review board could actually run during its monthly ninety-minute sessions to determine whether a proposed condition should be listed as a disease in the jurisdiction's health registry. The board has no philosophical consultants on staff and must defend its decision to both clinicians and disability advocates.

The procedure should take seriously both the naturalist claim that disease involves a deviation from species-typical biological functioning and the normativist claim that disease requires a state that undermines valued capabilities or well-being, but it must not allow one framework to silently absorb the other. Introduce a synthetic framing category — call it "environmentally contingent dysregulation" — defined as a physiological or psychological state that is statistically abnormal within a reference population yet may confer functional benefits in specific ecological or social contexts. The procedure must specify, at each step, what counts as adequate empirical input, where the inquiry stops with a negative determination, and where it must defer to an explicit normative vote rather than a technical finding.

Then apply the procedure to two proposals: first, a rare neurological variant that produces severe subjective distress exclusively in high-density urban settings but shows no impairment in other environments; second, a widespread physiological adaptation to sustained heat exposure that falls outside standard clinical reference ranges yet is associated with reduced metabolic morbidity over the lifespan. Include at least one case in which the procedure produces an outcome the board's clinical members are likely to resist — for instance, a negative disease classification that conflicts with patient advocacy demands — and identify precisely where the framework reaches its limit and cannot resolve the conflict without an external value judgment. Conclude by stating what kinds of disputes the procedure is structurally incapable of settling.

Keep the language formal, precise, and suitable for publication in a science-and-ideas magazine; do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations.
