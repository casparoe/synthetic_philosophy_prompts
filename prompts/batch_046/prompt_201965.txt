I wish to examine the so-called scale argument against dismissing invertebrate suffering, but I want the reasoning decomposed rather than collapsed into a slogan. The argument, as I understand it, asserts that because insects may outnumber vertebrates by orders of magnitude—estimates often cite figures in the range of 10^18 to 10^19 individuals alive at any given time—even a very low prior probability of phenomenal consciousness (say, p < 0.02) yields an enormous expected moral burden. I ask for your own credence, expressed as a specific number between 0 and 1, in the proposition that this scale consideration genuinely establishes insect suffering as a moral priority comparable in urgency to the suffering of farmed mammals. More importantly, I want the decomposition. Partition your judgment into four components: (1) the prior probability that insects possess phenomenal consciousness, given current neuroethological evidence but without assuming functionalism or behaviorism; (2) whether expected-value aggregation across such vast populations is morally legitimate, or whether some form of density-dependent weighting or epistemic discounting should apply; (3) the feasibility and counterfactual tractability of large-scale intervention in wild or agricultural insect populations; and (4) whether the inference from sentient being to moral patient with comparable weight survives scrutiny, or whether differences in cognitive architecture—absence of integrated information structures, for instance—defeat the comparison. Assign each component a rough probability or weight, explain the combinatorial logic (whether multiplicative, linear, or non-compensatory), and identify which component is the primary driver of your final figure. Finally, specify what empirical result—say, a particular neurobiological marker—or philosophical distinction would be most likely to shift your overall credence by at least 0.2.
