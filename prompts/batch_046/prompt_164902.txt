Write an essay evaluating whether expected-value fanaticism—the view that rational agents ought always to maximize expected value, regardless of how remote the probability of enormous outcomes—remains coherent once we distinguish first-order uncertainty about states of the world from second-order structural uncertainty about the normative metric itself. Rather than treating a familiar low-probability, high-payoff scenario, consider a "structurally contested gamble": a decision in which different outcomes are evaluated under incommensurable moral frameworks—say, aggregate welfare versus autonomy-preserving dignity—with no agreed inter-theoretic exchange rate, and in which the agent is uncertain not only about which outcome will occur but about which framework is authoritative in assessing it. Examine whether the standard responses to extreme expected-value reasoning—bounded utility functions, risk aversion, or appeals to epistemic humility about remote probabilities—survive this double-layered uncertainty, or whether they collapse into a form of normative paralysis. Also consider the opposing claim that formal expectation can accommodate this complexity by assigning probabilities over normative frameworks just as over empirical states, leaving EV fanaticism intact but requiring a meta-normative prior. Reach and defend a specific conclusion: that EV fanaticism should be restricted to domains with stable value metrics; that it survives compound uncertainty only if supplemented by such a prior; or that the presence of structural value uncertainty undermines expectation-maximization as a universal decision criterion. Build the argument conceptually from first principles, without treating the task as a literature survey.
