I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, filing three to four long-form features per week under deadline pressure and with an editor who must sign off on framing. Before I decide how to present a study, report, or expert claim, I need to know whether its real meaning sits in the explicit findings, in the packaging that instructs me how to read them, or in the deeper structural commitments that the text enacts without stating them. I'm working from the tripartite distinction developed in Douglas Hofstadter's Gödel, Escher, Bach: frame messages (the contextual instructions that govern interpretation), outer messages (the explicit propositional content), and inner messages (the self-referential or worldview-level commitments embedded in formal structure, methodological choice, or representational form).

Please design a reusable decision procedure—a sequence of ordered questions or tests I can run in roughly fifteen minutes—that classifies where the meaning of a scientific communication is actually located, and that flags conflicts among the three levels. For each step, specify the indicator I should examine, what counts as a sufficient answer, and where the step is a judgment call rather than a mechanical check. The procedure must handle cases where the frame message overstates or undermines the outer message, and cases where an inner message—say, a metaphysical commitment carried by the choice of statistical model, comparison group, or graphical representation—does work that the explicit claims disavow.

Apply the procedure to three cases. First, a hypothetical epidemiological study whose abstract uses cautious probabilistic language, but whose press release and headline framing present the result as proof that lifestyle factor X prevents disease Y. Second, a climate-model publication whose explicit results are ranges of probabilistic projections, but whose graphical choices—axis truncation, color saturation, absence of baseline comparisons—embed a narrative of deterministic inevitability. Third, construct a case in which the procedure identifies a strong inner message—for example, a reductionist metaphysical commitment carried by the formal structure of the study design—that I, as the journalist, would prefer not to be present in my story; explain precisely where the procedure tells me to stop and where my own editorial values must take over.

Conclude by stating explicitly what the procedure cannot do—whether it can adjudicate, for instance, whether a frame message is deceptive rather than merely emphatic—and flag any step that is essentially a value judgment dressed in procedural clothing.
