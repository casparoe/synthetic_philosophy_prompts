In my philosophy of physics seminar, students repeatedly invoke the slogan that "time is an illusion" whenever we discuss Gödel's rotating universe, using it either to dismiss worries about closed timelike curves or to claim that the absence of a global time coordinate dissolves the paradox. The phrase clearly does argumentative work for them, but I am not sure what precise thesis it expresses in this cosmological setting. Could you disambiguate it into a menu of distinct philosophical readings—distinguishing, for example, a metaphysical denial of temporal becoming; a structural claim that the Gödel metric lacks a global time function; an epistemic claim about the conventionality of temporal ordering under rotational symmetry; and a phenomenological claim about the subjectivity of duration? For each reading, indicate whether it is a trivial consequence of the mathematics, a substantive philosophical addition that Gödel or Einstein actually held, or a misreading that conflates coordinate freedom with ontological unreality. Further, specify which interpretations, if any, actually bear on whether the rotating universe is physically possible, as opposed to merely illustrating the formal flexibility of general relativity. I want the layout as a list I can bring to class, and I am particularly interested in whether any defensible reading requires importing commitments about presentism or eternalism that are not already contained in the metric itself.
