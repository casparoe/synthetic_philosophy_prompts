The long-running standoff between Searle’s insistence that original intentionality is an intrinsic, biologically grounded property of minds and Dennett’s reply—pressed through scenarios like the two-bitser, in which predictive success seems to license full intentional ascription without any biological “ground floor”—has produced little movement in decades. Each side rehearses familiar moves: Searle argues that all derived aboutness (texts, artifacts, simulated agents) remains parasitic on prior intrinsic aboutness in living brains; Dennett argues that adopting the intentional stance makes no principled distinction between original and derived cases when explanatory and predictive utility are on par. My working hypothesis is that the impasse is not mainly about ontology but about explanatory stopping conditions—Searle treating biological origin as the necessary terminus for genuine aboutness, Dennett treating stable predictive integration as sufficient—while both tacitly share an unstated “ground-floor” architecture that forces the dispute to look metaphysical when it may actually be methodological. Diagnose the stall concretely: is it verbal (centered on competing uses of “intrinsic” or “derived”), substantive but disguised (about the norms that govern cognitive-scientific explanation), or a conflation of two distinct questions—one about what minds are made of, one about when intentional explanation is warranted—pressed under a single heading? In particular, is there a coherent position that rejects the ground-floor model entirely, allowing intentionality to be thoroughly derived “all the way down” without paradox or circularity? Give me a committed answer: take a definite stance on what kind of disagreement this really is, defend it by engaging with what each side actually says about derivation, parasitism, and the stance, and tell me honestly whether anything—a result about neural mechanisms, a formal result about predictive modeling, or a conceptual distinction—could genuinely move the debate, or whether the structure of the disagreement makes persistence inevitable.
