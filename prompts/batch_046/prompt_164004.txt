I'm a novelist running a weekend craft workshop for other fiction writters — mostly people who do literary novels but never studied philosophy — and I want to spend one session on authorial intention versus what the text actually does. I'm tired of the usual death-of-the-author lecture they've heard a million times, so I'm injecting this weird idea I made up called the shadow manuscript: an author writes two versions of the same scene, one where the narrator lies and one where they tell teh truth, then destroys one but hides a private note saying which was real. The surviving text is word-for-word identical, but that hidden note changes what the writer intended.

Plan the teaching arc for me: the order the pieces have to come in so the hidden-note problem feels inevitable rather than contrived, the one concrete carrying example (maybe a short scene I should draft or adapt) and what it must have and lack, the misconceptions these writters will bring — like the reader always owns the meaning or my private intent is sacred property — and the exact point in the sequence to head off each one, the check at the end that shows they've grasped the tension rather than just picking sides, and what philosophical machinery I should definatly leave out and say so. No bibliography, no citations, no further reading — half of them wouldn't touch it anyway.
