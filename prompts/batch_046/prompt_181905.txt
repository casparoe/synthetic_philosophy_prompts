Below are notes for an essay I am drafting. I want to examine how epistemicism and supervaluationism about vagueness apply not to standard descriptive predicates like "bald" or "tall," but to normative ones like "unjust" or "exploited." I suggest that in the normative domain, epistemicism entails a precise moral boundary that agents cannot know, which is compatible with moral realism but threatens practical guidance: one might commit an injustice without any possible epistemic access to that fact. Supervaluationism, by contrast, allows genuinely indeterminate cases where no sharpening is correct, but I worry that extending its framework of admissible precisifications to ethics produces not mere semantic indeterminacy but a deontic gap—actions that are neither required, permitted, nor forbidden. I then ask whether this gap is a feature or a bug: it might capture the structure of moral dilemmas, yet it also risks rendering normative theory useless for decision-making. Please comment on whether my framing of the normative extension is coherent, whether I have mischaracterized either view's resources for practical reasoning under vagueness, and whether the distinction I draw between epistemic ignorance and deontic indeterminacy holds up or collapses into a familiar form of moral uncertainty.
