I defend a restricted version of van Fraassen's Reflection principle: it constrains present credences only when the anticipated future belief is formed through reasoning processes, evidential updates, or methods that my present self genuinely endorses. If I can predict with confidence that unavoidable social dynamics, institutional incentives, or predictable cognitive biases will cause me to hold belief B tomorrow—processes I regard as epistemically illegitimate—then knowing this future state imposes no Reflection-based obligation to align my current credence with B. On this view, Reflection binds across endorsed rational pathways but not across anticipated corruption. What would count decisively against this restriction? I want the map of its exposure: formal arguments that any such filter collapses diachronic coherence into something useless; empirical or decision-theoretic cases where maintaining belief against predictable future change was stable and successful; the objection that nearly all belief-formation involves some disendorsed influence, rendering my principle vacuous; and whether the position can be qualified away from every counterexample so thoroughly that it risks unfalsifiability—together with an honest assessment of whether that insulation is a vice for a normative principle of this kind. Please distinguish what standard Reflection defenders would themselves concede counts against my restriction, separate merely awkward predictions from genuinely fatal ones, and note whether there are scenarios in the literature on embedded agency or precommitment where my qualified view yields the wrong answer.
