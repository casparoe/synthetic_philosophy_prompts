I am drafting a novel about the archives of a fictional mid-century administrative state, and in researching the moral texture of bureaucratic reason I have found myself returning to the debate over whether Enlightenment rationality is the engine of progress or the architecture of domination—the line of argument associated with Zygmunt Bauman and the broader Historikerstreit on uniqueness, comparability, and modernity. To deepen my own understanding, and to share it with a small circle of fellow writers and artists, I am convening a workshop of ten people, meeting for ninety minutes around a single table, none of them professional philosophers. I want them to feel, through action rather than lecture, how an instrument conceived for human liberation—classification, measurement, the elimination of ambiguity—can generate dehumanization not as an accidental corruption but as an internal structural byproduct.

Please design a structured role-play or game with precise rules, roles, and a token-based payoff scheme that uses only paper, pens, and small personal stakes. In the first round, participants should act as rational administrators tasked with solving a simulated crisis—perhaps the equitable distribution of scarce resources across a fictional population—using only principles of clarity, efficiency, and categorical order. In the second round, they must govern the same population with the system they have built, and the rules should be arranged so that the same logical moves that solved the crisis now produce necessary exclusions or reductions of personhood, which the participants discover through the mechanics of play rather than through any facilitator’s announcement. The names Bauman, modernity, domination, and the specific historical events under debate should be withheld until the debrief.

Include a minute-by-minute timeline, the exact role assignments, the incentive structure that makes optimization feel consequential, and a debrief that maps each phase of the game onto the conceptual distinction between rationality as progress and rationality as domination. Be explicit about which feature of the philosophical position the activity cannot reproduce—whether the industrial scale of the violence, the question of historical uniqueness versus comparability with premodern cruelty, or the specific role of ideology in masking rational ends—and instruct me how to supply that missing element in the final discussion without collapsing into lecture. Finally, tell me what will predictably fail the first time I run the workshop—perhaps an early moral recoil that breaks the rules, or a confusion between role and self—and how to correct for it while preserving the force of the discovery.
