Exam question: Jon Elster's Ulysses-contract framework distinguishes precommitment from delegation. Apply this distinction to constitutional entrenchment of amendment rules, addressing Alf Ross's paradox of self-amendment. Does entrenching the amendment clause itself count as precommitment, or does it become delegation?

Student answer:
Elster distinguishes precommitment—where a rational agent binds his own future self—from delegation, in which decision rights are transferred to a different agent. In constitutional design, an entrenchment clause requiring, for instance, a supermajority for amendment operates as precommitment: the constituent moment restricts later majorities to prevent temporary passions from eroding fundamental commitments. Alf Ross noted a logical tension in self-amending provisions: if the rule governing amendment can itself be changed through the procedure it prescribes, the system appears to require an endless hierarchy of meta-rules to avoid circularity. By shielding the amendment rule from ordinary revision—making it alterable only through its own stringent procedure—the constitution fixes the meta-rule and dissolves Ross's tension without infinite regress. The familiar dead-hand objection—that future generations are bound without consent—is not an external critique but, on Elster's account, an internal boundary of the Ulysses model, since binding strangers is not authentic self-binding. Consequently, constitutional entrenchment is better understood as delegation to past generations than as pure precommitment, which means Ross's paradox is not resolved but merely displaced onto questions of original legitimacy. Thus the Ulysses analogy collapses exactly at the point where democratic authorization is required.
