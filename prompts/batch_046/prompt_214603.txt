I've been tracking a long-running philosophical exchange about Buddhist anattā and personal identity, and it feels stalled in a way I can't diagnose on my own. On one side are analytic philosophers who treat anattā as a reductionist bundle theory: they cite the skandha analysis and the chariot analogy in the Milindapañha to argue that there is no enduring substrate, only causally linked mental and physical events. On another side are Buddhist-studies scholars who say this framing imports a Cartesian "substantial self" that the tradition never posited in the first place; they insist that anattā targets specifically a permanent, unitary, autonomous ātman with svabhāva (inherent existence), not the conventional, dependently-originated person (puggala) recognized in moral and legal contexts. A third camp — some contemporary philosophers of religion — argues the whole debate is a category error: anattā is primarily soteriological, a practice-oriented denial meant to undermine clinging (upādāna), not an ontological thesis about identity conditions over time.

My working hypothesis is that these groups are not disagreeing about a single proposition but running three parallel disputes under one word — "self" — with different evidentiary standards: logical coherence for the reductionists, philological and doctrinal fidelity for the contextualists, and pragmatic efficacy for the soteriological readers. But I want an actual diagnosis rather than a survey.
