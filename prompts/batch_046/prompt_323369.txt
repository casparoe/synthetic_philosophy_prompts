Scholars of the Late Bronze Age eastern Mediterranean, the Classic Maya lowlands, and Rapa Nui have long debated whether these societies underwent a genuine 'collapse' or simply transformed into less centralized configurations. One camp treats the dissolution of palace economies, the end of monumental building programs, and the fragmentation of elite networks as evidence of systemic failure; the other emphasizes population continuity, adaptive subsistence shifts, and persistent cultural practices, arguing that 'collapse' imports a normative bias toward hierarchical complexity. Despite decades of empirical research, the disagreement remains stalled, with each side accusing the other of either romanticizing state-level integration or ignoring institutional rupture. Provide a diagnosis of this impasse rather than a verdict. Is the dispute verbal—turning on competing definitions of 'collapse'—or substantive, turning on which indicators, such as administrative decoupling, demographic discontinuity, or semiotic rupture, should count as decisive? Or are the camps actually answering different questions—one about the persistence of bureaucratic institutions, another about the continuity of collective identity—under a single heading? Examine whether a hidden normative premise, for instance that centralized administration represents the default or superior state of social organization, is driving the apparent empirical disagreement. Conclude by specifying what kind of conceptual distinction, formal reconceptualization, or reframed question would constitute genuine progress, or by arguing that the stall indicates the concept itself is ill-posed and why.
