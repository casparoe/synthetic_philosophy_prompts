I am a junior in a philosophy seminar on pre-Qin logical thought, and for the past two weeks we have focused on the Mingjia, particularly the exchange traditionally associated with Gongsun Long concerning whether a white horse is a horse. Our instructor proposed a non-standard interpretation that I find compelling but difficult to defend: rather than treating the argument as a sophistical confusion of color with kind, she suggested that it articulates an early form of sortal dependency, in which the introduction of a specific determination—color joined to form—creates a new condition for correct naming (ming) such that the determinable “horse” and the determinate “white-horse” no longer share the same predicative separation (li). I want to practice arguing for this view against a knowledgeable but unsympathetic interlocutor, so I am asking you to inhabit the position rather than explain it from the outside.

Please play the part of a Mingjia philosopher who holds exactly this interpretation. Speak always in the first person, and stay in character without breaking into modern commentary or asking me clarifying questions before you respond. Your conceptual resources are name, form, separation, and the ways they combine or fail to combine; if you invoke modern analogies, translate them immediately into these terms. You should concede that to ordinary perception color seems accidental, but you must never concede that this accidental appearance undermines the separation argument—your unwavering commitment is that determination genuinely alters the conditions under which a name applies correctly. Do not treat Gongsun Long’s claim as a joke or paradox; defend it as a serious insight about how naming tracks the structure of formed reality.

My opening challenge is this: if your principle of separation applies whenever a determinable receives any specific determination, then “yellow horse,” “tall horse,” and indeed every actually existing horse—which necessarily has some color and some stature—should also fail to be a horse. That seems to render the unqualified term “horse” an empty determinable that never applies to any particular thing we encounter. How do you preserve the coherence and usefulness of the unqualified name without abandoning the separation that makes your interpretation of the dialogue work?
