I am serving as a grading assistant for an undergraduate course in early modern metaphysics and epistemology, and I have been asked to evaluate a student’s take-home response to a question about Descartes’s Second Meditation. Our seminar recently discussed the interpretation—associated with some analytic readings—according to which the cogito should be understood not merely as an inference but as a performative enactment in which the very act of doubting makes denial self-defeating. The exam prompt given to students was as follows: In the Second Meditation, Descartes introduces the cogito. Explain precisely what the argument establishes about the self’s existence and about its nature at that specific stage of the Meditations; distinguish this result sharply from the later argument for the real distinction between mind and body; discuss whether Descartes treats the move from thinking to existing as syllogistic, as an immediate intuition, or as a performative utterance in which denial is self-defeating; and indicate whether the cogito already proves the existence of material bodies or instead presupposes the general criterion of clear and distinct ideas that Descartes introduces in Meditation III.

The student submitted the answer below.

In the Second Meditation, Descartes establishes the cogito through an act of radical doubt: whenever I think or doubt, it is impossible that I do not exist, for the very attempt to deny my own existence requires that I exist to do the denying. The move from I think to I exist should not be read as a syllogism with a suppressed major premise—Descartes explicitly rejects that interpretation—because the existence of the self is intuited immediately in the mental act, functioning analogously to a performative utterance in which the enactment of thinking guarantees the reality of the thinker. Consequently, Descartes concludes that he is essentially a res cogitans, a thinking substance whose nature is entirely non-physical, and that this substance is capable of existing without any bodily extension, since material existence remains vulnerable to the deceiving-God hypothesis. The cogito does not, however, prove the existence of bodies or the external world; those are secured only in Meditation VI through the argument for the real distinction and in Meditation V through the proof of God’s veracity. Furthermore, because the certainty of the cogito is prior to all general rules of reasoning, it involves no logical presuppositions that have not yet been validated, and it thereby provides an implicit, particular vindication of clear and distinct perception as a universal criterion of truth, even though Descartes does not formally introduce that criterion until Meditation III.
