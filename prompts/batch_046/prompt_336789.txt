Here is the exam question I assigned:

Lon Fuller argues in The Morality of Law (1964) that any functioning legal system must satisfy eight formal conditions—generality, promulgation, non-retroactivity, clarity, consistency, possibility of compliance, stability over time, and congruence between declared rules and official action—which together constitute what he calls the internal morality of law. H.L.A. Hart responds, most directly in his review of Fuller and with related remarks in The Concept of Law (1961, 2nd ed. 1994), that these conditions are merely instrumental requirements for social efficacy; a tyrant could satisfy all eight while issuing morally atrocious commands, so they establish no necessary conceptual link between law and morality. Evaluate whether Fuller's internal morality is best understood as (a) a moral constraint on legal validity, (b) a set of design specifications for rule-guided social coordination that carry no independent moral weight, or (c) something closer to Ronald Dworkin's later interpretive framework, in which moral principles are embedded in legal practice itself. In your answer, be precise about which texts contain which objections, and about whether Fuller's claim is purely procedural, substantively moral, or both.

Here is the student's answer, reproduced without correction:

Fuller lists eight conditions that law must meet, like being clear and not retroactive. He calls this the internal morality of law, which means that if a system fails all eight, it is not really law at all. Hart, in the postscript to the second edition of The Concept of Law, says these are just conditions for a law being effective. If a dictator uses clear, stable rules to oppress people, the rules still work, so there is no moral link. This is Hart's separability thesis. However, Fuller's view is really closer to Dworkin's idea of law as integrity, because Fuller's conditions require judges and officials to interpret rules in light of the underlying moral purpose of the legal system. So Fuller's internal morality is not just procedural; it has substantive moral content that constrains interpretation. Therefore Hart misses the point: he treats Fuller's conditions as mere efficiency tips, but they are actually interpretive moral principles embedded in legal practice, which is why Dworkin later adopted a similar framework in Law's Empire.
