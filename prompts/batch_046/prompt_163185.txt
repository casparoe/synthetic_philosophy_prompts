I'm looking for an established term—if one exists—for a specific asymmetry in how people treat everyday conventions like tipping, queuing, or jaywalking norms when applied to visitors versus locals. The pattern is this: when an outsider violates the convention, people tend to treat it as a straightforward moral failure, as if entering the context automatically binds one to the rule regardless of comprehension. But when an insider violates the same convention, people often excuse it precisely on the grounds that the insider grasps the convention's functional purpose and can judge when it fails to serve that purpose—so deeper understanding weakens, rather than strengthens, the obligation. I notice this in discussions about whether tourists should tip like Americans, or whether visitors to Tokyo should queue with Japanese precision even in empty stations. Is there an established philosophical concept for this kind of epistemic inversion in normative bindingness? How does it differ from standard views about tacit consent or from role-based expectations about visitors? If the literature has no name for it, what would be the right way to define its boundaries—especially to distinguish it from mere hypocrisy or from legitimate cultural expertise?
