As a journalist who covers philosophy of science and ideas, I recently observed a seminar exchange that went well beyond the standard textbook framing of Derek Parfit’s repugnant conclusion, and I found myself unable to referee it cleanly. The first speaker, Dr. Lena Okonkwo, defended a modified total-utilitarian aggregation she called “capacity-sensitive weighting”: rather than treating welfare as a linear scalar summed across lives, she proposed that the moral weight of an additional being be scaled by the cognitive and aesthetic complexity that being can realize, even when its net hedonic value is barely above zero. She argued that this blocks the classic repugnant conclusion, because a world of one hundred billion minimally pleased but maximally complex lives does not automatically dominate a world of ten billion profoundly flourishing lives; the scaling factor prevents mere multiplication of barely-positive lives from overwhelming the total. Her interlocutor, Dr. Samuel Kessler, replied that this move either smuggles in an arbitrary coefficient—saving the intuition only by fiat—or abandons welfare-based axiology for a hybrid perfectionism without admitting the shift. He pressed a reverse-repugnant objection: if complexity genuinely carries independent weight, then Okonkwo’s framework should also prefer replacing a vast population of moderately complex beings with a tiny elite of hyper-complex ones, a conclusion she seemed unwilling to accept. Okonkwo countered that Kessler’s charge of arbitrariness ignores the structural point that any plausible axiology requires some non-linear response to quality differences, and that her scaling function is no more ad hoc than the diminishing-returns curves already used in risk-weighted expected utility. Kessler then observed that this analogy fails, because in risk theory weights apply to probabilities over outcomes for the same agent, whereas here they apply across different lives, which demands an interpersonally comparable metric of complexity that no one has actually supplied. Okonkwo’s final response was that Kessler’s demand for a fully specified cardinal metric is a form of false precision: we routinely make comparative judgments about experiential richness across species and cultures without such a scale, and refusing to do so in population ethics is an unreasonable standard.

Please adjudicate this exchange point by point: where is Okonkwo correct, where is Kessler correct, and where are both missing something? Specifically, does her capacity-sensitive aggregation genuinely avoid the repugnant conclusion, or does it merely relocate the paradox into the weighting function? Does Kessler’s reverse-repugnant objection land, or does it conflate quality-weighting with elitism? And is there a deeper tension—perhaps involving the non-identity problem, the demands of impartiality, or the comparability of lives across radically different architectures—that neither speaker has addressed? I am not asking for a bibliography or a literature survey, but for a rigorous conceptual assessment of the arguments exactly as they stand, including what principle each party is implicitly committed to and which further case would force them to diverge.
