Consider the curatorial practice in contemporary history museums—particularly at post-conflict memorial sites—of displaying historical events through polyphonic label texts: multiple first-person voices (victims, perpetrators, bystanders, officials) appear side by side without an overarching synthesizing narrative, and the institution explicitly declines to adjudicate among them, even as it rigorously verifies every date, name, and location. What philosophical commitments about historical objectivity does this practice presuppose? Does it implicitly align with Leopold von Ranke's aspiration to present events as they actually occurred, or does it instead reflect E.H. Carr's claim that historical selection is constitutive and Hayden White's argument that narrative form precedes factual content? Reconstruct the implicit epistemology: does the refusal to synthesize presuppose that synthesis is impossible, undesirable, or merely outside institutional competence? And is the practice philosophically coherent, given that the selection and spatial arrangement of voices itself constitutes a framing narrative? Finally, imagine the practice were replaced by a single authoritative account authored by an expert panel; what commitments about knowledge, authority, and the ontology of the past would that shift reveal?
