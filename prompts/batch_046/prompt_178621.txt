Here are my verdicts on split-brain cases and the unity of consciousness, and I want to know whether a single principle can hold them together. In the classic commissurotomy patient, the left hemisphere verbally reports an object in the right visual field while the left hand, guided by the right hemisphere, points to a matching object in the left field; the speaking hemisphere then confabulates an explanation for the hand's movement. I judge that there are genuinely two concurrent streams of experience here, not one stream with hidden knowledge. In the Wada-test suppression of one hemisphere, the remaining hemisphere sustains coherent perception, memory, and emotional response on its own; I take this as evidence that each hemisphere can host a fully unified consciousness independently. At the same time, I think long-term split-brain patients often show globally shared emotional and arousal responses—fear conditioning, for instance, seems to transfer—so I resist saying there are literally two persons in every case; rather, there are two cognitive centers that can diverge in content while remaining partially coupled below the level of narrative integration. I also think that if one imagines a gradual callosotomy performed fiber-by-fiber over years, with the subject reporting not a sudden split but a slowly intensifying sense of "having two opinions about things," then unity is a matter of degree: consciousness can be more or less integrated rather than bifurcating at a precise boundary. Finally, I think that if inter-hemispheric communication were restored only by an artificial bridge that shared information without merging it into a single narrative center, the patient might report being "one person with two viewpoints"—functional coordination without true phenomenal unity. Is there a coherent account—perhaps in terms of integrated information, narrative ownership, or the distinction between access and phenomenal consciousness—that delivers all four judgments? If a simpler principle requires overturning one, which should go, and what does the best surviving principle predict for a case in which only the anterior commissure remains intact and verbal report is silent? Please also say what consideration or case would change your mind, and identify where your reasoning is most vulnerable to collapse.
