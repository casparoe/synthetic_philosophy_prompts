At a recent interdisciplinary workshop on cosmology and moral philosophy, two colleagues—both trained in ethics but working in different subfields—fell into a sustained disagreement that I observed as a bystander. I do not think either view is obviously correct, and I am interested in a precise adjudication rather than a general survey.

(1) The first colleague argued that if the universe is spatially infinite and approximately homogeneous, then any action I take—say, donating resources to famine relief—is performed infinitely often by my physical duplicates elsewhere. On a standard aggregative framework, the net moral difference my particular donation makes is therefore undefined (infinite good minus infinite good) or effectively zero, since I could just as easily have done nothing and still had infinitely many duplicates who acted. She concluded that in such a cosmology, individual moral choice is cosmically arbitrary: there is no principled reason to prefer one local action over its duplicate elsewhere, and the very idea of making a difference collapses.

(2) The second colleague replied that this confuses global ontology with local moral agency. Moral reasons, he said, are indexical: they bind the agent to the causal nexus she actually inhabits. The existence of causally disconnected duplicates does not dilute the obligation I have to the suffering person in front of me any more than the existence of other planets dilutes it. He added that aggregation across causally isolated regions is not required by any plausible normative theory; even utilitarianism typically applies to the domain over which an agent can exercise influence.

(3) The first colleague pushed back. She granted that local obligations exist, but claimed they become incoherent at the margins. If I have a reason to prevent suffering in my local region, and the universe contains infinitely many equally suffering regions beyond my reach, then the total amount of suffering I leave unaddressed is infinite regardless of my local action. She argued that this generates a kind of moral paralysis: any local effort is infinitesimal relative to the infinite remainder, and without a principled cutoff for where my obligations end, I am either arbitrarily privileging my neighborhood or facing an impossible infinite demand. She said this is not merely a utilitarian problem but applies to any view that takes moral reasons to be universal in scope.

(4) The second colleague responded that this infinite remainder objection proves too much. By the same logic, he said, any finite agent in a large but finite world also faces an overwhelming number of strangers she cannot help; yet we do not conclude that local charity is arbitrary. The relevant distinction, he insisted, is not between finite and infinite unaddressed suffering, but between suffering that falls within an agent's effective causal and epistemic reach and suffering that does not. He further noted that if her argument worked, it would also undermine the rationality of any local preference—say, choosing tea over coffee—since infinitely many duplicates make the opposite choice elsewhere, which she clearly does not intend.

Please adjudicate this disagreement move by move: rate each of the four moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with brief justification for each rating. Then state who is right about which specific point, and identify what, if anything, both parties are missing. In particular, address whether the first colleague's argument genuinely threatens all normative theories or only aggregative ones, and whether the second colleague's appeal to causal reach adequately resolves the arbitrariness worry without merely assuming what needs to be shown.

Please say how confident you are in each adjudication and single out the point about which you are least sure.
