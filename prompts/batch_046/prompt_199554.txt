I'm working through where eliminative materialism about beliefs and desires actually has unsettled work to do—not the standard historical survey, but the live conceptual frontier. In discussions with colleagues in philosophy of mind and cognitive science, four lines keep coming up without anyone clearly ranking them. There's the scope problem: whether eliminativism should apply to all propositional attitudes—beliefs, desires, intentions—or only a subset, and whether phenomenal consciousness is left untouched or also eliminated by parity of reasoning. There's the replacement problem: whether a mature neuroscience can actually deliver a vocabulary precise enough to replace folk-psychological explanation in everyday social cognition and scientific practice, or whether we are permanently stuck with a pragmatic hybrid. There's the moral-practical problem: whether eliminating belief-desire psychology genuinely forces revision of moral responsibility, legal blame, or social coordination practices, or whether these can be reconstructed on behavioral, predictive, or purely neurofunctional grounds without loss. And there's the interpretation problem: how eliminativism should guide our attribution of mental states to artificial systems that behave intelligently but whose architectures share almost nothing with human neural organization.

If you were advising a researcher with about three years, a background in philosophy of science rather than experimental neuroscience, and an interest in how conceptual shifts in psychology affect normative practice—which of these four should be prioritized, and in what dependency order? I want a defended agenda, not a survey: for each line, is it upstream or downstream of the others? Is resolving the scope problem genuinely necessary before the moral-practical and interpretation questions can be pursued, or can a "local eliminativism" about specific domains proceed independently? Which questions are tractable with current conceptual tools, which depend on empirical neuroscience we do not yet have, and which should be deprioritized because their resolution would not substantially change either scientific practice or our normative institutions? Please defend the ordering with explicit criteria—tractability, downstream dependence, and practical significance—rather than just listing them.
