I sit on the Interpretive Standards Committee of the National Museum of Material History, and we must circulate a formal opinion before Thursday’s vote on the “Stratified Inference Protocol” proposed for the Kharra settlement exhibition (a hypothetical late Neolithic complex, c. 3200–2800 BCE, with no textual record). The protocol seeks to reconstruct specific institutional beliefs—above all, a system of distributed custodianship over surplus grain—exclusively from material traces: the spatial clustering of standardized storage vessels, uniform wear gradients on communal grinding implements, and the ritualized deposition of fractured ceramic seals in boundary ditches. Its central methodological innovation is counterfactual stability testing, which asks what collective rules of ownership and obligation would need to obtain for these object distributions to remain stable across generations in the absence of centralized enforcement mechanisms. Proponents argue this avoids naive analogical projection onto mute artifacts; critics on our committee contend that it commits what they term the institutional projection fallacy, smuggling modern organizational concepts into archaeological silence and treating the mere absence of private hoarding markers as positive evidence for a shared collective belief. Because the exhibition design already embeds these interpretations in its gallery labels and public-facing narrative, our recommendation—whether to approve the protocol outright, approve it subject to explicit epistemic constraints that the research team could actually revise for, or reject it for this exhibition—will carry immediate curatorial and funding consequences. Please draft the Committee’s written opinion: state a concrete, actionable bottom line; explain precisely how the inference from object pattern to institutional belief is warranted or unwarranted in this case; and treat the strongest version of the opposing view with genuine seriousness rather than restating it only to dismiss it.
