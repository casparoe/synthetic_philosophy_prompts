im grading a phil exam on whether real moral dilemmas exist. the question was: 'critique or defend the view that genuine moral dilemmas are impossible, using the distinction between directive residue (where an unfulfilled obligation keeps normative pull) and affective residue (just emotional aftermath). apply this to a surgeon with one unit of blood who must choose between two trauma patients with identical need, and discuss whether Ross's prima facie/all-things-considered framework actually eliminates dilemmas or just hides them.'

student answer (~200 words, paraphrased):

Genuine dilemmas are impossible because obligations can't contradict. Ross shows prima facie duties like beneficence can conflict, but weighing them yields one all-things-considered duty, so there's never two real obligations at once. The surgeon has a prima facie duty to both patients but only one actual duty after weighing urgency. Bernard Williams said we feel agent-regret after hard choices, and he took this to be directive residue—proof the lost obligation still binds us. But agent-regret is really affective residue, just feeling bad, so Williams is wrong. The ought-implies-can principle, which Kant invented, means if you can't do both you aren't obligated to both. So the surgeon picks one, and any leftover guilt is psychology not morality. Therefore Ross eliminates dilemmas, and Williams's argument fails.
