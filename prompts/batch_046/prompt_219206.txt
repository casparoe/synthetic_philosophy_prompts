Having spent three decades in experimental physics before turning to philosophy in retirement, I find debates about actual infinity simultaneously familiar and disorienting: quantum field theory integrates over infinite-dimensional spaces without ceremony, yet philosophers treat Hilbert’s hotel and Cantor’s argument as live metaphysical puzzles rather than formal conveniences. I am preparing structured notes for a small study group of retired scientists with no professional philosophical training, and I want a diagnostic taxonomy—a decision tree whose nodes are concrete commitments, not labels—to sort people by what they actually believe about completed infinities. The tree should begin with whether one accepts a definite, completed totality of all natural numbers; then whether Hilbert’s hotel reveals a genuine paradox or only a counterintuitive but consistent feature of infinite sets; then whether Cantor’s theorem establishes a real hierarchy of cardinalities or is an artifact of formal comparison rules; then whether one’s stance applies symmetrically to mathematics and to physical reality (for instance, infinite divisibility of space, an infinite past, or infinite degrees of freedom in a field); and finally whether any rejection of actual infinity is driven by ontological impossibility or by epistemic inaccessibility (we can never verify a completed infinite). Each branching question must be phrased so that someone can answer it without already knowing which camp—Aristotelian potentialist, strict finitist, Cantorian Platonist, Hilbert-style formalist, physical-finitist/mathematical-actualist, moderate cardinal actualist, or an unnamed hybrid—it points toward. Every leaf should carry either a standard position name or the mark “unnamed” together with a one-sentence statement of its most awkward commitment. To keep this from being answerable by lifting an encyclopedia entry, inject one niche but meaningful distinction into the tree: ask whether accepting the Axiom of Choice in infinite contexts is necessary to generate the hotel paradox, or whether an analogous paradox persists in a choiceless framework, since this separates formalists who reject choice from realists who embrace it. Please also flag any pair of leaves your tree cannot genuinely separate, and diagnose any combination of answers that seems unoccupied—is it incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored? Finally, indicate what specific argument or consideration about the relationship between mathematical formalism and physical ontology would cause you to reorder the branches or merge two leaves, and flag where your reasoning is most likely to fail, particularly if your separation of epistemic and ontological objections collapses under scrutiny.
