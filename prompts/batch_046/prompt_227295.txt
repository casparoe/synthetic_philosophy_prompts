Two people I know have been arguing about whether human beings genuinely possess free will, and they keep returning to the behavior of a particular burrowing wasp that repeats an identical inspection sequence in its nest; if researchers shift the surroundings even slightly, the wasp begins the entire routine over from step one rather than adapting. They refer to this kind of mechanical rigidity as sphexishness, and one of them insists that whenever a person acts in an equally fixed way—say, automatically reaching for the same product out of habit despite clear evidence that it no longer suits the situation—that person is not exercising free will in that moment, because real freedom requires the capacity to break the pattern. The other agrees that pure rigidity undermines freedom, but contends that pure, unstructured flexibility is just randomness rather than choice, and he proposes what he calls nested rigidity: humans maintain rigid commitments at a high level—core values, long-term projects, or identity-defining goals—while remaining flexible about the particular steps taken to pursue them. The first person responds that this merely relocates the problem upward. If those core commitments themselves were formed by upbringing, neurochemistry, and social conditioning in ways the person never selected, then the flexibility within them is nothing more than a predetermined script playing inside a predetermined frame, and labeling that frame “mine” does not make it free.

He then introduces a further idea he terms second-order sphexishness. Rather than asking whether actions are rigid, he asks whether the rules for revising one’s own rules are rigid. He claims that human cognition operates with a biologically fixed threshold—an essentially hard-wired level of discomfort or contradiction that must be reached before a belief or habit is actually updated—and that this threshold itself never changes. So even apparent acts of self-correction are mechanically triggered once internal error exceeds a constant value X. The first person says that if this is correct, then free will is illusory at every level, which supports her view rather than his. He disagrees, arguing that the ability to observe and describe one’s own revision threshold demonstrates a kind of standing outside it, which is precisely what freedom consists in. She then asks whether an advanced artificial system that monitors its own prediction errors and updates its goals whenever they cross a programmed boundary would also “stand outside” its programming, and whether he would therefore grant that system free will. He admits the parallel is deeply uncomfortable but maintains that human subjective experience distinguishes the cases, though he cannot specify exactly how.

Can you adjudicate this disagreement point by point? Is flexibility at the level of specific actions sufficient to preserve free will, or does rigidity at the level of values, goals, or revision-rules destroy it regardless? Is second-order sphexishness a meaningful distinction or merely determinism described at a higher order of abstraction? And in the comparison with an adaptive artificial system, what is each party missing—does subjective experience genuinely break the analogy, or does the analogy expose a gap in the defense of human freedom?
