I spent my career in biostatistics and have lately been reading about lottocracy—the proposal to replace elected legislatures with bodies selected by lot. The usual defense is statistical: a stratified random sample should mirror the population. Yet I keep returning to a problem familiar from longitudinal studies: once subjects interact, the sample transforms itself. Preferences shift endogenously through deliberation, so the assembly no longer estimates the polity it was drawn from. Write a Petrarchan sonnet—octave, volta at line nine, sestet, closing couplet—tracking exactly this tension. Use the octave to establish the lot as an unbiased estimator of popular will; let the volta occur when the assembled citizens begin reasoning together; in the sestet, ask whether they now represent a people that does not yet exist. Do not resolve the paradox in the couplet—hold it open, precisely calibrated. The verse should be technically sound enough that a student of democratic theory recognizes the distinction between descriptive representation and deliberative transformation, not merely a generic hymn to random selection. Afterward, include a brief note stating how confident you are that deliberation necessarily erodes representativeness, single out the claim about which you are least certain, and indicate where the poem takes liberties with the concept or relies on a contested interpretation—especially regarding whether stratified sampling can ever be designed to anticipate endogenous preference change.
