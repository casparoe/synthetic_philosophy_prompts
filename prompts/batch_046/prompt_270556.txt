I am a second-year undergraduate currently taking HIST302: Historiography and Historical Method, and I also serve as a marking assistant for the first-year survey course From Rome to the Renaissance. Every week I must evaluate around thirty short essays (1,000–1,200 words) in which students explain a historical transition—such as the shift from manuscript to print culture or the transformation of agrarian economies—yet almost every submission treats the past as an incomplete draft of the present. Earlier societies are measured by how closely they approximate modern liberal norms, technological efficiency, or democratic institutions, and the narrative arc is almost always one of progress towards us.

My lecturer wants me to flag this Whig tendency consistently, but I need something more precise than an intuitive warning. Please act as an expert critical historiographer and an assessment-design consultant. Before you construct anything, please think carefully and begin with a brief brainstorm: identify the three most common ways first-year writers smuggle presentist teleology into essays about pre-modern societies, and distinguish which of these can be caught by an objective procedural check from those that necessarily involve a value judgement about what counts as legitimate historical explanation.

Then, design a reusable, step-by-step evaluation protocol—expressed as a flowchart in words—that I can apply to any essay in under eight minutes, working alone at my desk without consulting the lecturer except when required. The protocol should translate the anti-Whig critique into three sequential tests—the Teleological Residue Scan, the Normative Asymmetry Check, and the Agentive Reconstruction Test—specifying for each:

- the exact question to ask;
- what counts as a sufficient answer (the pass condition);
- the point at which the protocol stops with a clear “no flag” verdict;
- the point at which it stops with a “flag required” verdict;
- the precise conditions that force escalation to the course lecturer because the issue is one of values rather than detectable anachronism.

Keep what the critique actually demands—no anachronistic moral weighting, no treatment of the present as the hidden telos of the past, recognition of historical agency on its own terms—while simplifying it for a non-specialist marker under time pressure. Include explicit guidance on hard cases, such as essays that use neutral language yet embed hidden structural bias, and be honest about where the protocol will misfire.

Please apply the protocol to three concrete essay scenarios:

1. An essay arguing that the decline of European serfdom was “inevitable because modern human rights were waiting to be recognised”, framing manumission as the unfolding of a moral truth already present.
2. An essay praising medieval craft guilds for restricting market competition, but judging them primarily by whether they anticipated modern welfare-state protections, thereby treating pre-modern economic life as deficient for lacking bureaucratic safety nets.
3. One case where the protocol yields a result you expect I will dislike—for example, an essay that employs anti-teleological language throughout yet still receives a flag because of a subtle normative asymmetry in its comparative framework. Explain why the flag is technically correct according to the procedure, even if I might sympathise with the essay’s political commitments.

Finally, state clearly what this protocol cannot do: which legitimate historical arguments it might falsely flag, what kinds of hidden bias lie outside its reach, and why it can never fully replace human judgement.

Present everything in polished British English (for example, “judgement”, “programme”, “centre”), and do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or requests for citations, as I have no access to external databases while marking.
