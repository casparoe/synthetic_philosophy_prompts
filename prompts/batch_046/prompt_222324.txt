I serve as chair of a nine-member allocation board for a regional cooperative that distributes an annual pool of roughly $400,000 among competing community infrastructure proposals. Our charter requires that the final ranking emerge from an explicit aggregation of members' individual preference orderings over the proposals, not from my unilateral decision or from unstructured deliberation alone. Over the past two funding cycles we have encountered Condorcet cycles—proposal A ranked above B, B above C, and C above A—and we have also observed that dropping a clearly losing proposal from the ballot reverses the ordering of the top two finalists, which members regard as illegitimate. I understand these phenomena fall within the scope of Arrow's impossibility theorem, but I need something more operational than the abstract result: a reusable decision procedure that my board can execute in a single two-hour meeting with only the submitted ballots and a printed checklist.

Please design that procedure by translating Arrow's four conditions—unrestricted domain, Pareto efficiency, independence of irrelevant alternatives (IIA), and non-dictatorship—into a sequence of concrete checks that a non-specialist board member can apply. Present it as a flowchart in words: the order of verification, what counts as a passing result for each check, the point at which the procedure outputs a collective ranking, the point at which it refuses to rank and explains why, and the precise juncture at which the conflict must be handed back to human judgment because it is a question of values rather than arithmetic. Include a small set of test ballot patterns or sentences that members can use at each step to determine whether the condition is satisfied. Then run the procedure on three concrete proposal sets: first, four proposals with an unambiguous Condorcet winner and no cycles; second, five proposals that generate a rock-paper-scissors cycle among the top three together with an IIA violation triggered by removing a weak fourth proposal; and third, a case in which all members unanimously rank the same proposal first (so Pareto is satisfied) but one member's idiosyncratic pairwise comparison introduces a cycle that makes the remaining unanimous preferences depend entirely on that single input—meaning that avoiding dictatorship forces a choice between sacrificing Pareto or sacrificing IIA. For the second and third cases, state exactly where the procedure stops and what answer it delivers, even if that answer is one I will dislike, such as declaring that no legitimate aggregation exists or identifying a de facto dictator.

In your explanation, clearly separate what is settled in social choice theory from what remains genuinely contested—particularly whether IIA should be treated as a normative requirement or merely as a technical convenience, and whether restricted-domain solutions genuinely solve the problem or simply redefine it away—without presenting any contested interpretation as the consensus view. Also state explicitly what the procedure cannot do: whether it will miss certain aggregation pathologies, whether it may certify a ranking that still strikes members as wrong, and whether there are philosophical disputes about the conditions themselves that undermine the procedure's authority in practice.
