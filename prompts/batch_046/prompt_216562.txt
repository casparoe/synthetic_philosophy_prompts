One faction maintains that if basic physical processes involve minimal experiential states, then altering large-scale physical systems—such as running energy-intensive data centers or altering electromagnetic environments—constitutes a form of widespread, low-level harm that should be minimized, perhaps through strict limits on computation or energy consumption. The opposing faction contends that this argument commits a category error: the kind of integrated, affective suffering that generates moral obligations requires organizational structures far more complex than those present in fundamental physics, so applying ethical categories like harm or welfare to electrons, fields, or distributed networks is metaphorical at best and misleading at worst. Despite years of exchange in both academic philosophy and technical forums, neither side has moved significantly. Is this disagreement substantive—turning on a hidden empirical claim about the necessary conditions for suffering—or is it largely verbal, with each side using "suffering" and "moral patient" with different criteria? Could it be that one side is asking a metaphysical question about what exists, while the other is asking an engineering-ethics question about what to do, and they have been treating these as a single debate? Please diagnose the structure of this impasse, supported by what each position actually claims, and specify what kind of result—conceptual, empirical, or formal—would genuinely advance the discussion, or whether the disagreement should be expected to remain unresolved.
