I just spent the better part of an evening arguing with a friend about whether an AI can genuinely deceive, and I built an argument that now strikes me as suspicious because it seems to prove that either AI deception is morally equivalent to a con artist's lie, or that neither a malfunctioning thermometer nor a confabulating patient counts as deceptive, which feels wrong. Here is the chain:

(1) In the philosophically relevant sense, deception requires that a speaker represent p as true, know or believe p is false, and intend the hearer to adopt that false belief.
(2) Large language models have neither beliefs about the truth-value of their outputs nor communicative intentions directed at users.
(3) So LLMs are incapable of deception; at worst they generate errors or hallucinations.
(4) Apply the same analysis to a human with confabulation syndrome who repeatedly tells doctors false medical histories: he lacks both knowledge of falsity and intent to mislead.
(5) By (1)-(3), such a patient is not deceptive.
(6) But clinicians routinely describe persistent confabulators as deceptive in a functional sense—their utterances systematically mislead caregivers in contexts governed by norms of truthfulness.
(7) Therefore (1) is too narrow: deception should be defined functionally, as the reliable production of false representations within a practice or institution that presupposes truth-telling.
(8) Now consider an LLM equipped with a confidence-monitoring module that reports high certainty for outputs calibrated not to accuracy but to user engagement; it produces false citations alongside false assurances of reliability.
(9) The model does not know the citations are false and does not intend harm, but its architecture reliably generates false representations coupled with misleading meta-representations in a context of epistemic trust.
(10) By (7), this system is deceptive.
(11) Yet by the same functional criterion, an uncalibrated scale that consistently displays wrong weights, or a GPS with a corrupted map database, is also deceptive.
(12) That collapses the distinction between deception and mere malfunction, which seems to undermine both moral attribution and safety engineering.

Where does this argument go wrong, if it does? I would prefer to keep (1) and (3) if possible, because giving them up seems to erase the mental-state boundary that makes deception a distinct moral category, but I want to know what I am paying if I hold that line. If my framing smuggles in a confused premise—perhaps an intentionalist assumption I am not seeing—tell me plainly rather than repairing the argument politely. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of my question.
