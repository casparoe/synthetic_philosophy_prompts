I am writing a feature for a magazine that covers science and ideas, examining what becomes of Einstein's hole argument when it is moved from continuous manifolds into discrete causal-set theories, where a "hole" denotes a region with a fixed causal boundary but no internal causal relations. I need a diagnostic decision tree—not a catalogue of named schools—that sorts interpretive stances by concrete commitments alone. The tree should open with the question: does the absence of internal causal links within such a bounded region generate a real physical ambiguity about which discrete events are present, or is the ambiguity only in our representation? From there, branch according to (i) whether discrete events have intrinsic identity apart from causal relations, (ii) whether gauge freedom in the discrete structure can be removed without sacrificing predictive content, and (iii) whether any resulting failure of determinism is ontic or merely epistemic. The tree should contain about six leaves, including reinterpretations for the discrete case of the familiar smooth-manifold positions—manifold substantivalism, relationalism, and sophisticated substantivalism—alongside any stances unique to the discrete setting. Every leaf should state the view's standard label (or "unnamed" if it has no defenders) and, in one sentence, its most awkward commitment. Combinations that are logically possible but unoccupied must be noted and briefly diagnosed—whether incoherent, unappealing, or simply unexplored. The intended reader is educated but non-specialist, so keep the language precise yet accessible, and do not include bibliographies, citation lists, or suggested readings. Present the taxonomy as nested bullet points. Please finish with a single line beginning "Summary:" that gives the number of leaves and the count of unoccupied branches.
