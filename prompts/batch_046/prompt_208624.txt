Question: Explain why epiphenomenalism faces a special problem with judgments about cognitive phenomenology (the 'what-it's-like' of thinking, e.g., suddenly seeing why a proof works). How does the standard common-cause reply try to solve it? And does that reply secretly turn epiphenomenalism into type-B physicalism?

Answer: Epiphenomenalism holds that conscious states have no physical effects. The paradox is that if my feeling of understanding a proof doesn't cause anything, then my saying 'now I get it' is just caused by neurons. So I'm not actually reporting the feeling; I'm just emitting sounds triggered by brain chemistry. For cognitive phenomenology this is worse because the feeling seems tied to the reasoning itself, but it's still just a byproduct.

The common-cause reply says one brain event produces both the phenomenology and the verbal report. Leibniz originally proposed this with pre-established harmony: the mental and physical run in parallel because God synchronized them. So my report tracks the feeling not because the feeling causes it, but because both come from the same neural computation. That saves knowledge — correlation via common cause is enough.

But this actually collapses into type-B physicalism. If the brain state is doing all the work, and the phenomenal property is just along for the ride, then saying the judgment is 'about' the feeling is like saying smoke is about fire. The feeling isn't in the causal chain, so it can't be what the judgment refers to. Therefore the reply doesn't defend epiphenomenalism; it just admits that phenomenal properties are identical to physical ones we haven't mapped yet. So the paradox is solved only by giving up epiphenomenalism.
