In revising the historical architecture of my novel, I have adopted a narrative form I call concurrent focalization: the years 1945 through 1949 are rendered in parallel strands, each anchored to a different causal origin—Washington's 1947 doctrinal crystallization, Moscow's 1945 security-perimeter logic, and Warsaw's postwar consolidation—so that no single chronology governs the others. From this formal decision, I drew what now appears to be a philosophical overreach: I concluded that asking who started the Cold War is a category mistake, because I presumed that moral blame requires a singular linear origin, and concurrent focalization demonstrates that such origins are artifacts of narrative sequencing rather than properties of the historical events themselves. Yet my intuition refuses to follow. Even within these parallel strands, Truman's articulation of containment chronologically precedes Stalin's institutionalization of the Cominform and the Berlin blockade; this temporal priority seems to restore a form of first-mover liability that my narrative architecture was designed to disperse. I am therefore suspended between two possible confusions. Either historiographical framing genuinely undermines the coherence of initiating blame, in which case my lingering attachment to chronology is merely sentimental residue; or blame operates structurally independent of how we sequence causation, in which case my concurrent focalization is only stylistic ornament, and the question of initiating responsibility remains intact, merely requiring a multiply indexed answer rather than a singular one. Which error is the more fundamental? More precisely, does distributing historical causation across non-convergent timelines eliminate the validity of assigning initiating responsibility altogether, or does it only show that such responsibility must be parsed in a different register without being abolished?
