I want to resist that collapse, but not by assertion. The scenario should be realistic—no science-fictional brains, perfect predictors, or exotic physics. Use a concrete institutional setting: a modular hospital triage system composed of four autonomous components (diagnosis, resource allocation, ethics review, and patient communication). Each component maintains running models of the other three and of itself, but there is no master database, no root node, and no single symbol or register that denotes "this system" globally. Instead, self-reference is achieved through a closed loop of mutual pointers: diagnosis models ethics modeling resources modeling communication modeling diagnosis, with no terminus and no central aggregator.

Your deliverable must include:

A demonstration that Hofstadter's strange-loop account, interpreted as requiring a singular symbol activated across hierarchical levels to represent the whole system to itself, delivers the verdict that this system lacks an "I"—not because recursion is missing, but because the loop lacks the specific centralization that allows one symbol to stand for the entire loop.

An honest account of how a defender of the pure-recursion view would attack your case—likely by arguing that the distributed ring is functionally just one symbol physically spread across nodes, or that functional equivalence renders the distinction moot—and an assessment of whether that attack succeeds.

Keep the reasoning conceptual and self-contained. Do not include bibliographies, citation requests, or appeals to obscure literature. The point is to see whether the distinction between a strange loop and mere recursion can be made to do real work in a designed case, not to survey existing commentary.
