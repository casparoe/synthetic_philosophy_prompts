Exam question: Al-Ghazali argues that the connection between cause and effect in the created world is not necessary in itself, and that God is the direct agent behind every event. Yet he also accepts that medicine cures illness and that fire burns cotton, treating these outcomes as highly reliable rather than arbitrary miracles. Explain how Al-Ghazali reconciles the denial of inherent causal power in created things with the observed regularity of nature. In your answer, distinguish his position from (a) Hume’s account of causation as constant conjunction lacking both underlying power and divine guarantee, and (b) the stricter Ash’arite kalam view that refuses to assign any explanatory or intermediate role to created things. Be precise about what Al-Ghazali denies, what he retains, and whether he permits any form of secondary causation.

Student answer:
Al-Ghazali’s occasionalism denies that fire, medicine, or any other created entity possesses an intrinsic power to produce effects. When cotton burns, God creates the burning directly; the fire is present but exercises no efficacy of its own. Nevertheless, Al-Ghazali does not regard nature as chaotic, because God has established a habitual order (sunan) in which certain events regularly follow others. This theistic grounding distinguishes him from Hume, who also rejects necessary causal connections but explains them solely as mental habits formed through repeated observation, without invoking God to secure the pattern. Al-Ghazali further differs from the Ash’arite theologians: whereas they reject any causal or explanatory role for intermediaries, Al-Ghazali permits created things to function as secondary causes in a delegated or instrumental sense. The fire and the medicine are genuine instruments through which God works, so that we can correctly say that fire burns and medicine heals, provided we understand that all efficacy is ultimately divine. Thus his view combines occasionalism with a qualified acceptance of secondary causation, making it closer to an instrumentalist theory than to the pure denial of natural causality found in strict kalam.
