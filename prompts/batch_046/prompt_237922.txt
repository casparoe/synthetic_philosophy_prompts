I do not have any formal training in philosophy, but I have spent considerable time thinking on my own about moral uncertainty—the challenge of acting well when one is genuinely unsure which moral framework is correct. Most of what I have encountered treats this uncertainty as a choice between large-scale theories, such as consequentialism and deontology. I am interested in a narrower, more internal ambiguity. Imagine a decision in which one course of action risks violating a deontological constraint—specifically, the prohibition on treating a person merely as a means—while the other course avoids that risk but yields significantly less overall welfare. The difficulty is that the boundary conditions that trigger the constraint are themselves uncertain: the affected individual may be unaware of being used, there may be no genuinely available alternative course of action, and it is contested whether awareness or the presence of alternatives are necessary for the prohibition to apply. So the uncertainty is not about which grand theory is true, but about whether, within a broadly deontological outlook, the specific conditions for a rights-violation are satisfied.

Write an essay addressing the following controversy. When a decision-maker faces this kind of boundary-condition uncertainty—where a rights-violation is possible but not assured, and the facts that would settle its presence are epistemically opaque—should the decision-maker default to the option that avoids the potential violation, or should they apply expected-value reasoning across the competing interpretations of the boundary conditions? Weigh the principal arguments on each side. One line of thought holds that if a severe rights-violation has non-zero probability, it dominates the moral calculus regardless of the welfare gap. Another line holds that defaulting away from the best-supported interpretation of the boundary conditions is a failure of practical reasoning, particularly when the affected party is unaware and no alternative exists. Reach a definite conclusion and justify it fully. Do not conclude with an agnostic or non-committal position; defend a specific verdict on how moral uncertainty ought to be resolved in cases of ambiguous boundary conditions.
