I facilitate a philosophy seminar of about twelve graduate students and advanced undergraduates—most have some exposure to decision theory and counterfactual reasoning—meeting for a single 90-minute session. We have already worked through the canonical moral-luck literature in prior weeks, and I am tired of the discussion staying taxonomic. I want an in-class simulation that makes the conflict between the control principle and counterfactual robustness feel visceral: the intuition that moral assessment should depend not only on what actually happened, but on whether an agent’s choice was stable across nearby possible worlds.

Design a structured, no-lecture-first activity—part collaborative protocol design, part role-play allocation game—in which the group first builds a shared decision rule (for example, a safety-check or resource-allocation procedure) with full mutual knowledge of every reasoning step. Then, without anyone altering their intentions or processes, a hidden stochastic node—drawn randomly from a pool of failure modes that the participants themselves authored during the construction phase—determines which branch of the protocol realizes harm and for which assigned role. After the outcome is revealed, participants must distribute a fixed pool of reputation points and propose concrete sanctions—exclusion, restitution, public correction—across roles, knowing that every role followed the identical reasoning process but only some were on the realized harm branch.

Please include the exact round structure with time allocations; role cards that conceal outcome-relevant information without concealing reasoning; a payoff and reputation scheme using points and social standing rather than money; rules that prevent post-hoc rationalization, such as written justifications locked in before outcomes are revealed; and a debrief that maps the group’s actual allocation patterns onto the standard categories of moral luck and onto a simple counterfactual-robustness metric. Also provide an honest assessment of which feature—likely the distinction between constitutive and resultant luck—this mechanical setup cannot reproduce and must be supplied by the facilitator, plus a prediction of what will go wrong on the first run (for instance, participants conflating epistemic access with moral responsibility, or collapsing counterfactual proximity into actual outcome severity) and how to correct it without breaking the simulation’s illusion.

The group should leave having experienced, rather than merely named, the pressure to treat structurally identical reasoning differently because of a stochastic divergence they themselves seeded.
