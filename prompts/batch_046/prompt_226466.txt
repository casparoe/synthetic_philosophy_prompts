I've noticed this weird pattern in discussions about religious belief where people use Wittgenstein-style language-game talk, and I'm wondering if there's an actual term for it or if I'm just seeing things. Like, someone will defend prayer by saying it's not a hypothesis about causation - it's just part of a form of life, like playing chess, so asking 'does prayer change outcomes?' is a catagory mistake. Fine. But then the same person will say an atheist 'doesn't understand the game from inside,' which sounds like they're treating the form of life as a kind of knowledge you can lack. Or they'll say religious ethics apply to everyone because they're 'grounded in the nature of things,' but when you ask for evidence they retreat to 'well that's just not how this language works.' Another example: a theologian argued that the resurrection isn't a historical claim subject to archaeology, it's a 'grammatical' statement about hope - but then he used that same resurrection belief to argue that death isn't final in a literal sense, which sure sounds like a historical/physical claim to me. So there's this constant toggeing where religious belief gets the protective shell of being a non-cognitive practice when it's under pressure, but keeps the authority of a privledged cognitive framework when it's making demands. Is there a named concept for this specific asymmetry? Or should I just call it something like 'framework double-entry' and move on? I'm not asking for a bibliography, just whether philosophers have carved this up already, and if so where exactly the edges are - what's legitimate framework-protection versus this double-entry move.
