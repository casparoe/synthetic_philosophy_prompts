I have just finished a long argument with a friend about Montaigne's essay 'Of Cannibals' and whether his comparison of the Tupinambá with Europeans actually commits him to cultural relativism. Before I continue defending my view, I want to check whether my commitments hang together. They are:

(a) I read the essay as a sincere defence of Tupinambá social life, not as a mere rhetorical mirror for European vice; their harmony comes from nature, not from the artificial institutions that corrupt Europe.
(b) I therefore endorse a strong cultural relativism: moral and political standards are internal to forms of life, and there is no transcultural standpoint from which societies can be ranked.
(c) At the same time, I think Montaigne appeals to 'nature' and 'reason' as genuine universal norms that allow him to judge European religious warfare as more barbaric than Tupinambá ritual cannibalism.
(d) I also hold that this cannibalism is best understood as an honour-based ritual of enemy incorporation, not as sustenance-driven violence, and that this makes it morally less severe than European judicial cruelty — a judgement I take to hold across cultures.
(e) Nevertheless, I deny that Montaigne is an Enlightenment-style universalist; his appeals to nature are meant to destabilise custom rather than to establish objective moral law.
(f) I recognise that strong relativism renders any cross-cultural moral ranking incoherent, yet I want to preserve both my relativism and my comparative judgement that Europeans come off worse.

Please analyse which pairs are genuinely in tension, which conflicts are only apparent and dissolve on closer inspection, and rank the real tensions by severity. For each genuine tension, identify the cheapest revision and what it costs. Then tell me which single change would resolve the most problems. Please write in British English, keep the discussion conceptual rather than historical, and do not provide a bibliography or reading list.
