I live in a cooperative housing arrangement—call it a resource pool—where no direct orders or prohibitions are enforced. Any member may claim shared vehicles, workspaces, or equipment through an open-allocation app; there are no curfews, usage quotas, or behavioral rules. The pool also supplies extensive support: career coaching, healthcare navigation, and a modest basic income drawn from common assets. By practical measures my day-to-day options are wide open, and I may exit with thirty days’ notice.

The governance is structured as “stewardship.” A single steward—removable only by unanimous vote of every other member, a threshold never met—authors and may instantly revise the algorithm that sets resource-priority weights and membership-qualification criteria. The steward has never used this authority to harm anyone and promises never to. Nevertheless I feel a persistent sense of unfreedom, as though my security rests entirely on another person’s structurally unchecked will.

A close friend argues my feeling is irrational. She notes that I face no actual interference, so my negative liberty is maximal; that I possess capacities I would lack alone, so my positive liberty is high; and that I retain a credible exit option. Therefore, she concludes, I am among the freest people she knows, and my anxiety is mere ingratitude.

I want to know whether a philosophically serious distinction makes my unease rational rather than neurotic. Does the friend’s reasoning conflate different conceptions of liberty? Can an unexercised but structurally unchecked power—especially one embedded in an algorithmic allocation rule—constitute a form of domination that is worth resisting even if it is never activated? And if so, what does that imply about whether my situation is unjust, regardless of the steward’s benevolence?

Please proceed step by step: identify the conception of freedom that best explains my discomfort; explain precisely where the appeal to absence of interference and to available resources fails; and assess whether domination requires actual interference or can be sustained by the mere presence of arbitrary power. Conclude with your final diagnosis in one fixed line.
