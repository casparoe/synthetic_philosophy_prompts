I'm preparing my position paper for an interdisciplinary workshop on democratic boundaries and inclusion, and I want a consistency check before I circulate it among colleagues who work in constitutional design and political theory. Over the past semester I've been developing a framework that tries to combine a globalist intuition about affected interests with a more localist intuition about reciprocal cooperation, and I have tried to integrate a newer idea about domain-relative membership. Here is the package I have settled on, though I am genuinely unsure which parts pull against one another.

First, I accept a modified all-affected-interests principle: anyone whose basic life prospects are substantially shaped by the outputs of a particular collective decision-making procedure ought to have formal standing in that procedure, irrespective of citizenship or nationality. Second, I also think democratic legitimacy requires what I call a cooperation condition—the members of any demos must stand in a sustained scheme of reciprocal obligation, with mutual expectations of compliance and shared benefit over time. Third, I hold that permanent residents who are not citizens should enjoy full voting rights in local and regional elections, but should be excluded from voting on national constitutional amendments and foreign-policy matters. Fourth, I believe that non-autonomous affected parties—specifically children below the age of reason and future generations—should not be direct members, but should be represented through fiduciary or trustee mechanisms that carry weighted deliberative influence in intergenerational and environmental policy domains. Fifth, I maintain that citizens who have permanently emigrated, severed substantive economic and social ties, and are no longer subject to the state's coercive apparatus should forfeit their membership rights, regardless of whether they retain formal citizenship. Sixth, I reject the pure coercion principle: being subject to coercion is necessary but not sufficient for membership, since temporary visitors and transient workers fall under local law without belonging to the demos. Seventh, I endorse what is sometimes called the democratic solution to the boundary problem—that is, an existing demos has legitimate authority to determine its own membership rules, provided those rules respect basic human rights. Eighth, I think democratic membership requires a minimal threshold of deliberative autonomy—the capacity to understand and respond to reasons in public debate—though this can be satisfied through assisted or supported participation for adults with certain cognitive disabilities. Ninth, I want to integrate the view that membership is not a binary, unitary status but domain-relative; an individual can belong to the demos for ecological watershed governance without belonging to the demos for national fiscal policy, depending on which cooperative scheme actually structures their life prospects.

My colleagues have pointed out that some of these commitments pull in opposite directions, but they have not agreed on which tensions are genuine and which only appear so. I need an audit rather than a lecture: please rank the tensions by severity, specify for each pair whether the conflict is real or dissolves on closer inspection, identify which commitment would have to be revised or qualified in each case and at what cost, and indicate whether there is a single revision that would clean up the largest number of problems at once. I am not signaling which commitments I am most attached to—please treat all nine as currently held, do not assume I am prepared to drop any particular one without argument, and avoid framing the answer as a simple list of mistakes; I want to know exactly what commits me to what.
