As a journalist who covers the intersection of empirical psychology and moral philosophy, I am advising a colleague who teaches an upper-level seminar on moral epistemology and cultural variation. The course examines the well-documented tendency for psychological research on morality to over-sample Western, Educated, Industrialized, Rich, and Democratic populations, and asks what systematic cross-cultural divergence implies for moral objectivity. For the final examination, I want a single essay prompt that students can address in approximately 75 minutes—about 900 words—together with a model answer and a rigorous marking scheme. The question must not ask students to recite particular studies by name, to list authors, or to supply a bibliography; a well-prepared student should be able to answer it using only conceptual resources available in the course.

Please construct a question that presents the following conceptual challenge. Imagine that folk moral judgments about harm, fairness, and loyalty vary systematically across socio-ecological contexts—tightly knit, high-cooperation societies versus more individualistic, market-integrated ones—rather than scattering randomly. A defender of objectivity might advance what we can call a calibration argument: just as physicists in different laboratories disagree about boundary conditions yet converge on underlying laws, moral disagreement might reflect context-sensitive application of universal principles rather than the absence of principles. Ask students to evaluate whether this calibration argument succeeds, but with a specific twist that pushes past standard objections. They must examine whether morality possesses anything analogous to the predictive, replicative, or technological convergence mechanisms that allow scientific disagreement to be resolved—controlled replication, quantitative prediction, instrumental success—and whether the absence of such mechanisms renders the calibration analogy misleading, self-undermining, or merely incomplete.

Deliver the exam question itself, followed by a model answer of roughly 800 words, and a marking scheme organized around five must-hit analytical moves totaling 20 points. For each move, specify the mark allocation and include a brief note on the characteristic error—typically either conflating descriptive cultural variation with normative failure, or treating the absence of convergence mechanisms as automatically fatal without considering whether morality might possess its own domain-specific resolution procedures. The framing should remain formal and polished, appropriate for a journalist requesting an assessment instrument for a philosophy seminar.
