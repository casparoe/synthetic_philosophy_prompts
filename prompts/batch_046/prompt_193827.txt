The student wrote:

Benacerraf's dilemma, presented in his 1973 paper Mathematical Truth, demonstrates that standard Platonism cannot account for mathematical knowledge. The dilemma has two parts. First, mathematical statements are true only if they correspond to abstract objects like numbers and sets, which exist outside space and time. Second, any adequate account of knowledge requires a causal connection between the knower and the object known, as Goldman argued in his causal theory of knowledge. Because abstract objects cannot enter into causal relations, a Platonist has no explanation for how we acquire mathematical knowledge.

To resolve this, one can drop the causal requirement and substitute Goldman's reliabilism, which Goldman originally developed in 1967 specifically to address Benacerraf's concern about abstract objects. On this view, mathematical belief-forming mechanisms—such as intuition and formal proof-checking—are highly reliable, so they produce knowledge even without causal contact. This preserves the Platonist ontology intact while repairing the epistemology. Indeed, the strategy is essentially the same as Hartry Field's fictionalist approach, since both avoid the need for causal interaction with mathematical objects. Field treats mathematics as a useful fiction, and the reliabilist treats our beliefs as reliably formed representations of real objects; in either case, the causal gap is closed.

Benacerraf himself, however, was not persuaded by this kind of maneuver. As a committed structuralist, he believed that the only genuine solution was to deny that mathematical objects are individual entities at all, reducing them to mere positions within structures. Therefore, the reliabilist proposal fails: it still assumes that numbers are particular objects we must access, whereas Benacerraf's structuralism eliminates that very assumption. The dilemma is thus not dissolved but relocated—from epistemology into ontology.
