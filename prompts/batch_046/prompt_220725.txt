Question: The wasp Sphex ichneumoneus executes a fixed sequence—paralyzing a cricket, dragging it to a burrow, entering to inspect, and repeating the inspection if the prey has been displaced—without revising the overall plan. Philosophers call this sphexishness. Explain the phenomenon and evaluate whether human freedom escapes it only by degree, through richer representational states, or also by kind, through capacities for genuine revision of one's ends (sometimes characterized as meta-cognitive sphex-breakers).

Answer: Sphexishness denotes a rigid procedural loop in which an agent repeats fixed sub-routines regardless of changed conditions. Dennett introduced the concept to argue that without decoupled representations—mental states manipulable independently of immediate stimuli—behavior is mechanistically determined. Because determinism operates at the neural level, humans risk being sphexish unless we possess a categorically different capacity. Frankfurt's hierarchical model of second-order volitions supplies exactly this: by endorsing or rejecting first-order desires, we introduce a non-causal layer of evaluation that can revise our ends. This is not merely richer procedural branching, but a qualitative shift; an agent with meta-cognitive sphex-breakers can represent its own rigidity as an object of change. Hence human freedom differs in kind, and the sphexishness analogy fails for agents capable of such recursive self-revision.
