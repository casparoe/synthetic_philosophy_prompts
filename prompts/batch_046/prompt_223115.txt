Design a 90-minute seminar activity for about 14 philosophy graduate students that makes them feel the structural difference between Jeffrey conditionalization and naive Bayesian conditioning through play rather than exposition. Set up a multi-round prediction game with small point stakes convertible to a shared prize: in each round there is a hidden binary state, and every student receives a private envelope containing not an observation of the state but an instruction that shifts their confidence in a noisy "channel" they used in prior rounds—specifically, they learn that their own calibration score in round n was either higher or lower than they believed, but only with 60% reliability. Before betting on the current hidden state, each student must update their credence. Structure the rules so that half the table is forced to condition naively (treating the envelope's content as the observed event E and applying P(H|E) = P(H∧E)/P(E)) while the other half is constrained to Jeffrey-update (holding the internal likelihood ratios constant within each partition and redistributing credence across worlds according to their new confidence in the channel). After betting, reveal the true state and aggregate scores across rounds. Include a debrief that maps the moment of opening the envelope, the betting choice, and the score divergence onto the formal steps of Jeffrey's rule versus standard conditioning, and be frank about what the game cannot reproduce—continuous credence spaces, the old-evidence problem, and precise quantitative weighting—so you can supply those verbally. Specify the materials (envelopes, scorecards, hidden-state deck), the rules for forcing the two update regimes, the predicted failure mode that naive updaters will over-correct and dominate early rounds before the divergence becomes visible, and how you will correct that without breaking the simulation. Finally, inject one niche twist: frame the noisy evidence as second-order uncertainty about the participants' own reasoning calibration rather than about the external world, so that students experience Jeffrey-updating on a shift in confidence about their own prior beliefs.
