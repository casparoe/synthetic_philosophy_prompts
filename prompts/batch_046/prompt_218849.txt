I was arguing with a friend about whether Zhuangzi’s view of death offers genuine consolation or merely dissolves the problem away. She kept returning to the slogan that death is just another transformation—like falling asleep in a larger house—and used it to claim that grief is irrational: if there is no substantial self to be destroyed, then nothing is genuinely lost, only rearranged. I objected that the cessation of this particular first-person stream still looks like a harm, but she said that objection secretly imports a substantial self that Zhuangzi denies. I feel the slogan is hiding several distinct claims. Can you lay out the precise readings it could have—metaphysical (about personal identity and persistence across change), axiological (about whether death can harm the one who dies), normative or emotional (about the rationality of grief), and ontological (about qi or process versus substance)—and for each say whether it is a platitude, defensible, false, or question-begging? Tell me which readings actually support her move from “death transforms me” to “therefore I should not grieve,” and whether any of them require smuggling in modern physicalist or process-ontological commitments, or whether they can stand on Zhuangzi’s own terms.
