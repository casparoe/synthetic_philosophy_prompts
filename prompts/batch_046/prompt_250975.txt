I am writing as a PhD candidate in comparative historiography and philosophy of history. For some years I have been tracking the debate over the Great Divergence—specifically the comparative question of why sustained industrial growth consolidated in Britain rather than in comparable regions of East Asia, particularly the Yangzi delta. The literature seems to have reached a durable impasse. One family of arguments emphasizes institutional configurations, energy endowments, and Atlantic commercial integration as decisive British advantages. A second family, associated with revisionist macro-comparative scholarship, contends that living standards, labor markets, and technological capacities in advanced parts of China were broadly comparable to those in northwestern Europe until around 1800, implying that the divergence was contingent rather than structurally predetermined. A third family holds that the framing itself is conceptually flawed, importing a teleological narrative that treats industrialization as the implicit endpoint of economic development and thereby mischaracterizing non-industrial trajectories as failures rather than alternative equilibria.

Despite extensive empirical exchange—new reconstructions of productivity, energy consumption, and market integration—the disagreement does not converge. I want a diagnosis of the stall rather than another survey of positions.

My working hypothesis is that the debate conflates three distinct explanatory projects that are rarely separated with any precision. The first seeks the necessary conditions that made British industrialization possible. The second seeks the sufficient conditions that made it actual at that specific time and place. The third assesses the counterfactual robustness of the outcome—whether British industrialization was overdetermined, whether comparable Asian trajectories were genuinely available but blocked by specific barriers, or whether they were never activated in any meaningful modal sense. These projects answer to different evidential standards and modal commitments, yet they are pursued under the single heading of why Britain and not China.

Please diagnose the disagreement by engaging with what these positions actually treat as explanatory successes and failures—how they weigh coal endowments against property rights, how they treat demographic regimes, and what they dismiss as irrelevant to the comparison. Is the stall primarily a hidden empirical dispute? A disagreement about the standards of explanation appropriate to comparative macrohistory? A divergence over whether counterfactual reasoning about economic development is legitimate at all, with one side treating modal analysis as indispensable and the other as methodologically unsound? Or is there a bedrock intuition—perhaps about European exceptionalism or about the normative neutrality of economic history—that neither side can argue for and neither will abandon?

Given your diagnosis, what—if anything—would count as progress? Would a new quantitative reconstruction of pre-industrial living standards resolve anything, or would it be interpreted differently by each camp? Would a formal distinction between potentialist explanations and trigger explanations help? Or is the disagreement structural, such that no empirical finding could resolve it because the sides are asking different questions?

Finally, identify the weakest term in your diagnosis—the point on which your conclusion is most sensitive—and specify what conceptual clarification or empirical result would shift it.
