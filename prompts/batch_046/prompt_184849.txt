I'm caring for my father, who has advancing dementia and no longer recognizes me reliably. As a child I was emotionally neglected by him; he was capable of affection but withheld it unpredictably. Now, in his confusion, he sometimes reaches for my hand in a way that feels like it could be attachment, though it may just be reflex. My sister, who lives across the country, argues that I should move him to a skilled nursing facility and stop providing direct care. She says that because he was never a good father, I have no special obligation to him, and that treating family care as morally superior to professional care is a sentimental mistake that hurts women especially, since it usually falls to them. She also notes that the nurses can manage his physical needs more competently than I can, and that my emotional presence produces no measurable improvement in his health. Yet when I observe the staff, they interact with him efficiently but do not seem to treat his occasional gestures of connection as meaningful—more as symptoms to be managed. I find myself wondering whether there is a moral difference between caring for a body and caring for a particular person with a shared history, and whether my history of harm from him cancels any obligation, or whether the specific kind of attention I can give—attention shaped by knowing who he was—creates a responsibility that a paid stranger could not replicate. Is my sister right that fairness demands I treat care as interchangeable labor, or does the particularity of our relationship, and the quality of presence I can offer, generate an obligation that applies specifically to me?
