As a novelist constructing a long-form narrative in which both human and artificial characters must navigate worlds where every action ripples outward but never everywhere at once, I have become preoccupied with what philosophers of mind call the frame problem—not merely as a technical puzzle in artificial intelligence, but as a deeper question about how any finite mind knows what stays constant when something changes. Before I commit to a particular conceptual architecture for my characters' cognition, I want the full specification of what a theory of cognitive framing must actually deliver.

Write the desiderata: the complete list of requirements any adequate theory of framing—any account of how an agent updates its model of a world without exhaustive recomputation—must satisfy to be worthy of the name. For each requirement, provide the motivating case or distinction behind it, and indicate whether it is non-negotiable or a preference of a particular theoretical camp. In particular, the theory must:

- Resolve the classic frame problem proper: distinguish direct effects of an action from its non-effects without requiring an explicit enumeration of all unchanged facts, with the robot-in-the-room case behind it.
- Preserve what I will call narrative inertia: the persistence of emotional valence, symbolic association, and thematic weight attached to entities that undergo no physical alteration (for instance, a character's grief does not dissipate because the chair she sits in has not moved).
- Maintain diegetic boundary conditions: the agent must be able to determine whether a given change belongs to the world-internal logic of the situation or to an external framing intervention (an authorial insertion, a system error, a shift in genre conventions), without collapsing the distinction between epistemic limitation and ontological discontinuity.
- Respect bounded rationality: the framing mechanism must be computable within realistic resource constraints, not merely definable in principle.
- Handle frame conflict: cases where two framing schemas apply simultaneously and yield incompatible preservation rules (e.g., physical causality versus social etiquette versus narrative expectation), and specify whether resolution should be hierarchical, contextual, or pluralistic.
- Be consistent with the empirical results of cognitive science regarding human belief revision—it cannot contradict well-established findings about how actual minds update representations, though it need not replicate every biological detail.

Then, flag any pair of desiderata that appear jointly unsatisfiable—for instance, whether preserving narrative inertia conflicts with strict physicalist framing, or whether diegetic boundary conditions require a meta-representational capacity that violates bounded rationality. Finally, briefly—without scoring every contender—indicate how the classic families of approach (explicit axiomatic framing, non-monotonic logic, situation calculus, and predictive-processing or Bayesian framing models) stand with respect to the list, noting especially where they fail on the narrative or boundary-related requirements I have introduced.

Keep the weight on the criteria and their tensions rather than on the survey of theories. I am writing fiction, not a dissertation; I need to know which demands are genuine and which are prejudices of one camp, so that I can build characters whose minds feel structurally honest.
