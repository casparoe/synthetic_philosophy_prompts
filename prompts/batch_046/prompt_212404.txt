Design a 55-minute in-class simulation for a philosophy-of-mind seminar of about 16 graduate students, intended to make participants feel—before it is named—the conceptual tension between sensory and affective accounts of pain’s badness, using pain asymbolia as the central case. Assume students know the standard clinical profile (subjects report tissue damage and rate intensity normally, yet lack the usual aversive response and avoidance motivation) and are familiar with representational theories of pain, but have not treated the separation of nociception and aversion as a conceptual possibility rather than a mere neurological accident. Inject the following niche framing for them to navigate: distinguish “registering damage” (the bare sensory registration of noxious input) from “binding damage to concern” (the separable process by which that registration acquires negative motivational weight).

Specify a simulation with these elements. Divide participants into three rotating roles—Patients (6), Clinicians (6), and Observers (4)—using hidden-information cards distributed randomly at the start. Each Patient receives a private card pairing one of three sensory profiles (high-intensity localized damage, moderate damage with ambiguous localization, or referred sensation without actual tissue injury) with one of two affective profiles (normal aversive binding, or decoupled/neutral binding simulating asymbolia). Patients must report their state to a Clinician using only a restricted descriptor list you provide (e.g., “sharp,” “throbbing,” “localized,” “diffuse,” “urgent,” “distant”); they may not use evaluative language like “bad,” “awful,” “tolerable,” or “unbearable,” and must not reveal whether they feel concern. They should also display consistent non-verbal behavior: decoupled patients maintain neutral posture and calm vocal tone even when describing high-intensity damage, while normally bound patients show subtle avoidance behaviors (withdrawing a limb, wincing) even for moderate descriptions. Clinicians hold a fixed pool of “intervention tokens” representing limited analgesic resources and, in 90-second rounds, must decide which Patients to treat based solely on verbal reports and observed non-verbal cues. Observers silently record predictions about which Patients “truly suffer” versus which merely “register damage,” using score sheets you supply.

The payoff scheme should use non-monetary but consequential stakes. Clinicians earn “credibility points” when their allocations match the Observers’ collective predictions, but lose points for overtreatment or undertreatment relative to a hidden “normative standard” revealed only after three rounds. Patients earn “autonomy points” for being correctly understood—i.e., matched to their actual affective profile by the group—regardless of whether they receive treatment. Observers earn points for prediction accuracy. After three rounds, rotate roles once so that every participant experiences at least one round as a Patient with a decoupled profile.

The debrief must do four things. First, map specific simulation moments onto the conceptual distinction: identify the round in which high-intensity descriptors paired with neutral posture produced the sharpest disagreement among Clinicians and Observers, and show that this disagreement tracks the gap between registering damage and binding it to concern, not a disagreement about sensory intensity. Second, predict what will predictably go wrong the first time—Clinicians will likely treat reported intensity as a proxy for badness, overtreating decoupled high-intensity cases while undertreating normally bound moderate ones—and explain how to correct this by explicitly forbidding intensity-based allocation in round two and comparing outcomes. Third, be frank about which feature of real asymbolia the activity cannot reproduce: the first-person phenomenological absence of aversion that no third-person behavioral cue can fully capture; state that you must supply this directly, perhaps by reading a brief paraphrased first-person clinical description (without quotation marks) after the simulation ends. Fourth, state clearly what the activity does reproduce well—the social coordination problem of judging suffering from the outside—which supports the view that if aversion is genuinely separable, then pain’s badness may depend on a contingent binding rather than on sensory quality alone.

Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations. Do not invoke named theorists, specific dated texts, or obscure technical frameworks that a small language model could not be expected to have encountered in general training; standard philosophical vocabulary and the clinical term “pain asymbolia” are acceptable. Ensure the rules, materials (role cards, descriptor lists, token pools, observer score sheets), and rotation schedule are specific enough to run without ambiguity, and conclude with a brief note on the physical setup required.
