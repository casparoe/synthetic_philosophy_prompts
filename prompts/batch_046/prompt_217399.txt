I am drafting a philosophical novel and need a rigorously engineered thought experiment for a scene in which characters debate whether moral reasons aggregate with headcount. Design a realistic, one-paragraph rescue scenario—set in a hospital or workplace disaster, with no exotic physics, no perfect predictors, and no trolley mechanisms—in which a single decision-maker must choose between saving one life and saving five lives.

The scenario must embed what I call the mutual-agency condition: the five potential victims are not strangers but are co-participants in a long-term, voluntary collaborative project that requires continuous mutual cognitive dependency (for example, they are an integrated surgical team or a research unit whose work is inseparable), and they have previously agreed, in writing, that in any emergency they are to be treated as a single functional unit rather than as five discrete claimants. The lone individual is an independent party with no such relational structure.

After the scenario, verify that the case meets these specifications:
1. An aggregate consequentialist recommends saving the five, and the justification must be strictly quantitative (five lives outweigh one).
2. A Taurek-style anti-aggregationist concludes that the decision-maker has no stronger reason to save the five than to save the one, because pairwise comparisons do not accumulate and the mutual-agency condition does not create a multiplied claim.
3. A relational-contractualist view holds that the five, having fused their agency by agreement, now present exactly one valid claim, while the lone individual presents a separate, unfused claim; therefore, to avoid subordinating an independent claim to a fused one, the decision-maker should save the single individual.

Then do the structural analysis: identify the mutual-agency condition as the feature that drives the divergence; explain what happens if it is removed (the case collapses into a standard Taurek-style debate with no clear contractualist resolution); and defend the relational-contractualist verdict against the aggregate consequentialist's likely objection that the condition is an artificial redefinition of personhood. Finally, take a committed stance: argue that once the mutual-agency condition is genuinely satisfied, the relational-contractualist reading is correct, and explain precisely why both aggregation and pairwise anti-aggregation fail to respect the ontology of the victims. Do not present the three views as equally plausible options to choose among; defend the relational-contractualist position with definite reasons.
