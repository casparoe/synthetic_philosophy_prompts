I'm planning a fifty-minute session for upper-level philosophy students who understand basic welfare economics but have never separated normative time preference from instrumental discounting. I need a teaching plan, not the explanation itself. What must come first: the conceptual distinction between pure time preference and risk- or growth-based discounting, or the formal contrast between exponential and hyperbolic decay? Choose one carrying example — it must isolate pure time preference by holding risk, growth, and population size constant across generations, so avoid climate-change and investment frames — and specify what properties it needs so students cannot smuggle in instrumental reasons. Map the misconceptions they will form: I expect they will treat pure time preference as a psychological claim about impatience, and that they will identify it with using any constant discount rate. At exactly which point in the sequence should each misconception be blocked? Give one final diagnostic question that reveals whether a student understands pure time preference as a claim about the moral weight of future welfare rather than a claim about rational choice or human psychology. Also say explicitly what technical material — for instance, intertemporal utility maximization and the associated optimal-growth equation — should be left out at this stage and why.
