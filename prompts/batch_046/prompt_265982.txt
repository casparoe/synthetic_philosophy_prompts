I've been retired from the physics dept for about three years now and lately I'm trying to wrap my head around Gödel's incompletness theorems outside pure math. At dinner with old colleagues, two friends got stuck arguing about it. Marcus said the first theorem proves you can never have a complete scientific theory of the brain, since any theory powerful enough to model neurons will eventually be able to represent basic arithmetic and then can't prove every true statement about its own predictions. Elena shot back that this is a misreading: Gödel only applies to formal axiomatic systems that are consistent and recursivley enumerable, not to empirical scientific theories that get revised by experiment. Marcus replied that once you formalize a brain theory — say, as a computer simulation — it is a formal system, so the theorem bites. Elena said then you're describing the computer running the model, not the brain itself, and that real physics doesn't have to be axiomatized in a recursivley enumerable way. Marcus then argued that even modern probabalistic or Bayesian brain models can embed arithmetic relations over continuous firing rates if they're precise enough, so the escape hatch doesn't work. Elena said he was smuggling in a hidden premise — that "complete" means the same thing in empirical science as in formal logic — and that without that the argument collapses.

They asked me to referee since I actually used formal logic in my old work. Can you go through their exchange step by step and say who's right about each move? Not just who wins overall, but also what both are missing. And does Gödel's second theorem actually matter for this kind of argument, or is Marcus just waving it around?
