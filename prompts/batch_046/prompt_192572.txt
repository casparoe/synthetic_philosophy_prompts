Analyze John F. Kennedy's 1960 address to the Greater Houston Ministerial Association through the lens of John Rawls's framework of public reason, the proviso, and the place of comprehensive religious doctrines in democratic deliberation. Do not treat the speech as mere campaign rhetoric; treat it as a boundary case in which a public official must negotiate between a religious comprehensive doctrine (Roman Catholicism) and the shared political values of a pluralist society. First, map the episode precisely onto Rawls's distinctions: identify what counts as the public political forum in this context, determine whether Kennedy's appeal to religious liberty and strict church-state separation operates as a genuinely public reason or as a translated Catholic doctrine, and assess whether his pledge that his faith would not dictate presidential decisions satisfies the proviso's requirement that comprehensive reasons eventually be supplemented by proper political arguments. Second, say what Rawls's theory predicts Kennedy ought to have done differently—whether he should have avoided addressing religious identity entirely, whether he was permitted to declare his Catholic commitments before translating them, and where the theory is silent about the strategic electoral costs of such disclosure. Third, evaluate whether the speech confirms Rawls's hope that an overlapping consensus can accommodate religious citizens, or whether it exposes a tension within the proviso: namely, that requiring religious citizens to bracket their deepest convictions in public discourse may impose an asymmetrical burden that Rawls's own account of the burdens of judgment does not fully resolve. Conclude by stating what the episode reveals about the scope of public reason—specifically, whether it can govern not only legislative justification but also the pre-political space of candidate legitimacy and voter assessment of religious fitness for office.
