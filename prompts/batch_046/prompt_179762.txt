I am a senior archivist at a national oral-history institution. We edit multi-witness collections about single contested events—industrial closures, migrations, conflicts—into published volumes. Increasingly we receive testimonies that place the same actions in incompatible chronological orders: one witness insists that a strike preceded management’s announcement, another that the announcement caused the strike; a survivor describes a reunion with a relative in 1985, yet institutional records confirm the relative died in 1978. We have no external record to adjudicate these cases, and our editorial board is divided between those who wish to reconcile contradictions into one timeline and those who wish to preserve them as evidence of subjective experience.

Please turn Borges’s philosophical views on time—specifically his denial that time consists in real succession, his argument that past and present are simultaneous in consciousness rather than arranged in a line, and his associated scepticism about stable personal identity across moments—into a reusable editorial procedure. The procedure must be something my team can run in about twenty minutes per testimony cluster, without requiring philosophical training, and it must state clearly where it yields a definite editorial instruction, where it stops and requires a human judgement call, and where it will deliberately produce an answer that conflicts with institutional pressure for narrative coherence.

Structure it as a sequence of questions or tests applied in order, with the criterion for a sufficient answer at each step, a clear “stop—do not reconcile” point, and explicit flags for judgement calls that cannot be eliminated by further fact-checking. Then apply it to three cases:

1. Two factory workers describing a 1970s closure. Worker A places the occupation of the plant before the announcement of redundancy; Worker B places the announcement before the occupation, with no ambiguity about the events themselves, only about their sequence.

2. A refugee testimony describing a reunion with a sibling in 1985, but institutional records confirm the sibling died in 1978. The witness is otherwise reliable and shows no signs of confabulation.

3. A case where your procedure demands that we preserve contradictory timelines side-by-side without editorial resolution, but our funding agreement requires that every published volume present a single, authoritative chronology for accessibility.

For each case, show what the procedure directs, note where it hands the decision to a person rather than giving an instruction, and identify which step is doing the philosophical work. Finally, state the three most serious ways the procedure can misfire—particularly where Borges’s denial of real succession might lead an archivist to treat a genuine factual error as a legitimate temporal perspective—and say what the procedure cannot do that a fully philosophical analysis could. Write in British English, avoid citations or reading lists, and do not quote any text verbatim.
