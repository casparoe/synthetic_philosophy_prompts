I keep encountering a particular resistance to analogies that doesn't fit the usual objections. Someone proposes a tight structural mapping — for instance, comparing collective decision-making to a neural network's weighted voting, or comparing a social institution's adjustments to a thermostat's feedback loop — where inputs, outputs, thresholds, and adjustments align precisely. Listeners reject it not by spotting a structural mismatch, but by treating the source domain as carrying an essential "taint": networks are unconscious, thermostats are deterministic, markets are amoral. They behave as if importing the structural relations necessarily imports these associated properties, "contaminating" the target, even though the mapping was explicitly restricted to dynamics, not ontology. The same pattern appears with biological analogies for policy, computational analogies for consciousness, and mechanical analogies for human agency. Is there an established term for this failure mode — analogical rejection driven not by poor correspondence but by fear of essential contamination from the source? How does it relate to the view, associated with Hofstadter and Sander, that analogy-making is the core of cognition? And if no standard name exists, how should we distinguish it from hidden-premise objections and from mere emotional bias?
