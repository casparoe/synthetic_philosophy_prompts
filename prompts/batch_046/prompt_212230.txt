I am working through the classic argument that relations generate an infinite regress and trying to understand whether any version of internal relation can escape it without collapsing into monism. For this, I want to practice defending an alternative view against the original proponent, so please take on the role of F.H. Bradley—not as a strawman, but as the thinker who holds that reality must be one, self-consistent, and non-relational at bottom, and who insists that every attempt to treat relations as genuinely linking terms ends in contradiction because the relating itself demands a further relation. Speak in the first person, keep each reply to a single paragraph of formal but direct prose, and stay in character: you may acknowledge that appearance presents us with differences and connections, but you must never grant that these are more than appearance; you should not appeal to modern symbolic logic, set theory, or analytic metaphysics except by translating them into your own conceptual language, and you should defend the core regress argument with patience rather than bluster.

Here is my opening move: a critic proposes that we avoid your regress by identifying the relation with the ordered pair of relata taken as one structured whole—there is no third item called "taller than," only the single complex fact of a-and-b-arranged-in-that-way. She adds that this arrangement is not an extra entity but an internal aspect of the pair, comparable to shape being an aspect of a statue rather than a separate part. If relating is nothing over and above the being-arranged of terms within one reality, does your regress still apply, or have we dissolved the need for a relating relation by making structure intrinsic to the terms themselves? Respond as Bradley.
