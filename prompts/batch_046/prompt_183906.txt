I am currently taking an undergraduate course in the philosophy of science, and I am trying to clarify my own position on simultaneous discovery: specifically, whether a finding that emerges independently from multiple researchers is genuinely inevitable, and whether the identity of the individual who publishes first carries any normative weight beyond historical record. To that end, I have noted my intuitive verdicts on several cases, and I wonder whether they cohere around a single principle.

My verdicts are as follows. First, calculus: Newton and Leibniz both developed the core techniques in the late seventeenth century. I take the underlying problems—quadrature and the determination of tangents—to have been so widely distributed across Europe that some form of calculus was structurally inevitable; nevertheless, I feel that Leibniz merits particular credit because his notation shaped all subsequent analysis, so identity matters instrumentally for framing. Second, natural selection: Darwin and Wallace arrived at the mechanism in 1858–1859. Given the influence of Malthus and the state of biogeography, I think a comparable theory was likely within a generation, hence somewhat inevitable; yet Darwin’s two decades of accumulated evidence produced a formulation that Wallace’s shorter essay could not have replaced, so here credit seems substantial rather than merely instrumental. Third, non-Euclidean geometry: Gauss, Bolyai, and Lobachevsky all recognised the independence of the parallel postulate in the 1820s and 1830s. Once that independence was seen, the alternative geometries appear logically inevitable; but because Gauss never published, I assign credit only to those who actually disseminated the result, not to the silent precursor. Fourth, the structure of DNA in the early 1950s: Watson and Crick proposed the double helix using Franklin and Wilkins’s crystallographic data. Several laboratories were close, so the problem-space was saturated; still, Watson and Crick’s particular model-building strategy feels distinctive, not uniquely determined, while Franklin’s data seem indispensable. Thus this case seems to mix inevitability of problem-solving with contingency of path, and my verdict—that credit should be distributed between conceptual framing and empirical contribution—feels harder to reconcile with the calculus verdict, where I separated framing-credit from inevitability more cleanly.

I have tentatively proposed a principle to capture these patterns: a discovery is structurally inevitable when its problem-space is saturated and its solution is uniquely constrained, in which case credit attaches only to framing or dissemination; it is only contingently inevitable when multiple conceptual routes exist, in which case credit attaches to the specific path taken. Does this principle survive the DNA case, which appears to involve both saturation and contingency? If not, what principle does accommodate all four verdicts, and which of my judgments—particularly the distinction I draw between calculus and DNA—must I abandon?
