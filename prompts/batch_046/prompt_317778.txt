In an unnamed quarterly essay, the author characterized the view thus: negative utilitarianism holds that suffering is the sole intrinsic moral bad, while pleasure possesses no value whatsoever—not even as a permissible side-effect of suffering-reduction. Because pleasure is axially neutral, the view’s defenders, guided by what is called the asymmetry argument, hold that bringing a suffering being into existence is always wrong, whereas failing to bring a happy being into existence is never wrong. From this, the reviewer derived a strict institutional corollary: a society organized on these principles must function as a suffering-prevention apparatus, categorically forbidding any policy or technological advance that risks producing suffering, regardless of the magnitude of happiness it might yield. The reviewer further claimed that negative utilitarians reject rights-based moral frameworks altogether, on the grounds that rights protect existing persons’ interests in pursuing pleasure—interests the view treats as morally weightless—so that institutional design should abandon welfare-maximization in favor of absolute risk-aversion regarding harm.

Before I put anything like this into a character’s mouth, I need an accuracy audit. Which sentences in the summary capture what a careful defender of suffering-focused ethics would accept? Is the characterization of pleasure as having no value whatsoever a fair rendering of the asymmetry, or does it overstate the view’s indifference to well-being? And does the conclusion that rights must be jettisoned and that risk-bearing progress is categorically forbidden survive correction of the earlier missteps? Please address the summary in detail, separating accurate description from subtle overstatement, and tell me whether the institutional quietism attributed to the view is a genuine implication or a rhetorical amplification.
