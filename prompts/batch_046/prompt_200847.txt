In a scene I am drafting, a retired surgeon argues that legalizing kidney sales would save lives without coercion, a political philosopher counters that vote-selling undermines democratic equality, and a neighbor invokes price-gouging statutes as proof that markets possess moral boundaries; at the center of their disagreement hangs the familiar line, “There are some things money shouldn’t buy.” I first encountered this slogan not in a philosophical treatise but in a conversation about whether human organs could be treated as fungible commodities without corrupting their social meaning, and I have since heard it deployed to condemn everything from emergency price hikes to the auctioning of civic rights. The phrase operates as a conversational stop sign, yet none of the characters can say precisely what kind of “shouldn’t” is in play: is it a claim about intrinsic value being incommensurable with price, about the crowding out of altruistic motives, about the expressive degradation of treating persons or sacred goods as merchandise, about distributive injustice that markets exacerbate rather than remedy, about an epistemic failure in which pricing obscures the true nature of the good, or about a deontological constraint that certain transactions violate dignity regardless of consequence?

Could you disambiguate the slogan into a menu of distinct, precise theses—specifying in each case whose authority is restricted, what kind of “money” and “buying” are involved (allocation versus evaluation, explicit pricing versus implicit marketization), and whether the wrong lies in the transaction itself, in its social symbolism, or in its predictable effects? For each reading, I want to know whether it is trivial, substantive, and defensible, and, crucially, whether it actually yields the conclusion that kidney markets should be banned, that price gouging should be criminalized, or that votes should not be sold. I am especially interested in whether any interpretation avoids the charge of mere sentimentality while still blocking all three practices, or whether the slogan collapses into separate arguments that happen to share a rhetorical form. Please do not offer a bibliography; I need the analysis to stand on its own conceptual legs.
