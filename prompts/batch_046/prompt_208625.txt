I'm taking a graduate seminar on American pragmatism and have been arguing with a friend about whether John Dewey's philosophy can still illuminate democratic technologies that emerged long after his death in 1952. In particular, we have been discussing a recently proposed governance model—sometimes referred to informally as synthetic deliberation—in which citizens never meet face-to-face, read opposing arguments, or engage in direct public exchange. Instead, each participant submits private values and preferences to an algorithmic mediator, which synthesizes these inputs into an optimized policy draft and returns only the final compromise text for a binary up-or-down vote. Proponents argue that this eliminates status bias and group polarization, but critics contend that it severs the interactive loop that makes democratic judgment genuinely intelligent rather than merely aggregative.

Given Dewey's experimentalist conception of democracy—as cooperative social inquiry in which shared experience, fallibilist testing, and communicative exchange are inseparable—how would he likely assess this arrangement? Would he treat it as a legitimate extension of scientific method into social organization, perhaps even a way to realize the Great Community under conditions of scale? Or would he regard it as an extreme form of the eclipse of the public he diagnosed in his political writings, precisely because it replaces the friction of live disagreement with an opaque, black-boxed proxy for collective reasoning? Please address specifically how Dewey might weigh the elimination of status bias against the loss of direct communicative interaction in forming a shared public, and consider whether his commitment to experimentalism requires that democratic participants experience the process of problem-solving together in real time, rather than outsourcing the synthesis of disagreement to an algorithmic intermediary.
