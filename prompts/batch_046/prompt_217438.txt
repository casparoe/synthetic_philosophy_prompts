I'm working on a short essay for a graduate seminar in early modern political philosophy, and I'd appreciate critical feedback on the line of argument I'm developing. The core claim is that Spinoza's account of democracy in the Tractatus Theologico-Politicus and Tractatus Politicus is usually interpreted as a pragmatic preference for majoritarian rule because the multitude's power is too dangerous to suppress. I want to push further and argue that democracy, for Spinoza, operates as a specific kind of collective cognitive apparatus—one that doesn't merely aggregate individual judgments but actively restructures the affective field through which the multitude forms common notions. Specifically, I propose that democratic institutions function with what I call a "refractive index": they don't just channel pre-existing passions into decisions, but alter the qualitative intensity and direction of shared affects so that reason and passion converge rather than compete. On this reading, the rationality of the democratic multitude is not an aggregation of private rationalities but an emergent property of institutionalized disagreement, where the friction between competing affects produces more adequate ideas about common goods. I contrast this with Hobbesian aggregation, where the sovereign synthesizes private wills through representation, and I suggest that Spinoza's democracy avoids the need for a transcendent sovereign precisely because the institutional form itself performs the synthetic work. I also want to claim that this explains why Spinoza insists democracy is the most "natural" form of government: not because humans are naturally social in a naive sense, but because human cognition is inherently interdependent and democratic structures are the ones that best approximate the conditions under which common notions can stabilize without coercion.

I'd be grateful for comments on several points. First, is this interpretation of Spinoza's texts defensible, or am I importing an anachronistic cognitive-science framing into his metaphysics of affect? Second, is the "refractive index" concept genuinely illuminating, or does it obscure the more straightforward political dynamics Spinoza describes? Third, are there specific passages—particularly in the Tractatus Politicus—where Spinoza seems to resist this kind of emergentist reading? Finally, I'd welcome suggestions on how to sharpen the distinction from Hobbes without caricaturing either thinker. Please respond in American English, and feel free to point out any conceptual confusions or missing steps in the reasoning.
