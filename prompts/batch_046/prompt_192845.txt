Here is an argument I built for a philosophy of law seminar, and I cannot locate the weak joint; it seems to show that any coherent defense of marriage as a state-recognized institution either collapses into an arbitrary biological criterion or dissolves the institution entirely.

(1) A liberal state may single out a relationship form for special legal recognition only if that form serves a distinctive public function that other recognized institutions—contract, partnership, adoption law—do not already cover.

(2) The distinctive function traditionally assigned to marriage is the stabilization and public ordering of reproductive unions, specifically the linking of biological parents to the children they jointly produce.

(3) Same-sex couples cannot, even in principle, jointly produce biological children.

(4) Therefore, same-sex unions do not serve the distinctive reproductive function.

(5) If (2) is correct, then recognition should extend only to unions that actually or potentially serve that function.

(6) Many recognized opposite-sex marriages—between permanently infertile partners, post-menopausal spouses, or couples who have permanently renounced childbearing—also do not serve, and cannot serve, that function.

(7) The state nonetheless recognizes these marriages, which means either (a) the state accepts widespread misapplication of (2), or (b) marriage serves some other distinctive function.

(8) Suppose the other function is long-term mutual care and economic interdependence. But close friends, adult siblings, or multi-person households can serve exactly the same function, and contract law already allows them to formalize it.

(9) So if marriage is recognized for care rather than reproduction, there is no principled reason to restrict it to romantic dyads; polyamorous groups or platonic caregivers should qualify.

(10) Restricting it to dyads then requires reintroducing a reproductive norm—specifically, that the unit is structured around the potential for biological co-parenting—even for couples who never realize it.

(11) But applying that norm counterfactually (a post-menopausal couple “would have” reproduced had they met earlier) is metaphysically odd and applies inconsistently: a same-sex couple could also “would have” reproduced with different partners, yet we do not license marriage on counterfactual partner-swapping.

(12) Hence any consistent position either denies recognition to large classes of existing opposite-sex marriages (step 5 applied strictly) or extends recognition so broadly that marriage loses institutional distinctness (step 9).

(13) Since both outcomes are unacceptable, the argument proves too much. Where is the exit?

If forced to preserve one claim, I would rather keep (1)—that state recognition requires a distinctive public function—than accept (12). But I want to know exactly which step fails, why it looked plausible, and what abandoning it costs for the broader debate about what marriage is for. In particular, is there a hidden equivocation between “reproductive potential of the union” and “reproductive potential of the individuals within it,” or is the real damage done by smuggling in a normative closure condition in step (8)?
