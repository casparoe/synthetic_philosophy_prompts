I've been trying to take Frank Ramsey's line on the meaning of life seriously ever since my dad got his diagnosis last month — the idea that asking "what's the point of it all" is basically a grammatical confusion, like asking what color the number seven is, and that once you see the syntax mistake the feeling doesn't vanish but it stops being a philosophical problem. I think I mostly buy the dissolution, especially the version where the question doesn't disappear but gets translated into practical questions about which habits of expecation we should stabilize. But I'm worried I'm just using philosophy to insulate myself from something actually hard.

So what would genuinely count against this? Not the usual "but people really feel it" pushback — I mean the serious defeaters. Maybe empirical psychology showing that meaning-seeking is structurally unlike other pseudo-questions and behaves more like a normative inquiry we can't dissolve; maybe internal tension with Ramsey's own pragmatism about belief-formation, since if belief is just a habit of action and the habit of asking "why live" is deeply entrenched, then dismissing it as syntax looks like special pleading. There's also the awkward fact that Ramsey's own notebooks and late letters seem to wrestle with the question in ways that don't read like simple category mistakes. Which of these are fatal versus merely awkward? And does the dissolution risk collapsing into a kind of quietist ethics that Ramsey wouldn't have endorsed? I want an honest map of where the view is actually exposed — what it risks, whether it's been qualified into unfalsifiability, and whether that's a problem for a view of this kind.
