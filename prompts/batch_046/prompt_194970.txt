You are a professional philosopher of history and an experienced seminar facilitator. Before drafting, take a moment to think through the conceptual difference between institutional continuity and narrative continuity, and how a small-group simulation could make participants feel—rather than merely hear—that claims to ancient national identity depend on selective, retrospective construction. Then design the activity below.

Design a runnable 75-minute classroom activity for a graduate reading group of 10 participants on the constructivist thesis that modern nations are relatively recent formations whose apparent ancient roots depend on invented traditions and shared imaginative practices, in the spirit of Hobsbawm’s analysis of tradition-building and Anderson’s account of collective imagination, without requiring participants to have read specific texts during the session.

Specify the setting, group size (10), time allocation, roles, rules, hidden mechanisms, and debrief. The idea must emerge from what participants do: Founders should experience how easily they can fabricate convincing historical continuity using only modern materials; Archivists should run into irreconcilable disagreement about whether that continuity is real, driven by conflicting implicit epistemic standards they do not initially know they hold.

Structure:
- Setup and roles (10 minutes): Split into two subgroups of 5—Founders and Archivists. Founders act as a state-commissioned committee circa 1890 tasked with consolidating a new polity that must claim an unbroken 800-year lineage. They have 20 minutes to construct, using only physical objects in the room and handwritten materials, three public traditions and one founding charter. They must deliberately embed one visible but plausible anachronism.
- Simulation (25 minutes): Founders present. Archivists are secretly divided into two camps—Camp A requires direct documentary testimony for continuity; Camp B accepts structural-functional equivalence of institutions. Both camps share a hidden rubric that weights these criteria differently. They must reach unanimous written verdict on whether the nation genuinely existed 800 years ago, or forfeit collective recognition. They vote silently first, then discuss.
- Constraint injection (10 minutes): Reveal the hidden rubrics and the embedded anachronism. Note whether it was detected and by which camp’s logic.
- Debrief (30 minutes): Map the Founders’ fabrication ease onto invented tradition; map the Archivists’ deadlock onto the problem that imagined communities require shared but contested criteria of belief; map the missed or detected anachronism onto the retrospective construction of the archive itself, where present power shapes what counts as past evidence.

Predict two predictable failures: (1) groups aestheticize traditions rather than testing epistemic criteria—counter by requiring Archivists to cite specific rules before voting; (2) social pressure produces premature convergence—counter by enforcing silent written ballots before any discussion. Include explicit debrief questions.

End with an honest limitation: the activity cannot reproduce the macro-historical scale of state power in suppressing counter-narratives over centuries, nor the temporal distance that makes retrospective myth-making invisible to its own participants; the facilitator must supply this structural and temporal framing explicitly.

Keep all references conceptual—no verbatim quotations from real texts, and no requested bibliographies or citations. Make the rules, payoffs, and role conflicts specific enough that a facilitator could run the session from your description alone.
