Having spent three decades in experimental physics before turning, in retirement, to the philosophy of science and the history of early modern natural philosophy, I have lately been working through the theological manuscripts of Isaac Newton—not only the well-known General Scholium appended to the Principia, but also his anti-Trinitarian writings, his studies in prophetic chronology, and his extensive but unpublished alchemical notebooks. I am struck by how consistently he combines a rigorous mechanistic cosmology with the conviction that God occasionally intervenes to correct perturbations in the planetary system, and that the cosmos operates according to an intelligent but not logically necessary design. Given these specific commitments, I would like your reasoned speculation—without bibliographic references, citations, or a reading list, since I seek conceptual reconstruction rather than scholarly apparatus—on how Newton would likely have responded to three developments that emerged long after his death.

First, the discovery of the accelerating expansion of the universe, driven by dark energy, together with the thermodynamic prospect of a heat death or a big rip in which all cosmic structure ultimately dissolves. Would Newton have interpreted this accelerating expansion as yet another mechanical perturbation requiring occasional divine correction, or rather as an original feature of the design? Second, the multiverse hypothesis—specifically the proposal that our observable universe is one region within an enormous or infinite ensemble of causally disconnected domains with varying physical constants—seems to challenge the uniqueness and intentionality of the cosmic order he defends. Would he have rejected it as an unnecessary multiplication of entities, or might his alchemical interest in invisible active principles and vegetative forces operating beneath the surface of matter have made him receptive to hidden, parallel domains of nature? Third, the modern philosophical formulation of the problem of evil, particularly the tension between deterministic physical laws and the prevalence of natural suffering, raises the question of whether a benevolent, occasionally intervening deity is compatible with a universe governed by immutable mathematical rules.

Of these three posthumous challenges, which do you think would have been the most theologically disruptive to Newton’s particular worldview, and which the least? I want a defended ordering rather than a survey: for each case, identify the precise feature of Newton’s theology—his Arianism, his view of divine intervention, his prophetic chronology, or his alchemical natural philosophy—that drives the prediction. Please avoid the common simplification that Newton was merely a deist; I am especially interested in whether his more idiosyncratic commitments yield predictions that diverge from both standard theism and standard deism. You need not cite texts verbatim; paraphrase freely, and do not include a bibliography, since I have access to the primary writings in translation and require only the conceptual argument.
