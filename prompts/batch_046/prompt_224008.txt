As a novelist attempting to design a protagonist whose moral life is genuinely extraordinary rather than merely admirable, I want you to write a formal essay addressing the following controversy: is maximal moral goodness — the state of being a moral saint — genuinely desirable?

Frame your analysis not merely as an abstract ethical exercise, but as an inquiry into whether such a life could sustain narrative coherence and psychological depth. Specifically, distinguish between two figures that philosophical discussions often conflate: the saint of total self-abnegation, who systematically sacrifices every personal good for the welfare of others, and the saint of perfect moral attentiveness, who never fails to register a moral claim but does not necessarily annihilate their own projects, relationships, or interior life. Examine whether maximal moral goodness requires one form, both, or neither, and whether either version produces a life worth wanting.

Weigh the arguments that such lives represent the pinnacle of human flourishing against the objections — familiar in the philosophical literature but here applied precisely to these two portraits — that they generate moral monotony, eliminate the conditions for moral growth, or make genuine partiality and friendship impossible. Consider whether a life without moral failure is also a life without moral surprise, and whether that absence is a virtue or a deficiency.

Reach a definite conclusion: is maximal moral goodness desirable without qualification, undesirable, or desirable only in a restricted form? Justify your verdict with reasons that address both ethical theory and the novelist's concern with character depth.

Your essay must be structured exactly as follows, with approximate word counts for each section:

1. The novelist's framing and the problem (c. 250 words): establish why the distinction between the two saints matters for understanding human character.
2. Two portraits of moral excellence (c. 350 words): define the saint of self-abnegation and the saint of perfect attentiveness; show where they diverge.
3. The case for desirability (c. 350 words): present the strongest arguments that maximal moral goodness is the best human life.
4. The case against desirability (c. 400 words): present the objections, including those concerning partiality, moral growth, and narrative depth.
5. Verdict and justification (c. 300 words): defend your conclusion explicitly.
6. Limits of the reasoning (c. 200 words): state precisely what argument or consideration would change your mind, and identify where your reasoning is most likely to fail.

Write in British English, maintain a polished, formal tone, and do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations. Avoid verbatim quotations from specific texts; if you refer to well-known philosophical positions, paraphrase them without quotation marks. Do not invoke obscure theories or named doctrines that depend on specialised knowledge unavailable without research.
