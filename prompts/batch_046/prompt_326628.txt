As a novelist invited to deliver the opening remarks at the retirement workshop for Professor Alistair Vance, a philosopher of mind who spent three decades defending Norman Malcolm's position that dreams are not genuine experiences—since they lack the present-tense phenomenological criteria Malcolm requires—while engaging with Daniel Dennett's opposing view that dreaming involves narrative construction without direct phenomenal immediacy, I need a formal four-minute address for a divided audience: half are philosophers and cognitive scientists steeped in this exchange, and half are family and students from other fields who have never encountered it. Write the speech so that it does not lecture but instead uses the Malcolm-Dennett disagreement as a structural metaphor for how lives are reconstructed in memory. Begin from the novelist's observation that every autobiography is edited after the fact, draw a parallel to Dennett's draft-model of mental content, and acknowledge Malcolm's stricter demand for unmediated access. Then introduce the speculative idea that a culture might collectively choose whether to treat its shared past as an experienced continuity or as a reconstructed set of drafts, asking—without resolving—whether waking up and deciding what a dream meant is structurally analogous to a society deciding what its history meant. The speech should honor Vance's career without declaring a winner, should comfort those who never studied philosophy, and must not request citations, bibliographies, or reading lists.
