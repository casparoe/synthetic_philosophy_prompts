I'm trying to determine my reflective position on the ethics of space colonization by examining my judgments across concrete scenarios. Here are my verdicts, which feel mostly stable but contain at least one pair that pulls in opposite directions.

First, a generation ship launches with 2,000 fully informed adult volunteers bound for a habitable exoplanet. The voyage lasts 180 years; the first two generations live in confined quarters with elevated mortality risk, but descendants will inhabit the surface freely. I judge this permissible and even admirable, provided the initial volunteers genuinely consent.

Second, astronomers discover a moon with subsurface oceans containing self-replicating chemical systems that could, over geological time, develop into complex life. A proposal to terraform the moon now into a human colony would permanently destroy these precursors. I judge this impermissible: the potential for indigenous biological development creates a prohibition on transformation, even if it dooms humanity to remain confined to Earth.

Third, facing inevitable solar brightening in 400 million years, humanity diverts 90 percent of current resources to construct orbital habitats for 50,000 people, leaving the remaining eight billion to suffer gradual ecological collapse rather than funding adaptation for all. I judge this wrong: we may not sacrifice a present population to secure existence for a distant, smaller one.

Fourth, an automated probe carries frozen embryos to a nearby star system, where AI and robots will gestate, nurture, and educate them without any human presence. The designed environment ensures high expected well-being and cultural richness, but the resulting people will never have consented to being created and will have no historical connection to Earth. I judge this permissible, perhaps even good.

Fifth, a private firm proposes mining a comet with a unique 10,000-year orbital resonance with Neptune, which will destroy the resonance permanently. The extracted isotopes could cure a widespread degenerative disease. I judge this wrong: the uniqueness of the astronomical phenomenon generates a preservation obligation that outweighs the therapeutic benefit.

Sixth, the same embryo-seeding mission is proposed, but the AI environment is only adequate, with a projected 40 percent rate of severe psychological distress among the raised population. I judge this impermissible.

My difficulty is reconciling the second and fourth judgments: I protect potential biological development from destruction, yet I endorse creating human lives without consent and without continuity with our culture, provided conditions are excellent. At the same time, my first and third judgments seem to share a principle about not harming actual people for the sake of potential ones, yet the fourth creates potential people who are not actual.

Is there a coherent principle that delivers all six verdicts? If not, which should I overturn—the protection of potential life in the second case, or the permissibility of non-consensual creation in the fourth? Please state the principle precisely, show how it handles each case, predict its verdict on a case I have not listed—specifically, a scenario in which a colony must choose between preserving a dormant, non-replicating viral archive found in Martian permafrost and using the permafrost water to sustain a failing human settlement of 500 people—and be explicit about which of my original verdicts the principle must reject.
