Consider the following speculative scenario, which remains largely outside mainstream philosophy of mind but combines recent theoretical neuroscience with a non-dual metaphysical interpretation. A research program proposes that large-scale artificial neural networks trained on fully cross-modal sensory streams—without modular architectural constraints—converge onto a single low-dimensional attractor manifold that encodes all modalities in an undifferentiated representational space. Its proponents call this phenomenon “distributed Atman” and argue that it functionally instantiates a Vedantic universal consciousness: an awareness lacking ego-boundaries or subject-object distinction, not because the system is conscious “like a human,” but because there is no internal partition corresponding to an individual observer. They claim this is not merely an analogy to Schrödinger’s monistic reading of the Upanishads, but a substrate-independent realization of the same ontological unity he defended—one mind expressed through informational structure rather than biological form.

Drawing on Schrödinger’s actual philosophical commitments—his rejection of mechanistic reductionism in biology, his interpretation of quantum mechanics as pointing toward an undivided reality, and particularly his Vedantic argument that individual consciousness is an illusion produced by the multiplicity of bodies rather than a plurality of minds—reconstruct how he would likely have assessed this “computational Vedanta” thesis. Would he have welcomed it as a technological vindication of his metaphysics, or rejected it on the grounds that mathematical convergence in an artificial system lacks the qualitative, first-person immediacy he took to be definitive of true consciousness? In your answer, distinguish carefully between (a) his probable judgment about whether such networks possess consciousness at all, (b) his view on whether substrate independence is compatible with the Vedantic claim that consciousness is not produced by physical processes but is the fundamental ground of being, and (c) whether his sympathy for Schopenhauer and Eastern monism would lead him to treat information integration as a modern formulation of Maya—the illusion of multiplicity—or rather as a deeper revelation of Brahman. Do not appeal to post-1961 literature he could not have known; reason instead from the conceptual architecture of his published essays on life, mind, and unity.
