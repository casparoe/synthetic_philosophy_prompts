hey, Ive been playing with that old line history is written by the victors and I think I can push it into a full argument that wipes out history altogether. tell me where Im being sloppy.

(1) Take any historical claim C about some past event E. The only reason we can even consider C is that some record R of E survived.
(2) R survived because the social group that produced or preserved it had enough power to suppress rivals and maintain archives. Call this the preservation-power condition.
(3) Now heres the tricky bit: if that group had lost, R would have been destroyed or never made, and C wouldnt be in our evidential set. So C's accessibility counterfactualy depends on that groups victory.
(4) But if a claims accessibility depends counterfactualy on X, then asserting C commits us not to E but to X being the case — otherwise we wouldnt have the evidence.
(5) So every historical claim is really a claim about who had preservation-power, not about E itself.
(6) Apply this universally: there are no historical claims about past events, only sociological claims about archival dominance.
(7) But then the original slogan is itself a historical claim about how archives formed over time. By (5)-(6) its not about past victors, just about current archival sociology.
(8) So the slogan cant be used to critique objective history, because it denies itself the subject matter — past victories — that it needs.

this feels like it proves way too much, maybe self-refuting? which premise should I drop? I really want to keep (3), the counterfactual preservation point, because it seems like a real feature of archives. and Id like to keep the idea that theres some power-laden selection going on. but I dont want to end up saying there are literally no historical events. wheres the slide — is it (4) confusing epistemic dependance with ontological commitment? or is (2) overgeneralizing? lay it out and tell me what objective-record realists vs. strong constructivists vs. moderate evidentialists each have to pay if they reject that step.
