Construct a branching taxonomy—structured as a decision tree with split-nodes labeled by the claim that divides them and no more than seven leaves—classifying positions on whether a misfired performative utterance (for instance, a contractual promise made without authority, or a vow addressed to the wrong party) can be redeemed through subsequent acknowledgment by the relevant institutional audience, and, if redeemed, whether the result is the restoration of the original act or the generation of a successor act. Build the classification from logical commitments rather than from named theorists: the first node should separate views that hold felicity conditions fixed at the moment of utterance from those that allow retroactive satisfaction; the second should divide retroactive-satisfaction views between identity-preserving restoration and replacement; the third should divide replacement views by whether the successor act inherits the normative obligations of the original. Each leaf must carry a descriptive label (such as "fixed-condition denial," "restoration conventionalist," "successor-obligation view," or "unnamed"), one sentence stating its core commitment, and one sentence identifying its most awkward implication—especially regarding obligations that third parties relied on during the interval of misfire. Identify any logically possible combination that remains unoccupied and briefly diagnose whether it is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Explicitly flag any pair of positions that the tree's splits fail to separate. Frame the instrument for advanced philosophy students, phrase every node as a concrete claim about performative redemption that can be answered without prior knowledge of the labels, and do not include bibliographies, citations, or requests for further reading.
