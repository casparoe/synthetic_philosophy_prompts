I am delivering the opening remarks—approximately eight minutes—at an interdisciplinary workshop on institutional preservation co-hosted by our philosophy department and the graduate school of architecture and urban planning. The audience is divided evenly between moral and political philosophers and practicing designers who reason in terms of load-bearing capacity, zoning logic, and maintenance budgets rather than prescription or inherited practice. I want Burkean conservatism and the principle commonly known as Chesterton's fence to do genuine structural work in the remarks, not to serve as ornamental references. Specifically, I will present a case study: a 1950s pedestrian arcade in our city center, now commercially obsolete, that continues to organize foot traffic, informal exchange, and seasonal rituals in ways its original architects never intended. The central conceptual task is to introduce and defend a distinction—let us call it the constitutive fence—between preserving a structure because it solves a problem we can explicitly name (the instrumental case) and preserving it because its continued presence sustains social practices that depend upon it but that could never have justified its original construction (the constitutive case). The speech must be calibrated so that the philosophers recognize the Burkean logic immediately, while the architects receive it translated into design vocabulary—maintenance regimes, adaptive reuse, spatial syntax—without reducing the philosophical claim to mere nostalgia for old buildings. Crucially, the fence logic should frame the terms of disagreement rather than deliver a verdict: it should specify what it means to "know why the fence was put up" when the fence's social function has migrated far from its design intent, and whether a functionally equivalent substitute could ever replace a structure whose value is now constitutive. The remarks should leave the demolition question genuinely open, showing that the fence principle can constrain how we argue about preservation without dictating the outcome. Please avoid bibliographic appeals, citations of specific editions, or lists of further reading; construct the conceptual framework and its application to the arcade from first principles.
