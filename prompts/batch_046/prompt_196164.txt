My sister has enrolled in an experimental twelve-year protocol for an inherited neural disorder. Rather than replacing tissue all at once, clinicians substitute about eight percent of her brain each year with engineered analogues grown from her own stem cells. The distinctive feature of the program is a temporary bridge architecture: during each transition year, the biological and synthetic tissue in the same region are linked so that both substrates contribute to the same decision circuits. She describes the experience as feeling like she is co-authoring her choices with a gradually expanding presence that is neither fully alien nor fully familiar. Eventually the biological tissue will be retired entirely, leaving only the synthetic substrate running on the preserved connectivity map.

She has asked me to help her think through a concrete decision. She wants to sign a long-term research-participation agreement and update her medical directives now, but she wonders whether her present self has the authority to bind the fully synthetic agent she will become. She is not worried about memory loss—the map is preserved—but about whether the years of distributed, dual-substrate agency weaken the continuity of the self that can speak for the whole process. She asks whether this is genuinely different from ordinary personal change over time, or whether there is a specific philosophical reason to delay binding commitments until the replacement is complete and only one substrate remains in control. I want to advise her well, but I sense there is a knot here about continuity of authority rather than mere psychological continuity, and I cannot untangle it. How should she evaluate whether her present signature genuinely binds her future self?
