Write the opening remarks for the closing session of a workshop titled "Negative Valuation and Therapeutic Design." The audience is split evenly between philosophers of well-being and clinical practitioners who run craving-reduction programs. Throughout the preceding sessions, participants have implicitly operated under tranquilism—the view that only the absence of craving and suffering carries intrinsic normative weight, while positive goods such as achievement, attachment, or joy are at best instrumentally valuable as absence-maintainers and at worst generators of fresh craving. Your remarks should do real structural work: first, articulate precisely what a consistently tranquilist evaluation of the clinical outcomes presented would require, distinguishing it sharply from both hedonist and pluralist welfare theories without using jargon that loses the clinicians; second, identify one concrete institutional practice in how therapeutic outcomes are currently reported—such as the mandatory inclusion of "life satisfaction" or "positive affect" metrics—that quietly presupposes a rival theory of value and that a tranquilist should reject; third, propose a specific replacement reporting standard that respects the tranquilist commitment. The speech should run about five minutes, land as genuine synthesis rather than lecture, and shape what can and cannot be said in the room by treating the philosophical framework as governing the evaluation rather than decorating it. Do not cite authors, texts, or schools by name; speak as though you are articulating the workshop's own emerging logic.
