I'm working through Gödel's modal ontological argument with a study group, and we've reconstructed what looks like a valid extension that collapses into an obvious falsehood. In Gödel's S5 framework with the standard axioms—call a property positive if, necessarily, it does not entail the negation of any property; necessary existence is positive; and if a God-like being is possible, it exists necessarily in every world—we set out the following chain. (1) Being self-identical is positive, since identity imposes no limitation. (2) For any particular object a—say π, or the Eiffel Tower—define Φ_a as the property of being necessarily identical to a. (3) Φ_a does not entail lacking any property: if you are π, you have not been limited in any respect that Gödel's system treats as negative. (4) So by the closure axioms, each Φ_a is positive. (5) The being G that has all positive properties therefore bears every Φ_a, which means G is necessarily identical to a for each a. (6) By transitivity and symmetry of identity, all objects are identical to one another, leaving only one thing in existence. Since this monistic conclusion is absurd, the argument must fail somewhere, yet every step feels defensible: the closure principle is Gödel's own, identity is not a defect, and treating individuals as rigidly designated across worlds is standard modal semantics.

Then explain why that step looked plausible—especially why someone might think "being necessarily identical to π" is a legitimate positive property rather than a disguised relational or world-dependent construction.
