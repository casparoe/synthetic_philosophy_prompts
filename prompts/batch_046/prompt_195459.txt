As a novelist constructing a philosophical manuscript, I am trying to formalize how characters inside my narrative world should regard theories proposed after events have already occurred, and I need an external principle—Bayesian or otherwise—to adjudicate my own intuitions. Here are my verdicts on five concrete in-universe situations.

First, a detective-character proposes, after every clue has been revealed, a conspiracy requiring three hidden agents with motives never previously hinted at; the theory stitches together all prior events with perfect precision. I judge it barely confirmed, since the fit is clearly engineered after the fact.

Second, a physicist-character applies a law mentioned only in passing in chapter two to explain three previously narrated but unexplained phenomena—tidal locking, atmospheric color, and seasonal drift. The application is unexpected and exact. I judge this strongly confirms her view, despite all the evidence being old.

Third, two rival historians both account for a discovered archive that explains every prior political shift. One account requires a single hidden faction; the other requires three factions with contradictory aims. Both fit all data. I judge both are confirmed, but the simpler account is confirmed more.

Fourth, a seer predicts that "misfortune will strike before winter," and a famine occurs in late autumn. Given the narrative world's high base rate of calamity, I judge this confirms her prophetic gift hardly at all.

Fifth, and most troubling to me, are two parallel adjustments. A chemist predicts a yield of forty-seven percent and observes thirty-one percent; she revises her model by invoking a contaminant mentioned in an earlier scene but never linked to this reaction, and the revised model predicts thirty-one percent exactly. I judge she has been disconfirmed, not left neutral—the revision feels like damage control. Meanwhile, an astronomer predicts an eclipse on Tuesday; it occurs Wednesday. She revises her interpretation using a well-known ambiguity in the text's dating system. I judge she has salvaged her theory and remains roughly where she was. Both adjustments invoke prior-mentioned elements, yet my verdicts diverge sharply, and I cannot articulate why.

Is there a coherent principle of confirmation that accommodates all five judgments, and if not, which verdict must I abandon? Please respond as exactly five numbered items, each one or two sentences long, with no introduction or conclusion. Please also state how confident you are in your analysis and single out the specific point about which you are least certain.
