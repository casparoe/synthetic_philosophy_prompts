I hold that Galen Strawson's Basic Argument is sound: true moral responsibility requires that one be causa sui—the ultimate, uncaused cause of one's own nature and choices—and since nothing can be the ultimate cause of itself, no one can bear the kind of deep, desert-entailing responsibility that would make punishment or reward genuinely just in a metaphysical sense. I grant that practical, legal, and social accountability can remain intact; my target is specifically the "true" or "ultimate" variety. Before I treat this conclusion as settled, I want an honest audit of what it risks.

What should actually count against it? Please sort the defeaters by decisiveness: formal results in the metaphysics of causation that weaken or dissolve the "ultimate origin" requirement; conceptual distinctions—such as between synchronic and diachronic self-formation, or between linear and network causation—that allow authorship without a discrete first cause; empirical or phenomenological findings about decision-making and self-modification that suggest agency is iterative and open-ended rather than circular in the damaging way the argument assumes; and intuitive or experiential data about reactive attitudes that resist being fully explained away as useful fictions. For each, say whether it would overturn the argument, merely complicate it, or be absorbable as a qualification.

Next, distinguish what defenders of the Basic Argument themselves concede would damage the view—perhaps a coherent model of non-circular self-authorship, or a proof that the argument equivocates between causal explanation and moral authorship—from objections that defenders routinely dismiss as question-begging. Be specific about which premises are load-bearing and which are rhetorical.

Also assess whether the position has been qualified into unfalsifiability: has "true moral responsibility" been defined, or "causa sui" interpreted, so narrowly that no empirical discovery or conceptual refinement could ever falsify the claim? If so, is that insulation a vice—turning the argument into a conceptual stipulation rather than a metaphysical discovery—or is it defensible given the internal logic of the concept?

Finally, tell me what distinctive predictions the Basic Argument makes that rival compatibilist or libertarian accounts do not—regarding, for example, the uniformity of moral luck, the irrelevance of rehabilitation to deep desert, or the structure of blame—and whether those predictions are genuinely risky or are safely absorbed into the "practical accountability" the argument already permits. I am especially interested in whether a process-oriented or feedback-loop model of agency, in which the self is not a discrete origin but a stable, self-modifying node within an extended causal network, can defeat the argument on its own terms without simply rejecting the premise that responsibility requires authorship. What would have to be true for that model to succeed, and does it preserve anything worth calling "true" responsibility?
