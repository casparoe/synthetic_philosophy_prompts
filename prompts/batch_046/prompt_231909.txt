I'm preparing a graduate seminar on philosophy of mind, and we've been working through functionalism and the thesis of multiple realizability. Rather than running through the standard examples—pain in silicon, belief in a computer—I want to press the view with a mental state whose content and normative structure are more complex and temporally extended. Specifically, I'm interested in the belief that one has made an irrevocable error by committing to a long-term project on false premises; call this an irrevocable-error belief.

Please rank the following five candidate substrates according to how plausibly they genuinely realize this specific belief, ordering them from the strongest case (1) to the weakest (5). For each rank, provide a single sentence explaining whether the substrate succeeds because it preserves the right functional-causal profile, or fails because it misses some essential feature—whether representational, temporal, normative, or phenomenological. The candidates are: (a) a human brain in the appropriate emotional, mnemonic, and deliberative configuration; (b) a conventional digital computer running a symbolic program that tracks commitments, computes irreversibility, and updates a self-model; (c) a distributed system of ten thousand human clerks communicating exclusively through paper forms, collectively maintaining commitment-records and irreversibility-calculations with no individual grasping the whole; (d) a hypothetical instant-substitution architecture in which every functional transition is executed by a different physical object that exists only for the duration of that transition, leaving no persistent substrate; (e) a non-cognitive biological organism whose behavioral dispositions exactly mirror those of a human expressing this belief, sustained purely by evolutionary conditioning without any internal representational architecture.

After the ranking, please answer in a brief separate paragraph: does the ordering shift if we add a phenomenological requirement—that the realization include something like the felt sense of being locked into a past choice—rather than merely the dispositional and representational structure? And identify which of the five cases, if any, a committed functionalist should reject outright rather than merely placing lower, with one sentence of justification. I want this to be something a student could work through without consulting any particular text, relying only on the conceptual architecture of functionalism itself.
