At a recent colloquium on political liberalism, two colleagues — Amara and Ben — debated whether religious citizens bear a distinctive burden under the Rawlsian duty of civility. Amara opened by arguing that when citizens advocate coercive laws, they should offer reasons drawn from a shared political conception of justice rather than from comprehensive religious doctrines; otherwise they fail to respect fellow citizens as free and equal. Ben replied that this demand is asymmetrical: secular citizens rarely have to translate their metaphysical commitments — for instance, naturalism or a Kantian conception of autonomy — because the vocabulary of rights and equality is already treated as neutral public currency. Amara countered that the duty applies symmetrically: secular citizens are equally prohibited from appealing solely to their own comprehensive doctrines, such as utilitarianism. Ben then pressed that symmetry in principle does not guarantee symmetry in practice. Religious citizens must perform explicit hermeneutical labor to frame their convictions in secular terms, whereas secular citizens experience no comparable suppression of identity; this violates neutrality of aim, not merely neutrality of effect, by treating religious identity as a liability that must be bracketed before one may enter public justification.
