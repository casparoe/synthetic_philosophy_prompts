I am working through Adam Smith's The Theory of Moral Sentiments, specifically the sections in which he describes sympathy as an act of placing oneself in another's situation, and then introduces the impartial spectator as the figure through whom we judge our own sentiments by viewing them from an outsider's standpoint. To paraphrase the passage in my own words: when we observe someone in distress, we imagine how we would feel in their position; yet because self-love distorts our judgment of our own case, we must divide ourselves into two persons—the agent and the spectator—so that the latter, being detached, can approve or disapprove of the sentiment felt by the former, and only when the two coincide can the sentiment be called proper or merited.

What I am struggling to understand is whether this introduces a hidden circularity in the calibration of sympathy. If the impartial spectator is the very mechanism by which I correct my initially distorted feelings, then I seem to need an already-corrected sympathy in order to construct him in the first place. I have been calling this the calibration loop: the spectator is meant to fix sympathy, yet sympathy is required to constitute the spectator. Does Smith's account contain a resource—perhaps an innate standard of propriety, or a social process of mutual adjustment—that breaks this loop, or does he leave the circularity unresolved?

Please help me understand whether the passage addresses this tension, and if so, how. In your response, please indicate how confident you are in your reading of Smith, and explicitly single out the point about which you are least certain.
