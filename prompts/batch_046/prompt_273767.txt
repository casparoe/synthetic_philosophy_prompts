You are a senior philosopher of ethics and conceptual history, with deep familiarity with Bernard Williams’s work on thick evaluative concepts and practical reasoning, and with Kwame Anthony Appiah’s analysis of honor-driven moral revolutions. Before drafting anything, spend a few minutes brainstorming in writing: what is genuinely shared ground between an honor-structuralist and a reflective-equilibrium revisionist about how practices like slavery and dueling disappear; where do they genuinely disagree rather than talk past each other; and which distinctions—such as “normative scaffolding decay” versus “honor-code recalibration”—might clarify the dispute rather than obscure it?

Your task is to draft the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between two opposing camps, written as a formal document both sides could sign.

Camp 1 — the Honor-Structuralists (drawing on Appiah’s framework): The collapse of norms like dueling and slavery was not primarily driven by people recognizing logical errors or internal inconsistencies in their prior moral views. Instead, these practices became unsustainable when they conflicted with the honor codes of powerful elite groups; rational moral arguments served mainly as post-hoc legitimation and rhetorical coordination.

Camp 2 — the Reflective-Equilibrium Revisionists (drawing on Williams’s thesis about thick concepts and moral change): Moral revolutions involve a restructuring of thick evaluative concepts and practical reasoning within a form of life. Material interests and honor dynamics are important vehicles, but the causal engine is conceptual incoherence or reflective adjustment; moral arguments can play an independent, non-post-hoc destabilizing role.

Draft the document both camps could endorse after a year of good-faith engagement. Structure it exactly as follows:

1. A numbered section of agreed claims. These should cover: the historical trajectories of dueling and slavery/footbinding; the role of elite social groups; the inadequacy of purely logical-persuasion models; the importance of distinguishing causal mechanisms from rationalizations; and the need to avoid conflating economic interests with honor dynamics without argument.

2. A disagreements table. For each row, provide: (a) the crux in neutral wording; (b) Camp 1’s formulation of its own view in wording Camp 1 accepts; (c) Camp 2’s formulation of its own view in wording Camp 2 accepts; (d) one concrete historical case, comparative pattern, or conceptual test that both sides agree bears on the crux; and (e) each camp’s explicit prediction about what that case or test demonstrates.

The cruxes must include at least:
- Whether honor-code shifts are causally primary, or whether conceptual restructuring within thick evaluative practices is causally primary, in the end of dueling and slavery.
- Whether moral arguments ever play an independent causal role—not merely rhetorical or post-hoc—in moral revolutions.
- Whether material economic interests can be cleanly separated from honor dynamics in explaining abolition, or whether they are conceptually entangled in ways that make separation impossible.
- A synthetic crux about “normative scaffolding decay” versus “honor-code recalibration”: specifically, whether moral revolutions exhibit a sharp threshold effect in which a practice suddenly becomes dishonorable, or whether they exhibit gradual erosion of conceptual coherence, and whether any empirical marker could distinguish these dynamics.

Where part of the dispute is verbal—such as divergent uses of “argument,” “reason,” “interest,” or “honor”—identify that part explicitly, explain why it is verbal, and propose a disambiguated formulation both sides can accept.

Write with formal precision. Do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations. Do not invent direct quotations or specific page numbers; paraphrase all positions without quotation marks. Ensure every formulation is substantive enough that neither camp could sign it without genuinely conceding or clarifying its position.
