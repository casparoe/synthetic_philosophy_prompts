I am at work on a historical novel set in Copenhagen during the months following the Corsair Affair, and one of my secondary figures is a journalist who has begun reading Søren Kierkegaard's recently published review, Two Ages: A Literary Review (1846), with a mixture of recognition and dread. In order to write his internal monologue with precision, I am trying to understand a particular conceptual knot in Kierkegaard's discussion of the press and the public—specifically the relationship between simultaneity, abstraction, and ethical presence—that I have found difficult to parse despite the clarity of the prose. The passage in question, in Alexander Dru's translation, reads as follows:

"The real moment in time and the real situation being simultaneous with real people, each of whom is something; that is what helps to sustain the individual. But the existence of a public produces neither a situation nor simultaneity. The individual reader of the Press is not the public, and even though little by little a number of individuals or even all of them should read it, the simultaneity is lacking. Years might be spent gathering the public together, and still it would not be there. This abstraction, which the individuals so illogically form, quite rightly repulses the individual instead of coming to his help. The man who has no opinion of an event at the actual moment accepts the opinion of the majority, or, if he is quarrelsome, of the minority. But it must be remembered that both majority and minority are real people, and that is why the individual is assisted by adhering to them. A public, on the contrary, is an abstraction."

What I need help with is this: Kierkegaard seems to claim that the press destroys not merely opinion but the temporal conditions under which opinion can be ethically formed—namely, simultaneity with real others. Yet if the public is an abstraction that individuals form "illogically," why does the lack of simultaneity constitute an ethical loss rather than merely a logical error? Is the argument that genuine ethical decision requires co-presence in a shared situation, and that the press substitutes a statistical, retrospective aggregation for that presence? Moreover, for the purposes of my novel, I would like to know whether this critique is tied specifically to the rhythm of print circulation—its delay and dispersal—or whether it extends, by Kierkegaard's own logic, to any mediated communication that separates reception from the moment of the event. I have considered having my journalist anticipate the electric telegraph as a technology that might restore simultaneity, but I want to be sure that such a thought would be conceptually coherent within Kierkegaard's framework rather than a modern projection onto it. Could you walk me through the passage's internal logic and advise whether the critique targets mediation as such, and whether the telegraph would solve or merely accelerate the problem?
