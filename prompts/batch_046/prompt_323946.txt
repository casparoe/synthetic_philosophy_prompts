I have been working through a forecasting exercise that combines the inside view with what I am calling a "recursive reference-class audit." The procedure is this: whenever I form a belief using the inside view—reasoning from the specific details of the case—I correct it with an outside view drawn from a reference class of structurally similar past cases. Then, to honor epistemic modesty, I apply the same correction to my choice of reference class itself, asking how often people in my cognitive position misidentify the appropriate base rate. From there I apply it to that second-order judgment, and then to the third, and so on.

This seems to generate an infinite regress. At each level, the outside view appears to recommend discounting the confidence of the level beneath it, which eventually pushes every specific belief toward maximal uncertainty. The only stable endpoint looks like a global agnosticism: I end up suspending judgment not only about the original forecast but about whether I should have used an outside view at all, and then about whether that suspension is warranted.

My confusion is this. If epistemic modesty genuinely demands this recursive audit, then the outside view becomes self-undermining and methodologically useless. If modesty does not demand it, then I am arbitrarily halting the regress at first or second order, which feels like special pleading for my own reasoning. So I want to ask: at what precise order should the recursion stop, and what principled distinction allows me to apply the outside view to my object-level beliefs without being forced to apply it infinitely to itself?
