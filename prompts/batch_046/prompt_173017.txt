I have been trying to work out whether morality is objective, and I keep returning to the observation that people in Western, educated societies—often called WEIRD—seem to organize moral judgment differently from people elsewhere. In many cultures, issues of purity, loyalty, and authority carry genuine moral weight, whereas in others they are treated more like etiquette or tradition. This variation raises a cluster of related questions. Is the disagreement deep—about what morality fundamentally is—or shallow, reflecting different applications of shared rules? Can evolutionary accounts of cooperation predict which differences are stable and which are just noise? Do ethical frameworks developed largely within WEIRD populations—such as certain forms of utilitarianism or deontology—fail because they dismiss domains like purity as non-moral, or is that dismissal correct? And might some of the apparent disagreement be a failure of translation, where the same term hides different underlying concepts?

If my real aim is to determine whether moral objectivity survives this kind of cross-cultural variation, I need to know where to direct limited attention. Among these lines of inquiry—which one is upstream of the others? Which is tractable with current psychological and philosophical methods, and which depends on answers we do not yet have? Which would actually change my conclusion if resolved, and which are interesting but essentially dead ends for this particular purpose? I want a defended prioritization: the criteria you are using, the trade-offs between tractability and importance, and what would cause you to reorder the agenda.
