For my independent study in philosophy, I read a summary in an online magazine of Bernard Williams's argument that genuine moral dilemmas are possible. The author distinguishes a mere conflict of obligations, in which one duty clearly outweighs another, from an authentic moral dilemma characterized by synchronic incommensurability: two obligations are simultaneously active and cannot be ranked even in principle. According to the summary, Williams holds that in such a case the agent necessarily does something wrong regardless of which option she chooses, and that the appropriate emotional response is not ordinary regret but a rational, persisting moral cost—sometimes described as moral residue—that remains even when the agent has made the best available decision. The article further claims that Williams directs this specifically against Kantian and utilitarian theories, which allegedly deny dilemmas by treating all moral considerations as measurable on a single scale of value, and it concludes that because this emotional residue is rational rather than pathological, those frameworks must be false and genuine dilemmas are thereby established. I am inclined to think the summary captures the broad outline, but I worry it slides from Williams's phenomenological point about emotional remainder to a stronger claim about objective wrongdoing, and I am unsure whether his critique targets only theories that assume full commensurability or applies more broadly to any rationalist moral system. Could you evaluate whether this is a fair summary? Please separate what accurately reflects Williams's position, what counts as an acceptable popular simplification, and what actually misrepresents his view—particularly regarding whether he treats the persistence of regret as evidence that both obligations remain unfulfilled in an objective sense, and whether his argument depends specifically on rejecting the commensurability of reasons rather than moral rationalism in general.
