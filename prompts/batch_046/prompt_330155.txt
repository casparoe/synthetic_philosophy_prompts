In a recent study-group discussion, my peers and I realized that nearly all the canonical examples involve disagreements within a single discipline or shared methodological framework, which made us wonder whether standard peer-disagreement theory can handle cases where the very criteria for expertise and explanation are contested across disciplinary boundaries. That question led me to Alfred Wegener's proposal of continental drift, first presented in 1912, and its prolonged rejection by the mainstream geological establishment through the 1920s and 1930s. From what I've gathered, Wegener—whose primary training was in meteorology and astronomy rather than geology—marshaled cross-continental fossil correlations, matching geological formations, and paleoclimatic anomalies to argue that the continents had once formed a single supercontinent and had gradually drifted apart. The establishment rejected the hypothesis not only because Wegener lacked a convincing physical mechanism for how solid continents could move through oceanic basins, but also, according to many historical accounts, because his interdisciplinary status led geologists to treat him as lacking proper disciplinary authority. The idea remained largely marginalized until the 1960s, when seafloor spreading and plate tectonics supplied the missing mechanism and broadly vindicated his geographic claims.

I'd like to treat this episode as a case study in peer disagreement, but with a specific analytical twist: I want to examine whether the dispute was not just about shared evidence interpreted differently, but rather a deep disagreement over what counts as an acceptable explanatory mechanism in earth science—something closer to a clash over fundamental methodological commitments than a routine evidential dispute. Please address the following in your analysis. First, apply conciliationism: should Wegener have significantly lowered his confidence upon facing widespread expert rejection, and should the geologists have lowered theirs in response to his cross-disciplinary evidence? Or does his status as a disciplinary outsider mean the peer relation was asymmetric or nonexistent, so that conciliation was not required? Second, apply steadfastness: if a steadfast view allows maintaining belief when one has the right reasons, was Wegener rational to hold firm, and were the geologists rational to resist? Does his eventual vindication in the 1960s retroactively justify his steadfastness, or does it confuse being right with being rational? Third, consider whether standard peer-disagreement frameworks miss something important here—specifically, whether the establishment's dismissal involved a form of epistemic gatekeeping based on disciplinary identity, and whether that introduces an asymmetry that conciliationism and steadfastness, which typically assume symmetric peer relations, are ill-equipped to capture. Fourth, and finally, tell me what the episode reveals about the theories themselves: does it show that steadfastness is essential for scientific innovation, that conciliationism is too conservative when authority structures are biased, or that both views need to be supplemented with an account of how disciplinary boundaries construct the peer relation?
