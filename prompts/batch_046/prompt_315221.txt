In our philosophy of logic seminar this term, we have been examining Löb's theorem and its implications for rational agents capable of reasoning about their own reasoning processes. Following a tutorial on self-trusting agents, two of my peers became fixed in a disagreement over whether the Löbian obstacle genuinely extends beyond classical, axiomatised belief systems.

The first argued thus. Löb's obstacle arises only because formal systems such as PA satisfy the Hilbert–Bernays–Löb derivability conditions for a binary provability predicate. If we replace binary belief with graded credence — say, a Bayesian agent that conditions on the proposition that its reasoning module is sound — then no analogous collapse occurs. The agent might raise its confidence in a proposition P upon learning that it has derived P, but this is merely standard conditionalisation; there is no derivable principle forcing credence 1 onto arbitrary P. Consequently, she claimed, engineers of reflective artificial systems need not fear Löb's result, provided they avoid encoding explicit self-trust axioms in an object-language belief box.

The second responded with three counter-moves. First, he said that a probabilistic reflection principle — roughly, that the agent assigns high prior credence to the claim that if it assigns credence c to P, then P has objective probability at least c — is structurally parallel to the derivability conditions, and that combining such a principle with self-reference yields an incoherent assignment of extreme credences, not dissimilar to the Löbian collapse. Second, he conceded that explicit axioms are unrealistic, but insisted that implicit self-trust — for instance, a meta-learning rule that rewards internal consistency across reasoning episodes — satisfies the same conditions at the level of the update dynamics, so the obstacle is practical rather than merely formal. Third, he pointed out that even without full self-reference, an agent that treats its own past reasoning traces as evidence can enter a feedback loop in which confidence in P bootstraps confidence in the reliability of the reasoning that produced P, and hence in P again, approximating the Löbian spiral without requiring a formal proof predicate.

The first then replied that this conflates heuristic feedback with logical derivability. A learning system can weight its own outputs without treating them as premises in a deductive system governed by the derivability conditions; the conditions require specific closure properties that gradient-based updates do not possess. She also noted that the probabilistic reflection principle she was accused of holding is not mandatory — an agent can be calibrated about its own fallibility without endorsing any universal reflection schema.

They have asked me to adjudicate, and I am genuinely unsure where the boundary lies. Could you go through their exchange step by step and indicate who is right about which specific point? In particular, please assess whether the probabilistic analogue is genuinely parallel, whether implicit self-trust in meta-learning genuinely replicates the derivability conditions, and whether the first speaker's distinction between heuristic feedback and logical derivability holds up. Also, please say what both parties are missing — for instance, whether there is a stable middle position that permits local self-trust but blocks the global loop, or whether a particular formal result shows that graded credence escapes the obstacle only under conditions that are themselves unstable under reflection. I should emphasise that I am not seeking a bibliography or citation list; I want the arguments evaluated on their merits as presented.
