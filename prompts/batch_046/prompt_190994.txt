I'm taking a graduate seminar on practical reason, and after class last Tuesday my classmates Elena and Marcus got into a prolonged disagreement over coffee about how to act when you're genuinely unsure which moral theory is correct. They were discussing a concrete case: whether to accept a lucrative but ecologically destructive consulting contract that would fully cover a relative's urgent medical treatment, given that consequentialism seems to demand taking it, deontology seems to forbid it because of implicit commitments to avoid serious environmental harm, and care ethics offers no clear verdict without knowing the relational context. Elena argued that moral uncertainty should be handled by constructing an intertheoretic value ledger: assign each credible theory a credence, compute the moral value of each available action according to that theory, and choose the option with the highest expected moral value. She added a refinement she called an epistemic dampening coefficient: if a theory relies on metaphysically controversial premises, such as irreducible agent-relative duties or non-natural moral properties, then your uncertainty about those premises should reduce the theory's weight in the ledger, not just your confidence in the theory itself. So in her calculation, deontology might carry only a fraction of its raw credence because its foundational premises are harder to verify. Marcus objected that this is not neutral decision theory but a disguised meta-moral preference for empirically tractable frameworks; he proposed instead what he called robustness reasoning, where you select the action permitted by the broadest coalition of credible theories, even if that action is suboptimal for any single one. In the job case, he said, keeping a modest position and seeking alternative funding is permitted by both deontology and care ethics, whereas taking the contract is required only by consequentialism, so robustness favors the modest path. Elena countered that robustness is merely risk aversion mislapplied to morality, and that requiring broad coalition produces paralysis whenever theories genuinely conflict, which is precisely the situation moral uncertainty is meant to address. She also noted that Marcus's coalition criterion treats all theories as equally weighted regardless of one's actual credences. Marcus replied that her ledger requires an impossible common currency: there is no meaningful exchange rate between violating a perfect duty and failing to maximize aggregate welfare, so multiplying "duty-violation units" by credences is pseudomathematics that hides subjective judgment behind quantitative form. They turned to me, knowing I was just listening, and asked me to referee: who is right about which specific point, what if anything both are missing, and who is more right overall. Please adjudicate their exchange point by point in continuous prose only, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, as if writing a long reflective email; explain where Elena's dampening coefficient genuinely addresses an epistemic problem versus where it smuggles in substantive bias, whether Marcus's robustness criterion can avoid implicit weighting, and whether either approach has said anything adequate about the incommensurability problem Marcus raises.
