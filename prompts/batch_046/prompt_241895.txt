I'm trying to work out what I actually think about whether dreams are experiences, testing my intuitions against concrete cases. A vivid REM dream with flying over a city and sharp colors: yes, that's an experience, comparable to seeing while awake. A false awakening in which I dream I've gotten up and made coffee: yes, an experience of seeming-to-act, despite my body never having moved. A lucid dream in which I realize I'm dreaming and deliberately choose to fly: yes, and perhaps paradigmatically so. A brief hypnagogic flash — a sudden image of red — as I'm falling asleep: yes, minimal but still an experience.

But then: sleep-talking through a coherent dialogue that tracks a narrative plot, yet upon waking I sincerely deny any awareness or memory: not an experience; my mouth was running without me being present. Sleepwalking around the kitchen, avoiding chairs, but with zero recall and no sense that I was "there": not an experience. A "white dream" reported upon waking as "there was nothing, just blankness, no darkness even": not an experience — there's nothing it was like for me.

The pair that pulls apart: I sometimes wake with intense dread or grief, yet I can retrieve no image, narrative, or event; there is only the emotional residue. I want to call that an experience — the feeling was genuinely had — but that sits uneasily with the sleepwalker, who also lacks all narrative recall yet navigated complex space. And the case I keep oscillating on: a micro-arousal lasting perhaps a second, in which I hear a door creak, think "just the wind," and fall back asleep, retaining only the faintest trace if I am woken immediately after. Is that an experience in the relevant sense, or is it closer to the sleep-talking case?

Is there a principle — or a small family of them — that captures this pattern? I want one that explains why emotional residue counts while sleepwalking doesn't, that handles false awakening and lucid dreams naturally, and that delivers a clear verdict on the micro-arousal and on the "white dream." It should also say what to make of a sleeper in deep slow-wave sleep who, upon being woken, insists "I wasn't there at all — not even blankness," and of a coma patient whose brain shows REM-like activity but who later reports no experiential gap. State the principle precisely, indicate which clause responds to which case, give its verdicts on cases I haven't mentioned, and be explicit about which of my judgments it must reject if it is to stay simple.
