As a retired physicist who has recently turned to philosophy of history and collective memory, I am drafting a proposal for a six-month independent research project to be pitched at a small interdisciplinary workshop on narrative and politics. My half-formed idea is that philosophers and historians have studied national myths of defeat extensively—whether as ideology, identity-work, or distorted origin stories—but they have rarely examined whether these myths share a formal logical structure in how they process defeat that explains why some yield to correction while others do not. I want to examine four well-known cases: the Lost Cause narrative surrounding the American Confederacy; the Masada story in Zionist and Israeli collective memory; the Kosovo Field narrative in Serbian national identity; and the British cultural memory of Dunkirk, often called the Dunkirk spirit. Rather than grouping them all as glorified failures, I propose to construct a conceptual taxonomy of defeat modality: sacrificial closure, in which defeat is framed as a terminal moral testimony that ends a world; strategic transmutation, in which defeat is reinterpreted as a necessary precondition for a different, later victory; and causal inversion, in which defeat is attributed to betrayal or corruption rather than to enemy strength or internal error. I want to know whether this taxonomy predicts a specific kind of epistemic resilience—resistance not to all evidence, but precisely to the counter-evidence that should dissolve the myth’s coherence—and whether it explains differential resilience better than standard functionalist or psychological accounts.

The precise research question is therefore: does the logical form of a lost-cause myth’s handling of defeat determine its vulnerability to historical correction, and can we formalize that form without reducing it to generic group interest? Existing philosophical work on collective memory tends to treat narrative reinterpretation as a continuous, undifferentiated process, leaving open whether defeat plays a structurally distinct role within it; historical scholarship on these four cases remains largely case-specific and avoids cross-case formalization. My method will be conceptual and comparative, not archival. In months one and two I will map the internal logic of defeat in each case and construct the taxonomy. In months three and four I will test whether standard counter-arguments fail in modality-specific ways—for example, whether showing that Dunkirk was not a strategic victory undermines the transmutation narrative differently than showing that Masada was not a free collective suicide undermines sacrificial closure. In months five and six I will draft an approximately eight-thousand-word framework paper stating the taxonomy, defending its explanatory priority, and specifying conditions under which it collapses. I will not request a bibliography or citation list in this proposal, as I will supply those myself; the deliverable should stand on its conceptual architecture alone.
