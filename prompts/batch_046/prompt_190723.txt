My friend, who has been working through Mischel's research on personality coherence and Doris's subsequent critique of virtue ethics, insists that this passage presupposes a robust, global conception of character: a stable trait that manifests consistently across wildly different situations, which she takes to be exactly the kind of entity modern social psychology undermines. I am skeptical that the text is so committed, but I need help seeing the argument's internal logic rather than relying on modern secondary debates.

Could you help me understand whether Aristotle's notion of hexis—this state or disposition formed through habitual action—necessarily entails cross-situational consistency in the manner demanded by modern trait-psychology, or whether it permits what I am calling domain-local coherence: a stable response pattern within a particular sphere of life that does not require uniform behavior across radically different contexts? I want to know specifically whether the builder analogy supports only the acquisition of an adaptable skill, rather than the possession of a rigid global trait, and whether Aristotle's account leaves conceptual space for the kind of contextual variability that situationists highlight without collapsing virtue into a mere collection of situational responses. Please focus on the logic of the passage itself, avoiding broad summaries of contemporary literature, and address whether the mean relative to us implies a flexibility that undermines or actually sustains the virtue-ethical project against the situationist challenge.
