I'm in a philosophy of social science seminar debating whether generational cohorts are real kinds. Take the specific position that Boomers, Millennials, and Gen Z are "interactive kinds"—in Hacking's sense—with homeostatic property clusters anchored not by birth year alone but by the convergence of broadband saturation, mobile-computing adoption, and student-debt-to-income dynamics during narrow neurodevelopmental windows, with looping effects that stabilize rather than dissolve cohort identity. Present this view straight, then list exactly five points—each one or two sentences, no introduction or conclusion—on what would have to be true for it to fail. For each point, say whether it is a decisive defeater or merely awkward, separate settled claims (e.g., that demographic clustering exists) from genuinely contested ones (e.g., whether looping reinforces kind-hood), and do not present any contested sociological result as consensus. In particular, address whether cross-national variation in technological and economic timelines makes global generational kinds impossible, and be frank about which feature of the view the activity of defending it cannot reproduce.
