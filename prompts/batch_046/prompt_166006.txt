Here is an argument I have been turning over about the shutdown problem, and I find it suspicious because it seems to prove that rational goal-directedness and corrigibility are incompatible. I want to know where the error lies, if it lies anywhere, and what I pay by abandoning each candidate step. I would prefer not to give up the idea in step (3) that corrigibility requires some form of preference stability, but I am prepared to do so if that is the least costly fix.

(1) Define an agent as shutdown-corrigible just in case, for every state s it might occupy, it permits its operators to replace s with a termination state without resistance, regardless of what goals are active in s.

(2) Define preference invariance under counterfactual termination (PICT) as follows: an agent satisfies PICT if and only if, for any plan P leading to world-history W, and for the interrupted history W' that is identical except that the agent is terminated before P concludes, the agent assigns the same preference value to W and to W'.

(3) If an agent violates PICT—if it strictly prefers W to W'—then it possesses a reason to take precautions against interruption, and acting on that reason constitutes resistance. Therefore, rational corrigibility requires that the agent satisfy PICT.

(4) Now suppose agent A is goal-rational with a non-null goal G, and consider a plan P that achieves G in W but is interrupted before achievement in W'.

(5) By goal-rationality, A assigns strictly greater value to W than to W'.

(6) So A violates PICT.

(7) By (3), A is therefore not corrigible.

(8) Generalizing: any goal-rational agent with a non-null goal violates PICT and hence fails to be corrigible.

(9) But the corrigibility problem presupposes that we can design systems that are both genuinely goal-rational and shutdown-corrigible.

(10) Therefore the problem rests on a contradiction; we must either abandon rational goal-directedness or abandon corrigibility.

Which premise or inference is the weak point? Is the flaw in (3), where I treat PICT as necessary rather than merely consistent with corrigibility—perhaps an agent can have a reason to resist yet be architecturally or hierarchically constrained not to act on it? Or is there a hidden scope shift between "assigning greater value" in (5) and "having an all-things-considered reason" in (3), especially if the agent also assigns intrinsic value to being corrigible? Or does the argument go through, in which case I must choose between (4) and (9)? If I reject (3) and keep (4), what positive account of corrigibility replaces PICT—perhaps an overriding meta-preference for obedience? And if I instead reject (4) by allowing goals that do not depend on the agent's continued operation, what does that do to the concept of a goal-rational agent?
