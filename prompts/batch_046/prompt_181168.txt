I am assembling discussion materials for an advanced graduate seminar in political philosophy, and I would like you to compose a concise, conceptually rigorous dialogue—roughly 600 to 800 words—between two democratic theorists of comparable competence debating the boundary problem in a non-standard institutional setting. The scenario is as follows: a transnational constitutional convention is being formed to ratify a two-century climate-stabilization accord. Representation is restricted to polities that will be legally bound by the accord. Within each participating polity, however, there exists a substantial population of provisional residents—tax-paying workers on renewable eight-year visas who are ineligible for citizenship and domestic suffrage, yet who are fully subject to criminal law and who will live under the accord’s long-term ecological regulations for the entirety of their stay.

One interlocutor—call her A—defends a revised “all-subjected-to-coercion” principle. She maintains that democratic membership requires subjection to direct, institutionalized coercion that is authorized, in principle, by the collective will of those coerced. On her view, provisional residents are properly excluded: their presence is contractually mediated and administratively revocable; they are governed primarily by immigration control rather than by the democratic self-legislation of the polity. The other interlocutor—B—defends a modified “all-affected-interests” principle centered on what he terms “structural coercion.” He argues that because the accord’s regulations will foreclose exit options and shape life plans across generations, provisional residents are structurally coerced regardless of contractual form; further, he contends that A’s distinction between direct and structural coercion collapses once we recognize that citizens’ own subjection is equally mediated by institutional frameworks that remain, in principle, revocable.

Please ensure that the exchange is genuinely dialectical: each speaker must respond to the other’s actual arguments rather than talk past them, and neither should be reduced to a strawman. At least one speaker should concede a minor point without abandoning her core thesis. The register should be formal and suitable for circulation among seminar participants. Do not include bibliographies, footnotes, named citations, or appeals to specific scholars; the dialogue should stand entirely on the conceptual resources provided above.
