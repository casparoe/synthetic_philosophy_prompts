Next Thursday I present to a joint history-of-philosophy and science-studies seminar my interpretation of Émilie du Châtelet’s essay on happiness. I defend the view that her account is not a variant of Aristotelian eudaimonism, as recent commentators claim, but a hybrid of Lockean sensationalism and Wolffian rational psychology in which intellectual pleasure is the sole necessary and sufficient condition for happiness, and in which passions are not moral vices to be moderated in Stoic fashion but epistemic obstacles to be eliminated through sustained study. The audience will include specialists in eighteenth-century French philosophy who know her engagement with La Mettrie and Condillac well; feminist historians of science who are skeptical of readings that strip away the gender-political context—namely, that her celebration of study was partly strategic self-fashioning within court and salon culture; and one moral philosopher who regards happiness debates as best left to empirical psychology. Give me the objections in roughly the order they will come, phrased in the vocabulary each faction would use, ordered by how damaging they are, with what I should concede up front, what I must hold, and the weakest point in my own textual argument that I should defend actively rather than hope goes unnoticed. Include the one question I am hoping nobody asks—likely whether my reading collapses her into an anachronistic precursor of modern flow psychology—along with a concise reply that does not depend on citing specific editions or page numbers.
