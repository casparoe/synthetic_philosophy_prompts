I serve on the Epistemic Integrity Committee of a national consortium that certifies predictive health models. We must circulate a written opinion before our March session recommending whether to certify Model H, a hierarchical Bayesian network developed by a member institution to forecast cognitive-trajectory decline. The model's hyperparameters and structural priors were calibrated using the full record of a completed two-decade institutional cohort study (concluded 2018). The researchers now propose to treat retrospective predictive accuracy against that same cohort record as primary confirmatory evidence, arguing that the tight fit validates the hyperprior architecture and licenses deployment on an independent 2024 cohort. Several committee members object that this treats old evidence as fresh: the cohort data already informed both the priors and the model form, so measuring fit against them risks epistemic double-counting rather than genuine confirmation. Defenders reply that in hierarchical Bayesian frameworks, calibration operates at the hyperparameter level while confirmatory testing operates at the predictive level, so retrospective accuracy genuinely checks whether the learned structure generalizes within the calibration domain rather than overfitting noise. Our mandate requires a concrete recommendation—deny certification until post-2018 validation is completed; approve only under an internal cross-validation protocol that partitions the historical record into disjoint calibration and hold-out sets; or approve without restriction—together with a clear implementation timeline. Draft the committee's formal opinion memo: state the recommended course with reasons a reviewing panel could act on, engage the hierarchical-separation defense on its own terms, and give an honest account of the strongest reason for the rejected option. Write for an audience that understands Bayesian updating but does not have access to external sources; avoid requests for bibliographies, citations, or reading lists, and do not invoke highly specialized sub-literatures that a general philosophical or scientific reader would not recognize.
