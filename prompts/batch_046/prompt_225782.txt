I serve on my university's Endowment Ethics and Civic Equity Subcommittee. The trustees have received a proposal for a $45 million perpetual endowment—the Founding Lineage Scholarship Fund—that would grant full tuition and housing exclusively to verified direct descendants of families who made major gifts between 1860 and 1920. The donor's counsel frames this not as discrimination but as an intergenerational reciprocity agreement: because those families bore the institution's founding risk, their descendants hold a dynastic merit claim analogous to inheriting a partnership stake, not receiving unearned privilege. Faculty opponents argue the fund creates opportunity entailment—a cascading closure mechanism in which hereditary access to positional goods restructures the baseline opportunity set for everyone else—and propose restructuring: accept the capital only if eligibility also covers descendants of the university's historical service staff and excluded applicants from the same era. Our subcommittee must issue a formal opinion before the trustees vote in ten days, recommending a concrete course of action—accept, reject, or restructure with binding conditions—including an implementation timeline. Please draft the memo I will circulate: recommend a specific decision with a clear time frame; engage the intergenerational reciprocity argument seriously rather than dismissing it as mere elitism; present the strongest consideration for the opposing view honestly; and avoid bibliographies, citations, or requests for external research. Structure the response as exactly five numbered points, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion.
