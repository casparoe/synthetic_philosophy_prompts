I am writing an essay for my A-level philosophy coursework on whether money pumps truly demonstrate diachronic irrationality. My notes argue that they only do so if one assumes preferences remain stable across temporal standpoints; if preferences are instead indexed to the agent’s present perspective, then cyclic choices need not be irrational. To develop this, I propose a notion I call the reversibility premium—a deliberately accepted, small cyclical loss that preserves an agent’s future ability to revise how she weights present against future welfare. I treat this not as mere preference change but as a form of intertemporal liquidity, and I contend that standard money-pump objections conflate diachronic consistency with static welfare maximisation. I should be grateful for focused comments on three points: first, whether my distinction between preference reversal and preference revision holds; second, whether the reversibility premium genuinely answers the pump objection or merely rebrands it; and third, whether the overall line of argument is philosophically interesting rather than merely semantic.
