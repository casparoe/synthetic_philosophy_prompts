I have been working through the tension between classical Western accounts of personal identity and the African philosophical tradition of Ubuntu, particularly the relational claim that personhood is constituted through interdependence rather than through an internal psychological thread alone. I am trying to imagine how John Locke—who in his Essay Concerning Human Understanding tied personal identity to continuity of consciousness and memory, and who famously separated the category of man, understood as the biological human animal, from that of person, understood as the thinking being capable of being held responsible—would respond to a highly specific contemporary scenario that pushes relational personhood to its logical extreme.

Consider the following hypothetical case, which I am using to test the boundaries of both frameworks. Imagine a legal and technological regime in which entities called interstitial collectives are recognized: these are neither ordinary corporations nor individual humans, but sustained, registered networks in which cognitive processing, memory storage, and decision-making are distributed across dozens of human and non-human nodes, with no single node retaining a complete consciousness or a continuous memory trace of the whole. Each node participates in the network's actions, but none can claim to be the same thinking being that performed an earlier action, because the network's continuity depends entirely on relational maintenance—contractual obligations, data-sharing protocols, and mutual recognition—rather than on any internal psychological thread linking one moment to the next. Given Locke's commitments, do you think he would reject these collectives as non-persons, insisting that without a single continuous consciousness they are merely aggregates of persons and that Ubuntu-style relationality here confuses the causal conditions for developing consciousness with the metaphysical conditions for being the same conscious being over time? Or might he adapt his memory criterion, arguing that if the network as a whole can recall and be held accountable for past actions through its distributed records, then it possesses a kind of extended consciousness that satisfies his conditions for personhood, thereby allowing relational structures to do metaphysical work he originally reserved for internal continuity? Furthermore, how do you think he would react to the Ubuntu claim that even an ordinary individual human is not a person prior to or independent of these relational structures—would he see this as a category error that collapses his carefully drawn distinction between man and person, or might he concede that social relations are necessary for the development of the very consciousness he takes as definitive? Please reconstruct the reasoning as if deriving it from Locke's published commitments, without appealing to later scholarly interpretations unless they are part of common knowledge, and address specifically whether his individualist boundary can survive this pressure without becoming incoherent.
