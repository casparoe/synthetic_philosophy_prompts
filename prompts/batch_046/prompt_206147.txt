Consider a clinician who examines a patient and forms the first-order belief that condition X is present, but who also possesses institutional audit data showing that her own accuracy in diagnosing X is only about 60 percent. Epistemic akrasia arises when she maintains the first-order belief at full confidence while accepting that she is likely unreliable. Some philosophers defend level-splitting: the idea that she can rationally keep her case-specific judgment intact and treat the audit data as a purely higher-order concern that does not diminish the belief's content-level strength. But if she genuinely accepts that she is unreliable, then refusing to reduce her confidence in X looks like nothing more than a failure to apply standard conditionalization across levels of belief—essentially treating two pieces of evidence as if they belong to separate mental compartments. Is level-splitting therefore a genuinely coherent epistemic position, or is it just epistemic akrasia redescribed in technical language?
