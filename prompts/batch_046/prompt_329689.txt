I am drafting a novel in which a jurist present at the Eichmann trial privately composes a poem that serves as a palinode—retracting the Romantic myth of the demonic perpetrator. Write this poem in unrhymed iambic pentameter divided into two equal sections, with a sharp volta at the midpoint. In the first section the speaker should invoke conventional images of monstrous intentionality: a will directed toward harm, consciousness steeped in hatred, a face that betrays the soul's corruption. At the volta the speaker should recognize that the defendant exhibits none of these qualities; he speaks in clichés, takes pride in railway logistics, and seems unable to view his actions from any standpoint outside his administrative role. The second section must therefore be rendered entirely in the passive voice, with each line closing in an administrative construction—files being approved, schedules maintained, persons transported—so that grammatical agency dissolves in parallel with the moral agency Arendt describes. The final couplet should articulate, without quoting any source, her central claim that extraordinary harm can proceed not from a wicked heart but from a profound habitual absence of thought. The poem should be philosophically precise enough that a scholar of Arendt's analysis of the banality of evil recognizes its architecture, yet emotionally complete enough to stand alone for a reader unfamiliar with the trial. Do not include citations, bibliographic references, or requests for external verification; treat the poem as an autonomous literary artifact within my fiction.
