I am preparing a six-month fellowship proposal in philosophy of collective action, and I need the document drafted in British English. The project concerns the unilateralist's curse — the structural problem in which a group permits any individual member to take an action that binds or affects all, with the result that those holding the lowest threshold for intervention tend to act, frequently generating collective outcomes that a majority would have rejected. Rather than rehearsing the familiar applications to technological risk or simple voting procedures, I wish to examine what I term 'graded unilateralism': situations in which an agent does not execute a single binary decision but instead makes a sequence of incremental, visible commitments — such as partially adopting an interpretive framework, irreversibly allocating a small portion of shared resources to one path, or unilaterally altering the procedural terms of an ongoing deliberation. The precise research question is whether the curse produces systematically different collective outcomes, and whether corrective feedback remains possible, when unilateral power is exercised through distributed gradations rather than through a discrete action node. The proposal should: (1) state this question with precision; (2) explain why current philosophical treatments of the curse, which generally presuppose a binary decision point, leave this graded variant unsettled; (3) set out a conceptual (not empirical) methodology for analysing commitment structures and feedback loops within collective deliberation, and justify why this philosophical approach is preferable to empirical case-study or formal-modelling alternatives given the six-month scope; (4) describe what a useful deliverable would look like for practitioners designing institutional decision procedures; (5) specify what result would count as failure (for example, if graded and binary unilateralism turn out to be structurally identical in all relevant respects); and (6) outline a realistic six-month scope. Do not include a bibliography, reading list, or specific citations; refer to positions, arguments, and theoretical frameworks in general terms only, and avoid dependence on obscure technical literature. The framing should be conceptually original but remain accessible to a philosophical audience without specialist knowledge of decision theory.
