I am drafting a novel set in the twilight of the Athenian golden age, and I have been studying Plato's Laws—particularly the discussion in Book I concerning the symposion and the proper use of wine—to lend philosophical weight to a scene between an ageing legislator and a sceptical young magistrate. In the passage I am working with, the Athenian Stranger argues, if I have grasped the older translations correctly, that wine ought not to be forbidden but rather administered under strict legal conditions, for it acts upon the soul rather as fire acts upon hidden flaws in metal, or as a mirror reveals what polished surfaces conceal: it removes the composed exterior built up by custom, fear, and social expectation, thereby exposing whether the citizen's apparent virtue is a durable part of his nature or merely a costume adopted for sober company.

What confounds me, and what I need to render truthfully in the dialogue, is the precise metaphysical status of this exposure. The Stranger seems to treat drunkenness as an epistemic instrument: by suspending the usual social checks, the lawgiver can observe the underlying character of the citizen. Yet this raises a puzzle familiar to modern metaphysics of dispositions—specifically the problem of finkish conditions and masks. If social inhibition functions as a mask that suppresses vicious impulses which are genuinely present in the soul, then the removal of that inhibition by drink reveals a pre-existing disposition, just as stripping an insulating sheath from a wire reveals its true conductive nature. But if, on the contrary, the sober state is the condition in which the soul's true dispositions are formed and maintained, then alcohol does not unveil a hidden self but temporarily creates an artificial one, much as applying heat to wax creates a shape that the cool wax never possessed.

Could you help me understand how Plato's argument is intended to navigate this distinction? Does he regard the symposion as a controlled test in which masks are deliberately lowered so that the legislator may inspect the genuine article, or does he think that wine transforms the soul, generating temporary dispositions that belong only to the intoxicated state? I should be particularly glad if you could apply the conceptual apparatus of dispositions, finks, and masks to clarify whether, for Plato, the drunken revelation is an uncovering of an enduring character-trait or the production of a fleeting, artefactual self, and how this bears upon his broader claim that law should engineer the very conditions under which citizens are tried. Please reply in British English, and I should be grateful if you would avoid citations, bibliographies, or references to specific editions; I require only the conceptual clarification itself.
