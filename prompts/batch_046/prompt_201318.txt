As a journalist who covers science and ideas, I am drafting a proposal for a six-month interdisciplinary fellowship and need it rendered in a formal, polished register suitable for a board that funds conceptual work. The project concerns a notion I am calling “surviving technology,” reconstructed from John von Neumann’s later reflections on self-replicating automata and the accelerating autonomy of technological growth. The precise question is whether an artifact—such as an open-source software ecosystem or a synthetic biological platform—whose replication and adaptation systematically outpace and sometimes contradict the intentions of its original designers occupies a genuinely distinct ontological and normative category, or whether its behavior can be fully explained by existing frameworks in the philosophy of technology and evolutionary biology.

Current answers leave this open because philosophy of technology typically treats artifacts as intentional extensions of human agency, while evolutionary accounts treat replication as governed by genetic inheritance and selection for reproductive fitness. Neither framework adequately addresses technological lineages that evolve primarily through compatibility selection with other artifacts rather than through deliberate human choice or biological heredity. Von Neumann’s intuition, drawn from his widely circulated writings on automata and technological singularity, points toward a middle space: technologies that persist and mutate not because they serve human ends, but because they have become ecologically embedded in ways that make them resistant to redesign or retirement.

The proposed method is conceptual rather than empirical. Over months one through three, I would reconstruct the logic of von Neumann’s position from his published essays and lectures on self-reproducing machines, deliberately avoiding dependence on specific archival manuscripts or precise bibliographic attributions that I cannot verify without direct access. I would then apply the reconstructed framework to two contemporary cases—decentralized software ecosystems that evolve through distributed contribution, and synthetic biological systems engineered for open-ended replication—to test whether “surviving technology” captures behavioral features, such as drift away from design specifications driven by inter-artifact compatibility, that the standard tool-organism dichotomy misses. The deliverable would be a conceptual taxonomy with explicit criteria distinguishing surviving technologies from tools, living organisms, and sociotechnical networks, intended to inform policy on autonomous replication without requiring original data collection.

A successful result would be a coherent framework that explains or predicts specific evolutionary behaviors in technological lineages better than existing alternatives. The project fails if the reconstructed category collapses into standard evolutionary theory on one side or intentionalist philosophy of technology on the other, leaving no independent conceptual work for it to perform. Given the six-month scope, this is tractable as a work of philosophical reconstruction and case analysis, but it is not a full historical or archival study; I acknowledge that a definitive manuscript-based reconstruction of von Neumann’s private views would require a longer timeline and direct access to papers, which this fellowship does not provide. Please write the proposal stating the precise question, why existing positions do not settle it, the month-by-month approach, what counts as success versus failure, and an honest assessment of scope and risk, all in a formal voice appropriate for an interdisciplinary grant board, and do not include a bibliography, reading list, or citation list.
