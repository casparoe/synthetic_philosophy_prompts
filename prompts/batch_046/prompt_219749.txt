As a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy or ethics, I hold a procedural view of valid consent: if a legally competent agent, not subject to explicit coercion, records agreement after being presented with disclosures of material risks—say, a timestamped click-through or a signed form—then consent is valid. I tend to treat emotional state, relational power dynamics, and whether the agent genuinely internalized the information as secondary; they affect the quality of the procedure, but they do not invalidate the core event, much as a poorly designed interface does not invalidate a logged transaction if the user pressed "accept." What would genuinely count against this position? I want more than the obvious cases of deception or duress: I want arguments, empirical findings, or conceptual distinctions—perhaps involving structural coercion that leaves no trace in an audit log, epistemic asymmetry between system designer and user, or competence failures that procedural checks systematically miss—ranked by how decisive they are. For each, tell me what a committed proceduralist should concede as genuinely damaging versus what could be dismissed as an awkward outlier fixable by patching the protocol. Would any defeater force me to abandon the procedural core entirely, or has the view been qualified into unfalsifiability by defining "valid" around what can be formally recorded? And if it is insulated in that way, is that insulation a legitimate feature of an engineering specification, or a philosophical defect?
