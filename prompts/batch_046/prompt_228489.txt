I am writing this for an independent project in my philosophy elective, where we have spent the last month on Sartre's Being and Nothingness, particularly the chapters on bad faith and radical freedom. I want to construct an argument that applies Sartre's framework to a contemporary structural problem, but the reasoning must stay strictly within his conceptual vocabulary—being-for-itself, being-in-itself, facticity, transcendence, and the denial of freedom—without appealing to empirical psychology, neuroscience, or sociological data.

Please argue for the following position: that algorithmic recommendation systems—by aggregating a user's past behavior into a persistent predictive profile and presenting that profile back as if it were an essential identity—do not merely constrain choice externally, but actively constitute an architecture of institutionalized bad faith. The core claim should be that these systems allow consciousness to treat its accumulated facticity (prior selections, dwell times, patterns of engagement) as if it constituted a fixed being-in-itself that determines future being-for-itself, thereby outsourcing the denial of radical freedom to a technological structure. You should distinguish this carefully from classic Sartrean self-deception, in which a single consciousness plays both the role of deceiver and deceived; here, the system performs the objectifying labor, enabling the user to live in the mode of being-in-itself while preserving the superficial appearance that each recommendation is freely accepted.

Address the standard objection that users retain full physical and logical capacity to reject suggestions—since Sartre insists that freedom is never literally abolished, only disavowed—by explaining why the mere availability of transcendence is insufficient in this case. Specifically, argue that when an external architecture systematically frames the self as a closed dataset of past preferences rather than as an open, self-negating project, it generates a form of social facticity that is structurally harder to transcend than private bad faith. The difficulty should not lie in the removal of options, but in the disguise of the very structure of choice: the user is invited to understand each decision not as a free transcendence of facticity, but as the expression of an underlying algorithmic essence. Keep the argument rigorous, avoid asking for bibliographies or secondary readings, and show whether this extension strengthens Sartre's ontology or reveals a tension within it that he did not anticipate.
