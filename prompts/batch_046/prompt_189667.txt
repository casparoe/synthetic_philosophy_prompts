I'm taking a philosophy-of-science seminar where the structural-realism unit has stalled in a loop of familiar slogans—"epistemic humility" versus "only structure is real"—and my study group wants to force the debate into somewhat less rehearsed conceptual terrain without retreating into technical gauge-theory formalism that only specialists have memorized. We want to focus on what gauge symmetries actually imply for ontology and knowledge, using only the conceptual logic of symmetry, identity, and representation.

Write the joint statement that a committed epistemic structural realist and a committed ontic structural realist could both sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration. In a numbered agreed-claims section, include what both accept about the formalism—specifically that gauge redundancy is a representational feature rather than an empirical failure—about what needs explaining (why physically equivalent states should be treated as one or not), about the limits of purely structural descriptions in accounting for individuation, and about what symmetry-based underdetermination arguments actually rule out versus what they leave open. Then, in a disagreements table, state the precise formulations of where they part ways, each phrased in wording its own side would accept: for instance, whether the elimination of gauge redundancy is best understood as an epistemic cleaning of our models or as an ontological reduction that removes non-structural relata altogether, with the ontic side's position carefully worded so it does not become the strawman that they deny all talk of objects.

Identify at least three cruxes—one being whether structural relations can be self-subsistent without relata in cases like quantum exchange symmetry; another being whether predictive success in physics gives abductive support to ontic structural realism or merely demonstrates the instrumental reliability of structural models; and a third being whether identical fermions in a symmetric state genuinely lack individual identity or merely have an identity that is epistemically inaccessible. For each crux, provide a concrete case or conceptual test that both sides agree bears on it—such as the thought experiment of two field configurations related only by a global gauge transformation, or the analysis of exchange-symmetric quantum states where particle labels drop out—along with each side's explicit prediction of what that case implies for their view and what result or reasoning would cause them to revise.

Format the deliverable as a numbered agreed-claims section followed by a disagreements table, and include within the table, for each crux, the shared test case and the paired predictions. The document should be formal, balanced, and free of bibliographies, reading lists, or citation requests; it should also avoid treating either camp as obviously correct and should not lean on highly specific theorems that require specialized physics training, relying instead on the philosophical logic of structure, symmetry, and individuation.
