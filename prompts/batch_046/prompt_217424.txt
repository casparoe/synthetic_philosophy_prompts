I'm currently taking a philosophy of medicine course, and I'm working through an essay on the naturalism versus normativism debate about health and disease. Rather than simply rehearsing Boorse's biostatistical theory against Wakefield's harmful dysfunction analysis, I'm exploring a framing I'm tentatively calling transactional normativism. The core idea is that disease should not be understood as a property of an isolated organism—whether defined by statistical deviation from species-typical function or by socially disvalued dysfunction—but rather as a breakdown in what I call autopoietic resilience within a specific organism-environment coupling. On this view, a condition like asthma in a high-pollution urban environment is pathological not merely because bronchial tissue fails to meet a statistical norm, nor merely because society judges the suffering harmful, but because the ecological transaction between respiratory tissue and atmospheric conditions has lost its capacity to regenerate its own organizational boundaries. The normative force, I argue, arises not from an external social evaluation applied to a biological fact, but from the internal collapse of a coupled system's self-maintaining dynamics.

My essay notes develop two subsidiary claims. First, that naturalist accounts presuppose a static reference class that cannot accommodate conditions whose pathological status depends on rapidly shifting environmental parameters—what I term context-relative dysfunction. Second, that pure normativism fails to explain why not all socially disvalued states are diseases; the transactional framing answers this by requiring that the normative evaluation coincide with a measurable disruption in organizational regeneration.

I'm writing to ask for comments on these notes, specifically: whether this position is genuinely distinct from existing hybrid accounts or merely renames them with ecological vocabulary; whether my critique of naturalism's reference-class problem is accurate or misrepresents the flexibility of biostatistical norms; and whether there is a hidden contradiction in treating resilience as both empirically measurable and constitutively normative. Most importantly, please say what argument or observation would change your mind about whether this framing contributes anything philosophically substantial beyond the standard debate, and point out where your own reasoning is most likely to fail—especially regarding whether I have smuggled normative commitments into the biological description of resilience without adequate justification.
