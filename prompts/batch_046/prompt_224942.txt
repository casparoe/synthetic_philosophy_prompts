Then say whether psychological continuity, like Parfit talks about, can fit with anatta without falling into eternalism or annihilationism.

Student answer:
The Abhidharma schools, especially Theravada, break the person down into five skandhas — form, feeling, perception, mental formations, consciousness. There's no atman behind them, just momentary dharmas arising and passing away. This is the bundle theory: the self is a conventional label for a stream of these momentary events.

Madhyamaka takes this further. Nagarjuna argues that not only is there no self, but the dharmas themselves lack svabhava, or inherent existence. So emptiness applies to everything. This means the skandhas aren't ultimately real either, they're dependantly originated.

Now, about psychological continuity. Parfit says personal identity is just psychological connectedness and continuity, not a seperate soul. I think this lines up pretty well with Abhidharma, because both reject a permanent substrate. But there's a risk: if you say the mental stream continues, you might be sneaking eternalism back in — like there's one continuous thing flowing through time. To avoid that, you have to deny that there's any causal link between the mental states at all, so it's just isolated moments with no connection. That way you avoid eternalism.

But wait, if you deny all causal links, then you fall into annihilationism — nothing carries over, so there's no person from one moment to the next. The Madhyamaka middle way avoids both by accepting conventional continuity without inherent existence. So Parfit's view actually works if you treat continuity as conventional, not metaphysically real. Actually, Nagarjuna's view is basically the same as Hume's bundle theory, just with emptiness added on top. So psychological continuity is compatible with anatta as long as we don't reify the stream.
