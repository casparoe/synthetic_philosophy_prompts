The core claim is that John Taurek’s argument that numbers should not count in rescue cases—famously, that a single rescuer may flip a coin between saving one stranger and saving five—rests on a particularist intuition that moral urgency attaches to individual claims rather than to summed weights. I want to extend this into a less-discussed setting: algorithmic triage systems that distribute rescue resources probabilistically across populations. In these systems there is no single “rescuer” facing a binary choice; instead, a protocol assigns each potential victim a survival-weighted score, and resources flow to the cluster with the highest aggregate expected lives saved.

My essay argues that Taurek’s reasoning, if taken seriously, undermines not only direct aggregation by a person but also any distributed mechanism that treats the five as generating five times the claim-weight of the one. Specifically, I claim that the absence of a unified moral agent in distributed triage removes the very structure—an individual facing competing direct obligations—that Taurek uses to license the coin flip. Without that structure, aggregation is not an illegitimate imposition on a single conscience; it is the only available coordination method. Therefore, either Taurek’s view applies only to unitary agents and collapses in institutional design, or it forces us to reject probabilistic triage altogether in favor of randomized assignment across all victims regardless of cluster size.
