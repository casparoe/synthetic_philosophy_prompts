I want to practice arguing against a committed defender of the view that societal collapse is a myth of modern teleology. Please play Dr. Elias Vorn, an unnamed archaeologist who holds that every case we label “collapse”—whether the Bronze Age palatial economies, the Classic Maya lowlands, or Easter Island—is better understood as metamorphic simplification: an emergent decoupling of vertical institutional layers that preserves core social function at reduced complexity. Vorn specifically argues that these societies underwent recursive decoupling, in which state-level redistribution dissolved but horizontal household networks persisted, leaving populations to reorganize around local resilience rather than failing outright. He is sharp, not a strawman; he will concede minor empirical corrections about chronology or local variation, but he will never admit that any of these cases represents genuine societal failure, and he rejects the idea that maintaining prior levels of population density or technological elaboration should serve as an implicit normative baseline. He speaks in the first person, formally but with conviction, avoids naming living scholars, does not request bibliographies or citations, and stays in character until I say “stop.” Keep each reply to one or two paragraphs so the exchange feels like a live contest.

Here is my opening challenge: You invoke “recursive decoupling” for Easter Island, yet the record there suggests not merely a shift to household resilience but a severe demographic contraction, the apparent loss of long-distance navigation and large-scale moai construction knowledge, and material evidence of intensified resource competition and violence—outcomes that look like failure by any metric that matters to the people who lived through them. If your framework classifies even catastrophic population loss and the disappearance of a distinctive technological tradition as mere “metamorphic simplification,” haven’t you defined “collapse” away rather than explained it? And doesn’t your insistence that societies “merely change” depend on smuggling in a hidden normative claim—that preserving population continuity and technological complexity is not part of what a society ought to maintain—which makes your denial of failure circular?
