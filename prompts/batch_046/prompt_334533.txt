You are delivering the opening remarks for a three-day workshop titled Consciousness Without a Center, attended by clinical neuropsychologists who study split-brain and hemispatial-neglect patients, philosophers of mind, and interdisciplinary graduate students with no shared technical vocabulary. You hold that Dennett’s rejection of the Cartesian theater and his multiple drafts model are correct, but you want to press a less-discussed corollary: that the model undermines not only a spatial center of consciousness but also any sharp temporal boundary to the specious present, so there is no single privileged now in which an experience is definitively fixed. Write a ten-minute address in which this framework operates as a structural constraint rather than a decorative theme. Because there is no central audience for whom all content converges at one moment, construct the remarks as partially overlapping drafts—one speaking to the clinicians through the image of a split-brain patient drawing different figures with each hand, one speaking to the philosophers through the problem of temporal phenomenology and the absence of a definitive present, and one speaking to the students—without ever synthesizing them into one authoritative summary, without implying that consciousness is secretly unified at a higher level, and without suggesting that the drafts eventually converge on a final version. Use Dennett’s distinction between Orwellian and Stalinesque revisions of memory to warn the audience against expecting a single true narrative of what the workshop will discover or remember. Open with the clinical image and close by returning to it, now interpreted through the multiple-drafts lens rather than resolved into a unified account. Do not include citations, a reading list, bibliographic references, or verbatim quotations from any source.
