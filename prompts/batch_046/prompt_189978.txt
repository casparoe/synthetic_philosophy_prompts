Consider a moral philosopher—associated with a well-known mid-20th-century defense of intentionalist ethics in war—whose judgments on harming innocents include the following: she condemns the atomic bombing of Japanese cities as wrong because innocent civilians were deliberately killed as a means to secure surrender; she permits a surgeon to remove a cancerous uterus knowing the fetus will die, provided that death is not intended and saving the mother is proportionate; she permits strategic bombing of a factory that foreseeably kills present civilian workers, provided they are not targeted and the military value is proportionate to the unintended harm; she condemns the execution of one innocent hostage to prevent a riot that would kill thousands; and she excuses a soldier who accidentally kills a non-combatant when aiming only at enemy forces and exercising due care.

Now consider a new case: a defense ministry deploys an autonomous strike system programmed never to select civilians as primary targets and operating without human approval for individual engagements. The system’s statistical model predicts that across thousands of strikes in dense urban environments, misclassification will kill civilians at a rate of approximately 0.3 percent. The designers and commanders do not intend any particular civilian death; they foresee only an aggregate statistical risk distributed across potential strikes, judge each military objective proportionate to that aggregate expected harm, and authorize deployment. A strike is executed and kills three civilians.

Predict her verdict on both the deployment and the specific strike: permissible, impermissible but excused, or impermissible and culpable? Then identify the implicit principle running through her earlier judgments—whether it is the doctrine of double effect governed by direct intention and proportionality, a stricter direct-intention absolutism that forbids only harm directed at identifiable innocents, or a framework that conditions permissible unintended harm on the presence of direct human practical judgment in each lethal decision. Note at least one other principle among these that also accommodates her first five verdicts but predicts differently on this autonomous case. Finally, propose a further hypothetical case—one that does not involve automation, statistical aggregation, or machine agency—that would most sharply distinguish the leading principle from its closest competitor.
