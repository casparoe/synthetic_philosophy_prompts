I am a student in an upper-division philosophy seminar on the meaning of life, and our workshop group has sixteen participants with seventy-five minutes available next session. I need a structured, runnable classroom simulation that makes Schlick’s distinction between intrinsic play and instrumental work visceral rather than abstract. Schlick argues that life gains meaning through activities pursued for their own sake—what he describes as play—while adulthood tends to collapse every action into work by demanding external justification, thereby draining it of value. Please design a game with clear rules and small real stakes. The activity should open with a genuinely playful collective task, such as collaborative drawing or improvised storytelling, in which no participant holds a hidden objective and nothing is scored. Midway through, without warning, introduce a hidden rule change: half the group becomes silent auditors who must rank every contribution according to an explicit instrumental purpose, while the remaining players must now verbally justify each move within ten seconds or see it erased or penalized. Use tangible but minor payoffs—for example, the group whose contributions survive the audit decides the music for the final ten minutes, or every player must contribute one sentence to a shared text that is read aloud at the end. Provide a precise timeline for the opening play phase, the hidden-rule shift, the pressured justification phase, and the resolution; specify assigned roles, penalties, and how the facilitator should manage the transition secretly. After describing the mechanics, include a debrief plan that maps the opening phase onto Schlick’s concept of play, the shift onto the intrusion of instrumental reasoning, and the resolution onto the loss of meaning he describes. Be explicit about what will predictably go wrong—for instance, auditors may over-identify with evaluation, or players may invent hollow justifications strategically—and advise how the facilitator should intervene in the moment. Finally, state frankly which feature of Schlick’s view—most likely the long-term temporal continuity of a life lived in play, or the specific affective quality of youthful absorption—this single-session simulation cannot reproduce, and indicate what the facilitator must supply directly. Do not include a bibliography, reading list, or citations; the design should stand entirely on its own.
