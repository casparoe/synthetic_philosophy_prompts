I was discussing this with a colleague after our rationalist meetup's philosophy-of-medicine thread, and I realized that the usual statistical-function versus harm-based framing obscures the exact commitments people actually hold when pushed on edge cases. I'm drafting a handout for our reading group—mostly engineers and researchers who are comfortable with formal taxonomies but don't have clinical backgrounds—so I want a decision-tree taxonomy rather than a literature review. The root node should ask whether statistical deviation from species-typical biological function is conceptually necessary for a condition to count as a disease; from there, branch on whether evolutionary design is relevant, whether subjective suffering can be sufficient on its own, and whether impairment of social role counts as an independent criterion. Every node should be phrased as a concrete yes-or-no about one of three test cases: pregnancy, asymptomatic sickle-cell trait in malaria-endemic regions, or age-related cognitive decline—never as 'do you agree with theorist X?' The leaves should locate not only the standard naturalist and normativist positions but also the awkward intermediate views, such as one that treats pregnancy as a healthy dysfunction or one that denies disease-status to asymptomatic carriers despite clear functional deviation.

Where your tree cannot separate two positions—say, a pure normativist and a hybrid view that both invoke suffering—flag the overlap explicitly. Where a logical combination of answers has no occupant—perhaps a view that accepts evolutionary etiology but rejects both suffering and social-role conditions—note the empty leaf and diagnose whether the gap is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Please don't include bibliographies, citation lists, or requests for further reading; the deliverable should be fully usable by someone who hasn't read the primary literature, and I specifically want the taxonomy built from the logical space of answers rather than from a list of named theorists.
