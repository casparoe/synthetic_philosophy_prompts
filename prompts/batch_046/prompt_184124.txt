Question 4 (30 points): Weber argues that modern capitalism retains an ascetic discipline inherited from the Protestant conception of labor as a calling (Beruf), even after the theological framework that originally justified it has largely dissolved. Drawing on (a) the secularization of this calling, and (b) two contrasting modern institutional responses — the French reduction of the standard workweek and the Japanese phenomenon of death by overwork (karoshi) — evaluate whether the work ethic can be coherently separated from any claim about leisure. In particular, does the persistence of extreme overwork in advanced economies show that the ethic survives without its religious foundation, or does it reveal an internal contradiction in the secularized form? Use the labor-as-vocation versus labor-as-temporal-occupation distinction discussed in the course.

Student answer:
Weber's central claim is that Calvinist predestination created the calling, which then required constant labor in one's worldly station to prove one's election to salvation. Once this theological anxiety was secularized, the calling became a purely economic imperative: work is still morally elevated, but now it serves capital accumulation rather than divine assurance. In Japan, karoshi demonstrates that this secularized ethic survives intact — workers internalize overwork as a duty even without any religious belief — which shows there is no contradiction, only a translation of the same ascetic drive into corporate culture. By contrast, the French legislation reducing the workweek was explicitly designed as a legislative repudiation of this ethic, framing leisure not merely as absence from labor but as a positive moral right that supersedes vocational obligation. From this it follows that any society retaining the secularized calling while failing to guarantee substantial leisure is violating a negative right to idleness, because once the theological justification for work is gone, the remaining obligation is purely instrumental and therefore cannot outweigh the individual's claim to non-work time. Thus the modern work ethic does contain an internal contradiction: it demands ascetic labor without providing the theological reward that made that demand coherent, and the only resolution is to recognize leisure as a right that limits labor.
