After a long argument with a friend about whether the Church-Turing thesis should be read as an empirical boundary condition—like a conservation law—or as something closer to a coordinate convention that maps an informal domain onto formal machinery, I found that the framing changed how I evaluated student reasoning. Here is a philosophy-of-computation exam question together with one student answer; please grade it precisely.

Question: "What is the epistemic status of the Church-Turing thesis? Contrast it with both a mathematical theorem and an empirical law of nature, and explain why the thesis resists straightforward categorization in either box. Be sure to distinguish Church's original claim from later physical extensions."

Student answer:
"The Church-Turing thesis is essentially an empirical generalization about all physical computing devices, analogous to the law of conservation of energy. Church and Turing jointly proved in the 1930s that lambda calculus and Turing machines capture every intuitively effective procedure, establishing that effective calculability is co-extensive with both formalisms. Because 'effective calculability' is an informal, pre-theoretic notion, the thesis cannot be derived as a formal mathematical theorem; it can only be supported or undermined by empirical investigation. The physical Church-Turing thesis, which Church originally introduced to cover not just algorithms but all natural physical processes, asserts that nature itself computes only Turing-computable functions. This physical version is genuinely falsifiable—if a quantum hypercomputer or an analog oracle device were built that solved the halting problem without relying on an uncomputable physical constant—whereas the original logical version is immune to refutation because any proposed counterexample can always be dismissed as not truly 'effective.' Consequently, the thesis sits uneasily between mathematics and physics: it uses formal machinery to describe an informal concept, yet makes a substantive claim about the limits of computation that feels more like a discovery than a convention."

Also address the claim about joint authorship and the argument that the logical thesis is unfalsifiable.
