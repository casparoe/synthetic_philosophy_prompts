Below are notes for a short essay on a shared logical structure in Darwin and Turing. Darwin demonstrated that cumulative selection can generate complex adaptive competence without any guiding comprehension of the end state; Turing showed that a mechanism entirely lacking semantic comprehension could nevertheless exhibit conversational competence indistinguishable from a human interlocutor. I wish to argue that both cases exemplify what I call a competence–comprehension decoupling, but with a striking asymmetry: in Darwin the decoupling is temporal and distributed across generations, whereas in Turing it is instantaneous and localised within a single system. I further propose that this asymmetry explains why we readily deny comprehension to evolutionary processes yet feel compelled to attribute it to language models that pass the imitation test, despite both lacking it, and I ask whether the difference is genuinely metaphysical or merely psychological. Please comment on these notes. In particular, I would welcome remarks on whether the proposed asymmetry holds up, whether I have mischaracterised either argument, and whether the comparison sheds light on present artificial language systems. Please write your response in British English.
