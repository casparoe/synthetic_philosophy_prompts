After a long and somewhat exhausting argument with a close friend about whether consciousness presents a genuinely hard ontological problem or merely a stubborn explanatory gap, I find myself unable to disentangle a specific confusion. I have come to think that Chalmers's meta-problem—accounting for why cognitive systems generate the judgment that subjective experience is irreducible to physical processes—is not an independent task but rather the endpoint of the easy problems: once we possess a complete predictive-processing account of how cortical broadcasting architectures produce self-modeling reports, and once we deploy the phenomenal concept strategy to show that our "what-it's-like" judgments are transparent yet functionally anchored, we have explained both the mechanism of phenomenal judgment and the seeming intractability of qualia. From this, I inferred that solving the meta-problem necessarily dissolves the hard problem into a semantic illusion generated by the transparency of phenomenal concepts. My friend insisted that this only offers a psychological genealogy of our resistance to physicalism, leaving untouched whether there remains an ontological residue—actual qualitative character—that survives the explanation of judgment. So I want to ask: if a functional-cognitive account coupled with the phenomenal concept strategy fully explains the genesis and apparent hardness of phenomenal judgments, does that entail that the hard problem has been ontologically dissolved, or does it merely map the architecture of our mistake without determining whether experience itself is physical?
