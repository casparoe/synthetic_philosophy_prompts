I'm working through the materials for an advanced seminar on moral psychology and have found that my students consistently conflate two different objections to right action for the wrong reasons. Rather than adjudicate between them, I want to model what a rigorous adversarial collaboration would produce. Please draft the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between two camps on the nature of moral worth and acting for the right reasons.

Camp A, the deontic exclusivists, hold that moral worth attaches to an action only when the agent acts from the recognition of moral obligation itself; other motivations—empathic distress, long-term prudence, or an aesthetic preference for social harmony—may produce correct behavior but do not confer worth, and if they are the sustaining reasons, even unknown to the agent, they vitiate it. Camp B, the axiological directivists, hold that moral worth consists in direct responsiveness to the objective moral features of a situation—the suffering present, the dignity at stake, the promise broken—and that framing this responsiveness as recognition of duty is one legitimate mode but not an exclusive one; they deny that a cognitively unmediated concern for the victim's welfare is inferior to a deontically structured one.

The statement should include: (1) a numbered section of agreed claims—about the distinction between moral rightness and moral worth, about cases where correct action clearly lacks worth, and about the formal conditions under which motivation can be evaluated; (2) precise disagreement formulations, each worded so that the respective camp would endorse its own statement; (3) identification of the cruxes, especially the status of reasons-substitution (when an agent performs a right action sustained by a non-moral reason of which she is unaware) and the proper aim of moral education; and (4) for each crux, the specific case or thought experiment both sides accept as bearing on it, together with each camp's predicted assessment recorded in advance. I suggest using the shared case of a bystander who rescues a drowning child, where the rescuer's sustaining motivation is ambiguous between direct concern for the child's life, a sense of categorical obligation, and an unrecognized desire for social esteem. Format the result as the numbered agreed-claims section followed by a disagreements table. Do not provide bibliographies, citation lists, or references to specific texts; the argument should be evaluated purely on its internal coherence.
