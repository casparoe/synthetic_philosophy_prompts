Below is a short essay outline and argument about the conditions for valid consent. I have tried to isolate a condition that is not usually treated separately from deception, coercion, or incompetence, and I would like comments on whether it genuinely adds anything.

Essay summary:
Standard analyses hold that consent is invalidated by deception, coercion, or incompetence. I want to propose an additional condition—structural reversibility—by which I mean that a consenting party must retain a practical capacity to retrospectively withdraw or reframe their agreement without facing disproportionate penalties that are built into the interaction itself, rather than imposed only as external threats.

To examine this, consider a case of temporal-scale deception in digital consent. A user is told precisely which data points are collected and gives explicit agreement. The framework does not disclose, however, that within a decade aggregation algorithms will be able to infer health status, political orientation, and relational instability from those same points. No present statement is false, and the user is competent and uncoerced in the usual sense. Yet if the architecture of the agreement makes withdrawal or reframing practically impossible—because the data is irrevocably pooled and the inferred profiles are already embedded in institutional decision-making—I argue that consent lacks structural reversibility and is therefore defective, even absent explicit falsehood or direct threat.

My specific questions:
1. Is structural reversibility genuinely distinct from a subtle form of coercion, or can it be reduced to a competence failure (the agent failed to grasp long-term implications)?
2. Does temporal-scale deception actually involve deception if every claim about present collection is true?
3. Are there unstated premises—perhaps about the moral weight of retrospective agency or about institutional design obligations—that require defense?

Please comment on the accuracy of the conceptual distinctions, the coherence of the argument, and any hidden assumptions. Respond in American English.
