I recently heard a popular-philosophy podcast summarize the special-composition question and Peter van Inwagen's answer in the following way. The problem, the host said, is essentially one of physical adhesion: under what conditions do parts stick together to form a larger object? Van Inwagen's answer, he claimed, is that genuine composition happens only when parts are arranged so as to constitute a living organism; tables, rocks, and clouds are therefore not composite objects at all, but merely simples arranged table-wise or cloud-wise, with no further entity present. By contrast, the universalist holds that every arbitrary collection of parts—however dispersed—composes exactly one object. The host concluded that van Inwagen's view represents the moderate, commonsense middle ground, and that the debate is largely semantic: all parties agree on the underlying particle arrangements and differ only over whether to apply the label "object" to them. I have also heard that metaphysicians regard this as a substantive ontological dispute rather than a verbal disagreement. Is this summary fair? Please distinguish what is accurate, what is a defensible simplification, and what subtly misrepresents either van Inwagen's criterion or the nature of the disagreement—particularly whether his condition concerns physical bonding rather than life, and whether the universalist–restricted-composition split really collapses into semantics.
