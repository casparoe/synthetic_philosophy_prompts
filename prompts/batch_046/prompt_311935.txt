You sit on an agricultural ethics review board that evaluates roughly six large-scale intervention proposals per quarter—pesticide applications, genetic suppression programs, habitat modifications—each affecting insects or other invertebrates. The board includes ecologists, agronomists, and community representatives but no professional philosophers; you have ninety minutes per proposal, must produce a signed recommendation, and are required in the report to distinguish empirical conclusions from value judgments.

Turn the philosophical problem of insect sentience combined with the scale argument—where affected populations can number in the billions or trillions while the probability of sentience for any individual remains low—into a reusable decision procedure your board can apply case by case.

Present the procedure as an ordered checklist or flowchart in words. For each stage specify: the precise question; what evidence or reasoning counts as sufficient; whether the stage is factual or an unavoidable judgment call; and the branching rule—approve, reject, impose conditions, or escalate to a values-based vote. The procedure must treat separately the expected number of sentient subjects and the expected aggregate suffering, and must state whether these metrics are combined, traded off, or kept independent in reaching a recommendation. It must also handle three structural hard cases: astronomical numbers paired with weak analogical arguments for sentience; severe direct harm to a smaller population with stronger but still contested analogical support; and replacement scenarios in which avoiding one large-scale harm requires imposing another.

Then apply the procedure to two proposals. First, a broad-spectrum soil treatment projected to kill approximately eight billion soil-dwelling invertebrates over three years, necessary to prevent catastrophic crop failure in a drought-vulnerable region. Second, a gene-drive release intended to suppress mosquitoes carrying a painful human disease, projected to reduce the population by trillions across ten generations, with the analogical case for individual pain-capacity remaining disputed. Ensure that one application yields a recommendation that a board member focused exclusively on direct human welfare would strongly reject, and identify the exact stage where that tension originates.

Conclude by stating explicitly what categories of proposal or what dimensions of moral harm lie beyond the procedure's competence. Throughout, separate what is philosophically settled about this domain from what remains genuinely contested among informed specialists, and do not present any contested view—particularly regarding how moral weight should aggregate across vast numbers of beings with low individual probabilities of sentience—as if it were consensus. Where the procedure depends on a disputed premise, label it clearly and sketch how the recommendation would change if that premise were rejected.
