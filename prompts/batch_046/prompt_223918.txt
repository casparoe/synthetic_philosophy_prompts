I sit on the Research Governance and Institutional Ethics Panel of a mid-sized research foundation, and we are presently reviewing a proposal to deploy an AI-assisted peer-review allocation system. The designers claim the system achieves intent alignment in the narrow formulation of the alignment problem: it infers what reviewers intend for a given proposal — procedural fairness, methodological rigour, disciplinary fit — without encoding any substantive values about which research programmes deserve funding. My colleagues are divided. One faction, familiar with the rationalist literature on AI alignment, argues that this is exactly the right framing; we want the machine to do what we mean, not to internalise our values, since values are contested and shift over time. The opposing faction contends that intent is underdetermined in multi-step delegation chains; once the system translates a reviewer’s vague procedural intentions into allocation weights, it is already performing value-laden interpretation, and the distinction between intent and value alignment collapses. They also raise a more niche concern: the proposal relies on extracting counterfactual intent by asking reviewers to simulate what they would have decided under full information, which introduces a narrative-identity instability — the reviewer is effectively constructing a fictional self whose preferences may not cohere with the actual reviewer over repeated interactions — making the extracted intent unreliable as an anchor.

I need a formal policy memo recommending whether the panel should approve the proposal, reject it, or approve it subject to conditions. The memo must present a clear, actionable bottom line; treat the strongest consideration on the losing side with intellectual honesty; and spell out which philosophical commitments about the nature of intent, delegation, and identity drive the recommendation. Please use British spelling throughout.
