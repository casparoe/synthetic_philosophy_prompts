I am a high school senior presenting a twenty-minute paper at the state-level philosophy and history symposium next month. My thesis is that large-scale moral revolutions—specifically the end of dueling in Britain and the abolition of Atlantic chattel slavery—were driven primarily by shifts in state interest, economic restructuring, and the bureaucratic monopolization of violence and status, rather than by the honor-based moral reasoning that Kwame Anthony Appiah describes, or by the internal critical reasoning Bernard Williams associates with normative change. I further argue that honor and moral argument played only a post-hoc rationalizing role, and I apply this framework to a less-discussed case: the rapid disappearance of judicial corporal punishment (the pillory and stocks) in Britain between 1816 and 1837, which I claim collapsed not because society developed a new concept of bodily dignity but because the state found public punishment administratively inefficient and politically embarrassing.

My audience will be precisely this: three university historians (one specializing in early modern Europe, one in Atlantic slavery, one in legal history), two philosophy teachers who regularly assign Appiah’s work on honor, a debate-coach adviser who values rhetorical clarity over nuance, and about thirty peer students from AP History and ethics clubs who tend to assume moral progress is inevitable and argument-driven.
