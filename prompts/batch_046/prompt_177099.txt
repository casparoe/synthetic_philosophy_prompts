I'm working through a philosophical profile on Keynes's conception of the good life and its normative implications, and I want a consistency audit before I present it in a seminar. Here is where I stand:

(a) Keynes's essay Economic Possibilities for our Grandchildren should be read primarily as a normative argument rather than a failed economic forecast: once absolute needs are satisfied, further labor ought to be voluntary and directed toward intrinsic goods—love, aesthetic experience, knowledge, and friendship—rather than toward accumulation.

(b) Keynes's ethical framework is Moorean: certain mental states possess non-natural intrinsic value independent of their instrumental effects. This grounds his view that treating monetary accumulation as an end in itself is a kind of moral pathology, and it supports the claim that leisure and culture are objectively higher goods, not merely preferences.

(c) Keynes's macroeconomic activism—counter-cyclical spending, demand management, and the mitigation of unemployment—is justified on consequentialist grounds, whereas personal ethics should remain non-consequentialist. I take these to operate at different normative levels and therefore not to conflict.

(d) The failure of Keynes's prediction of a fifteen-hour workweek is best explained as a category mistake in social psychology rather than an economic error: he treated relative needs as genuinely relative, failing to see that status competition can structurally embed them, causing them to function like absolute needs in their demand on labor.

(e) I reject Keynes's implicit elitism—the idea that only a cultivated minority can properly enjoy leisure—while retaining his objective value hierarchy. I hold that democratic education can universalize access to aesthetic and intellectual goods without reducing their value to preference satisfaction.

(f) I also endorse a principle I call structural leisure preservation: a society that achieves abundance has a duty to institutionalize reduced working hours—through mechanisms such as work-sharing mandates and productivity dividends distributed as time rather than income—so that future generations inherit the social possibility of leisure, not merely the private wealth to purchase it.

Please audit these commitments. Tell me which pairs are in genuine tension and which only appear to be, ordering the genuine ones by severity. For each tension, identify the least costly revision and what it sacrifices. Specifically, examine whether (c) and (f) are compatible with (b), and whether (d) undermines (a). I am not sure which commitment is the weakest link, so do not treat any as obviously disposable; trace what each commits me to.
