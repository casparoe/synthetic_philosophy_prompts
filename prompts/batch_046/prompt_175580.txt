In discussions of formal systems, artificial intelligence, and reflexive reasoning, I keep encountering the slogan “you can’t jump out of the system”. It is invoked both to dismiss attempts at meta-level logical reasoning and to argue that an artificial agent could never genuinely revise its own architectural constraints. I suspect the phrase conceals several distinct theses, but I cannot tell which one is actually doing the work in each case.

Could you disambiguate the slogan into a precise menu of readings? In particular, separate: (a) a claim about syntactic closure — that every derivation inside a formal system must employ rules internal to that system; (b) a claim about semantic ascent — that representing a framework as an object requires a richer metalanguage which the original framework cannot fully encode; (c) a claim about pragmatic frame-dependence — that any act of reasoning, including self-revision, presupposes background norms or categories that cannot all be made explicit at once; and (d) a claim about epistemic circularity — that using one’s own cognitive apparatus to evaluate that apparatus yields no independent warrant.

For each reading, state whether it is a tautology, whether it expresses a substantive result about formal systems (such as the limitations illustrated by the MU-puzzle and Hofstadter’s discussion of JOOTSing), and whether it genuinely blocks “jumping out” in the sense of leaving a system behind rather than merely operating within a larger one. Then indicate which reading is typically meant when the slogan is used to reject proposals for AI self-modification, and whether that reading survives once Gödel’s second incompleteness theorem is distinguished from a general claim about agent-level reflexivity. Please answer in British English, and lay the readings out as a menu with verdicts rather than selecting a single interpretation.
