I'm working on a research paper for my philosophy elective about whether chaos theory actually changes anything important about determinism. So far my reasoning is that determinism means every future state is uniquely fixed by present conditions and the laws of nature, which should imply that the future is predictable in principle. But chaos theory introduces Lyapunov exponents that set a hard predictability horizon: beyond that point, errors grow exponentially, and since no physical measurement can have infinite precision, even a Laplace-style demon could never actually compute distant future states of a genuinely chaotic system. So determinism starts to look metaphysically true but physically empty. Then I read about the shadowing lemma, which apparently guarantees that any approximate numerical trajectory of a chaotic system stays close to some exact trajectory of the same system. If that's right, then our failed predictions aren't random noise; they're always shadowing some real deterministic path. That makes determinism feel like it's doing no real work: the future is fixed, but we can never access which path it is, and our errors are systematically tied to other real paths rather than to indeterminacy. So my question is this: doesn't this show that determinism and indeterminism are practically indistinguishable for chaotic systems? And if the distinction collapses in practice, doesn't that also collapse the standard argument that determinism threatens free will, since determinism wouldn't be constraining anything in a way we could ever detect?
