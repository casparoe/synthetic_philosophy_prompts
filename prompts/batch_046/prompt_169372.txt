I'm writing this after a frustrating discussion in my moral philosophy seminar last Tuesday, where we were examining whether hypocrisy genuinely undermines one's entitlement to blame others. A fellow student kept invoking the proverb that people in glass houses shouldn't throw stones, using it to claim that anyone who has engaged in a particular wrong—say, an environmental activist who takes frequent flights—automatically forfeits all standing to blame others for that same wrong. I tried to resist the inference, but I found myself unsure whether my resistance was directed at the proverb itself or merely at the way my classmate was applying it. Before I return to office hours, I want to understand exactly what precise theses that one-liner could be expressing, since it seems to be doing several different kinds of work at once.

Could you lay out the distinct readings as a menu? I am especially interested in separating: a purely descriptive claim about social credibility or hypocrisy as a character flaw that makes blame seem graceless; a normative claim about forfeiting a specific entitlement or authority to issue blame for a particular wrong; a stronger global claim that any moral failing cancels one's standing to blame anyone for anything whatsoever; a pragmatic claim about the conditions under which blame can be effectively received or justified as a communicative act; and perhaps a distributive or comparative claim about whether blame must be equally available to all members of a moral community regardless of their own conduct. For each reading, please indicate whether it is trivial, substantive but defensible, or false, and—crucially—say which ones actually license my classmate's conclusion about the activist and which ones do not.

There is one further distinction I keep returning to, and I would like your assessment of whether it can be made conceptually stable: the idea that hypocrisy might undermine standing only within the specific domain or type of wrong one has committed, rather than globally. Is there a principled way to draw that boundary—perhaps by appealing to the particular relationship between blamer and blamed, or to the specific moral authority required for addressing that kind of failure—without the position collapsing into either the view that hypocrites retain full standing or the view that they lose it entirely? I am not asking for a bibliography or named theorists; I simply want to know whether that intermediate, domain-restricted conception of forfeited standing is coherent, and what it predicts about nearby cases where the wrongs are analogous but not identical—such as an activist who drives an inefficient car blaming others for flying, or one who avoids meat criticizing others for food waste. Please treat the proverb not as a settled doctrine but as a compressed argument that must be unpacked before it can do any real work in our debate.
