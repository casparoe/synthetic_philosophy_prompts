I am trying to determine whether a recurring argumentative pattern I have noticed has an established name, or whether it marks an unrecognized boundary case. The pattern arises in discussions of distributive principles, especially in contexts shaped by contractualist reasoning about reasonable rejection. It unfolds in three stages, illustrated by a scenario in which a severe harm to one person can be avoided only by distributing an equivalent total burden as a trivial cost across many people.

In the first stage, a principle that concentrates the severe harm on a particular individual to benefit the group is rejected: that individual is said to have a decisive complaint, and the fact that many others benefit does not dilute it. In the second stage, a principle that distributes the equivalent total burden evenly across the group is accepted, on the grounds that each person's share is too small to ground a reasonable rejection, even though every affected person is worse off than they would be under the first principle if they happened to be among the beneficiaries. In the third stage, when this tension is pressed, some defenders invoke an ex ante or lottery framing: before knowing one's position, each person might rationally accept the risk of being the severely burdened one in exchange for avoiding the dispersed burden. Critics respond that this framing collapses the moral distinction between actual victims and merely potential ones, effectively erasing the difference that made the first two stages seem incompatible.

What I am asking about is not the ex ante/ex post distinction itself, nor the standard aggregation problem about whether numbers carry moral weight. Rather, it is the specific oscillation between indexing complaints to actual individuals (stage one), to dispersed actual burdens (stage two), and then to hypothetical roles under uncertainty (stage three), in order to preserve both the non-aggregability of individual standing and the significance of group size. Does this three-stage oscillation have a precise label in the literature on contractualism, moral risk, or aggregation? If not, I want a careful carving: what exactly distinguishes it from the standard numbers problem, from the ex ante defense of aggregate welfare, and from the non-identity problem; whether its occurrence requires that total harm be held constant across the stages; and where its edges lie — for instance, whether it can arise with incommensurable burdens or only with quantitatively equivalent ones.
