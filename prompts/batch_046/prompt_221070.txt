As a novelist drafting a historical novel set in the seventeenth century, I am trying to formalize my working philosophy of historiography for a craft essay, and I need an audit of whether these commitments actually cohere. Here is where I stand. (1) I accept methodological presentism: every interpretation of past mental life requires translation into conceptual categories available to us now, so there is no unmediated access to historical actors on their own terms. (2) I reject evaluative presentism: moral judgment of past figures should be suspended in favor of reconstructing their internal normative horizons, and labeling them by modern ethical standards is an anachronism that obstructs understanding rather than deepening it. (3) I insist on a sharp separation between hermeneutic reconstruction and ethical assessment, holding that the two tasks can be conducted in parallel without contamination. (4) I maintain that fiction depicting real dead people carries a duty of non-sanitization: one must not suppress actions or social conditions that contemporary readers would find abhorrent, even in sympathetic portrayals, because omission falsifies the historical record and erases victims. (5) I hold that practices radically alien to modern experience—those lacking direct analogues—must be rendered through conceptual bridge analogies drawn from present social institutions, and that these analogies necessarily import normative coloring from our own moral ecology. (6) I believe the highest achievement in historical fiction is a sustained double vision, in which the reader simultaneously grasps the actor's self-understanding and retains an uncollapsed modern critical awareness, neither perspective being reduced to the other.

Go through these commitments and audit them for joint tenability. Specifically: identify which pairs are in genuine tension, which only appear to conflict but dissolve on inspection, and rank the real tensions by severity. For each tension, specify what the conflict is, how badly it threatens the set, and what the cheapest revision would be—whether giving up one thesis, weakening it, or introducing a distinction that saves both. Then tell me which single revision, if I made it alone, would eliminate the most tension across the board, and what that revision costs my larger artistic and intellectual aims. Be precise about whether the problem lies between interpretation and evaluation, between fidelity to the dead and accessibility to the living, or in the architecture of double vision itself.
