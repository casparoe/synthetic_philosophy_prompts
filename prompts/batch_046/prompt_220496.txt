Following a recent bereavement, I have been consulting with clinicians who treat prolonged grief disorder, and I have noticed that their therapeutic disagreements—whether to address grief through cognitive restructuring of appraisals, through somatic regulation of bodily arousal, or through mindful acceptance of qualitative feeling—mirror a philosophical deadlock I now find personally urgent. For decades, theorists have disputed whether emotions are essentially judgments of value or significance, perceptions of bodily states or environmental threats, or conscious feelings with distinctive phenomenology. The arguments are canonical: judgment theories account for intentionality and rational evaluation but struggle with non-human and infant cases; perception theories accommodate evolutionary continuity but seem to miss the normative dimension; feeling theories capture immediacy but risk reducing emotions to raw sensation. Yet no side concedes ground. I am not asking for a verdict on which theory is correct. Instead, I want a structural diagnosis of why this debate has stalled. Is the disagreement verbal—centered on competing stipulations of the word "emotion"—or substantive but hidden? Do the camps answer genuinely different questions—one about rational assessment, one about biological function, one about phenomenological essence—while believing they compete over a single nature? Is there an unacknowledged empirical dispute, perhaps about whether emotional awareness requires conceptual content or can occur in pre-linguistic organisms, that both sides assume resolves the issue? Or is the stall a sign that "emotion" is a family-resemblance category with no unified essence, making the demand for one theory a category mistake? Please diagnose using what each side actually says—how they treat fear as judgment of danger, as perception of threat, or as felt constriction—rather than offering a survey, and specify what, if anything, would count as progress: a distinction that separates the questions, an empirical finding, or an argument that resolution should not be expected.
