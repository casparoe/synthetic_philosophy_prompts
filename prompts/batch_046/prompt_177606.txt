Over the past year, as my mother’s Alzheimer’s has eroded her episodic memory, I have become acutely aware that large fragments of her personality—her ironic reflexes, her particular moral weighting of loyalty over fairness—now seem to operate inside my own decision-making with an autonomy that feels less like memory and more like borrowed architecture. Reading Hofstadter’s account of the self as a strange loop sustained not by neural substrate but by a self-referential pattern, I began drafting an essay that asks whether an “I” can be genuinely distributed across two brains, not merely represented in one. I want to argue that in long-term intimate bonds—particularly in caregiving relationships where one brain gradually fails—the strange loop can become inter-brain: Brain A’s self-model treats outputs from Brain B as internal reference points, and vice versa, so that a single loop of self-reference spans both substrates. I further want to claim that this distribution is structurally asymmetrical; when one substrate degrades, the loop does not vanish but contracts into the remaining brain, carrying with it fragments of the distributed self that now run without the original reference frame. I call this “asymmetric contraction,” and I take it to be a literal consequence of a pattern ontology rather than a metaphor for grief.

Yet I am unsure whether I am conflating two distinct Hofstadterian claims: that each brain contains representational copies or “souls” of others within its own private loop, versus the stronger thesis that there is one loop distributed across substrates. I also worry that my argument smuggles in a requirement for unified phenomenal experience—something like a single “what it is like” spanning both brains—when Hofstadter’s framework might only permit parallel loops with high mutual fidelity. Finally, I wonder whether asymmetric contraction is coherent: if the loop is genuinely distributed, can it survive the loss of half its substrate without becoming a different loop altogether, or does the remaining fragment merely simulate the lost portion?

I am writing these notes not as a finished argument but as the conceptual skeleton of the essay, and I would value your comments specifically on whether the distinction between intra-brain representation and inter-brain loop is maintained consistently, whether asymmetric contraction respects a substrate-independent pattern view or secretly reintroduces substrate-dependence, and whether there are obvious objections—particularly about phenomenal unity or personal identity over time—that I am missing. I do not need citations, reading lists, or references to secondary literature; I am working from the general framework of strange loops and pattern-based identity and want to know whether the architecture I have sketched holds together conceptually.
