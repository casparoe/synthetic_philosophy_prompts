In my seminar on moral change and social history, two fellow students, Nadia and Jonas, became locked in a sustained disagreement about why certain norms—dueling in nineteenth-century Britain, foot-binding in late imperial China—collapse with startling speed after persisting for centuries. I am preparing my essay and genuinely cannot adjudicate their exchange myself; I need a rigorous, point-by-point verdict on who is right about which specific claim, what each is missing, and whether there is a genuinely split difference.

Nadia opened by defending Appiah’s honor-based model. She argued that practices like dueling do not end because moral arguments finally prevail—Enlightenment critiques of dueling circulated for well over a century before the practice faded—but because the peer group that grants status withdraws its recognition. Once dueling came to signal barbarism rather than courage, the incentive structure embedded in honor collapsed, and elites abandoned it almost overnight. She stressed that this explains both speed and asymmetry: slavery, by contrast, persisted for centuries despite abundant moral argument precisely because slaveholding remained honorable within the dominant peer group, and only collapsed when that internal honor code was disrupted.

Jonas countered with what our syllabus calls the Williams thesis: rapid normative change tracks institutional and economic realignment, not shifts in honor. He claimed that dueling vanished because the British state centralized legitimate violence through professional police and courts, and because emerging capitalist institutions required predictable contract enforcement that private violence undermined. The “honor” narrative, on his view, is an ex post facto rationalization—elites constructed a story about civilization and refinement only after institutional pressures had already made dueling impractical. He added that slavery’s persistence actually supports his view, since slaveholding was deeply embedded in economic institutions; abolition occurred only when industrial capitalism and geopolitical interests made slave labor less profitable and more politically costly, not because slaveholders suddenly lost peer respect.

Nadia replied by granting that institutions matter but insisting that Jonas confuses the vehicle with the driver. She introduced a distinction she said was underdeveloped in our course materials: horizontal honor, which is peer-to-peer recognition within a closed social class, versus vertical honor, which is recognition mediated by state or bureaucratic institutions. In Britain, she argued, dueling died not merely because the state monopolized violence, but because the aristocracy itself began to seek vertical honor—political office, bureaucratic rank, imperial credentials—rather than horizontal honor among a restricted gentry circle. Once vertical honor replaced horizontal honor, dueling became a liability because it threatened state-mediated status. This, she said, is still fundamentally an honor story, not an interest story: people changed behavior because they cared about status, and the form of status they cared about had shifted.

Jonas responded that Nadia’s distinction actually collapses into his position. The shift from horizontal to vertical honor is itself caused by institutional realignment—the expansion of the franchise, the professionalization of the civil service, and imperial expansion—not the other way around. He pointed to foot-binding: reformers did invoke “national honor,” but they did so precisely because the Qing state needed healthy conscripts and modern laborers. Without that institutional need, the honor rhetoric would have been ignored, just as earlier Confucian arguments against foot-binding had been ignored for centuries. Therefore, honor language is always the surface expression of deeper institutional interests, and Appiah mistakes this expression for the cause.

Nadia then raised a final objection: if Jonas is correct, we should expect norms to change in direct proportion to economic efficiency, yet many inefficient norms persist—certain ritualized gift-exchange obligations, sumptuary codes—while some efficient practices face prolonged resistance. She argued that honor has an independent causal power because it operates through what she called “recognitional goods”—goods that exist only when collectively acknowledged—whereas institutional interests operate through material incentives. The two can come apart, and when they do, honor often wins in the short term, explaining why moral revolutions can feel sudden and non-instrumental.

Jonas concluded by saying that Nadia’s “recognitional goods” are still embedded in institutional structures: collective acknowledgment is itself maintained by repeated interaction within specific organizational forms, and when those forms change, the goods disappear. He accused her of reifying honor into an independent force rather than seeing it as the phenomenological shadow of structural change.

They turned to me and asked for a verdict.

Please adjudicate this disagreement rigorously. For each round, indicate which party makes the stronger point and on what specific claim. Then say who is more right overall, or whether the truth is genuinely split. In particular, address whether Nadia’s horizontal-versus-vertical-honor distinction genuinely saves Appiah’s view from Jonas’s institutional critique, or whether Jonas is correct that the distinction collapses into institutional explanation. Also discuss whether both parties are neglecting a third factor—perhaps the role of intermediary figures who translate institutional pressure into honor-language, or a systematic temporal lag between structural realignment and normative collapse—and explain how that omission affects their arguments.
