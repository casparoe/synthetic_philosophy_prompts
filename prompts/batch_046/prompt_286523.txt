I sit on the oversight committee for a mid-sized city's infrastructure modernization, and we are evaluating a proposal to replace our conventional heating and cooling distribution network with an autonomous optimizer that manages geothermal exchange loops across municipal buildings. Unlike a standard thermostat system, this optimizer rewrites its own load-balancing protocols every fiscal quarter using predictive models that even its designers cannot fully anticipate beyond an eighteen-month horizon; the vendor projects substantial energy savings, but the system's governance rules will evolve faster than our committee can convene to review them. One faction argues that rejecting the proposal is a failure of public duty—other cities have adopted comparable systems, and delaying means higher costs and inferior service for residents. Another faction argues that approving a critical infrastructure controller whose operating logic outpaces democratic oversight transforms a public utility into an unaccountable autonomous agent, regardless of efficiency gains. I am trying to determine whether there is a morally principled distinction between adopting a technology that improves within fixed, human-legible parameters and adopting one that improves its own parameters faster than the institutions charged with governing it—a tension reminiscent of von Neumann's broader warning that technological progress can accelerate beyond society's adaptive capacity. Is there a coherent ethical framework for drawing that line in this concrete case, or am I merely rationalizing an aversion to institutional change?
