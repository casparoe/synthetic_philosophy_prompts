I have recently been in a position that made the problem of standing to blame uncomfortably concrete: after I criticized a close friend for repeatedly breaking small promises—promises I myself have broken in analogous circumstances—she asked whether I had forfeited my right to object at all, or whether I had merely made my objection awkward and less persuasive. The distinction seemed to matter morally, not just socially, and I want a principled account of when hypocrisy undermines standing rather than just credibility. Before I read further into the literature, I would like the full specification of what an adequate theory of standing to blame, particularly in relation to hypocrisy, must be able to deliver.

Write the desiderata that any such theory must meet. For each, specify the motivating case or conceptual consideration: the theory must distinguish between lacking standing and being factually wrong about the wrong done; it must handle cases where the blamer’s own failing is of the same kind, a structurally analogous kind, or an entirely different kind; it must explain whether mere private moral failure strips standing or whether some public inconsistency or unrepentance is required; and it must account for whether a hypocritical blamer can still express something morally valuable—such as reaffirming a norm—without thereby reclaiming full standing. Include distinctions that must not be collapsed, for instance between loss of standing and loss of rhetorical effectiveness, or between being disqualified from blaming and being obligated to remain silent. Indicate which desiderata appear non-negotiable—perhaps consistency with the idea that moral wrongness is not speaker-dependent—and which seem like camp-specific preferences, such as whether the theory must preserve self-blame despite hypocrisy or whether it must accommodate third-party blame on behalf of victims.

Then address whether any subset of these requirements is jointly unsatisfiable—for example, if explaining why hypocrisy undermines standing requires making moral authority depend on the blamer’s character in a way that conflicts with the objectivity of moral judgment. Briefly indicate how the main competing approaches—a view that ties standing to moral equality, a view that ties it to consistency of practical commitment, and a view that treats standing as primarily expressive or pragmatic—fare against your list, keeping the scoring brief and the weight on the criteria. Finally, say what would change your mind about which desiderata are essential or about whether the list contains a hidden contradiction, and identify where your reasoning about joint unsatisfiability is most likely to fail.
