I'm noticing a recurring pattern in practical reasoning that I don't have a precise name for, and I want to know whether philosophy has already carved it out. Here are three concrete instances.

First, in environmental policy debates, someone argues that a particular ecosystem configuration "ought to be preserved" and treats this as generating a genuine obligation for present agents. But when asked whose welfare or rights are at stake, they deny it's about any present or future sentient being; the reason is said to inhere in the ecological structure itself, as if the arrangement has a rational claim on continuation that doesn't depend on anyone being able to recognize it.

Second, in discussions of institutional fairness, a designer insists that a decision procedure must be unbiased even in a hypothetical scenario where every affected party is permanently ignorant of how the decision was made and where the bias produces no experiential difference for anyone. The justification isn't that bias might eventually be discovered or that it corrupts the agents running the procedure; it's that unfairness in the structure is intrinsically rationally defective, regardless of who, if anyone, can register the defect.

Third, in AI alignment, people sometimes speak of "the right output" for a system as if there's a normative fact about what it should do that isn't reducible to user preferences, harm avoidance, or internal coherence—just a standalone rational requirement that binds the system even though the system has no subjective standpoint from which the requirement could be grasped.

The common thread: normative force is attributed to non-mental structures—ecosystems, procedures, algorithmic states—without being anchored in any agent who could act for that reason or suffer from its neglect. It isn't exactly agent-neutral morality, because those reasons are usually still addressed to agents (everyone has reason to promote them). Here the reason seems to float without a reasoner.

Is there an established concept for this specific configuration—reasons that apply to no one in particular yet are treated as genuinely action-guiding for someone? How does it differ from objective list theories of value, from institutional reasons, and from category errors about attributing mental properties to systems? If there's no precise term, can you coin one, define it, and mark its boundary cases—especially distinguishing it from cases where a reason is agent-neutral but still addressed to all agents?
