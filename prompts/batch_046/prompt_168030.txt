In Borges's essay "A New Refutation of Time," he denies that temporal succession is anything more than a mental fiction, arguing that because we never perceive duration itself—only discrete, simultaneous impressions—there can be no real distinction between past, present, and future. From this one might infer that Borges is committed not merely to a Humean skepticism about time, but to a radical eternalism in which every moment is literally co-instantiated in a single, timeless present, analogous to the Aleph that contains all spatial points at once. If that metaphysical picture is correct, then the branching causal structure of "The Garden of Forking Paths"—in which a single present diverges into mutually exclusive futures—appears incoherent: there is no shared present from which branches can diverge, nor any temporal dimension in which divergence could occur. Should we therefore read Borges's fiction as operating within a pragmatic, narrative fiction of linear causation that he simultaneously denies at the philosophical level? Or does his rejection of succession actually permit a non-temporal form of branching—perhaps analogous to the logical divergence of worlds described in "Tlön, Uqbar, Orbis Tertius"—that requires no real temporal substrate?
