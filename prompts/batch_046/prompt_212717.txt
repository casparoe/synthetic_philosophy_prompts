Consider the conceptual tension between Raphael Lemkin’s broader original framing of genocide—which included cultural and social destruction of groups—and the narrower, perpetrator-intent-focused definition codified in the 1948 UN Convention. A persistent problem is the “naming gap”: legal classification demands documented specific intent to destroy a protected group as such, whereas moral and political recognition often responds to experienced collective harm, group vulnerability, and structural destruction even when physical killing is absent or intent is diffuse, as in cases of forced assimilation, ecological destruction of indigenous lifeways, or demographic engineering. If you are advising a philosopher of law with about three years, no empirical fieldwork capacity, and a background in conceptual analysis rather than international relations, which two or three open questions in this space deserve priority? Rank them by upstream importance—settling which would most reshape how borderline cases are classified, how epistemic authority is divided between victim testimony and legal investigation, and whether the category should be expanded, pluralized, or kept deliberately narrow. For each, defend its tractability without archival or empirical legal research, identify the downstream debates that depend on it, and specify what kind of conceptual argument—not citation—would cause you to reorder them.
