I'm a logician who works mostly in reverse mathematics — calibrating the axiomatic strength required to prove classification results about discontinuities in real-valued functions — and I recently tried to explain my field to a philosopher friend over dinner. She asked whether there's any parallel between deciding that a theorem requires a stronger axiom and deciding that a belief needs revision in light of experience. That sent me back to Quine's critique of the analytic-synthetic distinction, which I haven't revisited seriously since graduate school, and I'm now trying to map the entire debate onto the conceptual vocabulary I actually use.

Quine rejects the idea that there's a sharp boundary between truths holding by virtue of meaning and truths holding by virtue of fact, insisting instead that statements face experience as a corporate body and that we can preserve any belief if we're willing to make adjustments elsewhere in the web of belief. I want you to restate this position — both the distinction Quine is attacking and the holistic alternative he offers — using only the resources I deal with daily: formal systems with restricted comprehension, conservative extensions, interpretability between theories, and the practical decision of whether to weaken a desired result, strengthen the base system, or conclude that the result was never "purely definitional" relative to one's axioms after all.

More precisely: does the analytic-synthetic boundary map onto the difference between what's provable in a minimal base system without additional ontological commitments versus what requires stronger axioms? Does Quine's claim that we hold some beliefs more firmly and revise peripheral ones instead correspond to a mathematician's reluctance to weaken the base system versus adjusting an auxiliary lemma? And when Quine describes holistic confirmation — protecting a central belief by revising distant parts of the web — is there a counterpart in how I actually work, say, when a classification theorem for discontinuities fails in my weak system and I must choose between adding an existence axiom, restricting the class of functions, or denying that the theorem was ever analytic relative to my definitions? Then provide the residue report: which parts translate cleanly into this axiomatic vocabulary, which require distortion — for instance, does "truth by convention" survive as anything other than "true in every model of the language"? — and is there anything in Quine's argument, perhaps about language learning or the social negotiation of meaning, that simply has no counterpart in how formal systems are calibrated and extended? I want to know whether the gap lies in my framework, missing a genuine philosophical point about convention and meaning, or in Quine's debate, dissolving once you see how mathematicians actually revise belief; and I want the edges of whatever concept survives drawn precisely, not just named.
