I have been trying to steel-man the concern, often raised in rationalist and philosophy-of-math circles, that our foundational systems—ZFC, Peano arithmetic, or fragments used in applied fields—might harbor an inconsistency that is currently beyond our proof-theoretic reach, not hidden in an exotic branch but buried at a depth that exceeds present computational or human resources. Rather than debating whether this is likely, I want the full specification for what a coherent theory of mathematical practice under live foundational risk would actually need to deliver.

Please write the desiderata list. For each requirement, specify the phenomenon it must account for—such as why working mathematicians continue to build on apparently risky bases, how a discovered contradiction should be localized rather than globalized, and the difference between deriving a false statement and encountering an undecidable result that is misread as contradiction—the concrete case or distinction it must preserve—such as an inconsistency in basic arithmetic versus one in large-cardinal set theory, the historical revision of naive set theory, or a computer-verified contradiction in a system previously thought safe—and whether the requirement is a hard constraint or negotiable given the purpose of the theory.

In particular, the framework must preserve the distinction between logical risk, meaning the system actually derives a contradiction, and epistemic risk, meaning we lack sufficient warrant to rule one out; between repair through restriction of the system and total abandonment; and between an axiom being false and being unusable in its present form. It must also remain consistent with standard results in logic, including the monotonicity of consistency strength and the limitations on self-verification, without reducing these to platitudes.

Then identify which single desideratum does the most work in excluding the common informal stances: that mathematicians would simply patch the hole as occurred with earlier paradoxes, that an inconsistency renders a system entirely useless, or that consistency is merely a matter of not having looked hard enough. Note any tensions where the desiderata appear jointly unsatisfiable.

Keep the weight on the criteria and their motivating cases. Brief notes on how well-known orientations—formalism, Platonism, pragmatic continuation—fare against the list are welcome, but do not substitute them for the specification itself.
