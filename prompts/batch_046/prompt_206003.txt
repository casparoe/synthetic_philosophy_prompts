I've been running a grad seminar on philosophy of science and got kind of stuck defending Friedman's F-twist—specifcally the claim that unrealistic assumptions in economic models aren't just tolerable but actually do positive epistemic work, since stripping away psychological complexity lets us see the structural logic of markets more clearly. Lately I've been pushing it even further with my students: maybe a model is actually better the more wildly false its core assumptions are, provided it predicits well, because extreme falsehood functions like a control condition that isolates the mechanism we're actually tracking. I'm not sure this stronger version holds water, though.

What would count against it? I want the arguments and evidence that should move me—sorted from fatal to merely awkward—including whether there's a principled line between 'usefully false' and 'just wrong,' whether predictive success can really redeem any assumption no matter how absurd, and whether this view has been quietly qualified into unfalsifiability (if every predictive failure gets blamed on auxilliary hypotheses rather than the unrealistic core). Also, which defeaters would defenders of the F-twist themselves concede as damaging, and which would they dismiss as missing the point? I'd especally like to know if there's a result in the idealization literature or in actual modeling practice showing that unrealistic assumptions sometimes actively mislead researchers even when predictions hold—basically, whether falsehood can be harmless in the way Friedman claims or whether it always risks distorting the explanatory story.
