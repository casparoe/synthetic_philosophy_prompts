I'm trying to diagnose an argument from a decision-theory seminar that seems to show Aumann's agreement theorem makes rational interdisciplinary disagreement impossible. The reconstructed steps are:

(1) Rational agents with a common prior and common knowledge of each other's posteriors must assign identical probabilities to any event.

(2) In policy-relevant forecasting panels—e.g., climate economists and epidemiologists weighing carbon-tax adjustments against pandemic preparedness—participants are rational, share access to the same evidence base, and disclose their assessments.

(3) If their disagreement concerns substantive predictions rather than raw data, they necessarily differ in their structural or auxiliary models, which means they do not share a common prior over the relevant state space.

(4) Conversely, if they do share a prior over that space, then disclosure should drive convergence by (1).

(5) Hence persistent, apparently rational disagreement entails either that common priors are absent across disciplinary boundaries, or that at least one party is irrational.

(6) If common priors are systematically absent, then Bayesian rationality cannot adjudicate collective expertise: every dispute reduces to an incommensurable prior clash beyond normative evaluation.

(7) If we instead preserve rationality, we must classify virtually all sustained expert dissent as hidden cognitive failure.

(8) Both conclusions are implausible.

(9) So the theorem, applied to real-world expertise, either collapses collective rationality or delegitimizes disagreement.

The conclusion feels absurd, yet each step seems defensible. Which premise or inference should be rejected? I am inclined to protect (1) and am wary of jettisoning (2) entirely, but I wonder whether the move from (3) to (4) equivocates between priors over data and priors over model-structured hypotheses, or whether (6) misstates what Bayesian norms require. Where does the overreach occur, and what does abandoning that step cost for modeling disagreement in science-policy contexts?
