After my mother received a diagnosis of a rapidly progressive neurodegenerative disorder last autumn and our family was guided through a standardized advance-directive protocol at her care center, I became preoccupied not with the choice of death itself but with the architecture of the decision: the way institutional safeguards distribute authority across clinicians, family surrogates, and ethics consultants so that no individual appears to authorize the lethal outcome, even as the collective procedure moves inexorably toward it. I want to translate this observation into an experiential learning moment.

I am facilitating a 90-minute graduate bioethics seminar with twelve participants—mostly clinical-ethics trainees with some background in moral philosophy—on the structural ethics of assisted dying. Design a runnable classroom simulation, with explicit rules, assigned roles, and either real or symbolic stakes, that makes participants feel the force of what I call delegated mortality: the fragmentation of moral agency across procedural roles such that each actor maintains individual moral cleanliness precisely because the lethal authorization is distributed, while the patient’s relational autonomy is translated into a sequence of bureaucratic checkpoints.

The activity should be arranged so that participants run into the coordination failure or moral distancing themselves—through hidden incentives, sequential commitment rules, or role-based information asymmetries—before any debrief names the phenomenon. Specify the division into roles (e.g., treating physician, family surrogate, ethics consultant, institutional reviewer), the time structure within the 90 minutes, and what participants should come away seeing about the gap between procedural fairness and moral complicity.

Then provide a debrief plan that maps specific moments in the simulation onto the components of delegated mortality: which rule produced which fragmentation of responsibility, what will predictably go wrong when the exercise is run (for example, participants interpreting procedural neutrality as personal innocence), and how the facilitator should correct or channel these reactions. Be explicit about which feature of real-world assisted dying—the irreversibility of the final act, the temporal asymmetry between revocable delay and irrevocable consent, or the absence of the dying patient from the committee room—the simulation cannot reproduce and that the facilitator must therefore supply verbally.
