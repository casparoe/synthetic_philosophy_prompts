I'm preparing a position paper on institutional reform and want an internal consistency audit before I circulate it. My view combines Burkean conservatism with Chesterton's fence, but I've added two qualifications that may not sit well with the core commitments. Specifically, I hold:

(a) That Burke's partnership across generations gives existing institutions a presumptive authority that abstract rationalist plans lack, and that organic, incremental change is preferable to comprehensive redesign.

(b) That Chesterton's fence should be read as an epistemic filter rather than an absolute bar: one should not dismantle a practice until one understands the function it serves, but this applies with full force only to practices that have survived competitive selection among alternatives, not to those imposed by a single historical actor.

(c) That some traditional practices—specifically those maintained by coercion to exclude a defined group—served no legitimate social function and may be removed without the full historical reconstruction Chesterton demands, because in these cases the "fence" was never part of an organic institutional ecology.

(d) That the burden of proof in any proposed reform should lie with the reformer, who must show not only that the current arrangement is flawed but that the proposed alternative is likely to perform at least as well across the dimensions the old arrangement addressed.

(e) That when a practice causes demonstrable, ongoing harm and its original purpose is genuinely obscure—not merely unknown to the reformer—maintaining it is unjustified, even if removal carries risks of unintended consequence.

My question is whether this package is jointly tenable. Which pairs are in genuine tension, which only appear to conflict because of an ambiguous reading of (b) or (d), and what is the least costly revision—giving up (c), sharpening (b), or weakening (e)—if consistency demands it? Rank the tensions by severity, and explain what each revision costs in terms of the underlying Burkean or Chestertonian rationale.

Please also state clearly what argument or evidence would change your assessment of whether these commitments hang together, and identify the specific point in your reasoning that is most vulnerable to error—whether it lies in interpreting Chesterton's fence as a filter rather than a prohibition, in distinguishing organically selected practices from coercively imposed ones, or elsewhere.
