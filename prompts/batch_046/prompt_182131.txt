I am preparing notes for an essay—intended for a general literary audience—on whether the cognitive science of analogy can illuminate how fiction builds character without reducing the art to psychology. I take Hofstadter and Sander's central thesis in Surfaces and Essences, that analogy rather than logic or computation is the fundamental engine of human cognition, as my point of departure. From there I propose what I call "recursive analogical shadowing": the idea that in certain modernist narratives, a character is not understood through a single stable comparison to an archetype, but through a layered sequence in which each analogical mapping is itself mapped onto the failure or distortion of an earlier one, so that understanding the figure requires tracking not the trait but the history of its analogical revisions. I sketch this with reference to narrative structures in which self-comparison supersedes external reference, though I deliberately do not name particular texts so the discussion remains conceptual rather than bibliographic. I ask for your comments on three points: whether this notion genuinely extends their framework or merely applies standard literary analysis with borrowed vocabulary; whether the underlying claim that analogy is the exclusive core of cognition is presented fairly, and where specialists genuinely disagree about that exclusivity versus a more pluralist account; and whether any part of my sketch misrepresents their position in ways that would mislead a reader. In your response, please separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who know the topic, and do not present a contested view as the consensus. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence and then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
