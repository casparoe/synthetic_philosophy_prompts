I'm writing a paper on moral responsibility and affect. At a workshop on embodied cognition, a speaker dismissed attributing anger to pre-linguistic infants with the line: "Anger isn't a feeling; it's a judgment that you've been wronged — and without the concept of wronging, there's nothing to feel." I need to unpack that for our next session. Please lay out the distinct precise theses that slogan could be advancing: for example, that anger requires a propositional normative belief; that it requires a non-propositional appraisal; that bodily feeling is causally downstream of judgment; that the judgment is conceptually prior but not conscious; and serious alternatives such as an interoceptive-inference or enactive-appraisal version in which "judgment" is not representational. For each reading, say whether it is trivial, substantive but defensible, or false, and indicate which ones actually entail that infants and animals cannot be angry. Present this as a disambiguated menu I can test against my sources, not as a single interpretation, and do not provide a bibliography or citation list.
