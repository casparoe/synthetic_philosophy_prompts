Read Ursula K. Le Guin's short story "The Ones Who Walk Away from Omelas" through the lens of Burkean conservatism and Chesterton's fence. The narrative presents a prosperous city whose happiness depends on the hidden, continuous suffering of one child; the citizens mostly accept this arrangement, while a few reject it and leave. I want a reading that treats neither the stayers nor the walkers as mere illustrations of philosophical positions, but takes both the story and the conservative framework seriously. Specifically: does Chesterton's fence illuminate why the citizens remain—on the grounds that tearing down the social order, however cruel its hidden foundation, would destroy goods they do not fully understand— or does the story expose that reasoning as a rationalization for complicity? Does Burke's suspicion of rationalist reform apply to the walkers, who abandon an inherited social contract in the name of an abstract moral principle? At the same time, identify where the work resists the conservative reading: is the imprisoned child a "fence" whose original protective purpose has been forgotten, or is it precisely the kind of hidden cruelty that tradition papers over and that reform ought to expose? Be faithful in both directions—do not reduce Le Guin to an allegory for conservatism, and do not distort Burke or Chesterton to make them fit the plot. Conclude by saying what the story adds to the philosophical debate about reform, tradition, and unintended consequences that argument alone does not capture.
