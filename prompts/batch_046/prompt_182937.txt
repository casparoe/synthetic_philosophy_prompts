I am preparing a conceptual instrument for a workshop on recursive deliberation architectures. Rather than treating HCH-style factored cognition—where a complex judgment is decomposed into sub-questions answered by specialized sub-processes or agents, with answers aggregated into a final output—solely as a scalable-oversight technique in AI alignment, I want to examine it as a normative idealization of collective or institutional reasoning: the claim that good deliberation ought to mirror this decomposed, consultative structure even when no single participant can survey the entire chain.

Please design a branching taxonomy (or decision tree) that classifies positions on whether this idealization succeeds. Begin with a root question that separates those who reject decomposed deliberation in principle from those who accept it in some form. From there, construct at least four successive diagnostic nodes—each phrased as a concrete claim or case judgment that a respondent could answer without knowing which philosophical label it maps onto. The nodes must distinguish:

1. Whether non-decomposable, holistic judgments—for instance, the overall coherence of a policy framework or the gestalt assessment of a moral dilemma—represent an irreducible epistemic resource that factoring destroys, or whether they are merely heuristic shortcuts that ideal deliberation should eliminate.
2. Whether strategic distortion, specifically epistemic leakage in which a sub-process frames its answer to anticipate and manipulate the aggregation rule, can be contained by institutional design at the factor boundaries, or whether such leakage necessarily corrupts the root judgment.
3. Whether the model presupposes an unrealistic competence gap: that aggregators possess meta-cognitive capacities, such as judging reliability, weighting conflicts, or recognizing framing effects, that are not themselves subject to the same scarcity and decomposition problems as the sub-processes they oversee.
4. Whether correctness is itself factorizable—that is, whether a correct overall answer can be reconstructed from correct answers to properly isolated sub-questions, or whether some correct judgments depend on cross-domain interactions that the factoring procedure necessarily suppresses.

Each leaf should carry a position name, or be marked unnamed if the combination is not defended in the literature, plus one sentence stating its most awkward commitment—the consequence its defenders rarely highlight. The taxonomy should also identify one pair of leaves that the tree fails to separate cleanly, explaining why the diagnostic questions do not pull them apart, and should flag one unoccupied region of the logical space: specify the combination of answers that lands there, and diagnose whether its emptiness is due to incoherence, unattractiveness, or simple neglect.

Keep the instrument oriented toward reasoning about institutional and cognitive design rather than toward AI safety per se, and avoid asking for bibliographies, canonical citations, or reading lists.
