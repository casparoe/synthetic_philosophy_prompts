I've been reflecting on a stronger variant of the hedonistic imperative — the proposal that a biotechnological programme should not merely suppress suffering or elevate baseline mood, but restructure human affective architecture so that negative valence becomes conceptually impossible rather than experientially absent. In this framing, germline engineering wouldn't just adjust a hedonic thermostat; it would eliminate the phenomenological category of suffering in much the same way that congenital colour blindness removes certain wavelengths from experience altogether. I find this more radical formulation compelling, particularly given recent discussions about the tractability of genetic intervention in emotional valence, but I want to stress-test it with the kind of crux-mapping one might apply to any high-stakes normative thesis.

What would genuinely count against this position? I'm interested in decisive defeaters rather than the familiar gestures about authenticity or the instrumental value of adversity: for instance, empirical results in affective neuroscience that would render such restructuring incoherent; conceptual arguments demonstrating that negative valence is structurally necessary for learning, agency, or moral cognition; or ethical considerations showing that dissolving the category of suffering collapses into a state worse than our present condition. Which objections are live rather than merely logically possible? Has the stronger formulation been qualified into unfalsifiability — and if so, does that undermine its normative force, or is insulation appropriate here?

Please provide your answer in American English, and rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward. I'm particularly keen on whether current lines of work in predictive processing or allostatic regulation bear directly on the feasibility claim, and whether the position risks anything substantial enough to be genuinely exposed.
