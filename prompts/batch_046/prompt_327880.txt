I'm running a 90-minute upper-level undergraduate seminar on happiness in early modern philosophy with about twelve students, and I want an activity that makes them feel, rather than merely learn about, the severity of Émilie du Châtelet's position. I am particularly interested in her argument that happiness is not an affective state or a balance of pleasures but the sustained exercise of rational faculties in conditions of radical self-sufficiency — an account, developed across her writings on the subject, in which dependency on external goods, on bodily comfort, and especially on social recognition are not just absent from happiness but actively corrupt it. The activity should put students in a situation where they must choose between immediately gratifying options and intellectually demanding ones, then experience how her framework judges those choices, so that they discover for themselves that on her view ordinary consolations like peer approval or relief from fatigue are actually obstacles rather than supports.

Please design a runnable simulation or structured role-play with clear phases: an initial choice round with tangible (if small) stakes or assigned roles; a middle phase in which participants must operate under the constraints of her framework — for example, being required to justify any satisfaction they report solely by reference to internal mental activity and its continuity, with penalties or exclusion mechanisms for appeals to comfort or reputation; and a debrief that maps specific moments in the activity onto parts of her argument without naming the view beforehand. Include rules or payoff schemes that don't require real money, specify the timeline across the 90 minutes, and be explicit about what will predictably go wrong the first time — for instance, students conflating her demanding conception with simple asceticism, or resisting the exclusion of social approval as implausible rather than examining why she demands it. Finally, be frank about which feature of her account — perhaps its implicit elitism about who possesses the requisite rational capacities, or its difficulty accommodating embodied or emotional well-being — the activity structurally cannot reproduce and that you, as facilitator, must supply in discussion.
