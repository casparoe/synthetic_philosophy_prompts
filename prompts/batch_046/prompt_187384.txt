As a retired physicist who has only recently turned to philosophy of science and political thought, I keep encountering John Dewey's compressed claim that democratic politics and experimental inquiry are not merely analogous but share an identical logical form. In a recent seminar, a speaker rendered this as the slogan that democracy is merely inquiry writ large, and used it to argue that democratic legitimacy ought to rest on institutionalized peer review and revisable policy hypotheses rather than on majoritarian voting as such. I am unsure whether the one-liner can actually carry that weight, and I suspect I am missing distinctions that are philosophically live. Could you lay out the different precise theses the slogan might express—distinguishing, for instance, a structural isomorphism between deliberative procedures and scientific method, a normative fallibilism about collective decisions, an ontological claim that social truths are constituted by the processes that investigate them, and a methodological prescription that policies be treated as falsifiable hypotheses? For each reading, please indicate whether it is trivially true, substantively defensible, or plainly false, and specifically whether it actually supports substituting peer-review mechanisms for voting. I am not asking for historical exegesis of Dewey's texts, but for a conceptual menu: which precisifications are coherent, which collapse under scrutiny, and which ones genuinely license the institutional conclusion the speaker wanted.
