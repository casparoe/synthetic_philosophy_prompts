I've been taking an informal course on Indian epistemology through some old forum threads, and I keep returning to Jain anekāntavāda—the doctrine that reality possesses an infinite number of aspects and that no single viewpoint can exhaust it. At first glance this resembles the rationalist habit of maintaining several competing models simultaneously, or perhaps the idea of perspectival pluralism in contemporary philosophy of science. But I think anekāntavāda makes a stronger claim: not merely that our knowledge is partial, but that objects themselves genuinely possess apparently contradictory properties that are resolved only by specifying the aspect under consideration. The Jains formalise this through syādvāda, conditional predication, which qualifies every assertion with the phrase 'in some respect'.

This leads me to a comparison I'm struggling to articulate clearly. How does anekāntavāda differ from dialetheism, the view—associated with some modern paraconsistent logicians—that certain contradictions are literally true? Does anekāntavāda allow that a single object is both hot and cold in exactly the same respect, or does it avoid true contradiction by distributing properties across different facets? Furthermore, if syādvāda is interpreted as a logical framework rather than merely a rhetorical caution, can it be formalised in a way that respects the law of non-contradiction, or does it require a genuinely non-classical logic? I'd appreciate a careful comparison that clarifies whether anekāntavāda is best understood as a metaphysical thesis about the structure of reality, an epistemic thesis about human limitation, or something in between. Please provide your response in British English.
