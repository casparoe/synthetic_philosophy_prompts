I'm in a historiography seminar and we got into Braudel's longue durée — you know, that framework where geography and climate are these deep, almost immobile structures, and events and individuals are just foam on the waves. One classmate kept pushing the analogy that it's exactly like geology: bedrock is the physical environment, sedimentary layers are economies and social structures, and topsoil is politics and battles. He used it to argue that writing about, say, Philip II's wars or some random diplomat is basically cataloging weather patterns on a mountain range — interesting, but not where the real causality lives.

But I'm not sure the mapping holds up that cleanly. Can you lay out the correspondance table — what's playing bedrock, what's sediment, what's surface foam — and then tell me where it breaks? For instance, Braudel talks about mountains as refuges and plains as zones of state control, yet he also notes that irrigation and deforestation slowly reshape the 'immobile' geography over centuries. In real geology the bedrock doesn't get reworked by topsoil, but here the 'surface' digs back into the base. Then there's climate shifts like the Little Ice Age altering the 'deep' layer, which in rock terms is more like an earthquake than part of the slow structure. So does the analogy's conclusion — that historians should mostly ignore events and individuals — survive once you see the layers interact and the 'deep' stuff isn't actually static? Or was the geology picture smuggling in a determinism that Braudel's own examples undermine? And if the analogy collapses, is there a better one — maybe ecology or software architecture — that keeps the scale point without pretending the base is inert?
