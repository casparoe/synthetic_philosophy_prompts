In a recent graduate seminar on genocide and modernity, a colleague offered the following gloss on Zygmunt Bauman’s Modernity and the Holocaust and its place in the Historikerstreit. She argued that Bauman treats the Holocaust not as a singular moral catastrophe but as the accidental overflow of Weberian rational-legal bureaucracy—essentially a technical failure of modern organizational logic in which ethical restraints were bypassed rather than actively dismantled. From this, she inferred that Bauman’s framework collapses the distinction between the Holocaust and other modern bureaucratic atrocities, placing Auschwitz and the Gulag on the same structural plane as comparable expressions of instrumental reason run amok. She further claimed that this reduction of the Holocaust to a generic modern pathology is precisely why Jürgen Habermas attacked Bauman during the Historikerstreit, accusing him of relativizing German crimes and undermining the uniqueness thesis required for adequate historical remembrance.

I am skeptical of several moves in this characterization, but I want to check my instincts before writing back. Is Bauman’s account of bureaucracy and moral distancing best read as describing an accidental system malfunction, or does he argue for a more specific convergence of modern conditions—division of labor, functional separation, and the suppression of moral proximity—that produces a distinctive form of violence? Does his sociology genuinely equate Nazi genocide with Stalinist terror in the manner implied, or does he preserve analytical specificity that the uniqueness and comparability debate requires? And was Habermas’s well-known intervention in the Historikerstreit actually directed at Bauman’s sociological thesis, or was the primary target a different set of historians advancing explicit comparability arguments?

Please assess the summary point by point: identify which parts are defensible glosses of Bauman’s position, which conflate his structural analysis with accidentalist explanations he rejects, and which misattribute the lines of debate in the Historikerstreit. In particular, tell me whether the conclusion—that Bauman ultimately supports the comparability position—actually follows from his claims about modern rationality and moral distance, or whether it rests on the mischaracterizations you identify.
