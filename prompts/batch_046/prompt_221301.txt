Design a 90-minute in-class simulation for a graduate seminar of about 12 students on the theme of archives and power—specifically, who gets recorded and what silences are structurally produced in the sources—without giving a preliminary lecture. The activity should make participants feel, through their own actions, that archival authority operates less through explicit censorship and more through differential capacity to produce durable, verifiable traces. To inject a conceptual tension that is not standard in textbook treatments, split the group into two role-types: “provenance nodes,” who may only record an event if they can generate a deterministic, timestamped token (analogous to a cryptographic hash or immutable ledger entry), and “oral-history nodes,” whose testimony is epistemically valid but cannot be tokenized and is therefore excluded from the official reconstruction mechanism. Give the group a shared scenario to experience in real time—for example, a 15-minute simulated community meeting in which different nodes witness different aspects—then ask them to collectively produce an “official account” using only the tokenized records, followed by a vote on what “really happened.” The rules should be arranged so that the oral-history nodes discover that their contributions are systematically absent from the reconstructed narrative before anyone names the phenomenon. Specify the physical materials needed (e.g., cards, a whiteboard for the token ledger, colored badges for roles), a step-by-step timeline with clear rules and a non-monetary payoff scheme (e.g., collective grade points or narrative authority contingent on whose records prevail), and a debrief that maps specific moments onto the idea: the archive as a system of enunciation rather than a neutral container, structural silence produced by format conditions rather than by deletion, and the retroactive construction of the past through present selection criteria. In the debrief, also predict what will go wrong on the first run—likely, provenance nodes will over-record trivial events to maximize tokens, and the group will conflate absence of record with absence of occurrence—and explain how to handle it. Be explicit about which feature of archival power the simulation cannot reproduce (for instance, the long-duration institutional inertia by which certain voices never reach the archive at all, rather than being present but un-tokenized) and state how the facilitator must supply that gap in discussion. Write in a formal, polished register; do not request bibliographies, citations, or reading lists.
