I am drafting an essay for my sixth-form philosophy course on whether conscious experience of motion and colour change is reconstructed rather than recorded in real time. To frame the argument, I compare two modes of historical revision—Orwellian revision, in which archives are continuously rewritten so that the present always appears consistent with the past, and Stalinesque revision, in which individuals or events are excised entirely so that no contradictory record remains—with two competing interpretations of the colour phi phenomenon, where observers report that a dot changing colour as it moves appears to change colour before it reaches the point at which the physical change occurs. I propose that an Orwellian model of consciousness treats the brain as retroactively editing the narrative of an earlier perceptual moment once new sensory information arrives, thereby preserving temporal continuity but altering specific details, whereas a Stalinesque model treats the intermediate perceptual states corresponding to the dot's earlier positions as never having been registered at all, leaving no false record but only an absence in the archive of experience. I then ask whether either model can account for the subjective timing of the colour change without implying a literal backward causal influence, and whether this distinction maps coherently onto the broader debate between reconstructive and predictive accounts of perceptual consciousness. I should be grateful for comments on the philosophical coherence of this analogy, on whether I have mischaracterised either the empirical literature on colour phi or the historical revisionism comparison, and on whether there are obvious objections—particularly about whether mental representation can genuinely be erased in a Stalinesque fashion, or whether that comparison collapses once we distinguish neural processing from political documentation.
