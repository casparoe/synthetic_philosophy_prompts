I have been trying to understand Lewis Carroll's little dialogue about the tortoise and Achilles — the one in which the tortoise keeps insisting that Achilles justify every single step of deduction with yet another premise, so that even when Achilles grants all the premises, he can never quite force the tortoise to accept the conclusion. From what I gather, philosophers treat this as a puzzle about whether rules of inference can stand without being turned into premises themselves, but I am not sure I grasp the standard answers. At the time, many mathematicians objected that the proof was not genuinely verifiable, since no human could check every computational step by hand — it seemed to rely on an unexamined authority.

Could you analyse this controversy through the lens of Carroll's tortoise? In particular: is the mathematicians' objection best understood as the tortoise demanding an infinite regress of justification for each computational step, or is it a different kind of worry altogether? Does the computer function like Achilles quietly accepting a rule of inference, or like the tortoise refusing to accept one? And does the gradual acceptance of computer-assisted proofs — including later verifications with formal proof-checking software — show that the tortoise's challenge was genuinely answered, or merely that the mathematical community decided to stop asking? I am not asking for a biography of Carroll or a technical account of graph colouring; I want to know what the 1976 episode reveals about whether deductive justification can ever be fully grounded, and whether Carroll's paradox was ever meant to apply to cases like this.
