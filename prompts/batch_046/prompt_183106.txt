I'm a high school student writing a paper for an advanced philosophy seminar, and I've been tracking a pattern in category disputes that I can't place precisely. When people argue over whether competitive video gaming counts as a sport, one defender cites physical exertion; faced with chess, they switch to strategic depth; faced with checkers, they pivot to institutional rules and leagues; and when someone mentions an unregulated neighborhood basketball game that everyone still calls a sport, they retreat to competitive intent or spectator culture. The same rotation appears in debates over whether digital photography is art (craft versus vision versus institutional recognition), whether a large language model is thinking (behavioral fluency versus causal history versus intentionality), and whether a particular communal ritual is a religion (doctrine versus practice versus social function). In each case, the defenders seem to hold the category together not by one necessary feature but by a web of overlapping similarities, yet they still argue as if there must be a sharp, correct boundary rather than admitting that membership is distributed across a network of traits. Is there an established concept in philosophy of language or conceptual analysis that captures exactly this dynamic — the argumentative rotation through non-universal, overlapping criteria in defense of a category with no essential core — and is it recognized as something different from ordinary vagueness, where there is usually one dimension with fuzzy edges? If no precise term exists for that rotational defense, what would distinguish it structurally from simple equivocation, and where exactly does the phenomenon break down — at what point does rotating criteria become an illegitimate rescue rather than a legitimate description of how the concept actually functions?
