I am a doctoral student in philosophy of mind. Please draft the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between two camps on whether dreams are experiences: (A) the Malcolm-inspired denialist position, which holds that dreams fail to be genuine experiences because there is no waking criterion for a subject present to itself in the absence of perceptual and bodily anchoring; and (B) the Dennett-inspired constructionist position, which holds that dreams involve real phenomenal content but that the narrative coherence, temporal ordering, and self-attribution found in reports are largely assembled upon awakening, with real-time oneiric phenomenology being thinner or of a different kind than waking experience.

Structure the document as follows. First, a numbered section of claims both sides can sign, covering: the neurophysiology of REM and lucid REM sleep; the logical conditions for ascribing experience to a subject; the reliability and structural features of first-person dream reports; and the occurrence of volitional behavior (e.g., eye-movement signaling) during lucid REM. Formulate each claim precisely so that agreement is substantive, not vacuous.

Second, a disagreements table stating the residual disputes in wording each side accepts for its own view. Separate at least: (i) whether the absence of an egocentric spatial and proprioceptive framework in ordinary non-lucid dreams undermines experience-status; (ii) whether phenomenal properties can be attributed backward from an awakening-constructed narrative to the sleeping state itself; and (iii) whether volitional signaling during lucid REM demonstrates real-time experiential access or only behavioral correlation without phenomenal presence.

Third, identify the cruxes—the sub-questions such that resolving them would move at least one side. I take the central cruxes to be whether pre-reflective self-awareness requires intact proprioceptive feedback loops, and whether the temporal structure of dream reports supports real-time encoding or post-hoc fabrication. For each crux, specify a concrete case or piece of evidence both sides agree bears on it—for example, lucid dreamers who report maintaining volitional control without full bodily awareness, or neuroimaging correlations between prefrontal reactivation and subsequent report accuracy—and record in advance what each side predicts about that case.

Fourth, briefly assess whether any portion of the dispute is verbal, for instance whether the two sides operate with different extensional conditions for "experience," and if so specify exactly which portion and why.

Please separate what is settled among philosophers and cognitive scientists who work on this topic from what is genuinely contested, and do not present any contested interpretive claim—including contested readings of the denialist criterion or of the constructionist model—as if it were the consensus view. Write formally, as a document both parties could co-sign, with the exact residual disagreement made precise.
