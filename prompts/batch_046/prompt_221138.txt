As a retired biologist who has only recently begun studying philosophy of mind in earnest, I have been tracking an instructor's rulings on whether various hypothetical practices fall afoul of Wittgenstein's argument against private languages. Her verdicts on the cases discussed so far are these: first, a solitary explorer who coins a single symbol for an internal headache and records it in an unread journal is not using a private language but rather failing to use language at all, since there is no public criterion of correct application; second, a mathematician who invents a personal shorthand for mental proofs and then teaches it to a student is not private either, because the notation can be corrected by others once shared; third, a person in total sensory isolation who attempts to name successive moods with invented vocalizations lacks any external calibration point, and she judges this to be precisely the kind of private language that collapses into meaninglessness; fourth, a programmer who assigns idiosyncratic semantic meanings to variables in otherwise standard code is not engaging in private language, because the syntax and compiler provide a shared structural framework within which errors can be caught; fifth, a blind individual who presses tactile patterns onto her own forearm to record emotional states—where the pressure sequences are physically detectable by others even if never explained—is also judged not to constitute a private language, on the grounds that the physical traces supply an in-principle public check independent of her private memory.

The new case is this. A synthetic microbiologist engineers a bacterial strain to secrete a unique, non-toxic fluorescent peptide specifically whenever she experiences a particular qualitative mental state—say, the precise phenomenal character of a specific autobiographical memory. She logs the temporal fluorescence sequence in a private laboratory notebook and uses it as her personal notation system. The peptide and its emission pattern are fully detectable by standard instruments, and the correlation with her mental state is stable over time; yet only she can induce the mental state, and other observers, though they can record the fluorescence precisely, have no access to the internal referent and are never given the mapping.

I am less interested in a bare yes-or-no answer than in the underlying principle she is running. Please lay out at least two plausible rules that accommodate all five earlier verdicts equally well—for instance, the principle that language requires an actual community of users who can correct one another; the principle that it requires only an in-principle external criterion of correctness that does not depend on the speaker's private memory; and the principle that the referent itself must be in principle accessible to other minds regardless of physical traces. For each rule, tell me what it predicts about the microbiologist's fluorescence notation, identify the one earlier case that best discriminates that rule from its rivals, and explain why. Then give your best-guess prediction of her actual verdict, with your reasoning, and propose one further hypothetical case—ideally drawn from a natural-scientific practice rather than a standard philosophical example—that would conclusively settle which principle she is applying.
