You serve as the methodological advisor to a municipal heritage-impact board in a mid-sized post-industrial city. The board receives frequent proposals—renaming streets, erecting monuments, designing school curriculum modules, funding archive digitization—to commemorate local history. The board has adopted, as its governing framework, Nietzsche’s analysis of history’s service to life: history can operate monumentally, as exemplary inspiration for present action; antiquarianly, as preservative identification with a continuous past; or critically, as liberating dissolution of a burdensome past. Any mode, however, becomes destructive when it dominates the others or when the past is made to rule over present life rather than serve it.

Your task is to translate this framework into a reusable decision procedure that a staff member with no philosophical training can apply in approximately thirty minutes to any proposal. The deliverable should be an ordered sequence of diagnostic questions, with specified conditions that yield one of four outcomes—approve, approve with conditions, reject, or escalate to board judgment—at each stage. The procedure must explicitly distinguish the three modes, identify when a proposal activates more than one in conflict, and flag the precise point where a value judgment about the city’s present needs—rather than a factual determination about the proposal—must replace the checklist.

In particular, the procedure must be designed to handle these hard cases: (i) a proposal that is monumentally inspiring but relies on significant historical simplification or falsification; (ii) a proposal that is critically liberating—designed to dissolve a harmful narrative—but risks destabilizing community identity to a degree that inhibits rather than frees present action; (iii) an antiquarian preservation project that accurately documents a past but does so in a way that makes present inhabitants feel they live in the shadow of an unrecoverable grandeur.

Apply your procedure to three proposals: (a) a monumental statue and accompanying school module celebrating a local labor uprising, using a narrative that elides the uprising’s internal ethnic divisions; (b) a critical museum exhibit that systematically deconstructs the founding myth of the city’s industrial prosperity, with no accompanying narrative of present capacity; (c) an antiquarian digital archive of every building demolished during urban renewal, meticulously documented but presented without curatorial framing.

For each application, show the procedure’s path through the questions, note where it reaches a judgment that the board will likely find unwelcome, and state clearly what the procedure cannot determine—where it must hand the decision back to human judgment about whether present life in this particular city needs inspiration, continuity, or liberation more urgently.

Additionally, include a brief misfire warning: identify one type of proposal that the procedure will consistently misclassify, explain why the error is structurally unavoidable given the simplification required for practical use, and say what safeguard—if any—can be added without destroying the procedure’s usability for non-specialist staff.
