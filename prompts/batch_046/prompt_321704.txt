I am not a philosopher or psychologist, but I have been trying to work out for myself whether dreaming counts as genuinely experiencing something, or whether it is more like a false memory that gets assembled only after we wake up. I want to see if my judgments about particular cases line up behind one idea, and I want to know where that idea breaks.

Here is how I react to different situations.

First, an ordinary vivid dream: I am flying over a city, I feel the wind, and when I wake up I remember it clearly. My instinct is that I really did experience flight, except my body was asleep. But then I notice that if someone had shaken me awake in the middle and asked what I was doing, I could not have answered. That makes me wonder whether the experience only comes into existence when I wake up and report it.

Second, a lucid dream in which I realize I am dreaming and deliberately choose to fly higher. Here there is a sense of agency and presence that feels unmistakably like real experiencing. Even though I am asleep, it seems like something is actually happening to me, not just being invented later.

Third, sleep paralysis with a visual hallucination: I am awake, unable to move, and I see a dark figure at the foot of my bed. This feels like a real experience of seeing something, even though nothing is there. If my brain can generate a genuine experience while I am partly awake, why should it be unable to do so while I am fully asleep?

Fourth, people who say they never dream, or that sleep is just a blank nothing. I think they are either wrong about what happened—there was an inner life they cannot access—or they genuinely had no subjective content. But I do not think the absence of memory proves the absence of experience.

Fifth, a false awakening: I dream that I have gotten out of bed, made coffee, and started my day, and only later do I really wake up. During the false awakening, I seemed to be experiencing my ordinary morning routine. If that was not an experience, then what was it? Yet it was also completely false.

Sixth, I have heard about people with aphantasia who report dreaming without any visual imagery—only thoughts, feelings, or abstract understanding. They still insist they had a dream. This suggests to me that dreaming does not require pictures, but it does require some kind of subjective happening.

Seventh, and this is the one I keep going back and forth on: a sleeping person whose brain shows patterns identical to those of waking perception, but who upon waking reports absolutely no memory and denies that anything happened. Was there an experience that left no trace? Or was there only brain activity with no subjective side?

Putting these together, my verdicts are roughly these: ordinary dreams feel like experiences but seem to depend on later reporting; lucid dreams feel like clear experiences with real agency; sleep-paralysis hallucinations are genuine experiences despite being false; people who deny dreaming are probably lacking access rather than lacking experience; false awakenings involve real experiences of unreal situations; and non-visual dreams count as experiences as long as there is subjective content.

Is there a single coherent principle—perhaps about presence, agency, or retrospective access—that captures all of these judgments? Or do they force me to say that dreaming is sometimes an experience and sometimes a confabulated story? If there is a principle, what does it predict for the brain-scan case above? And please be direct about which of my verdicts I would have to give up to keep the principle simple.
