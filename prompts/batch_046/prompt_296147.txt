Write a short story that dramatizes George Berkeley's economic thesis, developed in his 1730s series The Querist, that money is not itself wealth but rather a token or ticket that directs labor and facilitates exchange, deriving its efficacy from convention and institutional trust rather than from any intrinsic value in the metal or paper that bears it. To keep the treatment from collapsing into a generic parable about currency, embed the following niche scenario: the narrative unfolds in a small, self-contained maritime republic that has recently abolished metal coinage and replaced it with handwritten "labor-tickets" denominated in hours of work, but where a sudden scarcity of ink and parchment threatens to halt all commerce—not because productive capacity has diminished, but because the physical substrate of the token system has become unexpectedly rare.

Center the plot on the interaction between a skeptical accountant, who insists that the republic's wealth resides in the accumulated tickets locked in the treasury vaults, and a shipbuilder who realizes that feeding her crew depends not on holding more slips of paper but on the actual circulation of grain, timber, and repaired sails through the harbor. Through their disagreement, the story should explore the tension between money as a symbolic directing mechanism—what Berkeley might call a "ticket for conveying power"—and money as a falsely reified store of value that people mistake for the goods themselves. Keep the narrative compact—approximately 500 to 700 words—with a formal, slightly allegorical tone, and do not include direct citations of Berkeley's texts, quotations from secondary scholarship, or bibliographic references; the story should stand entirely on its own conceptual feet.
