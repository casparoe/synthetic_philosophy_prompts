Consider Machiavelli's judgments across four canonical cases drawn from The Prince and the Discourses. He treats Moses as an armed prophet whose success rested primarily on his own virtù rather than on fortune's gift; Cesare Borgia as possessing extraordinary virtù and seizing the occasion fortune offered, yet ultimately destroyed because fortune turned and he had not built institutions deep enough to survive its reversal; Agathocles of Syracuse as displaying sufficient criminal virtù to conquer but failing to achieve true glory because his methods corrupted the state he founded; and the Roman republic as enduring precisely because its collective institutions distributed fortune's blows across many citizens rather than concentrating risk upon a single prince.

These four verdicts are jointly consistent with more than one governing principle. On one reading, individual virtù is decisive in every moment of action, and Borgia's fall demonstrates only an incomplete execution—he failed to finish the institutional work—rather than any structural ceiling imposed by fortune. On this view, Moses succeeds through total personal capacity, the republic succeeds by aggregating individual capacities across citizens, and Agathocles fails because his personal virtù was directed toward criminal ends. On a competing reading, fortune always sets a hard structural ceiling on any individual agent; Borgia is the norm, not the exception, while Moses succeeds only because armed coercion and exceptional circumstance insulated him, and the republic endures precisely because it eliminates dependence on any single person's virtù.

Now consider a new case: imagine a secular, unarmed founder of exceptional strategic and organizational genius who comes to power during total institutional collapse, designs and implements a constitutional and bureaucratic framework that functions automatically, dies peacefully of natural causes, and leaves behind a state that persists for centuries without requiring successors of comparable ability.

Predict Machiavelli's verdict: would he treat this as the supreme triumph of individual virtù over fortune, or as proof that fortune was never genuinely adverse because the leader had merely transformed personal capacity into collective structure? State the governing rule that best explains all four earlier judgments together with your prediction about this case. Then identify the competing rule that explains the earlier cases equally well but yields the opposite verdict here. Finally, specify one further hypothetical scenario—perhaps involving distributed technological agency or collective failure despite individual brilliance—that would most sharply distinguish between these two interpretations.
