I am preparing a presentation for an interdisciplinary seminar on rational belief under cosmological uncertainty, and I have been working through the literature on anthropic reasoning with a colleague who specializes in formal epistemology. Last week she offered me an informal summary of a position she attributed to a recent, unnamed review essay—she could not recall the author—on the dispute between the self-sampling assumption (SSA) and the self-indication assumption (SIA). Her summary was as follows. She said that SSA requires you to reason as though you are a randomly selected token from the entire set of actually existing observers within your reference class, which automatically assigns lower prior probability to cosmological models that generate smaller populations of observers, since the selection probability is inversely proportional to the total number. SIA, by contrast, instructs you to update your credences in direct proportion to the absolute number of observers a hypothesis predicts, so that a model postulating, say, ten to the fiftieth conscious observer-moments across an eternally inflating multiverse receives a correspondingly massive boost relative to a model predicting only ten to the twenty-third. She then explained that Bostrom's well-known presumptuous-philosopher scenario is intended to demonstrate that SIA is not merely counterintuitive but actually self-defeating: if applied consistently, it allows one to derive near-certainty about the enormous scale of the universe without any empirical observation whatsoever, thereby violating what she called the empiricist calibration constraint—the idea that cosmological beliefs must remain answerable to astrophysical data rather than to pure indexical reasoning. From this, she concluded that Bostrom ultimately rejects SIA and endorses SSA as the only framework that avoids presumptuousness, and she added that the notorious reference-class problem—whether one's reference class should include only human beings, all biological consciousness, or even Boltzmann brains in vacuum fluctuations—is essentially a technical footnote about boundary conditions, not a fundamental challenge to the logic of self-sampling itself. She also mentioned, almost in passing, that in branching cosmologies with eternal inflation, the cosmological measure problem makes it impossible to define a uniform distribution over observer-tokens anyway, so SSA cannot be literally applied without first solving an unsolved problem in cosmological measure theory.

I find the overall architecture of her summary compelling, but I am concerned that several of its moves conflate distinct issues or overstate conclusions that are more tentative in the original literature. In particular, I want to know whether SSA really functions as a mechanism that assigns lower prior probability in the way she suggests, whether the presumptuous-philosopher objection is best understood as showing self-defeat rather than some more specific kind of epistemic impropriety, and whether Bostrom's own stance is being misrepresented. I also wonder whether her dismissal of the reference-class problem as a technical footnote survives scrutiny, and whether the injection of the cosmological measure problem—something she presented as an afterthought—actually undermines her defense of SSA more seriously than she acknowledged. Could you provide an accuracy audit? Please distinguish what is a faithful rendering of the standard presentation, what is a conventional simplification, and what is a subtle misrepresentation, especially with regard to the logical structure of the two assumptions, the specific force of Bostrom's objection, and the status of the reference-class and measure problems. I am not asking for a bibliography or a list of further readings; I want to understand whether I can rely on her summary as a working framework for my presentation.
