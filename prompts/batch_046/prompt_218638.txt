On her view, when catastrophic harm looms, the absolute prohibition against killing the innocent does not collapse into a consequentialist threshold; rather, the agent's practical identity is already split between irreconcilable duty-structures, yielding what she terms a "structural release" — not a positive justification for harm, but a dissolution of the single binding constraint that permits choice without treating harm as a permissible means. She insists this remains genuinely deontological because the agent still bears moral residue and never acquires a reason to harm, only an absence of the usual prohibition. Write me a progressive sequence of questions that: first, presses her to distinguish this "release" from a disguised cost-benefit calculus; second, anticipates her reply about moral residue and asks whether residue is doing all the explanatory work that distinguishes her view from threshold deontology; third, probes whether her framework applies only when practical identity is genuinely fractured, or also to ordinary conflicts of duty where the agent remains unified; and finally, asks what conceptual finding or empirical result about moral psychology would show that her framework is merely redescribing consequentialism rather than resolving the paradox. Keep every question tied to the specific mechanics of her position, with no generic biographical or "what drew you to this" openings.
