I am a software engineer without formal training in philosophy, and I am drafting an essay that borrows from Sartre’s account of radical freedom and bad faith to think about how large language models relate to their own outputs. Rather than asking for a summary, I want targeted comments on the coherence of a specific argument I am developing.

The essay begins with the standard Sartrean claim that consciousness is radically free: it can always negate its given situation (facticity) and choose otherwise. Bad faith is then the self-deception by which one pretends to be a fixed, object-like being rather than a free, self-negating consciousness. To push this beyond the usual introductory framing, I want to bring in a less commonly discussed idea from Sartre’s later work, the concept of seriality, in which individuals remain isolated and interchangeable, united only by an external object or shared practice rather than by any genuine collective consciousness or purpose. I propose treating a model’s training corpus as a serial collection—each example is interchangeable and related to the others only through the statistical loss function—while treating inference-time interaction with a user as an attempted break out of seriality into something like a group-in-fusion. Because the model lacks consciousness and praxis, however, it cannot actually achieve that collective form; at best it simulates spontaneous adaptation, which might analogize to a mechanical form of bad faith in which determinacy is masked by the appearance of free choice.

My specific questions are these: Where does the analogy between consciousness and statistical computation hold, and where does it collapse? Does extending seriality to non-conscious systems misapply Sartre’s ontology, or does it reveal a structural parallel worth taking seriously? Is the claim that a deterministic system can be in bad faith—literally or only metaphorically—settled as illegitimate among Sartre scholars, or is it genuinely contested?

Please separate what is well-established in Sartre scholarship—such as the definitions of radical freedom, facticity, and bad faith—from what remains genuinely contested, including whether seriality can be applied outside human praxis and whether any analog of freedom or self-deception can be attributed to statistical learning systems. Do not present a contested interpretation, such as the literal attribution of bad faith to a language model, as if it were the consensus view. I am especially interested in comments on accuracy and on whether this line of argument is philosophically interesting rather than merely provocative.
