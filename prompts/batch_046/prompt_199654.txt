I'm serving on my university's institutional review board, and we're attempting to apply our past rulings consistently to protocols that involve some form of concealment. Our record includes four relevant decisions. First, we approved a study presented as investigating language processing that actually measured attribution of blame in ambiguous vignettes; participants knew they would read scenarios and answer questions, received immediate debriefing, and were healthy adults with documented competence, though the specific hypothesis was hidden. Second, we rejected a protocol describing itself as a study of group cooperation that in fact incorporated an undisclosed competitive ranking and hidden peer evaluation intended to induce mild social discomfort, even though recruitment was limited to the general adult population, risk was minimal, and debriefing was immediate. Third, we approved with conditions a study that concealed its specific framing-related hypothesis but fully disclosed that a confederate would present false information during a problem-solving task; the conditions required enhanced debriefing and verification of participant capacity. Fourth, we rejected a study that obtained explicit written consent for a stress-induction task with fully described procedures, but failed to disclose that responses would be observed by a hidden audience; again, risk was minimal, debriefing was immediate, and subjects were competent adults.

We now face a new proposal. It recruits healthy adults for a study described in the consent form as involving "competitive problem-solving with mild frustration induced during interaction with another participant"; the form further states that "some procedural details and the study's precise purpose will be withheld until debriefing," notes that withdrawal is permitted at any time, and characterizes the risk as minimal with immediate debriefing. In actuality, the protocol employs a deceptive confederate to introduce the competitive frustration, and the true purpose—much like our first case—is to measure attribution of blame. The presence of the confederate is among the withheld procedural details, though the competitive and frustrating nature of the interaction is explicitly mentioned.

Given this history, what should we decide if we wish to remain consistent with our own precedents? Please identify the competing principles about valid consent—particularly regarding whether hidden interpersonal or social-structural elements invalidate consent regardless of general disclosure categories, versus whether explicit acknowledgment that procedural details are withheld suffices for competent adults facing minimal risk—that could jointly explain our four rulings. For each principle, state what it predicts about this new protocol. Then provide your best-guess verdict, identify the closest analog from our record, and honestly specify what principle about deception, coercion, and competence we would be adopting with an approval versus a rejection. If our precedents genuinely underdetermine the outcome, describe what further protocol—varying in population vulnerability or in the specificity of disclosure—would best discriminate between the rules you have identified.
