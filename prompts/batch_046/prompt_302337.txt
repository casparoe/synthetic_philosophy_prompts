I am a software engineer with no formal philosophy background, and I am preparing a structured interview for a podcast series on conceptual design. Next week's guest is an unnamed philosopher who defends a stakeholder-democracy account of the boundary problem: the view that membership in the demos should be fixed by the causal reach of collective decisions, so that anyone whose interests are affected by a decision belongs to the decision-making body, regardless of citizenship or residence.

I need eight to ten interview questions that go beyond exposition and press the view where democratic theory actually presses it. Each question should be paired with a brief follow-up keyed to the reply the guest is most likely to give. The sequence must cover the following, in any order:

First, a question that frames the boundary problem using a distributed-systems analogy—specifically, treating the demos as a consensus group with a membership protocol, and asking what happens when an external node (for example, a non-citizen impacted by a climate-policy decision) claims state-change authority over the cluster's internal configuration. The follow-up should anticipate the reply that affected parties can be represented by proxy or consultation without full membership, and should push on whether that representation collapses stakeholder democracy back into a territorial or citizenship-based model it was meant to supersede.

Second, a question that pits the stakeholder principle against the rival all-subjected principle, asking how the view avoids over-inclusion without smuggling in a hidden jurisdictional cutoff—such as enforcement territory or legal coercion—that it officially rejects as the boundary criterion. The follow-up should target the likely answer that subjection tracks actual exposure to coercive enforcement, and ask what that does for globally enforced but locally uneven decisions like international trade agreements.

Third, a question about the meta-boundary problem: which demos has the authority to determine the membership rules for the demos? The follow-up should address the likely appeal to iterative democratic revision or reflective equilibrium, and ask whether that appeal presupposes a prior boundary that the principle itself is meant to justify.

Fourth, a question asking what would genuinely count against the stakeholder principle—not merely practical objections about scale or administrative burden, but a result that would show the principle mischaracterizes the normative structure of democratic authority itself. The follow-up should distinguish between an awkward institutional consequence and a fatal conceptual collapse.

Please keep the language precise but accessible to an audience with technical but not philosophical training. Do not ask for bibliographies, named scholars, citation lists, or reading recommendations. Avoid generic biographical or career-focused questions.
