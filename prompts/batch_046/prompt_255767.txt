I serve as a senior program officer at a social-science funding agency that receives interdisciplinary proposals linking macro-level outcomes—regional economic resilience, epidemic diffusion patterns, institutional decay—to micro-level processes such as individual risk assessment, network ties, or organizational routines. I need a reusable evaluation procedure, structured as an ordered checklist with explicit stop conditions and judgment-flag points, that operationalizes the core distinctions between methodological individualism and methodological holism for a non-specialist reviewer who must complete an assessment in about thirty minutes and sign off on it. The procedure should not require philosophical training to apply, but it must preserve the actual conceptual difference between explaining a collective phenomenon by aggregating individual actions and explaining it by attributing causal powers to a collective entity that are not reducible to those actions.

Specifically, the checklist should run through the following in order: first, whether the proposal identifies every macro-level explanatory variable; second, whether it specifies the micro-level mechanisms that generate or sustain each such variable; third, whether those mechanisms are presented as sufficient to reproduce the macro-pattern (aggregation) or as mediated by an irreducible collective property (holism); fourth, whether the empirical design can distinguish these two readings; and fifth, where the proposal falls on a four-part classification I want you to embed—direct aggregation, distributed mechanism with emergent pattern, autonomous collective cause, or black-box placeholder—together with the decision rule for funding, revision, or rejection attached to each class. At each step, mark precisely where the reviewer must make a value-laden or interpretive judgment rather than apply a technical rule.

Apply the procedure to two concrete proposals. Proposal A examines regional vaccine hesitancy by modeling individual belief-updating rules in social networks and comparing the simulated aggregate to observed geographic clusters; it treats regional culture only as a descriptive summary of distributed beliefs. Proposal B examines the same outcome but posits collective risk aversion as an autonomous property of the region that directly suppresses vaccination rates, measuring it solely through aggregate survey indices and offering no individual-level mechanism; however, the preliminary data are unusually strong and the principal investigator is highly prominent, so I am inclined to fund it despite my reservations. Run the procedure on both, showing where Proposal B triggers a stop or revision condition, and identify the exact point where my inclination to fund conflicts with the procedure’s output—this should be flagged as a judgment call the checklist cannot resolve.

Finally, state explicitly what the procedure is not designed to do: for example, whether it can adjudicate ontological debates about the reality of social structures, whether it applies to purely descriptive (non-explanatory) macro-studies, or whether it can substitute for domain-specific empirical critique. Keep the procedure free of citations, bibliographies, or references to specific texts; ground it in the conceptual structure alone.
