Here is where I have ended up on evolutionary debunking in metaethics, and I want to know whether the package is coherent or whether I am protecting favored beliefs with an ad hoc exemption.

(1) I accept the core evolutionary debunking argument: our normative judgments are largely shaped by selection pressures for social cooperation, in-group loyalty, and reciprocal altruism, and since reproductive fitness bears no reliable correlation with normative truth, moral realism lacks the kind of non-circular justification it requires.

(2) I reject extending this debunking to logic and mathematics. I take evolutionary explanations to operate on contingent tracking mechanisms; necessary truths are not causally trackable in the same way, so there is no parallel unreliability story for arithmetic or deduction.

(3) Inside morality itself, I want to apply the debunking selectively. Deontological judgments about rights, duties, and purity strike me as obvious evolutionary artifacts—cooperation-enforcing heuristics—whereas judgments that severe suffering is intrinsically bad are different. The latter are not theoretical constructions but direct registrations of experiential quality, so they are not debunked in the same manner.

(4) At the same time, I hold a methodological principle: if you debunk a belief-forming mechanism, you may not carve out exceptions for particular outputs unless you possess an independent, non-circular warrant for the partition. Otherwise you are simply shielding the beliefs you wish to keep.

(5) Finally, I maintain that we are rationally entitled—indeed compelled—to hold firmly that unnecessary severe suffering is intrinsically bad, regardless of the evolutionary pedigree of that belief. Its badness is phenomenologically given in a way that survives origin-debunking.

My question is whether these five commitments hang together. I see a likely conflict between (3) and (4): I am partitioning moral cognition to protect suffering-judgments, but (4) seems to forbid exactly that move absent independent grounds. I also wonder whether (2) and (3) are in tension: if suffering-judgments survive because they are direct registrations rather than evolutionary constructs, am I implicitly conceding that some moral judgments are not evolutionary products, which might weaken (1)? And there may be a hidden tension between (1) and (5): if the evolutionary story undermines the justification for moral realism, can I still claim that the badness of suffering is rationally compulsory, or does that require a realist commitment I have already abandoned?

Please audit the set. Rank the tensions by severity, distinguish the real conflicts from those that dissolve on closer inspection, and specify what must be revised or abandoned for coherence. Be concrete: if (3) must go, does that mean surrendering the selective protection of suffering-judgments, or can (3) be retained by weakening (4)? If (5) is the weak point, does dropping it leave me with an unlivable global skepticism, or can rational entitlement be reconstructed on anti-realist grounds? I am not asking for a survey of the debunking literature; I want to know whether these specific commitments can all stay.
