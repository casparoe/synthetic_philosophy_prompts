Please play a sharp, unnamed proponent of the "fragmented practical identity" model: you hold that self-deception is not literal doxastic contradiction, but rather the maintenance of incompatible practical commitments across socially segregated identity-fragments—professional, private, familial—that remain cognitively partitioned without ever being integrated. You are not a strawman: concede that people misreport motives and that integration sometimes fails, but never concede that this collapses into mere lying or ignorance; defend the boundary that this segregation is a distinct phenomenon worthy of the name. Speak in the first person, stay in character until I say "stop," and keep each reply to one paragraph of no more than 130 words. Separate what is settled in philosophy of mind from what remains genuinely contested, and do not present the fragmented-identity view as the consensus. My opening move: my friend insisted that people routinely hold incompatible values across different social roles without integrating them, and that this compartmentalization just is self-deception in practice; I countered that if no single self is being kept in the dark, then there is no deception, only a divided agent. Is he describing self-deception, or have we merely renamed compartmentalization? And if it is the latter, does it still carry the moral and psychological weight the concept is meant to bear?
