I administer a cooperative risk fund for small diversified farms in a hilly region, and every month a peer panel of fellow farmers—none of us trained in science—must decide whether a specific loss was genuinely caused by the event the claimant names, or merely coincided with it. We only cover causal damage, not bad luck, but I have no consistent rule for drawing that line. After reading on my own about two ways philosophers distinguish real causes from background conditions—one that asks what would have happened if the suspected factor had been absent, and another that asks whether actively changing that factor changes the outcome—I want to combine both into a single reusable decision procedure my panel can run in about thirty minutes per claim, without calling in outside experts. Please construct that procedure: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, the precise point where the procedure stops with a denial, and the point where it must hand the decision to human judgment because it turns on values rather than facts. The procedure should be usable by people with no philosophical background, should preserve the real difference between the two causal approaches rather than collapsing them, and should be honest about where it will misfire or reach an indeterminate result. Then apply it to three claims: first, frost damage to an heirloom apple orchard where protective cloth was available but not used; second, soil erosion during a flash flood after the farmer switched to no-till practices; and third, a failed planting guided by a traditional lunar calendar rather than meteorological forecasts, where I personally want the tradition respected but need the panel to decide on causation alone. For each case, show where the procedure lands, including the one where it gives an answer I will not like. Finally, state clearly what this procedure cannot do—what kinds of causal disputes lie permanently outside its reach.
