Read Ursula K. Le Guin’s short story The Ones Who Walk Away from Omelas through the lens of Peter Singer’s drowning-child argument, but with a specific conceptual twist: treat the suffering child in the closet not merely as a distant victim to be rescued, but as a structural precondition for the prosperity and happiness of Omelas itself. Does this embedding support Singer’s claim that physical proximity and causal indirectness are morally irrelevant, or does it instead suggest that direct rescue—simply freeing the child—is incoherent or futile without dismantling the social system that depends on that suffering? Point to concrete narrative details—the citizens’ collective knowledge, the child’s anonymity and lack of a name or face, the option to walk away versus to stay and reform the city—and explain how each functions in this philosophical debate. Where does the story resist being reduced to an illustration of either direct or structural duty? Finally, what does Le Guin’s refusal to particularize the child add to the philosophical discussion of anonymity, statistical lives, and moral urgency that Singer’s pond case, with its visible, singular victim, tends to suppress? What would the honest critic have to concede about the limits of the rescue analogy here?
