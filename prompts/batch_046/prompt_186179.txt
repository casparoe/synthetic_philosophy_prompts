I've been roped into giving the opening remarks at our new community dialoge centre this Saturday — there'll be neighbours, a couple of academics from the university who actually study culture and ethics, and my usual mates who'd rather be at the pub. I want to welcome people properly, but I've been stuck on this thing I read about folk moralitiy — apparently people in different cultures don't just have different rules, they have genuinely different gut instincts about what's fair, harmful, or loyal, and there's this idea that WEIRD societies are wierd compared to the rest of the world. It got me wondering: if our deepest moral feelings are partly just where we grew up, does that mean moral objectivity is dead? Or can there still be right and wrong that isn't just 'my culture said so'?

I don't want to sound like I'm lecturing, and I definitely haven't solved philosophy — I work in logistics, not a university. But I also don't want to fall back on 'everyone's different and that's lovely', because that feels like a cop-out when some things really do seem wrong no matter where you come from. I want the speech to actually wrestle with the tension: it should shape what I can and can't claim. So I shouldn't pretend there's one hidden universal rulebook we all forgot, but I also shouldn't say it's all just opinion and there's nothing solid to stand on. Maybe the point is that we have to build some shared floor from our different starting points, admitting they're local without letting that excuse everything. Could you write me a five-minute speech that does that? It needs to land for the experts without losing everyone else, and please don't put in any reading lists or citations — I wouldn't know what to cite, and half the room wouldn't care.
