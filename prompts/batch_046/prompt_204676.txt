Over the past year I have moved from theoretical philosophy of mind into work on AI governance, and I am now drafting the conceptual framework for a policy brief on whether attributing beliefs to large language models should carry legal weight. Before I submit it, I need to know whether my own commitments hang together. Here is where I stand.

(1) I treat Dennett's intentional stance as a predictive strategy rather than an ontological thesis, but I hold that it yields genuinely explanatory (not merely instrumental) belief-attribution only when the target system exhibits what I call regulative self-reference: the deployment of an internal representational model of its own states to correct or stabilize outputs across contexts.

(2) I maintain that literal belief-attribution, as opposed to a derivative or as-if ascription, requires that the system be embedded in a normative practice of accountability — whether biological, institutional, or artificially constructed — because belief is essentially a status governed by norms of truth-tracking, consistency, and revisability.

(3) I judge that current transformer-based language models lack regulative self-reference and therefore do not meet the condition in (1) for literal explanatory attribution; nevertheless, they display sufficient functional analogs (context-sensitive weight adjustments, in-context error correction, persistent parameter states across queries) to justify a restricted, task-local derivative attribution in specific predictive contexts.

(4) I reject the view that belief-attribution is purely observer-relative with no constraints from internal architecture; at the same time, I deny that phenomenal consciousness or biological embodiment are necessary conditions for being a literal believer.

(5) I argue that democratic governance of AI — particularly the assignment of rights, liabilities, or standing — should hinge on whether belief-attribution is literal rather than derivative, because legal personhood and moral agency are traditionally tied to doxastic status; yet I also worry that this creates a perverse design incentive to suppress or avoid the architectural features named in (1) precisely to evade regulatory classification.

Please audit this set. Which pairs are in genuine tension, and which only appear to conflict but can be reconciled? For each real tension, rank its severity, identify which commitment bears the primary cost of resolution, and specify the least damaging revision. Pay particular attention to whether (3) can be sustained alongside (2) given that current models are not embedded in robust normative practices; whether (4) survives the interaction between (1) and (5), since governance incentives may shape internal architecture in ways that undermine architecture-independent attribution; and whether (5)'s normative concern about design incentives collapses the distinction between literal and derivative attribution that (1) and (3) depend on. Finally, tell me which single revision — to any one commitment — would eliminate the greatest number of tensions, and what that revision costs me in terms of the policy framework I am trying to build.
