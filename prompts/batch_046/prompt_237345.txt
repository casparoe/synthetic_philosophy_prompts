I sit on the institutional review board for predictive analytics in our university's partner public-health clinics. By next Friday we must recommend whether to approve a pilot deployment of a risk-stratification algorithm, and the specific decision is this: should we mandate a zero-cost retrospective validation study—using only our existing de-identified records, with no patient contact or risk—before any live deployment, or should we approve an adaptive rollout that updates its parameters in real time?

Several board members invoke Good's theorem on the value of information. They argue that because the retrospective study is free (it requires no new data collection, poses no harm, and uses staff time already budgeted for compliance), and because our collective utility over patient outcomes is linear in welfare, a rational Bayesian agent should never make the deployment decision without first observing the study results. They treat refusal as a direct violation of expected-utility maximization.

Other members reply that this applies the theorem outside its domain. They contend that (1) the study is not genuinely free once opportunity costs, staff attention diverted from other safety checks, and the risk that the audit's framing will anchor heterogeneous board priors are included; (2) our board is not a single decision-maker with a fixed prior but a collective with distributed beliefs, and the study design itself is endogenous—whoever sets the validation metrics may shift institutional power rather than resolve disagreement; (3) our decision problem is iterated, not one-shot, because committing to a mandatory pre-deployment audit protocol changes the strategic incentives of future applicants, who may then tailor their submissions to anticipate the audit rather than to serve patient welfare.

Please draft the short written opinion I will circulate before Thursday's meeting. It must recommend a concrete course of action—either mandate the retrospective study with a defined timeline and conditions for moving to deployment, or approve adaptive rollout with explicit safeguards and a review date—and state the bottom line unambiguously (for example, "approve adaptive rollout contingent on X, with mandatory review at Y months"). The reasoning should engage the strongest version of the opposing argument seriously rather than dismissing it, and should include an honest treatment of the consideration that weighs most heavily on the losing side.

In particular, separate what is settled in the formal decision-theoretic literature—such as the conditions under which Good's result holds for a single agent with a well-defined prior and genuinely costless information—from what is genuinely contested among people who understand both decision theory and institutional design, such as whether collective governance with endogenous audit protocols falls within the theorem's scope, whether hidden opportunity costs invalidate the free-information premise in organizational settings, and whether strategic anticipation by future applicants undermines the expected-value calculation. Do not present a contested interpretive claim about the institutional applicability of the theorem as if it were the consensus view.
