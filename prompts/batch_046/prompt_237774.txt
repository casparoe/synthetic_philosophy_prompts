James's defense of belief without sufficient evidence applies only to options he calls living, forced, and momentous, yet his central examples involve religious commitment. This seems to presuppose that such belief is uniquely involuntary — that exposure to the right conditions makes belief inevitable — which would render the will in his title a misnomer. By contrast, Clifford's universal prohibition against believing on insufficient evidence treats belief-formation as directly voluntary, analogous to the ship-owner who sends passengers to sea without inspecting the vessel. But if belief itself is largely involuntary and only our preliminary inquiries are subject to the will, then James is not offering an ethics of belief at all, but rather an ethics of exposure: we may voluntarily place ourselves in evidentially ambiguous situations knowing they will trigger belief-formation. Given this distinction between causing belief and forming it, is James's position actually compatible with Clifford's prohibition applied strictly to voluntary inquiry, or does the boundary between belief-causation and belief-formation collapse?
