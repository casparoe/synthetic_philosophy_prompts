In clinical-ethics discussions about patients with fluctuating cognitive capacity, I keep encountering a pattern that seems related to, but not identical with, the familiar tension between an earlier self's documented wishes and a later self's expressed interests. Consider the following configuration. A patient executes an advance refusal of a particular life-sustaining intervention should severe dementia develop. Once the dementia is present, the patient appears satisfied and repeatedly requests the intervention. During brief, well-documented lucid intervals, however, the patient does not simply restate the refusal; rather, they speak in a governing mode, instructing caregivers to disregard the requests made during the demented phases and framing those requests as not truly theirs, even while acknowledging that they feel no present aversion to the intervention. Once lucidity fades, the requests resume. The result is not a binary conflict between two stable selves, but a cyclical structure in which an intermittent, meta-level authority claims jurisdiction over a persistent, contented state that it disowns. Does this tri-layered dynamic—original directive, intermittent governing self, and surrounding contented state—have an established name or analytical framework in the literature on personal identity and medical authority? If not, can you delineate its boundaries: specifically, how it differs both from standard cases of changed preference under dementia and from situations in which a patient simply oscillates between two equally stable value sets?
