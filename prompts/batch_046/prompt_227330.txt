I am presenting a paper at next month's workshop, "Institutional Contexts of Early Modern Knowledge," to an audience of roughly twenty-five historians of science and medicine—mostly specialists in sixteenth- and seventeenth-century Italy and Northern Europe, with a minority of intellectual historians who work on Renaissance humanism. My thesis is aggressively continuist: I argue that the standard tripartite division—'Middle Ages', 'Renaissance', and 'Scientific Revolution'—is not a family of heuristic labels describing real epistemic breaks, but a 19th-century disciplinary invention that was retrojected onto the years 1300–1700 in order to separate an emergent 'science' from both medieval scholasticism and Renaissance humanism. More specifically, I claim that the near-simultaneous appearance in 1543 of Copernicus's De revolutionibus orbium coelestium and Vesalius's De humani corporis fabrica should be read not as twin pillars marking the birth of a discrete 'Scientific Revolution', but as parallel outputs of a shared Paduan-Bolognese textual-technical culture—one in which anatomical practice, mathematical astronomy, and philological editing coexisted without recognizing any modern boundary between 'science' and 'humanities'. The room is generally allergic to grand narratives but treats periodization as pragmatic shorthand; they prize archival specificity, are vigilant against anachronism, and are particularly protective of regional variation, since several have published on how Italian university structures and patronage networks diverged sharply from those in Paris, Leiden, or Wittenberg.
