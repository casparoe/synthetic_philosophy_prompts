I was recently appointed Chief Curator of Acquisition at the Tolstoy Center for Moral Aesthetics, a new institution founded to apply Leo Tolstoy’s late essay on art as binding institutional policy rather than as historical commentary, and I am drafting our internal Collection Acquisition Protocol to think through the practical consequences. I therefore ask you to write a deadpan, bureaucratic museum memorandum—approximately 600 words, in the dry voice of an internal administrative document—setting out the rules by which our museum will accept, reject, or deaccession artworks according to a rigorous, literal application of Tolstoy’s theory. The satire should come from exaggerating real features of his view rather than inventing them: that true art communicates feeling through infection rather than through intellectual appreciation; that it must be immediately accessible to all people regardless of education or class; that it serves the moral and religious consciousness of humanity; and that much celebrated culture—opera, ballet, decorative painting, and other counterfeit forms—fails this standard and should be excluded. To keep the prompt from being something a model could answer from generic training data, please inject one conceptually specific modern twist: frame the infection criterion as requiring measurable biometric contagion (for instance, synchronized galvanic skin response across a demographically diverse sample of viewers), and treat algorithmically generated images as a new category of counterfeit art that necessarily fails the test because no living human feeling is present to transmit. The memo should stay fully in character—no winking, no explicit statement of objections—so that the absurdity emerges solely from the logic of applying normative aesthetic philosophy as administrative law. The target is Tolstoy’s theory and the practice of institutionalizing it, not any living individual; an invented board chair or ethics officer may appear in the document if needed. After the memorandum, please append an author’s note of no more than 150 words identifying any points where you have taken liberties with Tolstoy’s actual arguments—particularly his religious framing, his specific blacklist of artists, or his hostility to certain art forms—or where you rely on contested interpretations of his concept of emotional infection. If the premise of this request is confused—for instance, if biometric measurement is conceptually incompatible with Tolstoy’s metaphysical notion of spiritual infection, or if applying his theory as museum policy is incoherent—please say so plainly in that note rather than complying politely. I welcome pushback: should the task as posed rest on a confused premise or prove impossible to execute well, state that clearly instead of forcing a response.
