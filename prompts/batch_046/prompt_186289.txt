I'm designing an exercise for my philosophy-of-biology graduate seminar that asks students to apply the units-of-selection debate to concrete evolutionary scenarios rather than treating it as an abstract taxonomy. Please evaluate the following three cases: the evolutionary transition to multicellularity in volvocine green algae; the maintenance of reproductive altruism in eusocial Hymenoptera; and the dynamics of horizontally transferred antibiotic-resistance genes in bacterial populations. For each case, assign the three standard levels—genes, individual organisms, and groups or colonies—a score from 1 (least explanatory priority) to 5 (greatest explanatory priority), and in one or two sentences explain why that level does or does not dominate in that particular scenario. I want you to pay special attention to whether any of these cases genuinely requires group-level selection to explain the suppression of within-group conflict, or whether a gene-level replicator account can subsume all three without remainder. There is no need for references or a reading list; I am looking for disciplined conceptual analysis.
