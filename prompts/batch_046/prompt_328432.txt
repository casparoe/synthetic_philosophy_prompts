You are an expert philosopher of action specializing in Frankfurt-style counterexamples to the principle of alternative possibilities. Before answering, please brainstorm the main candidate rules that could unify the judgments below, and identify what kind of further case would best separate them.

As a high school student writing an essay on moral responsibility, I am trying to understand my teacher Dr. Vasquez's view. She has said:
(1) A prisoner physically forced by guards to sign a false confession is not responsible, even though he tries to resist.
(2) A recovering addict with an implanted craving-blocker feels an urge, suppresses it herself, and the device never activates; Dr. Vasquez says she is fully responsible for refusing.
(3) A sleepwalker who damages property has no conscious awareness; she says he is not responsible.
(4) A chess player faces a hidden AI that overturns any illegal move, yet he makes only legal moves of his own accord; she says he is fully responsible for his strategy.

Here is a new case I invented for my essay: In a virtual-reality ethics simulation, a participant named Jones knows an invisible ethical governor can block harmful actions but cannot affect his thoughts. Jones feels tempted to take a hidden unfair advantage that harms no visible party. He considers it, then rejects it purely out of long-trained habit; the governor never activates. Unbeknownst to him, he has also been on an empathy-suppressing medication that removes emotional motivation but leaves reasoning intact.

If Dr. Vasquez wants to stay consistent, what should she say about Jones's responsibility for rejecting the advantage? Which prior case is the closest analog? I am mainly interested in the rule she seems to be running, at least two other rules that fit her four verdicts just as well, how those rules split on this new case, and what single further scenario—say, one involving a predictive neural interface that suppresses an intention before it reaches consciousness—would best distinguish them.
