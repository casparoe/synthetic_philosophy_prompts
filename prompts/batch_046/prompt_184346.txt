As a novelist drafting a dinner scene between an exiled mathematician and a diplomat from a causally isolated colony, I need a joke that functions as actual spoken dialogue—wry, precise, and slightly melancholy, suitable for poor wine and worse cosmology. The subject is acausal trade: the idea that rational agents separated by cosmological horizons can still cooperate not through causation but through logical correlation of their decision procedures, each reasoning about what the other, reasoning about them, will do. I want the humor to turn on the genuine mechanics—superrationality, the absence of external enforcement except through the consistency of one’s own algorithm, the peculiar moral hazard that defection is causally free yet logically self-undermining. To keep it from feeling like a textbook illustration, please embed the joke within a specific, somewhat invented conceptual wrinkle: in this fictional world, the two civilizations share no physics, no language, and no observable history, except for an identical, long-forgotten error in their respective attempts to prove a variant of the Riemann hypothesis—an error so precisely structured that it serves as their sole coordination point. The punchline should derive from the tension between treating this mathematical mistake as a sacred, binding covenant and the practical reality that correcting one’s proof dissolves the contract. The joke should be intelligible to someone who understands acausal reasoning but must not depend on any particular named theorist, paper, or bibliographic reference; avoid citations, reading lists, or appeals to authority. Write it as polished literary dialogue—something a character might say with a raised glass, not as an explanation addressed to the reader.
