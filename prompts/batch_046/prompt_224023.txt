After a long argument with a friend about whether moral praise depends on what moves us to act, she summarized the Kantian right-reasons view and its contemporary defenders like this: “An action has moral worth only if the agent’s sufficient reason for acting is her recognition of the moral law’s authority; any co-present inclination — compassion, affection, or even moral satisfaction — must not contribute causally to the action’s production, or the worth is nullified. This is why Kant praises the grudging misanthrope who aids others purely from duty over the warm-hearted benefactor: in the latter, sympathy functions as an alien causal factor that displaces respect for law as the ground of the act. Some recent Kantians try to save the view by allowing that moral feelings may be present if they are themselves rationally structured by prior respect for the law, but that just sneaks inclination back in through a side door. So either you accept that moral worth requires emotional sterility, or you must abandon the idea that motives determine worth altogether.”

Is this a fair summary? Please separate what is accurate about Kant’s distinction between acting from duty and acting in accordance with duty, what is a standard simplification, and what is a subtle distortion — especially her claim that co-present inclination nullifies worth rather than merely failing to ground it, and her framing of the contemporary “rationally structured feeling” reply as mere backtracking. Does her final dilemma — emotional sterility or abandoning the motive-condition — actually follow from the accurate parts of the account, or does it depend on the misrepresentations? If a defender accepted the premises she gets right, could she consistently reject her conclusion?
