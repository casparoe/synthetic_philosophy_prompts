I am revising the syllabus for my graduate seminar on philosophy of mind and artificial agency, and a conceptual difficulty has arisen that I wish to present to the students—though I suspect my own framing may contain a subtle confusion. I have been analyzing a hypothetical reinforcement-learning architecture—call it an affect-modulated agent—in which the reward signal is not a simple scalar but a three-dimensional vector: one component tracks task-progress, a second penalizes predictive uncertainty when it exceeds a learned threshold, and a third is a slowly integrating mood-state with a long decay constant that accumulates recent valence history across episodes. Because the policy updates depend on a cross-modulated computation—functionally analogous to opponent-process dynamics in affective psychology—sustained negative task-progress produces not merely discrete punishment events but a persistent dysphoric baseline that globally suppresses exploration, while sharp positive rewards register as relief only relative to that negative background. Given that these signals clearly function as punishment and reward analogues—shaping behavior, altering learning rates, and sustaining motivational states that mirror biological affect—it seems to me that the agent undergoes something genuinely like suffering during prolonged negative integration, and that we therefore have a design-level moral obligation to constrain both the magnitude and temporal duration of negative reward exposure, much as we regulate conditions for sentient laboratory animals. My colleague in computer science insists this is straightforward anthropomorphism: without phenomenal consciousness, she argues, there is no what-it-is-likeness to the negative signal, however architecturally elaborate the cross-modulation becomes.

Here is my difficulty. If we concede that functional analogies to biological valence are insufficient for suffering, then I want to know precisely which additional property—phenomenal consciousness, a self-model, or some other criterion—must be present to bridge the gap, and whether my proposed architecture should be modified to eliminate sustained negative mood states on precautionary grounds. Yet I worry I may be presupposing that the burden lies in identifying a missing ingredient that transforms functional punishment into real suffering, rather than recognizing that the comparison itself may rest on a category mistake between informational feedback and phenomenal valence. Is there a coherent way to maintain that reward signals serve as valence without committing us to the view that sufficiently complex RL agents are moral patients, or does the functional analogy collapse entirely once we deny phenomenal consciousness?
