I'm currently working through Rousseau's 1758 Lettre à d'Alembert sur les spectacles for a chapter on republican aesthetics, and I keep returning to a particular argumentative sequence that I cannot quite reconstruct. In the section responding to d'Alembert's proposal for a Genevan theater, Rousseau contends that theatrical spectacle produces a distinct moral pathology: citizens who observe representations of virtue develop what amounts to a substitutive satisfaction, feeling that they have participated in civic excellence merely by witnessing it performed. He appears to argue that this is not a contingent effect of bad plays but a structural feature of the theatrical form itself—the physical separation between stage and auditorium, the temporal boundedness of the performance, and the emotional release that follows—such that even an edifying drama would exhaust the motivational resources that republican self-government requires. He then moves quickly from this psychological claim to an institutional one, asserting that the introduction of theater would dissolve the specific form of civic friendship that sustains Geneva's small republic, replacing participatory equality with hierarchical consumption.

What I find puzzling is the bridge between these two levels. Is Rousseau assuming that aesthetic emotion is inherently non-transferable into political action—something like a hydraulic model in which feeling virtue in the theater necessarily depletes the capacity to enact it in the assembly? Or does he have a more specific, perhaps spatial, argument about how the architecture of spectacle introduces a division between active and passive citizens that contradicts the egalitarian dynamics he associates with the general will? I am also unsure how to reconcile this with his own composition of Le Devin du Village, unless he distinguishes between genres of performance or between urban and rural contexts of reception. Could you help me understand the precise logical structure of this passage? Specifically, I want to see whether the argument depends on an unstated premise about the uniqueness of theatrical representation, and whether there is a consistent principle that allows him to condemn theater in Geneva while still defending popular festivals that also involve collective observation.
