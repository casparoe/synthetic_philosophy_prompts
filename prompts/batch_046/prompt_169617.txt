I've been trying to wrap my head around Hofstadter's idea that an "I" is basically a recursive pattern rather than the brain itself — sort of like a distributed system where the self is the protocol, not the server. Me and my mate were arguing about it after work and we sketched out what feels like a reductio, but I can't tell if it's actually valid or if there's a hidden slip somewhere.

(1) If the self is a strange loop of self-referencing information, then what makes me "me" is the specific causal and functional pattern, not the particular neurons running it. (2) Two different brains can share parts of that pattern — for instance, if they both contain the same core belief-structure, memory-trace, and recursive "this is happening to me" module. (3) Now, if brain A runs the full loop and brain B runs a sub-loop that includes the recursive kernel generating first-person experience, then brain B is literally running part of my self, instatiated in different meat. (4) There's no principled reason this overlap has to be all-or-nothing; with enough shared cognitive architechture (think future neural interfaces or just very similar upbringings), parts of my loop are running in other people right now. (5) So my self is already distributed across a network of brains, and destroying one brain wouldn't destroy "me", just like deleting one server doesn't kill the cloud. (6) But that means I should be experiencing other people's lives as parts of my own stream of consciousness, or at least feeling split when they diverge. I clearly don't. (7) Therefore either (a) I'm wrong about (1) and the self needs a unique biological anchor after all, or (b) personal identity is an illusion and there's just one big distributed mind.

This feels like it proves too much — it turns every shared belief into a shared self. I'd really like to hang onto (1) because as an engineer it makes perfect sense that identity is about the pattern. Is the mistake in (3), where I'm treating a shared sub-routine as part of the same token loop? Or is there an equivocation between "same type of loop" and "same instance of the loop"? And if I reject (4) by saying actual overlap never occurs, isn't that just dodging the point? What's the cheapest fix that doesn't throw out the pattern view entirely?
