Consider the following argument, which I constructed for a discussion on whether suffering and pleasure can be traded off on a common scale, and which now strikes me as suspicious because it seems to dissolve lexical-threshold negative utilitarianism into either self-contradiction or an unrecognizable view.

(1) Lexical-threshold negative utilitarianism (LTNU) holds that there exists some severity of suffering s such that no aggregate of pleasure, however large, can justify allowing s.

(2) If pleasure and suffering are incommensurable—lacking a shared metric that permits quantitative comparison—then the claim "no amount of pleasure outweighs s" is either meaningless or false, since "amount" has no cross-modal application.

(3) Therefore LTNU implicitly presupposes that pleasure and suffering are commensurable: they must occupy a single cardinal welfare scale for the "no amount" clause to be well-formed.

(4) Once commensurability is established, welfare values are at least in principle additive and comparable.

(5) So for any given s, there exists in principle a pleasure-aggregate P whose total welfare value exceeds |s|—for instance, a sufficiently large population experiencing intense but not extreme well-being over a sufficient duration.

(6) Hence, if (3) and (4) hold, LTNU is false: there is always some amount of pleasure that outweighs s.

(7) The defender might reject (4) by insisting that suffering carries a non-aggregative, categorical normative weight—what we might call a lexical veto—that operates independently of magnitude.

(8) But if suffering's veto power is genuinely independent of magnitude, then LTNU is not the claim that "no quantity of pleasure outweighs s"; it is the claim that quantitative comparison is categorically inapplicable to suffering.

(9) In that case LTNU collapses either into a deontological side-constraint (suffering must be prevented regardless of welfare balances) or into a form of extreme risk-averse negative utilitarianism with an unexplained discontinuity.

(10) Neither of these is the standard formulation of LTNU, which explicitly presents itself as a comparative, quantitative claim about welfare aggregates.

(11) So LTNU requires commensurability to be coherent, but commensurability makes the lexical barrier unsustainable; and denying commensurability transforms LTNU into a different view altogether.

(12) Therefore LTNU should be abandoned.

If forced to preserve premises, I would rather keep (1) and (7) than (6) or (9), because I think suffering does have a distinctive normative status. But I cannot see how to hold them together without contradiction. Which step is the actual exit? Explain why it seemed plausible, and price the cost of taking it—especially what it does to the idea that pleasure and pain might still be partially incommensurable without destroying LTNU entirely.
