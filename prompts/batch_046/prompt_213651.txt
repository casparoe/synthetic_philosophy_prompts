Next month I present to the philosophy of mind colloquium at my institution a paper I began writing after two years of caring for my mother through progressive dementia. Watching her narrative coherence dissolve without any corresponding loss of affective presence convinced me that the Cartesian theater is not merely false but structurally incapable of explaining graded fragmentation: there is no central screen displaying experience to a homuncular observer. I defend Dennett's multiple drafts model, supplemented by what I call editorial latency—the claim that drafts stabilize across variable temporal scales according to working-memory load and emotional salience rather than crossing a single publication threshold, which accounts for divergent retrospective reports of identical intervals without anyone being mistaken about an inner show. The audience is precisely this: three phenomenologists working in the tradition of Zahavi's minimal self-model and Gallagher's embodied cognition; four cognitive scientists for whom global workspace theory and the Baars-Dehaene framework are the default; two philosophers of science who regard consciousness studies as conceptually muddled; and one senior scholar who completed her doctoral work on Dennett's early writings and will recognize every move I make. I need a Q&A prep sheet, not a general defense. List the objections in the order they are likely to arise, phrased in the vocabulary of the faction that will raise them. For each objection: state whether I should concede part of it, identify exactly what I must hold, provide a reply calibrated to about two minutes, and flag the specific weakness in that reply that I should acknowledge rather than conceal. End with the objection I am hoping nobody asks—specifically, the one that targets my personal motivation—phrased as a skeptical philosopher of science would actually deliver it in the room.
