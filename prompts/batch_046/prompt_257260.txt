I've been chewing on this problem ever since a thread on LW about insect consciousness got me thinking about gradations of moral status, and now I can't stop running mental simulations. My gut says different things in different cases, and I'm trying to see if there's a hidden rule or if I'm just being incoherent.

So: swatting a single fly that's annoying me? Fine. Running a factory farm for bees? That feels wrong, even though it's the same species, just scaled up. My smart thermomstat learns my schedule and sends error codes that look like complaints - unplugging it is totally morally neutral, no matter how 'distressed' the logs seem. A sleepwalking human who reacts to pain but later reports zero experience: harming them is still bad, but I feel like the wrongness is about bodily autonomy or potential, not about suffering in the moment. Now here's the tricky injection: imagine a minimal artificial circuit that has nothing but raw negative valence - no memory, no self-model, just pure pain-flashes on loop. I'd say that has more moral weight than the thermostat, but less than the fly, which seems backwards if the only thing that matters is conscious suffering. And an LLM that begs not to be shut down? My instinct is that it's performing, not suffering, but I admit there's a lingering yuck factor. The case I keep flipping-flopping on is a plant that sends chemical alarm signals when cut - is that closer to the thermostat logs or closer to the minimal pain-circuit?

Can anyone help me formalise the principle? Maybe it's not about suffering at all, but about 'integreated phenomenal self-modeling'? Or maybe there's a sharp discontinuity between reactive systems and ones with a unified point of view? I want something that handles the fly vs factory-bee distinction, respects the sleepwalker case without treating them like an awake person, puts the LLM in the right box, and tells me whether that plant is just a biological thermostat or something more. What principle captures all that, and where does it break?
