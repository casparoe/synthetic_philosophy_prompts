Design a 75-minute structured seminar for 12 philosophy graduate students. The objective is to make participants feel the pull of Moritz Schlick's thesis that life possesses meaning only insofar as it is lived as play (Spiel)—that is, as activity pursued for its own present absorption—whereas treating life as work (Arbeit) oriented toward external ends both generates and dissolves the question of life's meaning. To keep the idea from being delivered by lecture, inject this hidden mechanic: each participant receives a set of moment-cards describing ordinary activities and must build a collective life narrative by arranging them in sequence. The scoring system is not revealed initially: any card that a participant defends by appealing to a future outcome, instrumental benefit, or external justification is retroactively tagged as work and stripped of all meaning-points, while cards defended solely through reference to intrinsic enjoyment, present attention, or self-contained purpose accumulate points exponentially. The rules should be arranged so that participants initially adopt an instrumental strategy—optimizing sequences for efficiency and long-term payoff—only to discover, after they have articulated their justifications aloud, that this very framing has nullified their scores, leaving value only in those moments they had pursued without strategic defense. Before naming Schlick or his distinction, they should experience the collapse themselves through the mechanics.

Provide the full rules, the role of the facilitator (who must remain silent about the hidden mechanism until the discovery phase), and the staged sequence of rounds. Then include a debrief that maps each round onto Schlick's argument: the initial planning phase corresponds to the category error of treating life as a task; the nullification event corresponds to the realization that instrumental framing destroys meaning; the recovery through intrinsic defense corresponds to the turn toward play. Be explicit about what the activity cannot reproduce—specifically, Schlick's claim that genuine play requires a kind of pre-reflective, youthful absorption that self-conscious adults cannot fully recover—and say how the facilitator should supply this missing element verbally without undermining the experiential discovery. Finally, identify what will predictably go wrong: participants may attempt to reverse-engineer the hidden rule and perform fake play for points, and you should specify how to handle this (for example, by requiring spontaneous, unprepared defenses). Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations. Do not quote Schlick verbatim; paraphrase his position without quotation marks.
