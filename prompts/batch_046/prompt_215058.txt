Question: In Jain philosophy, anekāntavāda holds that reality is many-sided, while syādvāda offers a method of qualified assertion using the particle syāt. Some contemporary philosophers compare this to "framework pluralism" in science—the view that different theories can capture genuinely different, irreducible aspects of one domain rather than being merely approximate. Evaluate whether anekāntavāda is best understood as a form of framework pluralism. In your answer, address (i) the metaphysical status of the infinite-attribute nature of substance (ananta-dharma), (ii) the function of syādvāda and its relation to the seven-fold predication (saptabhaṅgī), and (iii) whether contradictory properties (e.g., permanence and impermanence) can belong to a substance in the same respect or only across different standpoints (nayas).

Student answer:
Ankāntavāda is primarily a metaphysical doctrine: every real substance (dravya) possesses infinite attributes or modes (ananta-dharma), so no unqualified statement can fully capture it. Syādvāda then supplies the linguistic form—every assertion should carry the qualification syāt ("in some respect"), which produces the seven-fold scheme of predication (saptabhaṅgī): simple affirmation, simple negation, affirmation-and-negation together, inexpressibility, and the three combinations involving inexpressibility. The standpoints (nayas) specify which aspect is being highlighted, and together they block the error of absolutism (ekānta). Because a substance genuinely contains contradictory characteristics—such as being both permanent and impermanent—these characteristics can inhere in exactly the same metaphysical respect, provided each is assigned to the appropriate naya. Thus, Jain pluralism is structurally parallel to modern framework pluralism in science: just as Newtonian and relativistic mechanics describe genuinely different, irreducible features of space-time rather than being merely approximate, different Jain nayas capture real, coexisting features of a substance. The key distinction is that scientific frameworks are usually treated as conceptual instruments, whereas Jain nayas correspond to actual divisions within the object itself. Consequently, syādvāda does not avoid contradiction by separating respects; instead, it permits contradictory predicates to be jointly true by distributing them across standpoints. This makes anekāntavāda a form of metaphysical relativism—one that is stronger than mere epistemic humility, since it denies that there is a single, standpoint-independent fact of the matter about which properties belong to a substance.
