As a journalist who writes on the history and philosophy of science, I am preparing a long-form essay on how we narrate scientific change, and I want a rigorous consistency audit of my own position before committing it to print. Here is where I stand: (1) I reject Whig historiography in its strong form—judging past scientists by the predictive and explanatory standards of the present is anachronistic and flattens the conceptual worlds they actually inhabited; (2) I nonetheless hold that "scientific progress" is a defensible concept, provided it is operationalized strictly as a retrospective, monotonic increase in predictive scope and explanatory integration, with no implication of moral improvement or metaphysical convergence; (3) in science education, I defend what I call "strategic presentism": it is legitimate to teach obsolete frameworks—phlogiston, caloric, the geocentric model—as demonstrably wrong approximations that served as necessary stepping stones to current theories, precisely because this clarifies the logic of theory revision for students; (4) I deny that the trajectory of science possesses any intrinsic teleology or directional momentum; the path from Aristotelian physics to quantum field theory was radically contingent, and present theories are not the inevitable telos of earlier ones; (5) in recent columns I have argued that the canonical narrative of quantum mechanics—presenting Bohr's complementarity as the inevitable resolution of the wave-particle debate—constitutes a paradigmatic case of harmful Whig history, because it suppresses the live contingency of the Everett, de Broglie, and Bohm programs.

These commitments feel individually defensible, but I suspect they are not jointly stable. Is this package consistent? I want you to trace which commitments genuinely pull against each other—not merely which ones sound discordant, but where the underlying motivations are incompatible. For instance, does my defense of strategic presentism in (3) secretly rely on the very teleological directionality I deny in (4)? Does defining progress in (2) as monotonic predictive improvement smuggle in a retrospective inevitability that undermines my anti-Whig stance in (1) and (5)? Or is there an apparent tension—say, between (3) and (1)—that dissolves once we distinguish pedagogical framing from historiographical judgment? Please rank the tensions by severity, specify what each conflict costs (which commitment must be weakened or abandoned), and identify the least costly revision that restores coherence without forcing me to abandon my core anti-teleological view. Do not tell me which single belief to jettison; instead, deliver an audit: map the logical dependencies, flag the hidden imports, and say which pair is doing the real damage.
