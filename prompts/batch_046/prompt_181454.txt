I have been trying to work out the conceptual problem behind Douglas Hofstadter's old Letter Spirit project—namely, what makes every token of the letter 'a' belong to that single category despite enormous variation in shape, size, orientation, and medium. Rather than asking for a bibliography or external sources, I want to test whether my own intuitive verdicts about particular cases can be captured by one coherent principle, or whether I am holding commitments that pull in incompatible directions.

Here are my judgments. A printed 'a' in Times New Roman and a handwritten cursive 'a' are both the letter 'a', even though they share almost no geometric properties. A severely distorted 'a' in glitch art, where the loop is broken but the ascender and bowl remain recognizably arranged, still strikes me as an 'a', though I am less certain than in the normal case. By contrast, a symbol in an unknown writing system that happens to match the Times New Roman 'a' exactly is not an 'a'; its history and functional role within a notational system seem to matter. A child's drawing of a circle with a stick, which she intends as an 'a', usually gets corrected by adults, whereas a child who draws a loop with a stem in wild proportions is praised for writing an 'a', suggesting that authorial intention alone is not sufficient. In Hofstadter's Letter Spirit system, some computer-generated letterforms that do not occur in any human typography are immediately readable as 'a's, while others with very similar statistical feature profiles are not, which makes me doubt that recognition is merely a matter of matching to a mental prototype or template. Finally, I think that if we discovered a culture that used a triangular shape exclusively for the phoneme /a/, that triangle would be an 'a' for them, which points away from essential geometric properties and toward something like functional or conventional role.

Is there a single principle—perhaps involving family resemblance, functional embedding in an alphabetic system, intentional design within a notational practice, or a hybrid of these—that can accommodate all of these verdicts? If so, state it precisely and show how it handles both the glitch-art case and the unknown-alphabet case. If not, tell me honestly which of my judgments I should abandon to achieve consistency, and whether the resulting principle forces me to say that the readable forms produced by the Letter Spirit program are letters only derivatively, by analogy to human-designed ones, rather than genuinely being 'a's in their own right.
