Since returning to work after the birth of my child and attempting to coordinate childcare with my partner, I have become convinced that the standard frameworks for evaluating dual-earner family arrangements—whether they focus on present-time gender parity, child-development outcomes, or economic efficiency—fail to capture something essential: the diachronic structure of career and care burdens across a working life. Before I commit to any particular normative framework, I want to know exactly what an adequate theory of equitable temporal burden-sharing in dual-earner families with childcare obligations must deliver.

Write the desiderata: the full list of requirements any such theory must meet to count as adequate. For each requirement, provide the motivating case or distinction that makes it necessary—specifically addressing (i) how the theory must distinguish synchronic fairness (who does what in a given week) from diachronic equity (how cumulative career costs, skill depreciation, and pension accumulation are distributed over decades); (ii) how it must treat preference adaptation, where parents revise their career ambitions in response to structurally unequal constraints without thereby rendering the constraints just; (iii) which empirical results from labor economics and child development it must be consistent with and which it may legitimately override on normative grounds; (iv) how it must account for institutional scaffolding (parental leave policies, workplace flexibility norms, and childcare subsidies) without reducing justice to whatever the state currently provides; and (v) whether it can jointly satisfy the demand that arrangements be revisable over time (to accommodate changing family needs) and the demand that they provide stable expectations (so that neither partner faces chronic strategic vulnerability).

Flag any pair of desiderata you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied—particularly whether dynamic revisability and protection against strategic vulnerability are in tension. Then, briefly, assess how three broad approaches do against the list: a pure contractualist model that treats family labor division as a series of renegotiable agreements; a capabilities-based model focused on preserving each partner's long-term functionings; and a structural-institutional model that assigns primary responsibility to employers and states rather than to intra-family negotiation. Keep the weight on the criteria and their motivating cases rather than on the scorecard. Write your answer in American English.
