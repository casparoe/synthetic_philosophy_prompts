I am delivering the opening remarks at my mentor’s retirement symposium. She spent thirty years defending Schlick’s thesis that life bears meaning not through serving any purpose—cosmic, moral, or historical—but through what he described as the youthful posture of being absorbed in activity for its own sake. The audience is split between specialists in early analytic philosophy and her family and students from other disciplines. After a long argument with a friend last night, I am convinced that the remarks must do more than decorate a standard tribute with Schlickian vocabulary; the view itself should constrain what can and cannot be said. Specifically, I need to honor her contributions without framing them as the fulfillment of an external telos, without implying that her life’s value now depends on an institutional legacy, and without any consoling language about her watching over her work from beyond. Write a five-minute address that makes this constraint intelligible to non-philosophers, explains why her career mattered on Schlick’s own terms, and treats the absence of cosmic purpose not as a loss but as the very condition of the meaning she pursued.
