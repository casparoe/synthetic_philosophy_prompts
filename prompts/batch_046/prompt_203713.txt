Next month I defend my thesis to an interdisciplinary seminar on institutional economics and digital governance: open-access genomic databases do not suffer a Hardin-style tragedy of overuse, because storage and bandwidth are non-rival, but rather a "noise commons" in which unvetted submissions accumulate faster than curation capacity, degrading signal-to-noise without depleting the resource itself; I propose mandatory "curation reciprocity," requiring every substantial download to be paired with a verified annotation contribution. The audience will include committed Ostrom scholars who think commons governance requires clearly bounded user groups, graduated sanctions, and locally appropriate rules, plus empirical economists who regard analogies between ecological commons and digital goods as category errors lacking falsifiable predictions. They care about parsimony, empirical testability, and avoiding metaphor-driven policy. Prepare me for the objections in roughly the order they will come, phrased as these two camps would actually phrase them: first the category-error objection from the economists, then the misapplication-of-design-principles objection from the Ostromians, then the empirical-falsifiability challenge, then the hidden objection about enforcement feasibility that I am hoping nobody raises. For each objection, tell me what I should concede up front, what I should hold without apology, and the weakest point in my reply that I should prepare to defend rather than hope they miss. Identify the single reply that must land for the proposal to survive, and flag the part of my own argument that is most vulnerable to being read as merely relabeling a quality-control problem. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
