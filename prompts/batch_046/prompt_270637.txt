I'm a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy, but I've been trying to make sense of epistemic justification by mapping it onto concepts I already use—dependency graphs, root nodes, recursive validation, and integration testing. I want to know whether my intuitive verdicts on different belief-structures point to a coherent principle, or whether they conflict in ways that force me to abandon some of them. I find direct observation convincing as a terminating point—like inspecting a log file myself—so I treat experiential beliefs as justified without further backing. I also think that if belief A is supported by B, B by C, and so on in an infinitely long linear chain with no loops and no base case, the result is shaky, comparable to an infinitely recursive function with no termination condition; it is not obviously false, but it does not feel genuinely justified either. When beliefs form a tightly interconnected web in which each supports the others but none connect back to experience—what I take to be coherence—I think this can justify abstract or theoretical claims, yet it feels inadequate for concrete claims about my immediate environment, rather like a software module with perfect internal consistency but no tests against real-world inputs. I firmly reject circular justification, where belief A ultimately depends on itself, as equivalent to a self-certifying checksum that proves nothing. Yet I keep going back and forth on a case that blends these structures: a belief formed through a very long, non-circular chain of intermediate reasoning steps—say, accepting a complex, AI-generated explanation of a system failure that references further generated explanations—where the chain never loops back to direct observation and I have no independent grasp of the underlying premises. Sometimes the length and internal consistency make it feel acceptable; other times it feels indistinguishable from the infinite linear chain, merely obscured by complexity.

Is there a single principle—perhaps one that distinguishes the structural properties of the justification graph from the epistemic status of its nodes—that can accommodate all these verdicts, including my ambivalence about the long hidden chain? Or does preserving every intuition require an implausibly complex rule? Please state any proposed principle precisely, say which of my cases it captures correctly and which verdicts it forces me to overturn, and apply it to one further case I have not mentioned: a belief system that begins with an unverified axiom, builds an infinite non-circular tree of deductions from it, and then uses some high-level derived principle within that tree to justify the original axiom itself. I would prefer the answer in British English, and I am not looking for a bibliography, reading list, or citation of specific texts—just the principle, its costs, and its predictions.
