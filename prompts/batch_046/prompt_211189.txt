Consider the following rulings made by an epistemologist working on the reductionism versus anti-reductionism debate about testimonial justification. She has examined five cases and delivered a verdict in each, together with a brief rationale.

Case 1: A child comes to believe that snow is cold solely on the basis of her mother’s statement, with no prior tactile experience of snow or independent observation. Verdict: the child is epistemically justified, and the justification is not reducible to her own perception or inductive inference—testimony functions here as an autonomous source.

Case 2: A research scientist accepts a statistical finding reported in a peer-reviewed article after she has personally verified the data set, reproduced the analysis, and confirmed the absence of selection bias. Verdict: she is justified, but her justification is reducible to her own direct assessment of the evidence; testimony serves only as a transmission vehicle.

Case 3: A hiker follows directions carved into a tree by unknown prior hikers in a well-maintained national park. Verdict: she is justified, and her justification is reducible to an implicit background inference about the reliability of such markings in institutional contexts, even though she never articulates the inference.

Case 4: A patient accepts a diagnostic recommendation produced by a clinical-decision algorithm that lacks beliefs or intentions, knowing that the system’s outputs are audited by licensed physicians but having no direct access to the audit reports or the training data. Verdict: the patient is justified, and the justification is reducible to her warranted trust in the institutional audit framework and the algorithm’s validated track record.

Case 5: A juror comes to believe that a defendant was present at the scene based on a co-witness’s identification, but she also knows the co-witness was hypnotized prior to the lineup and that hypnosis can distort memory. Verdict: she is not justified; the testimonial chain is broken, and the failure is best explained by the collapse of the non-testimonial base that would otherwise support the belief.

Now consider a new case. A linguist studying an endangered language receives a phonetic transcription of a ritual chant from a digital archive. The archive was compiled automatically from decaying analog recordings by an algorithm designed at a reputable university, and the linguist understands the algorithm’s general architecture but cannot evaluate the original acoustic data. Separately, she interviews the last living elder who performed the chant decades ago; the elder confirms the transcription in detail, but the linguist knows the elder suffers from significant episodic-memory impairment and has been contradicted by other archived recordings on minor points. The linguist has no other independent access to the ritual.

Predict the epistemologist’s verdict: is the linguist justified in accepting the specific phonetic details of the transcription? Identify at least two distinct rules or theoretical frameworks—e.g., a form of reductionism, anti-reductionism, or hybrid transmission view—that are jointly consistent with her five earlier rulings but diverge on this new case. For each framework, state what it predicts about the linguist’s justification and whether it treats the belief as reducible. Give your best guess at her actual verdict, with reasons. Finally, specify one further hypothetical case that would best discriminate between the two frameworks you have identified.
