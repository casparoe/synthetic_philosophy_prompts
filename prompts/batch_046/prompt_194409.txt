Write a short, philosophically dense dialogue between a proponent of Malcolm’s thesis that dreams are not genuine experiences—because they lack the logical conditions for observation, correction, and intersubjective verification that govern ordinary experience-reports—and a proponent of Dennett’s counter-position that dream reports are post-hoc theoretical narratives rather than direct descriptions of occurrent inner states, but that this narrative structure does not automatically exclude dreams from being experiences in a cognitive-functional sense. Centre the exchange on a specific hypothetical case: a sleeper reports having dreamt of tasting a chemical compound she has never encountered while awake, and concurrent polysomnography reveals no activation in gustatory-related cortical or peripheral pathways. The Malcolm-inspired speaker should argue that without any behavioural criterion or possibility of waking correction, the report fails to meet the grammar of an experience-claim and collapses into mere fiction. The Dennett-inspired speaker should reply that this absence of verification conditions demonstrates precisely that all experience-reports—including waking ones—are interpretation-laden theoretical constructions, and should then defend the further claim that dreams can still qualify as experiences if we reject Malcolm’s verificationist framework rather than retaining it. Both interlocutors must be equally competent, must respond directly to each other’s points rather than talking past one another, and the dialogue should contain no bibliographic references, citations, or appeals to textual authority.
