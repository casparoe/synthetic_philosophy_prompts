Please explain the following conceptual issue in Scanlon's contractualism, particularly as it relates to the aggregation problem.

Scanlon holds that an action is wrong if and only if it is permitted by principles that no one could reasonably reject as a basis for informed, unforced general agreement. He is well known for rejecting aggregative reasoning: he denies that we can justify imposing a severe burden on one person by appealing to the sum of small benefits distributed across many others.

However, consider the internal mechanics of the reasonable rejectability test itself. To determine whether person A can reasonably reject a principle P, we must ask whether A's burden under P is excessive relative to what is at stake for others if P is adopted. This comparison seems to require weighing A's individual cost against the collective costs that B, C, D, and others would bear if P were rejected. In other words, the very assessment of whether a rejection is reasonable appears to involve an implicit aggregation of benefits and burdens across all affected parties—call this justificatory aggregation.

The conceptual issue is whether this form of aggregation is genuinely avoidable within contractualism. Can the contractualist maintain that comparing burdens across persons in the rejectability test is categorically different from the first-order aggregation Scanlon rejects? Or does the framework necessarily rely on an aggregative calculus at the meta-level, thereby undermining its anti-aggregative credentials?
