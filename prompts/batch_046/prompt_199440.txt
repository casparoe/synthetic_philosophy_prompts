I am a retired physicist who has only recently begun reading philosophy of mind, and I am trying to apply the discipline of adversarial collaboration—common in my former field—to a conceptual problem that resists direct measurement. I am concerned with pain asymbolia: clinical situations in which a subject retains precise nociceptive discrimination—localizing stimuli, describing them with pain-vocabulary, and sometimes even initiating withdrawal—while reporting no suffering, no emotional aversion, and no motivation to avoid repetition. I want to understand whether such cases show that pain’s badness is intrinsic to its sensory quality or whether badness is a relational property that depends on the functional integration of that quality with a separate evaluative or homeostatic mechanism.

Please draft the joint statement that two opposing camps could both sign after a sustained, good-faith adversarial collaboration on this question. The camps are:

A. The Integrationist camp: Pain’s badness is constituted by the functional integration of nociceptive discrimination with an affective-evaluative mechanism; pain asymbolia demonstrates that when this integration is disrupted, the sensory state lacks badness altogether, rather than merely lacking conscious registration of a pre-existing badness.

B. The Intrinsic-Valence camp: The sensory component of pain carries intrinsic negative valence; pain asymbolia represents an access or disconnection failure in which the badness of the sensory state is present but not registered, experienced, or behaviorally expressed by the subject, analogous to subliminal perception of an aversive feature.

I take the central cruxes to be: (i) whether there is a coherent concept of an intrinsically bad sensory quality that can exist without being experienced or evaluated as bad; and (ii) whether predictive-interoceptive or error-correction frameworks for pain can accommodate intrinsic valence without collapsing the distinction between sensory discrimination and affective appraisal.
