And here's the student's answer:

Split-brain patients are usually discussed through the interpreter module — Sperry's discovery that the left hemisphere makes up reasons for actions it didn't initiate, like explaining a left-hand choice of a snow shovel by saying 'it's cold outside.' This shows narrative selfhood is stitched together after the fact, not the ground floor of experience.

So when we ask if there's one consciousness or two, we have to distinguish phenomenal continuity — whether there's a single 'what it's like' running through both sides — from access continuity, which is just whether information can cross between hemispheres. In these patients, access is clearly broken because the corpus callosum is cut. Phenomenal continuity is broken too, but only derivatively: since there's no access, the left side never gets the right side's content, so the subjective stream looks split. If we repaired the callosum, both would come back together, because phenomenal continuity is really just access continuity operating over time.

The contradiction objection — say the left reaches for tea and the right pushes the cup away — only works if we assume one agent can't hold opposite intentions. But that's too strong; people have conflicting desires all the time. So there's no need to postulate two subjects. In fact, Sperry showed the right hemisphere has no emotional or subjective life at all — it's basically a sophisticated reflex arc — so there's clearly just one conscious subject (the left) and an unconscious pilot on the right. Gazzaniga later added the interpreter idea to explain how that single subject maintains coherence despite the pilot's hidden actions.
