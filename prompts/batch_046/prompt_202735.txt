Here is an argument I constructed for a seminar on normative ethics, and I now find it suspicious because its conclusion seems to undermine the very position it starts from. (1) A genuinely deontological constraint is agent-relative: it binds an agent regardless of what others will do or suffer, because its authority derives from the nature of the act rather than from the aggregate outcome. (2) If this is correct, then in collective scenarios an agent's reason not to violate the constraint remains constant even when she knows with certainty that her single violation would prevent n other agents from being coerced into violating the same constraint. (3) Consider a "normative cascade" structure: agent A can stop a mechanism that will force agents B1 through Bn to perform action φ (which violates constraint C), but A can stop it only by performing φ herself. By (1) and (2), A is still forbidden to perform φ, no matter how large n becomes. (4) Now add a universalizability condition: if C is valid, then every rational agent must be able to will that the maxim "do not perform φ, even to prevent forced performances of φ" be adopted as universal law. (5) In the cascade structure, universal adoption of that maxim entails that A does not intervene, the mechanism proceeds, and B1 through Bn are all forced to violate C—so universal adherence yields n violations. (6) A principle that, when universalized in this structural context, necessarily maximizes the number of its own violations is not merely suboptimal; it is practically self-defeating in a way that undermines its own normative authority. (7) Therefore, deontology must reject either agent-relativity (1) or universalizability (4). (8) But if (1) is rejected, deontology collapses into a form of threshold-sensitive consequentialism; if (4) is rejected, the categorical structure of moral law is lost. I want to keep (1) if I have to choose, but I want to know exactly which step slips—whether the move from (4) to (5) confuses maxim-testing with outcome-prediction, whether (6) illicitly imports a consequentialist criterion of "self-defeat," or whether the cascade scenario itself smuggles in an assumption about moral responsibility for coercion that deontology should deny. Where does the argument fail, and what does repairing it cost?
