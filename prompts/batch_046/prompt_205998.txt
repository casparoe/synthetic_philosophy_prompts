I am preparing to teach a session that applies Kierkegaard's concept of the teleological suspension of the ethical to modern moral catastrophe, and I want to read Sophie's Choice—the novel and its well-known film adaptation—through that lens. In the story, Sophie, imprisoned in a concentration camp, is compelled by a guard to choose which of her two young children will be killed immediately; she selects her daughter Eva to live and her son Jan to die. The narrative then traces her lifelong inability to integrate that act, including scenes where she dissociates when attempting to speak of it. Read the work through Kierkegaard's framework: does Sophie's choice represent a teleological suspension of the ethical universal (the duty to protect both children) for a higher end? Crucially, there is no divine command or religious telos in the scene—only the arbitrary violence of the camp—so does this reveal a secular analogue of Abraham's trial, or does the absence of God expose a different structure altogether, perhaps a suspension that is not redeemed but permanently destructive? I want you to show where the philosophical idea illuminates the selection scene and Sophie's subsequent silence and self-punishment, and where the novel resists being reduced to an illustration—particularly because Sophie is not a knight of faith exercising freedom but a victim subjected to coercive power. Please separate what is well-established in Kierkegaard's own account of Abraham from interpretations that extend his concept to secular atrocity, and do not present contested extensions as if they were Kierkegaard's own view. Where the work adds something to the philosophical debate that argument alone cannot capture, please say so.
