I hold a specific variant of the hyperbolic-discounting explanation of weakness of will: not just that present rewards are overweighted relative to future ones, but that the mechanism predicts a structural asymmetry. Specifically, it should be relatively common and stable for an agent to abandon a long-term plan in favor of immediate gratification, yet comparatively rare and unstable for an agent to maintain a long-term plan against strong, available immediate gratification with a distant payoff—what we might call sustained "reverse weakness." I take this asymmetry to distinguish hyperbolic discounting from rival accounts that treat temporal bias as symmetric or as a failure of rational evaluation rather than of weighting. Tell me what would count against this combined position. I want the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward: formal or empirical findings that the predicted asymmetry does not hold in observed choice behavior; conceptual arguments that hyperbolic discounting cannot be isolated from judgment-shifting or preference reversal without circularity; and constructed or observed cases of stable reverse weakness that the view must either absorb by redefining "stability" or "weighting" or dismiss as anomalies. For each category, indicate whether a committed defender could concede it as damaging but survivable, or whether it undermines the core claim. Finally, assess honestly whether the position can be qualified post hoc into unfalsifiability—say, by treating any stable long-term adherence as hidden hyperbolic weighting in reverse—and if so, whether that insulation is a theoretical virtue or a fatal vice for an explanation in philosophy of action.
