I have just come out of a long, somewhat exhausting argument with a friend about whether Derek Parfit’s hitchhiker genuinely demonstrates rational irrationality, and I would like an outside adjudication of our exchange. We were not debating the case in the abstract; we were trying to see whether the standard defense of the hitchhiker’s refusal to call actually holds up once you press on the gap between strategic planning and present welfare, and once you take seriously the reductionist view of personal identity that Parfit himself defends.

Here is the back-and-forth as it actually unfolded. My friend began by defending the classic reading: it is rational ex ante to form the intention to drive past the phone booth without calling, because only a credible threat of death will induce the driver to rescue you; and it is rational ex post to execute that intention, because if your future self could defect whenever the stakes spike, no commitment would ever be credible, so you would never have been picked up in the first place—the death is simply the price of a credible signal. I countered that this collapses two different evaluations: it may be rational to plan to be stubborn, but once you are safely in the car, the strategic game is over—the driver cannot un-save you—so actually refusing to call is pure self-harm with no remaining instrumental value. At that point rationality should be evaluated by present preferences, not by loyalty to a plan formed under duress. My friend replied that this misses the observable-resolve problem: if you privately harbored a mental reservation that you would call once safe, that very reservation might undermine the sincerity of your commitment, making your signal detectable or at least corrupting your own confidence in it; hence genuine credibility requires actually carrying out the refusal. I then pushed back with what I take to be a deeper tension: my friend seems to be smuggling in a diachronic constitutional picture of the self, treating one’s temporal stages as citizens in a single life-democracy bound by precommitment rules against secession by the present self. But if we take reductionism seriously—if personal identity is just psychological continuity with degrees—there is no deep self whose integrity binds the present stage to the past deal. The earlier stage’s contract should have no authority over the later stage’s survival unless there is an independent reason, such as a promise made to others. Here the promise serves only the strategic purpose of rescue, and once that purpose is fulfilled, honoring it is just irrational self-sacrifice disguised as consistency.

We are stuck. Please go through our exchange round by round and say, at each step, who has the stronger argument and where each side is right or wrong. In particular, address whether the observable-resolve reply genuinely closes the gap between planning and execution, whether reductionism actually undermines commitment in the way I claimed, and whether there is any way to maintain that driving past the booth is rational without implicitly relying on a non-reductionist conception of personal integrity. Then tell me what, if anything, both of us are missing—perhaps some distinction between one-shot and iterated commitment contexts, or some feature of costly signaling that does not require actual death. Structure your response as follows: first, “Point-by-point adjudication” (approximately 400 words), walking through each round; second, “What both sides overlook” (approximately 200 words), identifying gaps or unstated assumptions; third, “Overall verdict” (approximately 150 words), stating who is more right overall and whether the hitchhiker succeeds as an example of rational irrationality. Please do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; I want an analysis that could stand on its own reasoning without reference to external texts.
