I have recently been appointed to advise a regional governance board that must aggregate judgments from hydrologists, local agriculturalists, and indigenous knowledge-keepers on water-allocation disputes, and I am now confronting a practical problem that standard treatments of the Condorcet jury theorem do not resolve. Once we abandon both voter independence and homogeneous competence—conditions that clearly fail here—the theorem offers little direct guidance on how to weight inputs, whether to delegate to a subset, or whether majoritarian aggregation even applies when participants do not share a single factual target. If I could commit a small conceptual research group for approximately two years to open questions in this domain, which lines of inquiry should take precedence, in what order, and according to what criteria? I am less interested in an exhaustive survey than in a defended agenda: for each proposed priority, explain why it is upstream of practical institutional design choices, how tractable it is for philosophical or formal analysis rather than empirical fieldwork, and what result or argument over the next eighteen months would cause me to revise the ranking. Among the candidates, I am particularly uncertain about three: first, whether there is a normatively defensible method for competence-weighted aggregation when errors are systematically correlated across groups; second, whether Condorcet-style reasoning can justify selecting a deliberative subset by lot rather than consulting the full board; and third, whether the theorem's epistemic justification collapses when voters hold incommensurable values about risk and fairness rather than convergent beliefs about a single state of affairs. Please rank these—or substitute others if warranted—defend the ordering with explicit criteria such as tractability, neglectedness, and dependency relations among questions, and specify what evidence or conceptual breakthrough would move a lower-ranked item to the top.
