I am a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I am currently working with an editor on a feature about how historical narratives are constructed; in the course of my reporting, I have begun consulting with a philosophy of history professor to design the final examination for an upper-level graduate seminar on historical ontology. We want to avoid the standard exam format in which students simply rehearse whether Petrarch invented the Renaissance or whether the Scientific Revolution was merely a historiographical myth. You should approach this task as an expert historian of science and professional philosopher of history. Before you begin drafting, please take a moment to think carefully and do some brief brainstorming: identify the core logical tension between treating historical periods as explanatory kinds that carve nature at its joints versus treating them as retrospective narrative devices, and consider how one might construct a "weakened" period concept that retains predictive utility for institutional history without committing to essentialist boundaries.

Please write a 90-minute essay examination question, worth 25 marks, that asks students to evaluate the ontological status of the categories "Middle Ages," "Renaissance," and "Scientific Revolution." Do not request bibliographies, reading lists, or citations from the student; the answer must stand on conceptual argument alone. To keep the question from being answerable by recitation, inject the following niche framing: ask students to address the "explanatory asymmetry problem"—the observation that period-bound explanations tend to succeed for institutional and curricular histories (for example, the continuity of university charter structures across the fourteenth and fifteenth centuries) but fail systematically for practice-based histories (such as alchemical or medical traditions that persist across conventional boundaries). The question should require students to determine whether this asymmetry undermines the reality of the periods themselves, or whether it merely demonstrates that different historiographical objects demand different temporal granularities. In particular, students should be forced to apply this distinction to the specific case of the collapse of Aristotelian natural philosophy in the seventeenth century versus the simultaneous persistence of scholastic logical methods within the same temporal window.

Along with the question, provide a model answer of approximately 700 words that demonstrates the moves a top student must make: distinguishing "strong ontological periodization," "heuristic scaffolding," and "granularity-sensitive explanation"; showing that the asymmetry is an epistemic limitation rather than an ontological refutation; avoiding the collapse of all three periods into a single undifferentiated argument; and specifying what a genuinely "real" period boundary would have to predict that these conventional categories actually fail to predict. Then provide a marking scheme that separates an A answer from a B answer by naming the precise conceptual moves required—particularly the identification of the asymmetry, the differentiated treatment of the three periods, and the explicit discussion of predictive failure—together with a brief note on the typical mistake that costs marks at each stage. All of your output should be in American English.
