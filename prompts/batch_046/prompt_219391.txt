As a retired physicist who has only recently begun auditing courses in philosophy of science and the history of economic thought, I am drafting a short essay for a local discussion group on whether Thomas Malthus’s population principle can be judged “right but early” in any philosophically interesting sense, rather than simply wrong and subsequently rescued by the demographic transition. Instead of rehearsing that familiar narrative, I want to examine whether there exists a coherent category for theories whose core mechanism is structurally sound yet observationally falsified within any human-scale temporal window, only to remain compatible with data over civilizational or geological durations. To that end, I propose a tentative taxonomy of long-run predictive rescue that distinguishes three strategies: (i) auxiliary adjustment, in which peripheral parameters are modified to preserve the core law; (ii) domain restriction, in which the conditions of applicability are narrowed post hoc; and (iii) asymptotic convergence, in which the system is genuinely approaching the predicted limit but is currently deflected by transient dynamics that the original formulation could not model. I apply this framework to Malthus by arguing that the arithmetic-geometric divergence describes an asymptotic attractor rather than an imminent crisis, so that modern demographic data do not so much falsify the mechanism as reveal an extended transient—though I worry this risks collapsing into mere postponement of judgment rather than genuine theoretical vindication. I also draw a cautious analogy to long-range climate projections, not to borrow scientific authority, but to ask whether our norms of falsification should be scaled to the characteristic relaxation time of the system in question, and whether a theory can be rationally retained when its predicted equilibrium lies beyond any feasible observational horizon.

I would welcome focused comments on the conceptual architecture rather than on historical scholarship: specifically, does the distinction between domain restriction and asymptotic convergence survive scrutiny, or does it collapse once we allow that any falsified long-range prediction can be redescribed as delayed convergence? I am also uncertain whether my framing tacitly commits me to scientific realism about economic mechanisms in a way that a more instrumentalist philosophy of science would reject, and I would value an informal assessment of whether the argument depends on that commitment. Furthermore, I worry that comparing Malthus to dynamical-systems language introduces an anachronism that undermines coherence, since he possessed no formal notion of transient regimes or relaxation times; if that comparison is illegitimate, I would like to know where exactly the reasoning breaks down. Please feel free to identify where the essay sounds like a regurgitation of standard Popperian or Lakatosian positions, and where it genuinely requires sharper distinctions or abandonment. I have deliberately avoided requesting bibliographies, reading lists, or formal citations, as my present aim is to test internal logical consistency before seeking authoritative grounding; if a particular philosopher or economist has addressed this precise taxonomy, an informal mention would be appreciated, but it is not required.
