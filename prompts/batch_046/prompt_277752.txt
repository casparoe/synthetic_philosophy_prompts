I have been asked to deliver the opening remarks at a workshop on secondary curriculum design that I am co-organising with colleagues from both philosophy of education and classroom practice. The audience will be mixed: several Whitehead scholars who will bristle at mischaracterisation, a larger contingent of teachers and policy analysts who have never engaged with process philosophy, and graduate students who appear to think that process philosophy is a branch of agile project management. I want the speech to do something structurally awkward. I want Whitehead’s tripartite rhythm of education — romance, precision, and generalisation — to function as the reason I can and cannot make certain claims about assessment and measurement. Specifically, the talk must explain why treating examination results as inert ideas, that is, as accumulated facts without living use, is not merely a pedagogical mistake but a metaphysical error that repeats the fallacy of misplaced concreteness by treating dynamic processes as static, finished entities. The remarks should therefore refuse to present best practices as a checklist, reject the language of delivering outcomes, and frame the workshop itself as an exercise in keeping ideas alive. At the same time, it must not become a sermon or a lecture in the history of ideas; it should feel like an invitation that happens to be philosophically rigorous.

I have spent too much time on rationalist forums watching people treat educational debates as optimisation problems — maximising scores, minimising variance — without noticing that they have smuggled in an ontology of static entities. Whitehead’s view, as I understand it, is that education is not the transmission of completed knowledge but a disciplined adventure of ideas moving through successive stages of freedom and discipline. So the speech must navigate between the experts, who will recognise the references to his metaphysics of becoming, and the practitioners, who need to hear why this matters for Monday morning’s lesson planning. The tension should be explicit: for the scholars, the workshop’s emphasis on open-ended discussion might feel like a regression from precision back into romance; for the teachers, it might feel like an indulgence. The talk needs to defend that regression — or rather, that periodic return — as essential, arguing that without it even specialised knowledge becomes inert.

Could you write this speech? It should run to roughly six or eight minutes when spoken, address the room directly, and allow Whitehead’s philosophy to do real structural work: shaping what can and cannot be said about student performance, about measurement, and about the purpose of our gathering. Please do not include a bibliography, reading list, or citations. Do not quote Whitehead verbatim; paraphrase in your own language. And please inject one somewhat niche framing: treat the workshop itself as an instance of the romance stage for its participants, acknowledging that for experts this may feel like a retreat from precision, but arguing that without such periodic returns to romance, specialised knowledge itself becomes inert. The tone should be formal but not pompous, written in British English, and suitable for a room in which at least half the audience will be checking their phones.
