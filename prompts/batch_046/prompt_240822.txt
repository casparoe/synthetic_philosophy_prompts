I have recently retired from a career in experimental physics and, over the past year, have been reading systematically in philosophy of law and rational choice theory. In a reading group, I sketched an argument about self-amending normative systems that now strikes me as suspicious because its conclusion seems to swallow not only constitutional law but scientific methodology and moral reasoning as well. I want to identify the precise misstep—whether it is an equivocation, a hidden scope shift, or a modal slide—and understand what I must pay to reject it, since I am strongly inclined to preserve the intuition that a rational system ought to be able to correct even its own foundational rules.

Consider a normative system S governed by a revision rule R that authorizes amendment of any other rule within S; call this the openness condition. The argument proceeds as follows. (1) If S lacks such an R, then S is permanently hostage to errors in its unrevisable parts, so rationality requires R. (2) If R belongs to S, then R applies to itself, producing either an amended R' or a prohibition on self-amendment. (3) If full self-amendability holds, then every revision criterion is itself revisable, generating a vicious regress: no stable authority can govern the process of correction. (4) To block this regress, S must include an entrenchment clause E that removes R from R's scope, rendering R unamendable. (5) But then E is either within R's scope—reinstating the regress—or outside it, which means S contains at least one rule that is immune to rational correction. (6) Hence every rational system either lacks a stable revision authority or harbors an irrevisable dogma. (7) This structural dilemma generalizes beyond law to scientific paradigms, ethical frameworks, and logical calculi: each must either tolerate perpetual criterion-instability or accept an uncriticizable core. (8) Applied reflexively, the reasoning in (1)–(6) relies on logical principles that, by (7), must themselves either be unstable or dogmatic; therefore the argument undermines its own authority. (9) Consequently, there is no coherent conception of rational self-correction.

I find (1) compelling—it echoes the idea that rational commitment requires openness to revision—and I am reluctant to surrender (7), since the generalization to science and morality seems illuminating rather than absurd. Yet (9) is intolerable: it cannot be that rationality is conceptually impossible. Where exactly does the chain break? Is the error in (4), which assumes that blocking regress requires an explicit entrenchment clause rather than a hierarchical distinction between object-level and meta-level rules? Or does the flaw lie in (3), conflating the logical possibility of amendment with an actual infinite sequence of amendments? If I reject (4) and allow R to apply to itself without an explicit E, do I merely trade dogmatism for instability, or is there a third option—perhaps a contextual or procedural stability—that saves both openness and coherence? Please diagnose the weakest link, explain why it initially appeared sound, and indicate what each available repair costs in terms of the commitments I most want to keep.
