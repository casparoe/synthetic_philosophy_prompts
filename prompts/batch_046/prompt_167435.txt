You are a professional philosopher of art and aesthetics with expertise in authenticity, value theory, and institutional judgment. Before answering, take a moment to brainstorm: list at least three distinct principles that could explain the pattern of verdicts below, note where they overlap and where they diverge, and think step by step rather than rushing to a single conclusion.

Consider the ten-year track record of a major museum’s Authentication and Acquisition Board. They have rendered the following judgments:

1. They classified a painting executed almost entirely by a master painter’s apprentice, using period-appropriate materials in the master’s own studio, as an authentic original—provided the master personally signed and authorized its sale.

2. They rejected as non-original a mechanically reproduced image assembled from the actual chemical pigments recovered from a destroyed original painting, arranged by algorithmic guidance according to the original brushstroke data; despite being materially continuous with the destroyed work, they cataloged it as a reconstruction, not an original.

3. They affirmed that a rediscovered canvas with approximately 40 percent of its surface replaced by modern restoration materials—but with the remaining 60 percent chemically verified as the artist’s own hand—remains an original work of the artist, albeit with diminished curatorial and market value.

4. They ruled that when a living artist creates an exact duplicate of one of their own earlier works using identical techniques and materials, and signs it with a new date, the result is a new, independent original—not a forgery of the first—though it does not share the historical identity of the earlier object.

Now the board faces a new proposal: an estate-authorized “counterfactual original” executed in 2024. A research team used only materials and techniques documented in a deceased artist’s studio notebooks, depicted a subject the artist sketched but never executed, and employed robotic arms calibrated to the artist’s documented biomechanical brushstroke patterns. Every pigment is chemically identical to those in verified works. The estate has assigned it a catalog number in the artist’s complete works catalog, but the artist never touched, saw, or intended this specific object.

If the board wants to remain consistent with itself, what should it decide—original, forgery, reconstruction, authorized replica, or a new category? Which of their four earlier verdicts is the closest analog, and does their track record genuinely underdetermine the outcome? Identify the competing principles (for example, causal-authorship, material-procedural continuity, aesthetic-experiential identity, institutional authorization) that fit all four earlier cases equally well but diverge on this new one. Give your best-guess recommendation with reasons, and state explicitly what principle the board would be adopting with each possible decision. If the precedents do underdetermine it, name one further hypothetical case—different in kind from the four above—that would most sharply distinguish between the two leading principles you identified.
