Consider the historian and sociologist Ibn Khaldun, whose cyclical theory of civilizational rise and decline in the Muqaddimah hinges on asabiyyah—collective group solidarity that strengthens a dynasty until luxury erodes it, producing predictable generational phases. Now suppose he encountered the modern historiographical and scientific claim that macrohistorical trajectories are chaos-sensitive: that nonlinear institutional feedback means microscopic perturbations—a delayed courier, a mistranslated clause, a localized weather event—can cascade into radically different macro-outcomes, making long-range prediction structurally impossible. Would Khaldun treat this chaos-sensitivity as mere noise that averages out across the generational timescales of his cycles, or as a fundamental dissolution of deterministic civilizational phases? More specifically, how might he reconcile his substantive sociology of power with findings from agent-based models of institutional collapse, which show that macro-patterns can depend exquisitely on micro-level contingent events that carry no collective social meaning whatsoever? Address whether nonlinear contingency is compatible with his framework, and identify precisely where in his theory of state formation and decay the tension would be sharpest.
