I've been following discussions of political ethics on rationalist-adjacent forums for years, and I've noticed a recurring argumentative pattern around what philosophers call the dirty-hands problem—though I'm not asking about that concept itself. The pattern usually shows up after a leader takes an action that seems clearly wrong by ordinary moral standards—approving surveillance that violates privacy norms, ordering an intervention that predictably harms civilians—to avert what is framed as a larger catastrophe. The first response is familiar: defenders say the leader had no clean option and bears a real moral stain; critics say this is just corruption dressed up as necessity.

But there's a third move that keeps appearing, and I don't know whether it has a precise name. The defender accepts, in the abstract, that politics sometimes forces people to do wrong for right reasons. Yet when applied to the specific case, the moral stain is not carried but dissolved. The defender insists that because the decision was genuinely necessary and the outcome was good, there was never a true moral conflict—only a hard choice, not a dirty one. The language of tragedy is borrowed for rhetorical weight, but its cost is refused: there is no lingering guilt, no heightened burden of justification, no acknowledgment that the leader is now morally different from someone who never had to make such a choice. At the same time, any criticism of the execution—the particular civilians harmed, the particular rights violated—is dismissed as failing to understand that crisis leadership requires breaking ordinary rules. So the argument simultaneously claims the moral authority of having done something terrible and denies that anything terrible was actually done.

I've seen this in debates about emergency powers, about collateral damage in military action, and about institutional deception for public health ends. It feels different from standard dirty-hands reasoning, which I understand to accept the stain, and different from straightforward consequentialist denial, which never grants the stain in the first place. It also feels more specific than generic rationalization or moral self-licensing, because it depends on maintaining the framing of crisis and necessity as a permanent exemption rather than a one-time excuse.

Does this configuration have an established name in political philosophy or moral psychology? If so, how exactly does it differ from the standard dirty-hands position, from moral remainder, and from simple post-hoc consequentialism? If there is no precise term, I would value a coined concept with clear boundary conditions: what distinguishes it from genuine acceptance of moral cost on one side, and from ordinary hypocrisy or motte-and-bailey on the other?
