Here is an argument I built around the absent-minded driver problem, but shifted from decision theory to the philosophy of action, and I now find it suspicious because its conclusion dissolves ordinary agency.

(1) For an action to count as genuinely intentional, the agent must possess an occurrent, accessible mental representation of the specific action being executed at the moment of execution; a merely dispositional tendency or background habit is insufficient.

(2) In the classic absent-minded driver scenario, the agent takes the correct exit not through any present guiding thought—he has forgotten his plan and is operating on autopilot—so his taking that exit lacks the required occurrent representation.

(3) Therefore the driver’s correct exit is not an intentional action but a kind of skilled reflex, structurally analogous to post-hypnotic behavior.

(4) The same structure governs most routine skilled actions—typing familiar words, walking one’s own neighborhood, playing a rehearsed scale—where execution is driven by procedural memory rather than by an explicit, present intention.

(5) So the vast majority of everyday conduct is not intentional in the philosophically relevant sense.

(6) But moral responsibility, practical reasoning, and the distinction between action and mere happening apply only to genuinely intentional conduct.

(7) Therefore we are rarely responsible for routine behavior, practical reasoning is largely a fiction about exceptional moments, and the concept of agency collapses into occasional conscious intervention.

That conclusion is absurd; I would prefer to keep (1) if I can, but I am willing to pay a cost elsewhere. Where does the argument slip?
