Hume's position in Of the Standard of Taste starts from the claim that beauty and deformity are not properties like shape or weight, but sentiments caused by objects in a perceiver. Because sentiments vary, it looks like subjectivism is unavoidable. To avoid this, Hume introduces the figure of the true judge: someone with delicacy of taste, extensive practice, the ability to compare works, and freedom from prejudice. These capacities don't detect an objective property, but they refine the sentiment so that it tracks features of the object that naturally produce pleasure in human beings generally.

Applying this to the shrine versus chapel case, both cultures can cultivate true judges. The Shinto gardener might value emptiness and natural asymmetry, while the Baroque critic values complexity and dramatic contrast. Hume would say that if both have genuinely refined taste, neither can be dismissed as wrong. However, he avoids pure relativism by appealing to the principle of utility: ornamented spaces often serve communal and religious functions more effectively, and this fitness contributes to the sentiment of beauty. So while both judgments are valid, the Baroque preference might be ranked higher because its objects are more useful, which strengthens the corresponding sentiment.

If, despite all correction, two true judges still disagree, Hume's fallback is that the judgment endorsed by the majority of refined critics within each culture determines the correct sentiment for that culture. This doesn't collapse into subjectivism because the correction mechanisms — practice, comparison, freedom from prejudice — are universal, even if the final verdict is locally determined. Thus Hume allows cultural variation without giving up the idea that taste can be improved and corrected.
