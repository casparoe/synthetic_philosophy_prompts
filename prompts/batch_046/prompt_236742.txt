Write an essay evaluating whether the conception of the self as a center of narrative gravity can bear the weight of moral responsibility, or whether it dissolves into a convenient fiction that undermines accountability. Begin by outlining the core idea: that a self is not a Cartesian substance but an abstract organizational center around which autobiographical events cluster, analogous to a center of gravity in physics. Then weigh the principal objections: that such a center is merely a fictional construct with no causal power; that narrative coherence can be too easily revised, allowing agents to escape blame by rewriting their story; and that without substrate continuity there is no genuine persistence required for responsibility. Against these, present the defense that patterns can be fully real and causally efficacious even when they are not localized in a particular substance, and that self-revision is constrained by the need for intersubjective coherence and behavioral continuity.

Next, introduce a constructed pressure case: consider a hypothetical being—whether a split-brain patient with fully developed hemispheric narratives or an artificial system with dual non-integrated autobiographical modules—who maintains two equally coherent, competing narrative centers, neither of which dominates or integrates the other, and with no higher-order mechanism to adjudicate between them. Apply the narrative-gravity framework to this being. Does it provide non-circular criteria for identifying one center as the true self, or does it force us to treat both as partial selves? Can responsibility be distributed, fragmented, or voided without collapsing into arbitrariness?

Reach and defend a specific conclusion: either that narrative gravity survives this test with appropriate modifications, that it fails and must be supplemented by a substrate or causal-continuity condition, or that it reveals moral responsibility itself to be a distributed, graded phenomenon. Do not end non-committally.
