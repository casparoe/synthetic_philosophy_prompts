I sit on the evaluation panel for a national arts-funding body's "Radical Innovation" grant program. Our rubric requires applicants to score well on two mandatory axes: complete originality with no detectable debt to prior artistic traditions, and demonstrated mastery of canonical techniques in the medium. We routinely reject proposals that are merely derivative, yet we also reject work that lacks technical fluency in established forms. This strikes me as contradictory—how can one simultaneously break from tradition and master it? What implicit theory of creativity and artistic value does our rubric presuppose? Is it compatible with Douglas Hofstadter's view that creativity is essentially knob-twiddling—making structured variations on existing conceptual themes? Please reconstruct the theory our practice actually relies on, distinguish what it strictly requires from what it merely accommodates, say whether Hofstadter's model can make sense of both axes, and tell me what our evaluation criteria would have to change if we adopted that model as our explicit framework. If the contradiction I perceive is only apparent, or if applying Hofstadter's model to institutional grant evaluation is misguided, say so plainly rather than forcing a reconciliation.
