Two of my postgrads got stuck in the common room after seminar — Newton/Leibniz, Darwin/Wallace. They wanted me to referee but I was half-way through my coffee and dodged it. Here's the exchange, can you adjudicate it move by move?

A (Priya) said if calculus and natural selection were genuinely inevitible — if anyone with the right data and training would have hit on them eventually — then asking 'who discovered it?' is like asking which snowflake started the avalanche. The credit is purely conventional; we could have named the laws after Leibniz or Wallace and nothing about the science itself would change.

B (Tom) pushed back that this misses path-dependence. Even if the endpoint was inevitible, the route shapes what comes after. Leibniz's notation (dx/dy) made certain kinds of maths easier; Newton's fluxions pulled in a different direction. Darwin's long delay meant he bundled selection with domestic breeding and gradualism, whereas Wallace's essay was sharper but thinner — and that packaging influenced biology for the next century. So the 'who' isn't arbitrary: they determine the trajectory, not just the destination.

Priya came back that this just relocates importance from discovery to presentation, but if presentation was also inevitible — if Leibniz's notation was the only one that could have worked given the maths available — then we're back to inevitability. She said Tom was smuggling in false contigency: either the formulation was necessary (and then who writes it doesn't matter) or it was arbitrary (and then it's not deep).

Tom said that's a false dichotomy. The formulation might be one of several stable attractors, and which one gets locked in depends on who writes it down first and with what institutional weight behind them. Newton had Cambridge and the Royal Society; Darwin had the Beagle network and Lyell. The contigency is real at the level of social-scientific path-dependence, even if the underlying law was waiting.

They asked me who's right about what, and whether simultaneity actually undermines individual genius. I honestly couldn't tell. Can you go through their exchange point by point — rate each move from clearly mistaken to clearly right, say what both are missing, and tell me who is more right overall? Don't just say 'it depends'; I want an actual verdict, even if it's messy.
