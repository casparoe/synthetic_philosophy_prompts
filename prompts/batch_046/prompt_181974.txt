I serve on the oversight panel of a small international development fund, and I have no formal training in ethics, law, or philosophy, though I have been thinking about this problem on my own for some weeks. We are about to approve a partnership with a local cooperative in a region where presenting substantial ceremonial gifts before any formal negotiation is standard practice, understood locally as building mutual obligation rather than as a transactional bribe. One faction on our panel insists that accepting these gifts violates our charter’s anti-corruption clause, which was drafted according to Western legal norms; another faction argues that enforcing that clause here amounts to imposing an alien moral framework and dismissing a legitimate custom that functions as social glue rather than as corruption. Having reflected on this at length, I have come to wonder whether the crucial issue is not the material value of the gift itself but a particular kind of social expectation—what I have been calling “indebtedness without contract,” a state in which reciprocity is anticipated and felt as binding even though no explicit favour has been requested or promised. Could you please draft the brief written opinion I should circulate before Thursday’s meeting? It ought to recommend a definite course of action—approve the partnership with a revised gift protocol, reject it outright, or approve it subject to specific conditions—and it must treat the strongest argument against your recommendation with genuine fairness rather than dismissal. The reasoning should be practical enough that fellow panel members, none of whom are academics, can apply it directly to the decision at hand.
