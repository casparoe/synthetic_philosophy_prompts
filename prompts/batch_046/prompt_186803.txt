Read Cormac McCarthy's The Road through the lens of debates over when the Anthropocene begins and who holds the authority to declare its boundary. The novel depicts a post-catastrophic world without ever identifying the cause—whether climatic, nuclear, or biological—leaving the landscape as pure aftermath. Use that refusal to illuminate the problem of stratigraphic legibility: geologists typically seek a globally synchronous golden spike, yet proposed start points range from the Columbian Exchange (the Orbis hypothesis) to the industrial revolution and the post-war Great Acceleration, each leaving a different material signature. Consider whether the father and son's journey functions as a kind of walking core sample: they move through ruined infrastructure whose original purpose is unreadable, encountering traces that might support any of these chronologies but confirm none. Does the novel thereby take an implicit position—perhaps that human technological agency has been erased so thoroughly that only a pre-industrial or post-industrial marker could remain legible? Or does it resist periodization altogether by rendering the collapse both instantaneous and eternal, as if the Anthropocene were not a dated epoch but an unending condition? Examine specific scenes—such as the encounter with the underground bunker stocked with preserved goods, or the repeated image of ash falling like snow—to ask whether they dramatize a particular kind of geological trace, and whether that trace favors one disciplinary claimant (geologist, historian, climate scientist) over another. Finally, say what this literary treatment contributes that a purely technical argument about boundary selection cannot: does living inside an unnamed end supply a moral or epistemic reason to care who decides the start, and does the novel's silence about causation suggest that the authority to name the Anthropocene is itself part of the human power it seeks to date?
