Me and my flatmate had this absolutly exhausting argument last night about whether genuinely nasty art can still be brilliant—she kept saying separating the work from the artist is just a cop-out, and I ended up pushing this view that's a bit stronger than the usual line about the author being dead. Basically I reckon that for certain works the moral corruption isn't just separable from the aesthetic value; it's actually doing structual work inside the piece. The reason a particular novel or film feels formally powerful, on my account, is precisely because it refuses to condemn its own cruelty—if you edited out the moral danger you'd collapse the tension that makes the form hold together. So it's not beautiful despite being immoral, but partly beautiful because the immorality functions as a kind of formal prerequisite, like fuel for the engine. I'm not saying every bad painting is secretly good, just that there's a specific category where moral risk is structurally necessary.

But I'm worried I'm just rationalising my own taste here. So play it straight: what would actually count against this? I want the evidence or arguments that should genuinely move me—are there clear cases where removing the moral poison left the formal architecture intact? Could formal analysis show the aesthetic mechanism never actually depended on the immoral content in the first place? And if defenders retreat to "it only works with ironic framing," has the view been qualified into unfalsifiability, and is that a problem for a claim like this? I also want to know about rival explanations—maybe a purely formalist account—that describe the same phenomena without letting morality into the aesthetic evaluation at all. Be honest about how exposed this position actually is: what does it risk, where is it well-insulated, and is that insulation a virtue or a vice?
