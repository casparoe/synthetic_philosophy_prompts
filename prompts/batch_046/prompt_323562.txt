Zhuangzi's account of death is frequently reconstructed through the imagery of transformation (hua) and the well-known parable in which he questions whether he is a man who has dreamed he is a butterfly or a butterfly now dreaming he is a man. Interpreters commonly take this to entail that death is not annihilation but a change of form analogous to waking from sleep, which seems to presuppose that some continuous substrate—whether personal consciousness, vital energy, or an underlying self—persists through the transition. Yet this reading sits uneasily with Zhuangzi's practice of fasting the heart (xin zhai), through which one empties oneself of all fixed perspectives and dissolves the boundaries of personal identity. If the transformation model requires a bearer that undergoes change, while the fasting-of-the-heart model precisely eliminates any such bearer, does Zhuangzi's philosophy of death collapse into contradiction? Or should we understand death, not as an event experienced by a persisting subject, but as the complete dissolution of the life-death distinction itself, rendering the demand for a substrate a category mistake imported from a standpoint that Zhuangzi's own practice has already abandoned?
