My closest friend manages an independent bookstore that belongs to a fifteen-member citywide cooperative. The members pool funds for a shared marketing campaign and maintain a joint customer-referral ledger: when a shopper asks for a title that is out of stock, the bookseller directs them to a partner store and logs the referral. The group also established informal but explicit baseline norms—paid staff only, no shifts past midnight, and no discounting bestsellers below the publisher’s suggested price.

Six months ago, one member began keeping her shop open until two in the morning on weekends, staffing it with unpaid relatives, and selling bestsellers at thirty percent below the agreed floor. Because the cooperative’s shared referral app lists real-time “open now” status and aggregates customer reviews, her visibility spiked; shoppers began praising her “flexibility,” and referral traffic flowed disproportionately to her store. Two other members have since adopted the same practices. My friend is now losing both direct sales and logged referrals. The other booksellers privately complain about exhaustion and eroding margins, yet none have reverted to the original norms.

My friend wants to hold firm—fair wages, reasonable hours, standard pricing—because she believes the cooperative’s long-term credibility and her own sustainability depend on it. But she is unsure whether she is simply refusing to adapt to a new competitive reality, or whether there is a structural form to these dynamics that explains why well-meaning, rational people keep reproducing an outcome they all dislike. She asked me to help her think through whether her stance is naive business judgment, or whether she is caught in something with a recognizable shape that makes individual defection nearly inevitable regardless of personal values. How should I advise her?
