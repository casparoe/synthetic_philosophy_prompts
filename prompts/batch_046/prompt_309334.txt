I serve on the ethics review committee for a municipal public-health authority. Following a rather fractious preliminary discussion last Tuesday, we must now issue a formal advisory memo on a proposal for a digital “soft default” intervention embedded within the city’s housing-benefits portal. Under the scheme, any resident applying for housing support would be automatically enrolled in a fortnightly mental-health check-in unless they successfully navigate through three successive interface screens—each using ambiguous iconography rather than explicit textual labels—to opt out. The design team defends this as a permissible behavioural nudge, arguing that transparency about the opt-out route would defeat the programme’s welfare purpose; however, half the committee regards the deliberate obscurity as a form of deceptive manipulation that violates the conditions of genuine autonomy-preserving choice architecture. We need a clear recommendation—approval, rejection, or conditional approval with enforceable safeguards—together with reasoning that the portal designers could actually respond to, rather than purely abstract philosophical declarations.

The memo should address specifically whether the intentional concealment of the opt-out path transforms libertarian paternalism into an impermissible infringement of informed agency, and it must treat honestly the strongest consideration on the losing side: namely, that vulnerable applicants facing housing insecurity may lack the cognitive bandwidth or digital literacy to discover hidden mechanisms, which makes the obscurity not merely a gentle push but a structurally coercive design feature. At the same time, the memo should acknowledge the force of the opposing argument—that if mental-health monitoring genuinely prevents severe harm, and if explicit disclosure causes near-universal opt-out that renders the programme useless, then some degree of opacity might be justified as a proportionate means of protecting welfare without removing the formal option to decline.

Please draft this as a formal institutional document, with an actionable bottom line in the opening paragraph and a section outlining concrete conditions—such as mandatory textual labelling, independent usability testing with low-literacy users, and a published transparency report—should the recommendation be conditional approval. Avoid appeals to bibliographies or theoretical authorities that cannot be verified from first principles; the reasoning should stand on its own, and it should not request citations, reading lists, or references to specific texts.
