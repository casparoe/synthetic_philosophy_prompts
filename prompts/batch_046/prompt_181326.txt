I sit on the state residential-care ethics board at Oak Ridge. We're deciding on a pilot program called Structured Serenity that basically removes elective choice for residents—meals, schedules, activities get set by biometric feedback loops—with an opt-out that's technically legal but administratively buried. The facility's data shows anxiety way down and self-reported contentment up. Some staff are citing Dostoevsky's Grand Inquisitor: people don't actually want freedom, they want bread and certainty, and forcing choice on them is cruelty. Others say that's just a fancy rationalization for control.

The board needs a concrete reccomendation—approve with conditions, reject, or approve for 90 days—before next Friday's vote. Can you draft the decison memo? It should take the Inquisitor's argument seriously, not just dismiss it as authoritarian, and engage the idea that freedom can be an ontological burden that produces guilt and paralysis rather than flourishing. At the same time, it should ask whether the program eliminates mere first-order options or whether it also wipes out second-order identification with one's life—like, is there still space for residents to see themselves as authors even if the menu is small? Include an honest treatment of the strongest argument for the losing side, and recommend a specific course with a time-frame. Don't just restate patient-autonomy statutes.
