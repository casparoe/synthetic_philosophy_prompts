I serve on a municipal reconciliation panel in a region emerging from prolonged conflict. A former commander of a local armed group has offered to reveal the locations of unmarked burial sites, but only on condition that the panel formally records his actions as politically motivated rather than criminal, and that survivors waive civil reparations claims against his family estate. The families of the disappeared are divided: some regard the proposed record as a moral insult that taints the recovery of remains; others maintain that without disclosure the graves will remain lost. The panel holds no criminal jurisdiction; it may issue symbolic recognitions and recommend allocations from a limited municipal reparations fund. I am weighing a restructuring of the offer, under which disclosure would be contingent not on the symbolic record, but on a sustained restorative obligation — namely, that he and his family maintain the memorial grounds and undertake annual commemorative labour. I feel this relocates rather than resolves the tension between recovering truth and avoiding legitimation, yet I cannot pinpoint the exact philosophical difficulty. Please identify the underlying conceptual structure without initially applying standard labels; distinguish the normative considerations that govern conditional amnesty, restorative obligations, and the separation of truth-recovery from moral legitimation; and advise whether my restructuring genuinely addresses the problem or merely disguises it. I am not seeking mediation techniques, but a rigorous assessment of whether the intuition that the symbolic clause is the crux is defensible, and of what is gained or lost by substituting labour for language.
