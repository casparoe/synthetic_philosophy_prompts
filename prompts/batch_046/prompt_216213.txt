I have noticed a recurring pattern in debates over the authority of advance directives, particularly when a directive was drafted well before a significant medical innovation. Consider a patient who, in 2010, signed a document refusing "mechanical ventilation and other artificial life-sustaining measures if I am permanently unconscious with no hope of recovery." Today, that same patient is conscious but requires a novel respiratory intervention—say, an implantable bio-artificial lung that involves no external tubes, no traditional ventilation, and eliminates the kind of machine dependency the patient explicitly feared—while still facing a prognosis that, in 2010, would have been tied to unconsciousness and intubation.

The standard frameworks seem to miss the point. Appealing to "precedent autonomy" treats the past self's values as binding, but here the values—avoiding dependency on burdensome machinery while unconscious—may still be intact; what has collapsed is the contingent empirical premise that linked those values to the specific refusal. Treating the directive as ambiguous or vague also fails, because the language is clear enough; the problem is not interpretive uncertainty but technological novelty that falsifies the factual bridge embedded in the directive's practical reasoning. Meanwhile, a pure "best interests" analysis for the present patient seems to discard the directive without explaining why this case differs from one where the patient's values genuinely changed.

Is there an established concept or theoretical framework in bioethics or philosophy of medicine for this specific failure mode—where an advance directive's normative authority is destabilized not by a shift in values, not by linguistic ambiguity, and not by a simple best-interests override, but by the falsification of an essential factual premise that mediated between the patient's values and the refused intervention? If the literature does not contain a precise term, can you coin and define one that clearly distinguishes it from precedent-autonomy collapse, interpretive ambiguity, and best-interests substitution, and specify the conditions—such as the premise being explicit versus implicit, or being medical versus personal—under which the directive should be treated as partially or wholly inapplicable?
