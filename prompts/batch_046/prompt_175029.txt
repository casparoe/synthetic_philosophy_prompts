hey, i've been wrestling with subsidiarity in federal systems and my roommate walked me into this argument that seems to prove it either means nothing or means anarchism. can you help me find the exit?

(1) Subsidiarity says authority should sit at the lowest level that can actually do the job.
(2) "Actually do the job" has to mean "achieve the goal at least as well as any higher level could"—otherwise it's just politics.
(3) But for any policy—environmental rules, tax collection, school curriculum—there's always some smaller unit that can do it partially well, and always some larger unit that can coordinate slightly better.
(4) So the comparison in (2) never yields a clear winner; it just gives a smooth gradient from hyperlocal to global.
(5) Applied consistently, subsidiarity either pushes everything down to the individual (since no group is strictly necessary) or pulls everything up to the highest coordinating body (since coordination is always a bit better).
(6) Yet the US, Germany, Switzerland, and even teh EU have hard constitutional lines—states, Länder, cantons—that don't shift with every efficency calculation.
(7) So either those lines are arbitrary accidents, or subsidiarity is empty as a normative principle.

i want to keep (1) and (6) if i can—federal structures don't feel like accidents to me—but i think (2) or (4) is smuggling something in. which step do you reject, and what does giving it up cost? is there a hidden slide between "can do the job" and "does it best," or is the mistake somewhere else?
