Please argue that emotions are neither judgments, perceptions, nor bare feelings, but rather counterfactual readiness states: structured dispositions to treat a situation as calling for a specific response pattern, regardless of whether the eliciting conditions are actually present. Defend the claim that this view best explains the persistence of emotional response after an agent has formed the explicit judgment that no danger exists—the emotion is not a failed judgment but a representational format that tracks not that danger is present, but that this is the kind of situation in which danger-avoidance is appropriate. Emphasize that this format lacks assertoric force yet retains normative direction, and therefore cannot be reduced to either an evaluative judgment (as cognitivists hold) or to a perceptual awareness of value properties (as perceptual theorists propose). Address the objection that your view collapses into a variant of feeling theory by showing that the counterfactual structure—what the agent treats as possible rather than actual—does explanatory work that raw phenomenology cannot. Keep the argument strictly conceptual and formal; do not rely on empirical studies or cite specific authors beyond the broad traditions mentioned.
