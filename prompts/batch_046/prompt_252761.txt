I work as a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy or linguistics, though I have been reading informally in the philosophy of language after a colleague mentioned Grice in passing during a conversation about how people actually communicate in technical meetings. I have noticed a recurring pattern in professional discussions that I do not have a precise term for, and I am trying to understand whether it corresponds to a recognized concept or whether it needs to be carved out separately.

The pattern is this. A speaker produces an utterance that is literally true and directly responsive to the question or issue raised. Yet the framing—which details are highlighted, which obvious qualifications are conspicuously omitted, and the shared context of the conversation—makes it almost inevitable that the audience will form a particular belief that goes well beyond the literal content. For instance, during a code review, someone might say, "the authentication module passes all current tests," which is factually correct, but delivered in a way that everyone in the room takes it to mean the module is ready for production deployment. If a security edge case later fails, the speaker insists they only said it passes current tests and never claimed production readiness. If, however, the deployment succeeds, the same speaker will refer back to "what I told you about authentication being solid," treating the implied readiness as if it had been clearly communicated all along. I have observed the same structure in project updates ("the timeline is technically achievable" implying confidence in on-time delivery) and in architecture debates ("this design scales horizontally" literally true for certain benchmarks but framed to suggest it handles our specific load).

This is not straightforward deception, since the literal claim is true. It also does not seem to be ordinary conversational suggestion, because the speaker appears to maintain a deliberate ambiguity rather than genuinely expecting the audience to recover one specific intended message. And it is not exactly strategic equivocation, since there are not two cleanly separated meanings being swapped back and forth; rather, there is a single utterance that genuinely performs two communicative jobs at once, with the speaker able to emphasize or deny the second job after the fact depending on whether it proves advantageous. Is there an established concept—perhaps within frameworks that distinguish what a sentence literally conveys from what a speaker uses it to convey—that captures this specific "toggle" behavior? If not, can you define the phenomenon precisely, distinguish it from ordinary contextual suggestion, from the motte-and-bailey strategy, and from plain negotiation ambiguity, and propose a name with clear boundary conditions?
