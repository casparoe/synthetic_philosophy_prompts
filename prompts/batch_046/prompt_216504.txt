A colleague summarizes the ubuntu-based relational conception of personhood as follows. First, ubuntu holds that an individual possesses no intrinsic moral status whatsoever; personhood is not merely socially mediated but is literally conferred by communal recognition, so that an outcast is not just socially excluded but ontologically downgraded to the status of a non-person. Second, this view entails that rights are not pre-political possessions of individuals but are allocated by the collective according to need, which makes ubuntu incompatible with any liberal doctrine of universal human rights. Third, the colleague claims that this thick relationality is structurally identical to the feminist relational-autonomy thesis, except that ubuntu applies it to metaphysics rather than to ethics alone. The colleague concludes that, because ubuntu denies any pre-social properties, it cannot ground obligations to strangers or to future generations, since neither group can confer recognition upon the present agent. Is this a fair summary? Please separate what is accurate, what is a standard simplification, what misrepresents the view's defenders, and whether the conclusion about strangers and future generations actually follows from the premises given. In particular, address whether the conflation of ubuntu with feminist relational autonomy obscures a distinction between thin social embeddedness and thick ontological constitution that defenders of ubuntu typically maintain.
