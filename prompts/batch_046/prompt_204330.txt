Scholars interpreting Benjamin Franklin's project of moral perfection—his list of thirteen virtues, the weekly grid for tracking faults, and his well-known admission in the Autobiography that he never mastered Order—have long divided between those who treat it as a sincere Stoic-inspired practice of self-cultivation and those who treat it as an ironic, performative exercise in bourgeois self-fashioning. Both camps cite the same textual details, and the debate has barely moved in decades. I want a diagnosis, not a survey: is this stall substantive (a hidden disagreement about whether autobiographical narrative can serve as transparent evidence for lived practical commitment, versus being mediated by genre conventions like the humility topos), verbal (a dispute over what sincerity means in 18th-century life-writing), or bedrock (a clash about whether moral self-improvement is the kind of phenomenon first-person prose can document at all)? If it is methodological, would applying a distinction between expressive and strategic speech acts to the Autobiography help? Or is the impasse a sign that Franklin intended the text as public pedagogy rather than private documentation, making evidential questions about his real practice ill-posed? Give me the diagnosis with reasons drawn from what each side actually claims, and be honest about whether anything—conceptual, formal, or archival—should be expected to resolve it.
