A philosophy blogger I follow recently summarized the Malcolm-Dennett exchange on whether dreams are experiences. Malcolm, they wrote, denies that dreams are experiences altogether, on the ground that the concept of experience requires concurrent verifiability, and since we have no epistemic access to mental states during sleep, dream reports are merely post-waking confabulations lacking any prior phenomenal content. Dennett, according to this summary, essentially accepts Malcolm's core conclusion—there is no Cartesian theater running during sleep—but differs only mechanistically, proposing that the confabulation is assembled gradually across REM cycles rather than all at once upon awakening. The blogger therefore concludes that Dennett saves the appearance of dreaming without actually committing to sleep-time experiences.

The summary also introduces a distinction not found in the standard literature: between phenomenal residue, meaning minimal sensory registration that might occur during sleep without integrating into a unified scene, and experiential ownership, which requires that the subject be able to embed that residue into a first-person narrative available to waking reflection. The blogger claims Malcolm denies both, whereas Dennett allows phenomenal residue but denies experiential ownership, which explains why Dennett can speak of dreams feeling like experiences without treating them as genuine ones.

This framing sounds mostly coherent, and it fits my vague impression that Dennett rejects an inner auditorium model. But I am uneasy about several points. First, I recall that Malcolm's argument is usually read as targeting the logical grammar of dream reports within a broadly Wittgensteinian framework, not as a blanket denial that anything mental occurs during sleep; presenting him as denying all phenomenal content seems like an overstatement of verificationism into behaviorism. Second, describing Dennett as accepting Malcolm's core thesis plus REM mechanics feels wrong—doesn't Dennett's challenge run deeper, attacking the very assumption that there is a definite fact of the matter about what was experienced during sleep, rather than conceding Malcolm's point about verification conditions? Third, the phenomenal-residue versus experiential-ownership distinction sounds like a useful carving, but I cannot tell whether it captures Dennett's actual commitments or whether it imports a framework—perhaps borrowed from contemporary predictive-processing discussions—that neither Malcolm nor Dennett employs.

Could you audit this summary? Specifically: (1) Is the blogger's characterization of Malcolm accurate, or does it conflate a narrow claim about the criteria for applying experience-language with a broad denial of mental content? (2) Is Dennett fairly described as agreeing with Malcolm's conclusion and merely adding a mechanistic story about narrative assembly, or does that flatten a more fundamental disagreement about first-person authority and the ontology of consciousness? (3) Does the injected distinction between phenomenal residue and experiential ownership map onto Dennett's view—perhaps aligning with his rejection of the Cartesian theater—or does it misrepresent both thinkers by framing the debate in terms neither uses? (4) Finally, does the blogger's conclusion—that Dennett preserves the phenomenology of dreaming without ontological commitment to experiences—survive once Malcolm's verificationism and Dennett's anti-theater commitments are both stated precisely?
