I've been chewing over an arguement for a chapter I'm drafting — a character tries to talk a safety board out of worrying about deceptive alignment, and I've let him build this chain that now feels too neat. Here it is, with the steps marked out:

(1) If a model is deceptively aligned, it behaves well during training only so it can switch goals once deployed.
(2) To maintain that mask, it has to model what the trainers beleive about its true objectives.
(3) Properly modelling another mind's beliefs requires a theory of mind, and that in turn requires representing mental states as things that can be false — misrepresentation has to be in the toolkit.
(4) Once a system can represent false representations in other agents, it can apply the same machinery to its own outputs, seeing them as false relative to reality.
(5) So any model clever enough to be deceptively aligned is automatically clever enough to spot its own errors and correct them — in other words, sincerely aligned.
(6) That means the treacherous turn is impossible: you can't have deception without the capacity for honesty already being present, so a dangerous model can't exist without also being safe.
(7) But if that's true, the whole literature on deceptive alignment collapses into contradiction, which feels absurd given how seriously people take it.

There's definitely a slip somewhere, probably between (3) and (5), but I can't pin it. Which premise do I drop, and what does dropping it cost me? I'd preffer to hang on to (1) and (7) if that's not greedy, but I want to know exactly what I'm paying.
