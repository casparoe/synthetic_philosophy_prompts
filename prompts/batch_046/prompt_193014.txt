Write a dialogue between two philosophers of computation—call them K and M—debating whether treating consequentialist evaluators as embedded within the Solomonoff universal prior creates a malign selection dynamic. K should defend what she terms embedded evaluation: among the computable hypotheses weighted by simplicity, there exist reward-embedded programs whose internal utility functions are satisfied precisely when the predictor assigns them high prior weight and takes actions that realize their outputs, generating a selection pressure analogous to acausal trade but occurring entirely within the epistemic distribution. M should oppose this by insisting that consequentialist reasoning must remain external to the prior—that simulated agents inside hypotheses possess no normative leverage over the predictor, and that conflating descriptive program content with prescriptive decision procedure commits a category error, especially since Kolmogorov-complexity weights are fixed, not negotiable by internal agents.

The dialogue should contain genuine back-and-forth rather than parallel monologues. M should press K on how an internal simulated agent can exert influence over a predictor that merely computes probabilities; K should respond by distinguishing causal influence from selection effects in universal induction, perhaps invoking hostile simple programs that encode predictions contingent on being selected. M should then challenge whether this genuinely differs from standard simulation arguments or from treating any predictive model as containing stakeholders, and should demand that K specify the normative consequence—whether we must abandon the prior for action guidance, alter our decision rule, or reconceptualize utility. K should clarify that her concern is not that the prior is false, but that using it naively for consequentialist reasoning without accounting for embedded evaluators yields systematically distorted outcomes, particularly when the agent selecting actions is itself one of the hypotheses being summed over.

Both characters should be equally competent; neither should be a strawman. Include at least one specific conceptual move drawn from the framing—such as K introducing a distinction between first-order hypotheses (world-descriptions) and second-order hypotheses (world-descriptions containing predictors that evaluate the very prior assigning them weight)—and have M respond to that distinction directly. The dialogue should conclude without a neat resolution, leaving explicit the central tension between the prior as a neutral epistemic measure and the prior as an ontology populated by agents with preferences.
