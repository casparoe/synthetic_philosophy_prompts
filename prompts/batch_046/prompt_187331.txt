As a member of my university's committee revising the institutional values statement, I have recently been forced to take seriously a phrase that previously struck me as harmless background noise: 'Western values.' The phrase now dominates submissions from faculty and trustees, yet it never arrives with the same content twice. In one proposal, a historian uses it to defend a core curriculum anchored in Greek and Roman texts, treating 'Western values' as a specific cultural inheritance that belongs to a particular lineage and must be preserved as such; in another proposal, a political scientist uses the identical phrase to justify universal free-expression protections, treating it as a synonym for liberal democratic norms that any society could adopt. Both authors treat the meaning as self-evident, requiring no definition, and when critics object they do not dispute the content but instead accuse the speaker of having 'misunderstood Western values,' as if there were a hidden correct version that would settle the dispute. I have also noticed a structural slide that I have started calling, in my own notes, a boundary-to-content shift: the phrase begins as a marker of membership—who counts as part of the West—and then, without acknowledgment, becomes a list of norms—rational inquiry, individual dignity, scientific method—so that an argument about heritage quietly transforms into an argument about universal rights and then back again. This pattern feels like more than simple ambiguity; it is a recurring argumentative move in which a contested normative category is treated as an unexamined premise, its content is adjusted post hoc to match the speaker's policy preference, and its boundaries are redrawn to include or exclude audiences as needed. Is there an established concept—perhaps in rhetoric, philosophy of culture, or political theory—that captures this specific configuration of contradictory deployment, self-evidence, and boundary-content slippage? If the nearest concepts are essentialism, strategic ambiguity, or critiques of civilizational categories, how exactly does the pattern I describe differ from each, and what does its prevalence suggest about whether 'Western values' can function as a coherent analytic category at all?
