I am writing as a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, though I have been reading around the neuroscience of decision-making after a discussion with colleagues about deterministic algorithms and human agency. We kept circling back to Libet's experiments, where the readiness potential appears in the brain several hundred milliseconds before subjects report being consciously aware of having decided to move their wrist. The debate, both in the literature I have encountered and in our conversation, seems to have reached a kind of stalemate: one camp treats the pre-conscious neural activity as proof that the conscious "I" is merely observing a decision already made by subcortical processes, while another camp insists that free will was never meant to cover spontaneous finger-flexions in a laboratory setting and that the experiments therefore miss the phenomenon entirely. A third group raises methodological objections about the reliability of subjective timing reports, but even they do not seem to shift either of the first two camps.

What I find puzzling is that neither side appears to address a distinction that seems important to me as someone used to thinking in terms of system architecture: there is a difference between the high-level selection of a task or goal — for instance, agreeing to participate in the study and intending to move when one feels an urge — and the low-level execution of a particular motor command at a specific millisecond. The readiness potential recorded over the pre-supplementary motor area and the primary motor cortex might correspond to the latter, yet the philosophical debate about free will often concerns the former. Meanwhile, some recent work I have seen mentions predictive-coding frameworks in which the brain continuously generates and updates action predictions, meaning that "awareness" might not be a discrete event that either precedes or follows initiation but rather a graded process of error correction. Given this, I want to ask: is the long-standing impasse over Libet genuinely a disagreement about empirical neuroscience, a verbal dispute about what counts as "the decision," or two different questions — one about proximal motor causation and one about distal agency — being forced under a single heading? And if your diagnosis is that the sides are answering different questions, what kind of result — perhaps a formal model of hierarchical decision-making, or an experiment that separates task-selection signals from execution signals — would actually count as progress, rather than simply generating another round of the same stalemate?
