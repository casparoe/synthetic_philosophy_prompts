I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy training, but I recently listened in on a discussion about forecasting and practical reasoning that touched on what I understand is called the inside-view versus outside-view problem, together with the idea of epistemic modesty. Two other attendees — neither of them philosophers — debated what modesty actually demands when your own first-person reasoning conflicts with a statistical base rate. I am recounting their exchange below as a neutral bystander; I am not signaling agreement with either side, and I want a point-by-point adjudication rather than general advice.

The disagreement went through four moves:

(1) She said that if ninety percent of people in the relevant reference class — experienced engineers leaving stable employment to found startups — fail within two years, then epistemic modesty requires treating that base rate as the rational starting point. Ignoring it because you have a detailed business plan is the classic inside-view error; it is not humility but special pleading.

(2) He replied that this demands a kind of de-indexing: stripping away the indexical I and treating your own plan as though it were observed from a purely third-person perspective. But practical deliberation requires the indexical. He has direct access to his specific technical architecture, the actual capabilities of his team, and a verified market gap — information the reference class does not share. Modesty about reasoning processes should not erase legitimate informational advantages; to do so is methodological self-alienation, not rational humility.

(3) She countered that those advantages are precisely what every failed founder in the reference class cited. The subgroup founders with direct knowledge of their architecture and a verified gap still fails at high rates, because the feature used to distinguish oneself is present in the failing group too. His direct access is therefore contaminated by the same optimism bias that produces the outside-view statistics. Without an independent check — which he does not have — he cannot legitimately update on his private information.

(4) He argued that this position is self-defeating. Applying the outside view itself requires first-person judgments: that this reference class applies to him, that the data is reliable, and that he ought to update his beliefs. If all first-person reasoning is contaminated by optimism bias, then his belief in the authority of the outside view is also contaminated. So either she permits some trusted first-person reasoning — in which case modesty is a matter of degree, not an absolute prohibition — or she collapses into skepticism about her own position. She cannot have it both ways.
