Consider an institutional protocol used in multilateral treaty drafting: professional interpreters are formally required, under the “charitable reconstruction mandate,” to treat any delegate’s apparently contradictory or absurd statement as a rational, largely true belief expressed within a shared conceptual framework, attributing error only to demonstrable translation failure. The same institution, however, trains staff to apply “strategic skepticism” to domestic protests or opposition speeches—treating inconsistencies there as signs of deception, ideological bias, or irrationality, and deeming charitable reconstruction inappropriate because those speakers are not engaged in cooperative truth-seeking.

What theory of interpretation, belief attribution, and rationality does this asymmetry presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit view: does it commit the body to a Davidsonian claim that radical interpretation requires charity applied universally, or does it assume charity is only warranted inside a prior boundary of “rational interlocutors”? If the latter, what determines that boundary, and is the distinction compatible with Davidson’s argument that belief and meaning are interdependent and determinable only through widespread agreement? Examine whether the two protocols are internally consistent, or whether they rely on incompatible accounts of when belief-content is determinable. Then assess whether the practice is philosophically underdetermined—could a Davidsonian defend the asymmetry by arguing that domestic opponents genuinely fail the conditions for interpretation—or whether it smuggles in a non-Davidsonian separation (for example, between communicative cooperation and strategic action) that Davidson’s framework rejects. Finally, describe what concrete changes to interpreter training, drafting procedure, and documentation norms would be required if the institution replaced its implicit theory with a uniformly Davidsonian radical interpretation applied to all speakers regardless of political role.
