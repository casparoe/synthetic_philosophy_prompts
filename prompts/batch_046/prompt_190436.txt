You have been asked to deliver the opening remarks—about six to eight minutes—for an interdisciplinary workshop titled “The Revolution After Itself,” convened in the lecture hall of a civic museum whose building was briefly seized by sectional activists in 1793 and returned to municipal order within months. The audience is divided evenly: half are academic historians and political philosophers who specialize in revolutionary contingency, class formation, and the historiography of the Terror; half are museum trustees and invited local guests with no specialist vocabulary. You must address both groups without either romanticizing violence for the scholars or reducing the Revolution to a safe heritage object for the patrons.

The philosophical idea must govern the architecture of the speech, not merely decorate it. The question is whether revolutions devour their children, but interpreted here as a structural tension between revolutionary contingency and institutional sedimentation rather than as a moral story about individual leaders. You must let this tension constrain what you can claim: welcome the workshop’s scholarly purpose, yet acknowledge that convening orderly academic deliberation inside a space where disorder was once improvised and then erased performs the very institutional absorption the workshop studies. Do not resolve the tension into a neat synthesis.

To prevent the speech from being generic, work with a specific conceptual distinction: “accelerated causation,” the revolutionary logic by which future norms are invoked to justify present actions before those norms have any institutional reality, versus “retrospective legitimation,” the institutional logic by which past actions become justified only once embedded in durable structures. Use this distinction to explain why the workshop can study the Revolution without repeating it, and also why it can never claim to have fully recovered the revolutionary moment. Address the trustees about the room’s physical history without turning it into decorative local color; address the scholars without jargon that excludes the patrons. Do not provide bibliographies, cite specific monographs by name, or ask the audience to consult particular texts. The remarks should feel occasion-specific—something that could only be said in this room, to this divided audience, at the opening of this workshop.
