I'm working through some population-ethics material and keep returning to Benatar's procreation asymmetry: the claim that bringing a person into existence can be harmful to them if they suffer, yet failing to bring them into existence is not harmful to them even if they would have enjoyed life, because in the latter case there is no subject to be deprived. I want to examine a specific reformulation that hasn't come up in standard discussions. Write an essay on whether this asymmetry survives if we replace the direct comparison between actual existence and literal non-being with a modal-neighbourhood account of harm and benefit. On this view, harm is assessed not against the actual state of non-existence but against the closest possible worlds in which the subject exists without the adverse condition; benefit is assessed analogously against worlds in which the subject enjoys an improvement. Critics might argue that this merely relocates Benatar's asymmetry rather than dissolving it, since the modal comparison for harm can be anchored in the existing subject, whereas the modal comparison for benefit still seems to require an actual bearer to serve as the reference point for the counterfactual gain. Others might contend that once harm and benefit are both treated as counterfactual-comparative notions with identical modal structures, the asymmetry collapses entirely, because the absence of pleasure becomes structurally parallel to the presence of pain — both are deviations from a modal baseline that includes the subject. Weigh these positions carefully, but do not end on a non-committal note: defend either the verdict that Benatar's conclusion remains intact under the modal reformulation, or that the asymmetry dissolves once harm and benefit share the same counterfactual architecture. In reaching your conclusion, address whether the modal account illegitimately imports a subject of comparison into the benefit case where Benatar insists none exists, and whether that importation is avoidable without begging the question against the asymmetry.
