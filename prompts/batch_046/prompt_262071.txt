Two colleagues, Lena and Marcus, argued about a hypothetical neurobiological intervention—valence recalibration—that flattens affective experience to a steady mild contentment, eliminating suffering but also intense joy, grief, or longing.

Lena said this fulfills the hedonistic imperative unconditionally: suffering is the only clear intrinsic bad, and trading extreme peaks for reliable comfort is rational, like replacing fireworks with a pilot light that never fails.

Marcus conceded suffering should be abolished but insisted that flattening valence destroys contrast structure—the relational background that makes qualitative value judgments intelligible. Without possible failure, he argued, better and worse collapse into quantitative pleasure differences. Lena replied that qualitative distinctions remain fully cognitive: one can understand injustice without feeling outrage.

Marcus then claimed motivation without aversion becomes preference drift—pursuit of whatever is nearest, not what matters. Lena answered that aspiration needs no pain; curiosity and love drive sustained achievement, as shown in contemplative traditions that maintain rigorous discipline without distress.

Marcus countered that even if motivation survives, moral responsibility is emptied. An agent who never risks affective harm is like a thermostat maintaining temperature; praise and blame lose their point. Lena said responsibility tracks rational agency and consequences, not shared suffering; we do not weigh guilt as a necessary ingredient of accountability.

Can you adjudicate their exchange point by point—specifically: whether flattening valence eliminates qualitative distinctions or only their felt intensity; whether aversion-free motivation is necessarily drift rather than directed aspiration; whether moral responsibility requires affective risk; and whether the hedonistic imperative supports full recalibration or only targeted suffering-ablation. For each step, indicate who makes the stronger move and what both overlook. Please separate what is relatively settled in philosophy of value and philosophy of mind from what remains genuinely contested among people who know the topic, and do not present a contested view as the consensus.
