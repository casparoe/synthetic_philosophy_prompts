I'm not a political scientist—just someone who has spent a lot of time following direct democracy in Switzerland, the Brexit vote, and California ballot measures—and I've noticed a recurring pattern I don't have a word for. A referendum passes with a broad, enthusiastic majority on a resonant but general principle: "take back control," "protect local jobs," "limit overdevelopment." The result is treated as a democratic command. But when lawmakers propose a concrete mechanism—a specific trade arrangement, a numerical quota, a zoning formula—the original winning coalition fractures. One faction says the mechanism betrays the referendum; another says it's too timid; a third says it goes too far. What's striking is that if you list all the conditions each faction demands, no single policy could satisfy them at once. Yet each faction still invokes the same referendum result as proof that its own version is the "real" will of the people, while opponents claim the mandate was always incoherent. I've seen this with Brexit, where different Leave voters rejected every proposed deal for opposite reasons, and with Swiss immigration initiatives where supporters seemed to want contradictory relationships with the EU. Is there an established concept for this exact configuration—not just "ambiguous wording" or "voters changing their minds," but a binary democratic victory that creates a kind of negative space, where the only clear content is rejection of the status quo and every attempt to fill that space reveals the coalition was never united on a positive program? If so, how is it distinguished from ordinary political gridlock or from a badly drafted ballot? If not, can you define it precisely—its necessary conditions, whether the coalition's demands must be logically incompatible or merely inconvenient, and whether it applies only to yes/no votes or also to multi-option ones? And please say what would change your mind about this framing—perhaps evidence that winning coalitions usually do share one hidden positive program, or that I'm wrongly assuming voters have fixed preferences rather than expressing symbolic identity—and point out where your reasoning is most likely to fail.
