A friend who has been reading philosophy of mind summarized an argument about luminosity and the KK principle for me in these terms: "Luminosity is the claim that whenever a mental condition—say, feeling cold or being in pain—actually holds, the subject is necessarily in an epistemic position to know that it holds, without any hidden vagueness or margin for error. The KK principle then adds that knowing you are in pain entails knowing that you know it. Together they generate an infinitely ascending tower of self-knowledge. The standard counterargument runs a sorites-style thermal-gradient case: as temperature drops by imperceptible degrees, there must be a precise point at which you become cold, yet because the change is continuous you lack any reliable mechanism to detect that boundary. At that point you are cold but not in a position to know it, so luminosity fails. Furthermore, because this failure traces back to a general modal-safety constraint on knowledge—namely, that knowing requires not being easily wrong in nearby possible worlds—the KK principle collapses too: if first-order mental knowledge is already unstable near the boundary, there is no secure base for second-order knowledge." I suspect this blends together several distinct claims and may overstate the implications of the thermal case. Which parts are faithful to the standard anti-luminosity argument, where does it misrepresent either luminosity or the KK principle, and does the conclusion about the KK principle actually follow from the thermal-gradient failure?
