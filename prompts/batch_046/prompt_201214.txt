I'm serving as a teaching assistant for an upper-division course on Descartes and the early modern rationalists, and I've been asked to review a graded short-answer exam before the marks are finalized. The exam presented the following question to students: In Meditation II, Descartes reaches the conclusion that he necessarily exists—summarized in the familiar formulation that he is, he exists—whenever he thinks, and he holds that this result survives even the hypothesis of a malicious demon who deceives him about every other matter. The exam then asked students to address three specific issues. First, whether the cogito establishes only that thinking is occurring, or whether it also establishes the existence of a substantial self that serves as the subject of that thinking. Second, whether Descartes is entitled in Meditation II to describe himself as a thing that thinks—encompassing doubting, understanding, affirming, and willing—or whether that identification presupposes premises about substance and essence that he has not yet earned in the order of the Meditations. Third, they were asked to evaluate the modern performative interpretation of the argument—associated with readings that treat the first-person assertion of thinking as making its own existence true in something like the way a promise is made true by being uttered—and to explain whether, if that interpretation is correct, it undermines Descartes's subsequent claim in Meditation VI that the mind is a substance whose essence is thought, or whether the two can be reconciled.

Below is one student's answer. I can see that it correctly tracks the general sequence of the Meditations and avoids any gross anachronisms, but I suspect it contains one or two subtle interpretive errors: perhaps a conflation between what Descartes establishes in Meditation II and what he argues only in Meditation VI, and perhaps a mischaracterization of the performative interpretation as an objection that Descartes himself answers rather than as a modern analytical framing. I want an independent assessment. Please assign the answer a letter grade (A through F, with plus/minus if appropriate) and provide specific, point-by-point comments that justify it. For each of the three parts of the exam question, indicate whether the student is correct, partly correct, or mistaken, and explain precisely where a step fails to follow or where a view is misattributed. I am especially interested in whether any of the errors are subtle enough that a hurried grader might miss them, so please flag those explicitly. Do not indicate whether you share my own suspicions; assess the answer strictly on its philosophical merits.

Student answer:

Descartes's cogito in Meditation II proves that he necessarily exists whenever he thinks, and this survives the deceiving-demon hypothesis because even deception requires a subject to be deceived. At this stage he correctly suspends judgment about bodies, since sense-perception has been undermined. He then asks what he is and answers that he is a thing that thinks, including doubting, understanding, affirming, and willing. Some modern commentators, following Hintikka, offer a performative reading: saying that one thinks makes one's existence true in the act of saying it, comparable to how a promise creates an obligation by being spoken. This might seem to reduce the self to a grammatical placeholder rather than a real substance. But Descartes can reply that the very act of doubting reveals an agent who performs the doubt, so the performative structure actually confirms a substantial subject rather than dissolving it. By Meditation VI he proves through the real-distinction argument that mind and body are distinct substances, but he already knows in Meditation II that thought is his complete essence, since he can clearly and distinctly understand himself as thinking without understanding himself as having any bodily extension. The only gap is that he has not yet applied the clear-and-distinct-perception criterion—formally introduced in Meditation III to secure mathematical truths and to establish that God is not a deceiver—to validate his self-conception. Once God is proven veracious, that criterion confirms what the cogito initially revealed.
