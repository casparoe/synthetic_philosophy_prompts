I'm preparing a seminar session on moral psychology and perpetrator behavior, and I've noticed that students tend to resolve the Browning-Goldhagen debate by picking sides—treating Christopher Browning's situational account in Ordinary Men and Daniel Goldhagen's ideological account in Hitler's Willing Executioners as mutually exclusive—without ever forcing either theory to map onto the actual decision points of a real episode. To correct that, analyze the My Lai massacre of March 1968 through both lenses simultaneously. Treat the event as a sequence of distinct phases: the company's initial entry into the hamlet and separation of villagers from their huts; the concentrated killings at the irrigation ditch under Lieutenant William Calley's direct orders; the brief pause and subsequent resumption of violence; and the intervention by Warrant Officer Hugh Thompson's helicopter crew that halted the massacre. For each phase, identify the specific actors present, the options visibly available to them—active participation, direct refusal, passive withdrawal, or physical intervention—and whether the behavior observed is better explained by situational mechanisms such as peer conformity, diffusion of responsibility, authority pressure, and combat dehumanization, or by the kind of deep-seated, voluntary commitment to eliminationist violence that Goldhagen emphasizes. Be explicit about the soldiers who refused to fire and about Thompson's crew, since these are not marginal details but direct tests of whether the situation was genuinely overwhelming or whether willingness to kill varied independently of context.

Then turn the analysis back on the theories themselves. Does My Lai function as a confirming case for one framework, an anomaly for the other, or evidence that the situation-culture-character triad needs restructuring? Specifically, address whether the concept of "moral drift"—a gradual, largely unplanned shift in what a group treats as permissible, driven by incremental accommodation and local normalization rather than by prior hatred or pure external pressure—captures dynamics at the ditch and during the resumption that both Browning and Goldhagen miss. In doing so, distinguish carefully between what counts as evidence for situational pressure (for instance, Calley's explicit commands and the presence of armed peers) and what counts as evidence for ideological motivation (for instance, any expressed racial or political justification among the soldiers), and note where the episode offers neither.

Finally, be disciplined about silence. Identify which features of My Lai—such as the absence of long-term indoctrination comparable to Goldhagen's German case, the rapid emergence of refusal among some soldiers despite identical situational exposure, and the decisive effect of a single outside interruption—neither framework can explain without stretching its core concepts. Say explicitly what that silence implies about the limits of applying either model to atrocities outside its original historical context, and whether the episode suggests that a third factor, such as institutional fragmentation or the specific micro-dynamics of command presence and absence, needs to be added rather than substituted. Do not supply bibliographies or reading lists; the task is the conceptual mapping and its honest failure points.
