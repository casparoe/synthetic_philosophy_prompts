Last evening I had a prolonged argument with a close friend about Milton Friedman’s so-called F-twist—the thesis that the unrealistic character of a scientific model’s assumptions is not a defect, and may even be a virtue, so long as the model produces accurate predictions. She kept returning to contemporary climate modeling, where wildly unrealistic parameterizations—uniform cloud-layer approximations, linearized radiative-feedback loops—are constantly revised because minute errors cascade into policy-relevant projections, and she insisted that this demonstrates Friedman’s defense fails catastrophically outside economics. I countered that climate scientists still preserve deeply unrealistic sub-models inside numerically stable, architecturally isolated modules—parameterized convection schemes that are physically false but inferentially quarantined—suggesting that what actually licenses an unrealistic assumption is not predictive success per se, but a kind of epistemic modularity that prevents the falsehood from contaminating the outputs we care about.

I want to formalize this intuition into a short research proposal—suitable for a one-semester philosophy-of-science workshop or a small grant—asking whether Friedman’s F-twist should be reconstructed as a claim about modular isolation rather than predictive accuracy, and whether that reconstruction yields a principled distinction between acceptable and unacceptable idealizations. Please present the proposal as exactly five numbered points, each consisting of one or two sentences, with no introduction and no conclusion: first, the precise research question and why existing interpretations leave it open; second, the limitation of framing unrealistic assumptions as either always harmless or always dangerous, and what standard treatments overlook; third, the concrete method and timeline—what conceptual or comparative work I would do and within what scope; fourth, what a successful outcome would look like and what conditions would count as failure; and fifth, the boundaries of the project, specifying which scientific domains or model architectures fall within the inquiry and which are deliberately excluded.
