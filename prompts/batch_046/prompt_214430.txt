I am preparing a single 45-minute discussion for a final-year psychology reading group. The students have just completed a consciousness module and treat REM sleep as self-evidently involving rich, occurrent conscious experiences—essentially “inner movies” playing in real time. I want them to understand why Norman Malcolm and Daniel Dennett both reject this picture, yet for entirely different conceptual reasons, and to see that their clash is not about neuroscience but about what conditions must be met for something to count as an experience at all.

Plan the explanatory arc for me. First, set out the dependency order: which distinction must be secured before Malcolm’s argument can even be heard, and which before Dennett’s alternative becomes intelligible rather than absurd? Second, propose the one carrying example or protocol that should anchor the session—perhaps a lucid-dream button-press study, but tell me if that example backfires and what should replace it, specifying exactly what the example must possess and what it must lack. Third, map the misconceptions these students will raise in the sequence they will raise them, from “but I remember being terrified” through to “fMRI shows activation patterns matching waking perception”; for each, indicate the precise point in the arc where it should be anticipated and absorbed, and how to do so without merely asserting philosophical authority. Fourth, describe the check at the end that would demonstrate they have grasped the disagreement itself—distinguishing Malcolm’s conceptual point about reportability and wakefulness from Dennett’s claim about narrative construction upon awakening—rather than just pairing two names with two slogans.

Also say explicitly what should be left out at this level and why: for instance, whether Wittgensteinian considerations about private language are a necessary underpinning for Malcolm or a distracting digression here, and whether Dennett’s later pandemonium model needs any mention. The audience has no philosophy training beyond an introductory course. Write in British English, keep the response to no more than 400 words, and do not include reading lists, bibliographies, or citations.
