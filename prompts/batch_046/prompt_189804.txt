Please argue for a reconceived version of disjunctivism about perceptual experience—the view that a veridical perception and a subjectively matching hallucination do not share a fundamental mental kind—by showing that the classic argument from illusion succeeds only if it collapses three distinct dimensions into one. Specifically, defend the claim that the alleged common factor between seeing an object and merely seeming to see it is not an intrinsic phenomenal state but a structural isomorphism in the subject’s cognitive access conditions: the hallucination replicates the informational and introspective profile of the veridical case without instantiating the same intentional relation to an object. In your argument, sharply distinguish (a) phenomenal texture, (b) intentional directedness toward an object, and (c) epistemic accessibility from the first-person perspective, and demonstrate that phenomenal similarity across the two cases concerns primarily (c) and perhaps (a), while (b) differs categorically. Address the standard objection that introspective indistinguishability entails sameness of core mental nature, and explain why that inference fails on your account. Finally, clarify what this position implies for the transparency thesis—the idea that in veridical perception we are directly aware of the world rather than of an intermediate mental item—and indicate whether your version of disjunctivism preserves, modifies, or abandons that intuition.

In your response, please also identify what would change your mind: specify which empirical finding about the neural substrates of conscious perception, or which logical point about the structure of introspective reports, would cause you to reject this defense, and indicate the precise step in your reasoning—whether in the distinction between access conditions and intentional directedness, or in the treatment of phenomenal texture—where you are most vulnerable to error.
