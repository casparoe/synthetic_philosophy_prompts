For my philosophy of logic seminar, I am trying to formalize exactly what an adequate theory of future contingents—particularly in response to Aristotle’s sea-battle problem—must accomplish if it is to remain viable in light of physically indeterminate processes. Rather than treating contingency as a purely modal notion, I want the account to accommodate singular future-tensed statements whose truth depends on genuinely stochastic events (e.g., a naval conflict triggered by quantum decay with no hidden-variable determination). Write the full list of desiderata: the specific cases it must get right (the intuition that tomorrow’s battle is genuinely open now but will be settled once past, the asymmetry between past fixity and future openness, the failure of bivalence for singular future-tensed statements), the distinctions it must preserve without collapse (logical vs. causal necessity, metaphysical openness vs. mere epistemic uncertainty, determinism vs. fatalism), the formal and physical constraints it should respect (consistency with branching-time semantics, preservation of excluded middle for present- and past-tensed statements, compatibility with probabilistic physical predictions that lack deterministic underpinning), and the philosophical commitments that are genuinely non-negotiable versus those that are camp-specific preferences (e.g., presentism, eternalism, or particular interpretations of quantum mechanics). For each desideratum, provide the underlying consideration or motivating case. Then identify any subset of these requirements that looks jointly unsatisfiable—especially whether accommodating physical indeterminacy forces a choice between maintaining bivalence for the present and denying it for the future, or between preserving past fixity and allowing genuine openness. Finally, briefly assess where the standard responses stand against this list—Aristotle’s restriction of bivalence to future contingents, Ockhamist branching-time models, and deterministic hidden-variable reinterpretations—keeping the weight firmly on the criteria rather than on the scorecard.
