In philosophy seminars I've heard Zhuangzi's butterfly episode compressed into the one-liner 'for all we know, we might be dreaming right now' — used to suggest that because waking and dreaming feel identical from the inside, we have no internal criterion to tell them apart. Separate the precise claims hiding in that sentence: is it an epistemic thesis about the limits of self-knowledge, an ontological thesis that there is no fact of the matter about which state is 'real,' a phenomenological thesis that the two experiences share an indistinguishable qualitative character, or a therapeutic thesis about the dissolution of a fixed self? Say which are platitudes about fallibilism, which are substantive and defensible, which are false or self-defeating, and which the people who invoke the line probably intend. In particular, tell me which reading is actually doing the work when someone deploys it against a scientific realist who claims that neural imaging can determine whether we are in 'base reality' — and which readings, if any, that argumentative move genuinely supports.
