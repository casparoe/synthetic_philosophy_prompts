On one hand, every democracy I can think of makes it a serious crime—often treason-level—for a private citizen to kill or forcibly overthrow a sitting leader, even if that leader is obviously a tyrant who seized power illegally or is destroying the common good. On the other hand, those same systems usually have some formal removal mechanism—impeachment, parliamentary vote, whatever—where only people holding specific offices can remove the leader, and usually only for breaking proceedural rules, not for being morally monstrous.

So what's the implicit theory of authority and desert here? Is the practice actually presupposing an Aquinian view where institutional role determines whether resistance is legitimate, or is it closer to a pure rule-of-law view where we just never allow private violence because it causes chaos, regardless of desert? I'm trying to figure out whether the practice is philosophically coherent or if it's smuggling in a theory of moral desert that depends on who pulls the trigger, not on whether the victim deserves to be removed.
