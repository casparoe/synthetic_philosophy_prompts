As a retired experimental physicist who has recently begun working through the philosophy of ethics, I find myself repeatedly blocked by a maxim I hear in discussions of moral luck: "You are only responsible for what you can control." The phrase is usually deployed to dismiss the entire framework of moral luck—whether the case of Gauguin leaving his family, the negligent driver, or the problem of constitutive luck—but I suspect it compresses several distinct philosophical claims into a single sound bite. Could you lay out, as a precise conceptual menu, the different theses this slogan might actually be asserting? In particular, distinguish whether "control" is being interpreted as direct causal efficacy, as epistemic access to outcomes, as voluntary choice uncoerced by circumstance, or as the absence of deterministic external forces; clarify whether "only" functions as a necessary-condition claim or as an exhaustive exclusion of all other responsibility conditions; and specify, for each combination, whether it genuinely undermines all four of Nagel's categories of luck or merely targets one subset. I am especially interested in whether a suppressed intermediate position—what I would call graded control, where control is necessary but not sufficient and its absence admits of degrees—can be reconstructed from within the slogan's own logic, and whether that reconstructed position is the one that actually separates defenders of moral luck from their critics. Please do not supply bibliographies or reading lists; I want the conceptual architecture disambiguated so that I can evaluate it with the analytical habits I brought from laboratory work.
