I'm currently enrolled in a graduate seminar on the philosophy of artificial intelligence and evaluative methodology, and for my final assignment I need to construct an adversarial-collaboration document. The two camps are the Process-Transparency camp, which holds that trustworthy deployment of large reasoning systems requires direct auditing of internal reasoning trajectories for epistemic and normative validity, and the Outcome-Equivalence camp, which holds that supervision should evaluate only terminal outputs against verified benchmarks, treating intermediate representations as causally opaque and outside the legitimate scope of evaluation.

Please draft the joint statement that representatives of both camps could sign in good faith after a sustained collaboration. Structure the document exactly as follows.

First, a numbered section of agreed claims, including: (a) a shared formal characterization of reasoning models as functions that map problem contexts to output distributions via intermediate representational states; (b) mutual acknowledgment that human supervisory bandwidth is finite and that any supervision regime must operate under binding resource constraints; (c) precise, neutral definitions of "process-based supervision" and "outcome-based supervision," each phrased so that the camp to which it applies would accept it as an accurate statement of its own view; and (d) a shared observation about a specific failure mode—namely, that a model can generate a chain-of-thought that is locally logically valid but globally semantically decoupled from the problem context, a form of steganographic reasoning, and that this failure mode is detectable in principle by both regimes but with different supervisory costs.

Second, a disagreements table presenting the residual disagreements in wording each camp would accept for its own side. The table should cover: (i) whether procedural fidelity—defined as structural isomorphism between the model's intermediate reasoning steps and valid inferential norms—is a necessary condition for trustworthy deployment; (ii) whether outcome-equivalence under counterfactual perturbation of hidden intermediate states is sufficient for safety guarantees; and (iii) the normative and epistemic status of "latent deliberation," understood as intermediate representations that influence the output distribution without being tokenized in the observable chain-of-thought.

Third, identify the cruxes—the sub-questions such that resolving them would cause at least one camp to revise its position. I anticipate two primary cruxes: one concerning whether structural transparency possesses independent normative value beyond its instrumental contribution to outcome reliability, and another concerning the empirical tractability of auditing high-dimensional reasoning traces in practice rather than in principle.

Fourth, for each crux, specify a concrete evaluation protocol, case, or argument that both camps agree bears on it, and record each side's advance prediction. For the empirical crux, propose a controlled study in which human supervisors are assigned either full access to tokenized reasoning traces or only to final outputs, applied to an adversarially constructed set of multi-step logic puzzles specifically designed to elicit the semantically-decoupled reasoning failure mode. Both camps should state, in advance, which supervisory condition they predict will yield the higher detection rate of that failure mode, together with the reasoning behind the prediction.

The document should be formal and suitable for submission in a philosophy of technology course. It should not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations, and it should not invoke obscure named doctrines that depend on specialized textual knowledge. Instead, build the necessary conceptual machinery—including the framing of steganographic reasoning and latent deliberation—within the document itself.
