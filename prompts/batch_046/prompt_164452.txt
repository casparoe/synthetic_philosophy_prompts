I'm trying to isolate what makes pain intrinsically bad, working through concrete clinical and hypothetical cases rather than through general definitions. Here are my verdicts. A patient with pain asymbolia following right parietal damage reports intense burning and sharpness but shows no avoidance behavior and describes the sensation as curious rather than unpleasant; I judge that her state lacks the distinctive badness of pain, despite intact nociception. A neurologically normal subject exposed to the same thermal stimulus reports suffering and acts to escape; this is clearly bad. An individual with congenital insensitivity to pain who fractures a bone without awareness: the absence of pain is harmful only instrumentally, not because they are deprived of something intrinsically valuable. A masochist who seeks out an identical nociceptive input and reports it as intensely pleasurable: not bad for them, perhaps even good. A phantom-limb sufferer who knows the limb is absent but feels crushing, inescapable pain: genuinely bad, and the badness seems to lie in the suffering component rather than in any false bodily representation. Finally, a hypothetical case of pure dysphoria—unlocated, non-sensory suffering with no bodily content whatsoever, comparable in intensity to severe physical pain: I think this is also genuinely bad, and roughly equally so.

These verdicts point me toward an affective account, but I'm unsure whether they form a coherent set or whether I am smuggling in contradictions. Is there a precise principle—perhaps with separate clauses for sensory, affective, and representational conditions—that delivers all six judgments?
