I'm currently taking a graduate seminar on logical empiricism and the philosophy of science, and we've been working through Moritz Schlick's arguments about whether life derives its significance from achieving external ends or from intrinsic, present-oriented activity. As I understand his position—drawn mainly from his well-known essay on the meaning of life—Schlick treats the idea that existence is a form of labor directed toward some distant purpose as a fundamental category mistake. Instead, he holds that value resides in autotelic engagement, in what might be called play rather than work, where the activity is not an instrument for a further state but is itself the locus of meaning, and where the intensity and richness of immediate experience matter more than any outcome they might produce.

I'm also a doctoral student in machine learning, and I want to see whether this view can be translated systematically into the vocabulary I actually use: policies, environments, reward functions, intrinsic versus extrinsic motivation, exploration bonuses, instrumental convergence, and the distinction between terminal and intermediate rewards. Please restate Schlick's argument that life is meaningful precisely insofar as it is not purposive—where purpose is understood as an externally validated objective—using only these concepts. For instance, does his notion of play map onto an agent that optimizes an intrinsic curiosity bonus decoupled from the environment's extrinsic reward? Does his rejection of life-as-instrumental-labor correspond to rejecting a fixed terminal reward function in favor of open-ended, non-convergent exploration? And can his claim that purpose-seeking drains life of value be rendered as a claim about reward hacking or the misalignment that arises when an agent treats intermediate rewards as ends?

Then report what does not survive the translation. Schlick seems to insist that the qualitative, phenomenological density of immediate experience—the particular richness of being absorbed in an activity for its own sake—plays an essential role in his account, yet reinforcement learning represents value through quantitative reward signals and state-transition probabilities. Is that residue a genuine deficiency in the RL framework's capacity to represent value, or is it a sign that Schlick's original claim relies on an underspecified notion of meaning that dissolves once we demand precise operationalization? Furthermore, is there anything in Schlick's ethical or existential claim that abandoning teleology altogether transforms how one relates to one's own existence—something that might correspond to altering not just a reward function but the architecture of the agent itself—that has no counterpart in the design of learning algorithms, and if so, does that absence reflect a limitation of my vocabulary or an ambiguity in his?
