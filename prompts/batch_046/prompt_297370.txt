I'm preparing a consistency audit for a comparative-philosophy workshop on Jain epistemology, and I want to check whether my own commitments hang together before I present. I hold the following: (a) Anekāntavāda is correct—every object possesses infinite attributes, and any single judgment grasps only one aspect (one naya), so unqualified assertions are always strictly false. (b) This applies reflexively: even the claim that "reality has many sides" is only true syāt, in some respect, under some description. (c) I am a moral realist about suffering: it is mind-independently bad that sentient beings suffer, and this is not merely one perspective among others. (d) Moral realism requires that some normative claims hold without modal qualification—"torture is wrong" must be true simpliciter, not just "torture is wrong from the biological-perspective." (e) I accept syādvāda: every substantive judgment should be prefixed with "syāt" (in some respect) to avoid ekānta (one-sidedness). (f) I reject global relativism: it is not that "torture is wrong" is true-for-utilitarians and false-for-egoists; rather, there is one reality with many sides, and some normative sides carry determinative weight. (g) I treat competing metaphysical systems—Advaita Vedānta, Madhyamaka, Nyāya realism—not as false but as each capturing a different naya of a unified reality. (h) I take recent predictive-processing neuroscience as empirical support for (a): conscious experience is always a constructed aspect, never a direct grasp of "things as they are." (i) Yet I also want to say that anekāntavāda itself is not just a heuristic but a genuinely correct account of the structure of reality, not merely one useful naya among others.
