I recently heard a popular lecture summarize the debate over societal "collapse" as follows. The lecturer claimed that cases like the Late Bronze Age Eastern Mediterranean, the Classic Maya lowlands, and Easter Island all illustrate a single pattern: societies build increasingly centralized administrative and monumental systems until they reach a peak complexity, at which point an environmental or economic stressor triggers an irreversible failure mode. In this failure mode, palatial or state institutions vanish rapidly, writing and monumental construction cease, and population levels drop sharply—sometimes by half or more—leaving behind ruins that clearly indicate the society "died" rather than changed form. The lecturer argued that scholars who reject the term "collapse" and speak only of "transformation" or "reconfiguration" are ignoring this archaeological evidence of rapid depopulation and institutional disappearance. They specifically cited Easter Island's deforestation and the abandonment of its moai construction as proof that societies can drive themselves into ecological suicide faster than adaptive restructuring can occur, and insisted that applying this same model to the Maya and Bronze Age shows these were not gradual transitions but catastrophic failures of social organization.

Is this characterization fair to the actual scholarly literature? Please audit it claim by claim: distinguish what accurately reflects positions in archaeology and anthropology, what subtly conflates distinct arguments made by different scholars, what misrepresents the "transformation" or "resilience" view, and whether the lecturer's inference from rapid depopulation to "societal death" follows. In particular, discuss whether treating Easter Island—a small, isolated island with no state apparatus comparable to the Maya or Hittite palaces—as an analog for those larger societies involves a category error, and whether the disappearance of monumental writing and construction necessarily means the underlying population and social practices disappeared rather than persisting in decentralized, archaeologically less visible forms.
