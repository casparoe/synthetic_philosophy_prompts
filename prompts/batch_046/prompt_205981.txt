I am a third-year undergraduate in a course called Marriage, Family, and the State. For a peer-teaching session, I need to explain to first-year philosophy students—who mostly arrive thinking marriage is either a religious rite or a bundle of legal benefits—why the debate over same-sex marriage is not resolvable without first asking what marriage is for, and why that question splits into two live but incompatible frameworks rather than one obvious answer. I want to work with a distinction I have been developing: call one framework conjugal integration, the view that marriage is a union that makes spouses prior-to-the-individuals in a shared biological and social life oriented toward generativity, so that obligations flow from the union itself; and call the other framework contractual companionship, the view that marriage formalizes an existing emotional bond between ontologically prior individuals and distributes rights without requiring any shared orientation beyond mutual consent and care.

Plan the 30-minute explanatory arc. Specify, in order:

1. The sequence in which the components must be introduced and why—especially what idea has to be in place before conjugal integration and contractual companionship can be heard as serious live options rather than caricatures, and which dependency, if violated, causes the rest to collapse into slogan-trading.

2. The one concrete example or tightly paired examples that should carry the distinction—what they must include (e.g., a specific obligation or decision point) and what they must lack (e.g., religious framing, children present, economic dependence) so that the students feel the conceptual pull of each framework rather than just labeling them.

3. The misconceptions the audience will predictably bring or form, in the likely order they will arise: for instance, that rejecting conjugal integration means treating marriage as a revocable business contract, or that accepting contractual companionship eliminates all non-consensual marital obligations. For each, indicate the precise point in the sequence where it should be headed off, and how the sequence should absorb it rather than merely deny it.

4. The check for understanding that would reveal whether they have grasped the conceptual split rather than just picked a side—preferably an application to a case they have not seen in the session, designed to show whether they can identify which framework is doing the work and whether they can spot a hidden slide between them.

5. What should be explicitly left out at this level—particular legal histories, named theologians or philosophers, comparative family law—and a brief justification for each omission, so I know I am not accidentally leaving a dependency hole.
