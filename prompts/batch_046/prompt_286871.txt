As a journalist covering philosophy of science and neuroscience, I recently joined my university's Joint Ethics and Funding Review Board for interdisciplinary consciousness research. We are deliberating over the Residual Phenomenal Protocol, a joint proposal from Philosophy and Cognitive Neuroscience that runs a six-week sensory-substitution trial with calibrated color-inverting goggles; after neural and behavioral adaptation, participants report whether any pure qualitative residue remains—an alleged subjective feature that survives even once every observable, functional, and neural property has been fully specified. The investigators treat these first-person reports as primary data for mapping experience, but half the board, invoking the quining objection linked to Chase and Sanborn within Dennett's framework, argues that qualia in this precise sense are incoherent: once all reportable properties are accounted for, no defensible remainder is left to call a pure subjective quality, so institutional funding would be directed toward investigating a conceptual pseudo-problem. The neuroscience team replies that phenomenal residue functions only as a heuristic for tracking discrepancies between first-person report and third-person prediction—analogous to using friction in physics without ontological commitment—and emphasizes that participants are fully debriefed and never deceived about the study's philosophical framing.

We need a formal decision memo—approve, reject, or approve with conditions—that can be presented to both the researchers and university administration. The document should state a clear, actionable bottom line; provide reasoning precise enough that the investigators can respond point by point rather than with generic objections; and include an honest, substantive treatment of the strongest consideration on the losing side. It must avoid requesting bibliographies, reading lists, or citations, since the board's charge is to evaluate the conceptual coherence and ethical design of the protocol itself, not to audit scholarly coverage. Written in clear but technically precise prose accessible to non-specialist administrators, the memo should reference the Residual Phenomenal Protocol specifically, and if the recommendation involves conditions or revision, it should specify exactly which conceptual assumptions or methodological steps must be altered, or if it rejects the proposal outright, it should outline a defensible path and criteria for resubmission.
