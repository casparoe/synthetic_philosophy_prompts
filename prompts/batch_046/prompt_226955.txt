I just finished a long argument with a friend about whether a legal system can genuinely contain a rule that authorizes the amendment of that very authorization rule without generating an infinite regress or smuggling in an unamendable foundation. She thinks any such circular self-reference collapses into incoherence; I think there may be a coherent meta-level architecture that allows it. Rather than rehashing that dispute, I want to step back and establish the criteria for success. If a theory of legal self-amendment is to count as adequate, what exactly must it explain? I want the full specification: the concrete phenomena it has to accommodate—say, a constitutional clause that revises its own revision conditions, or an ordinary statute that modifies the legislative competence that enacted it; the distinctions it must preserve, such as that between altering a rule within a system and replacing the system's identity conditions; the paradoxes of self-reference it must genuinely dissolve rather than dismiss by fiat; and any formal or normative constraints—perhaps about logical consistency or democratic authorization—that it cannot violate. Which of these desiderata are genuinely non-negotiable, and which are merely prejudices inherited from particular legal traditions that treat hierarchical levels as essential? Finally, which single requirement does the most damage to the existing accounts, whether they rely on rigid hierarchical levels or on circular authorization structures? A brief table scoring the major approaches against the list would be welcome, but the weight should be on the specification itself.
