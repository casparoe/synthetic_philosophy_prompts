I'm designing a conceptual taxonomy for a philosophy of mind seminar on whether an ant superorganism—exemplified by the colony sometimes referred to as Aunt Hillary—can literally possess mental states. I want a decision tree, not a list of named theories, that classifies positions according to the answers a respondent gives to concrete diagnostic questions. Each branch node should be a claim or case on which positions genuinely diverge, phrased so that someone can answer it without knowing which philosophical label it leads to. The tree must include at least the following six leaves: (i) strict eliminativist/reductionist (no colony-level mental properties exist), (ii) distributed functionalist (there is a real but subjectless collective mind), (iii) collective phenomenal consciousness (there is something it is like to be the colony, with a unified perspective), (iv) symbiotic-extended cognition (the mind's boundary includes pheromone trails, soil microbiota, and fungal networks), (v) narrative-center view (the colony has mental properties only if its behavior supports a Dennett-style center of narrative gravity), and (vi) ant-fugue antirealism (the appearance of collective intelligence is a projection from individual ant behavior with no emergent ontology). Note that the symbiotic-extended and ant-fugue positions are included as niche framing devices to keep the taxonomy off well-trodden ground.

Please organize your response in exactly these sections, in this order:

A. Framing and logic of the tree (about 100 words): Briefly state the root question and the principle of division—for instance, ontological commitment first, then internal unity, then boundary conditions.

B. The decision tree itself (about 450 words): Present the tree as nested branches. For each internal node, state the splitting question in bold, then describe the branches it creates. At each leaf, name the position and provide one sentence summarizing its core claim. Build the tree from logical space: present the questions and combinations first, then map the named positions onto them. Identify any answer combination that yields no named position—an unoccupied node—and briefly diagnose whether it is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored.

C. Separation failures and ambiguities (about 150 words): Explicitly flag any pair of leaves that this tree cannot cleanly separate, explaining why the diagnostic questions fail to distinguish them and whether an additional node would be needed.

D. The awkward commitments (about 150 words): For each of the six named leaves, state in one sentence its most counterintuitive or practically awkward commitment—the consequence that makes defenders of the view wince.

The tree should avoid asking respondents to self-identify with labels like functionalism or panpsychism. Instead, use concrete scenarios: for example, whether splitting the colony in two with maintained pheromonal bridges produces one divided mind or two; whether destroying 90 percent of the ants but preserving pheromone architecture leaves the same mental state intact; whether the colony's decision to relocate can be wrong in the same way an individual's belief can be wrong. Do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations. Do not include verbatim quotations from texts.
