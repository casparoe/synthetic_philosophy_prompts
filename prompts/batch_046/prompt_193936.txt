Student answer: Gould and Lewontin use the spandrels of San Marco—the triangular spaces between the arches supporting the dome—to argue that many biological traits are not direct adaptations. In architecture these spaces are not designed for their own sake; they are unavoidable byproducts of placing a round dome on rectangular walls. The authors claim that biological features like the human chin or aspects of brain expansion can be analogous: they arise as structural byproducts of selection acting elsewhere, not because selection directly shaped them for their current functions. This undermines the Panglossian assumption that every useful trait must have an adaptive origin.

They distinguish spandrels from exaptations. A spandrel is a byproduct that is later co-opted for a useful role—feathers, for instance, were originally spandrels derived from reptilian scales that evolved for insulation and were only later exapted for flight. An exaptation, by contrast, is a trait that was originally an adaptation for one purpose and then repurposed for another, such as the mammalian middle-ear bones that were jaw bones in ancestors and were then exapted for hearing.

As for evo-devo, I think Gould and Lewontin's critique is largely resolved by modern work on modularity and constraint. Once we understand that developmental pathways are deeply integrated and that genetic architecture creates unavoidable pleiotropic effects, we can see that spandrels are predictable architectural prices paid for robust developmental systems. Natural selection favors these stable systems, so every feature—including spandrels—can ultimately be explained by selection, whether direct or indirect. Thus the Panglossian paradigm survives in a modified form: the explanation for any trait may invoke constraint rather than direct adaptation, but selection remains the universal explanatory framework.
