My friend and I spent most of last night arguing over whether the criteria of authenticity can ever settle who the historical Jesus was, and she walked me into an argument that now strikes me as suspicious because it seems to dissolve the very subject it claims to study. Here is how it runs. (1) The standard criteria—embarrassment, dissimilarity, and multiple attestation—function only as positive indicators: if a Gospel detail is embarrassing to the early church or dissimilar to both Judaism and early Christianity, that gives us reason to treat it as authentic. (2) If a detail lacks these markers, the criteria give us no positive reason to accept it, but they do not by themselves give us reason to reject it. (3) My friend proposes what she calls the principle of exhaustive exclusion: since the evangelists had both theological motive and narrative opportunity to invent or reshape any material that served their program, a detail that carries no positive marker of authenticity should be treated as presumptively inauthentic. (4) Applying this principle rigorously strips away nearly every biographical connection in the Gospels—Jesus’ movements between Galilee and Judea, the sequence of his teachings, the social composition of his followers—because none of these connections are embarrassing or sharply dissimilar. (5) What survives is only a scattered cluster of fragments: a baptism by John, a crucifixion under Pontius Pilate, perhaps a few isolated sayings. (6) But to ask “who was the historical Jesus?” is not merely to list disconnected events; it is to identify a coherent agent with intentions, relationships, and a narrative trajectory that makes sense of those events. (7) Because the principle in (3) systematically removes every narrative thread that would bind the fragments into a person, it entails that there is no “who” left to identify—only orphaned data points with no unifying subject. (8) Therefore, if the criteria of authenticity are applied consistently and rigorously, they do not merely leave us ignorant of Jesus; they prove that the object of inquiry, a historically coherent Jesus, does not exist. (9) My friend takes this to show that historical Jesus research is self-undermining. I feel that something has slipped between (3) and (5), or perhaps between (7) and (8), but I cannot name the error. Which step should I reject? If I hold onto (3), am I forced to accept (8)? And if I reject (3), do the criteria become so weak that they can justify almost any reconstruction we prefer?
