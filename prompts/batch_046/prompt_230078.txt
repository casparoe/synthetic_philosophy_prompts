I've been discussing with a friend who works in labor policy whether the philosophical debate about work and leisure rights—the tension between Weber's Protestant work ethic, France's statutory 35-hour week, and the Japanese phenomenon of karoshi—can actually illuminate a concrete policy episode rather than remaining a set of cultural comparisons. To test this, I want to analyze the passage and initial implementation of France's Aubry laws (1998 and 2000), which reduced the legal work week to 35 hours, through what I'll call an asymmetry thesis about labor rights: modern societies tend to recognize a robust right-to-work (access to labor as a source of dignity and social recognition) but treat protection from work only negatively, as a statutory cap rather than a positive entitlement to non-instrumental leisure. The episode is well-known—the Socialist government of Lionel Jospin, the 35-hour ceiling, negotiated exemptions for smaller firms, and the subsequent conservative revisions—but I want the analysis disciplined by the conceptual framework rather than by economic outcomes alone.

Specifically, using Weber's account of the Protestant work ethic as a normative structure in which labor is a calling and unproductive idleness is morally suspect, map how the Aubry reform positioned the French state in relation to that ethic. Did the law function as a direct secular inversion (treating leisure as a civic duty comparable to labor), as a welfare compromise that left the ethic intact by permitting extensive overtime and opt-outs, or as something else entirely? Then bring in karoshi not as a comparative labor-market datum but as a limit case: the extreme negation of any right-from-work, where employment becomes an unbounded obligation that can literally kill. Does the French case reveal that a statutory leisure right can only be protected by institutional caps that the Protestant ethic constantly undermines through voluntary overwork culture? And what does the reform's political viability in 1998, followed by its partial erosion, show about whether such a right can be stabilized?

Finally, be explicit about where the Weberian framework is silent. The theory explains the moral valorization of labor and the stigma of idleness, but it does not obviously predict why a leisure-cap reform succeeded in one advanced economy and failed to spread elsewhere, nor does it account for the role of union bargaining power versus state mandate in making the 35-hour week stick initially. Say whether that silence is a weakness in applying the ethic to modern labor rights, or simply a boundary that shows the theory was never meant to be a predictive model of legislative success. In other words, let the case illuminate the theory's explanatory limits as clearly as you let the theory illuminate the case.
