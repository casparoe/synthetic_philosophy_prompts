Consider the institutional and historiographical practice—exemplified by the post-1960s controversy over the historian Fritz Fischer’s attribution of primary responsibility to Germany for the outbreak of the First World War and codified in the war-guilt clause of the Versailles Treaty—of treating a nation-state as the principal bearer of both causal and moral responsibility for an international conflict. Reconstruct the implicit philosophical theory that this practice presupposes: what conception of collective agency, historical causation, and distributive blame must be in place for it to make sense to assign guilt to an entire polity rather than to specific decision-makers? Distinguish strictly what the practice requires from what merely accompanies it—specifically, examine whether the historiographical inference of a unitary national will from elite state archives commits us to an intentionalist ontology of collective action, and whether that ontology is consistent with the legal practice of imposing collective reparations on a population that includes non-culpable individuals. Assess whether these implicit commitments form a coherent view, identify which rival theories of collective responsibility—such as purely structuralist accounts that deny collective intentionality, or strictly distributive individualist accounts—are quietly ruled out, and specify what would have to change in either historical methodology or international legal doctrine if we replaced the practice’s underlying view with a straightforwardly structuralist or individualist alternative. Work from the conceptual architecture of the practice itself, without recourse to bibliographies, specific textual citations, or lists of further readings.
