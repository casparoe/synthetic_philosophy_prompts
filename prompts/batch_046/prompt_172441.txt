I have recently begun working in a cognitive rehabilitation center, and the question of when it is legitimate to ascribe beliefs and desires to patients with severe neural damage has become personally urgent. Here are my verdicts. When a patient with dense amnesia repeatedly asks for her deceased husband and shows distress at his absence, I think it correct to say she believes he is alive and wants his company; yet when the same patient is shown his photograph and shows no recognition, I think it wrong to ascribe any belief about him. A patient with frontal-lobe injury who strikes a nurse without warning or emotional display strikes me as lacking any genuine desire to harm—the behavior seems mechanistic. By contrast, a different patient with comparable injury who resists every alteration to her morning routine with visible anguish clearly wants that consistency preserved. My young child demanding candy plainly wants it. When a conversational agent trained on clinical transcripts outputs that it is frightened, I do not think it believes anything, though colleagues argue functional equivalence suffices. I have also been running a double-blind ascription audit, in which caregivers record behavioral markers without knowing diagnoses; inter-observer agreement collapses for the aggression case but holds for the routine case. Is there a coherent principle—perhaps tied to integrated emotional-narrative coherence or to something like functional surrogacy—that captures these judgments? Which verdicts must I abandon, particularly regarding the artificial agent and the two brain-damaged patients? And what should the principle say about a hypothetical neural prosthetic that restores fluent verbal self-reporting of mental states without restoring any subjective experience?
