I am preparing a draft for my junior-year philosophy of history seminar, and I would appreciate careful comments on the argument and its framing before I revise it into a full paper. My instructor has asked us to engage with the classic tension between covering-law explanations, of the kind associated with Carl Hempel's deductive-nomological model, and narrative explanations, as developed by philosophers of history such as Arthur Danto and Louis Mink, but she also encouraged us to avoid simply rehearsing the standard comparison. To that end, I want to propose what I am calling a "narratively bounded covering-law" account, applied specifically to the adoption and rejection of the Gregorian calendar reform in 1582 across Catholic, Protestant, and Orthodox territories.

In my draft, I argue that Hempel's framework is often dismissed in historiography because historical events appear unique, yet uniqueness is really a problem about the selection of initial conditions rather than about the absence of general regularities. I suggest that narrative structure does not compete with covering-law explanation but instead performs the work of demarcating which conditions are historically relevant—what I describe, borrowing from a discussion with a friend, as "temporal sovereignty," or the authority to define official time. Applying this to the calendar case, I propose that a law-like regularity about institutional resistance to centralized reform might hold across the three regions, but the initial conditions—whether papal authority, local princely autonomy, or conciliar tradition counts as legitimate—are constituted differently by the narrative each community tells about its own continuity. Thus the explanation is neither purely nomological nor purely narrative, but a covering law whose boundary conditions are themselves narratively constructed.

Could you comment on whether this hybrid genuinely avoids collapsing back into one of the two traditional positions, and whether the calendar-reform example is adequate to bear the weight I place on it? I am particularly concerned that I may be misrepresenting the scope of Hempel's statistical explanations or that I am attributing to narrative theorists a view about initial-condition selection that they would reject. I would also value any suggestions about whether the idea of "temporal sovereignty" adds real conceptual clarity or merely introduces an unnecessary neologism. Please do not provide a bibliography or list of further readings; I am working only with the texts assigned in our course and with my own reasoning.
