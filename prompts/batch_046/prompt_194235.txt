In my philosophy-of-religion study group, we have been working through arguments from divine hiddenness—the idea that if an omnibenevolent being who desires relationship with humans existed, we should expect far more widespread, unambiguous manifestations of that presence than we actually observe. At our last meeting, one participant dismissed the entire line of reasoning with the phrase "absence of evidence is not evidence of absence," using it as if it were a single, self-evident principle that blocks any inference from God's apparent non-intervention to a lower probability that God exists. I am sure the slogan is doing ambiguous work, but I need help seeing exactly how many different claims are packed into it.

Could you lay out, as a clear menu, the distinct precise theses that sentence might express—such as a formal logical claim about the difference between lack of proof and proof of lack; a probabilistic claim about priors, likelihoods, and the conditions under which silence should update our beliefs; an epistemic claim about the limits of human observation or the possibility of hidden reasons; and a methodological claim about where the burden of proof lies? For each reading, please say whether it is trivially true, substantively defensible, or plainly false, and—crucially—whether it actually supports the move my friend wanted to make, namely that the pervasive evidential silence in the world carries no weight at all against theism. I am especially interested in whether the slogan applies symmetrically to hypotheses that make risky, widespread predictions—where absence in the expected locations is genuinely surprising—versus hypotheses that can be adjusted after the fact to explain away any absence, so please distinguish structural evidential silence from mere local search failure. To keep the analysis grounded, separate what is conceptually settled—such as the bare logical point that failing to find X is not automatically equivalent to proving that X is not there—from what remains genuinely contested among people who work on this topic, for example whether sustained evidential silence rationally lowers credence in an interventionist deity, or whether theistic responses that invoke soul-making, epistemic distance, or free will successfully neutralize the hiddenness inference. Do not present any one contested interpretation as the consensus view, and please avoid bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; I want pure conceptual disambiguation rather than a survey of the literature.
