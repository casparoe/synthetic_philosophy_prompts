I am a journalist who covers the history and philosophy of science, and I have been asked to deliver the opening remarks at a retirement symposium honoring Dr. Elena Voss, a historian of technology whose career has centered on a single, counterintuitive thesis: that major industrial transitions are not macro-inevitable trends but contingent cascades triggered by microscopic institutional failures. The audience is deliberately mixed—half are her colleagues in history and philosophy of science who are fluent in her work on path dependence and nonlinear dynamics, and half are university administrators, former students, and family members who are not. I need an address of roughly ten minutes that uses the old nursery rhyme about the lost nail—the shoe lost for want of it, then the horse, then the rider, then the battle—not as decorative folklore but as the actual logical architecture of the speech.

Please construct the address so that chaos theory's concept of sensitive dependence on initial conditions gives that proverb rigorous philosophical content, rather than leaving it as a vague metaphor. Then apply the framework to her two signature comparative cases—technological lock-in, exemplified by the persistence of the QWERTY keyboard layout, and resource-transition lock-in, exemplified by the British turn to coal over timber in the early modern period—treating them not as parallel anecdotes but as structurally analogous manifestations of causal fragility. The speech must honor her insistence that contingency is never mere randomness but a precise form of causal vulnerability, and it must do so without lecturing the non-specialists or offering false comfort that history is predictable. It should feel like a journalist's attempt to make a technical idea public, not like a dissertation, and it should avoid any bibliography or appeal to sources the audience could not follow without specialist knowledge.
