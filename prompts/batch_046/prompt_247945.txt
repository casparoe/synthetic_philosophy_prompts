As a doctoral student in moral philosophy working on intergenerational ethics, I am developing an argument about the procreation asymmetry for a chapter draft. Rather than treating the asymmetry as a straightforward conflict between reasons to avoid suffering and reasons to produce happiness, I want to examine a more granular framing I am calling a conditional-threshold account. On this view, the normative force of not creating a suffering life derives not from an existence-independent harm to the potential child, but from a relational failure: the parents' decision to procreate violates a conditional obligation that is activated only when the expected welfare falls below a threshold calibrated to the parents' own justified life-plan commitments, not to an absolute welfare baseline. Meanwhile, the absence of any parallel obligation to create a flourishing life is explained by the fact that those same commitments do not generate positive duties to realize high welfare states in others. Please explain the central conceptual issue here: does this conditional-threshold framing genuinely dissolve the procreation asymmetry by relocating all normative work into the prior specification of parental commitments, or does it preserve an independent asymmetry at the level of obligation-activation conditions? In your explanation, address specifically whether the framing can consistently maintain that a couple with abundant resources has no duty to conceive a child who would live well, without implicitly committing them to a broader, aggregation-sensitive duty to improve expected welfare across possible lives.
