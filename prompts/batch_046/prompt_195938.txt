I'm drafting the final examination for an upper-level undergraduate seminar titled Philosophy of History and Political Judgment. Over the term we have examined why analogies to Munich, Vietnam, and the so-called Thucydides trap persist in strategic reasoning despite repeated predictive failures, and I want a question that students cannot answer by simply reciting these standard critiques. Instead, I want them to work through a concrete tension: that analogical reasoning in policy contexts fails not only when the historical mapping is descriptively inaccurate, but also when it suppresses normative commitments under the appearance of empirical comparison.

Please compose one essay prompt suitable for a 75-minute sitting with an approximate word limit of 1,000 words. The prompt should present a hypothetical scenario in which three factions within a defense ministry invoke these analogies in direct conflict—one side citing Munich to justify preemptive military commitment, another citing Vietnam to resist overextension in an asymmetric conflict, and a third framing the situation through the Thucydides trap to treat rising-power dynamics as structurally predetermined. Students should be required to identify the distinct logical form operating in each case—causal mechanism, institutional learning, or structural determinism—and then evaluate whether any neutral framework exists for adjudicating among them, or whether the disagreement is irreducibly a conflict of practical judgment masked as an empirical dispute. Crucially, the prompt should ask them to articulate what normative content is lost when these three analogies are treated as competing predictions resolvable by more data, and why the structural-determinist formulation is especially resistant to straightforward empirical falsification.

Alongside the question, provide a model answer of roughly 800 words that demonstrates the expected level of analysis: it should clearly distinguish descriptive similarity from normative salience, identify the problem of base-rate neglect in selecting a single historical parallel, explain how institutional-learning analogies depend on contested counterfactuals, and clarify why analogies that invoke structural inevitability shift the burden of proof in ways that make them immune to conventional disconfirmation. Finally, provide a marking scheme totaling 25 points, divided into five criteria of 5 points each—accurate reconstruction of the three analogies; correct identification of their logical forms; analysis of the normative commitments each analogies suppresses; evaluation of whether practical judgment can be eliminated in favor of empirical resolution; and clarity and argumentative structure—together with a brief one-line note on the typical failure mode for each criterion, particularly the common error of treating analogical conflict as something that further historical research could settle.
