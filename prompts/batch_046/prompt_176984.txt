As a doctoral student in ancient metaphysics and the philosophy of artifacts, I am trying to parse a passage from Plutarch's Life of Theseus, chapter 23: "The ship wherein Theseus and the youth of Athens returned from Crete had thirty oars, and was preserved by the Athenians down even to the time of Demetrius Phalereus, for they took away the old planks as they decayed, putting in new and strong timber in their places, insomuch that this ship became a standing example among the philosophers, for the logical question of things that grow; one side holding that the ship remained the same, and the other contending that it was not the same." Most contemporary discussions treat the paradox through modern mereological essentialism or material constitution, but Plutarch's phrase "logical question of things that grow" (auxēsis) suggests to me that he frames the case not as reconstructed artifact identity but as continuous organic increase—closer to biological persistence. Hobbes's well-known extension—rebuilding a second vessel from the discarded planks—seems to shift the problem explicitly toward numerical identity of matter versus form. Can you help me understand whether there is a coherent ancient reading on which the ship remains the same precisely because it grows like an organism rather than being replaced part by part, and how that interpretation would differ conceptually from Hobbes's framing?
