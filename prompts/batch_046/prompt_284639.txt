I have recently become personally invested in a debate within my philosophy of action study group about whether Strawson’s reactive attitudes can genuinely be directed at collective or institutional agents without losing their second-personal character, and this bears on how I understand my own resentment toward faceless administrative decisions—responses that seem moral but lack an identifiable individual target. A fellow participant and I have argued for months about the metaphysical status of these attitudes: one of us maintains that reactive attitudes such as resentment and gratitude are essentially interpersonal responses to quality of will, and that being an appropriate target of them is constitutive of moral responsibility irrespective of determinism; the other maintains that these attitudes are contingent psychological tracking mechanisms that indicate but do not constitute responsibility, which is actually grounded in functional roles within social practices of accountability and could survive the attenuation or elimination of affective response.

We wish to produce a formal adversarial-collaboration document that both of us could sign without either misrepresenting our view. Please draft it with the following structure: first, a numbered section of agreed claims about Strawson’s general framework, about the irrelevance of libertarian free will to ordinary responsibility practices, and about the shared observation that reactive attitudes can be distributed across institutional roles in ways that obscure the individual will—specifically addressing whether this phenomenon challenges the interpersonal core of the framework or merely extends it; second, a disagreements table stating each position in wording its own proponent accepts, precisely locating whether the dispute is about constitution, explanation, or normative justification, and explicitly distinguishing substantive disagreement from verbal disagreement over terms like quality of will or fittingness; third, the cruxes—sub-questions whose resolution would move at least one side—especially whether vicarious reactive attitudes directed at bureaucratic systems are genuinely second-personal in Strawson’s sense, and whether a deterministic world that eliminated reactive affect would undermine accountability practices or merely alter their phenomenology; and fourth, for each crux, a concrete case or argumentative scenario both sides agree is relevant, together with each side’s explicit prediction about what that case demonstrates. The document should be written in neutral, formal prose, should avoid bibliographic appeals, citations, or appeals to specific texts, and should not ask for reading lists or further sources.
