ok so im trying to figure out what a theory of humanitarian intervention actually has to deliver, and i feel like people usually just list one or two conditions and call it done. but if im being honest, the hard part is holding all the constraints together without them eating eachother. im especially thinking about kosovo, iraq, and libya, since those seem to sit differently but people rarely explain exactly how.

so heres my attempt at the full spec, but i want to know if im missing pieces or asking for contradictions.

1. it has to explain selectivity: kosovo and libya got airstrikes, iraq happened for other reasons, rwanda mostly didnt, and the atrocity levels dont clearly sort them. whatever the criterion is, it cant just be 'bad enough' because thats not doing the work.

2. it needs a line between protection and regime change. in kosovo the goal was technically not overthrowing milosevic, in libya it pretty clearly became removing gaddafi, and iraq was regime change from the start with humanitarian rhetoric added later. the theory should say whether regime change is a permissible escalation or a category mistake.

3. aftermath obligations. if you break a state to save people, do you own the reconstruction? or is the moral job done the moment the bombing stops?

4. moral hazard on the rebel side. if armed groups learn that provoking a massacre gets them NATO air cover, the intervention criterion might literally cause the atrocity its meant to prevent. the theory needs to either show why that doesnt happen, or build in safeguards, or admit its a real cost.

5. sovereignty conditions. not just 'sovereignty is conditional on protecting citizens' but exactly who certifies the failure, and whether that certifier can be self-interested.

6. consistency with internationl law as it actually exists, not just as philosophers wish it were.

which of these are hard requirements and which are liberal-internationalist preferences? and is there a joint unsatisfiability hiding in there - maybe between strict selectivity and avoiding moral hazard? also, briefly, how do the 'responsibility to protect' framework and a pure just-war approach each do against this list?
