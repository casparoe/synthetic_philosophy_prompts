I'm preparing the closing document for a semester-long adversarial collaboration in a philosophy of action seminar. The two parties are the "unitary preference-revision" theorists, who hold that hyperbolic discounting fully explains weakness of will and that talk of an executive "will" failing is a folk-psychological misdescription of intertemporal preference reversal, and the "bifurcated agency" theorists, who maintain that akrasia involves a genuine failure of an executive volitional faculty and that hyperbolic discount curves describe only the symptom, not the mechanism—particularly because commitment devices and self-binding strategies can succeed even when the underlying discount function remains unchanged. We want a joint statement both sides could sign, and we have agreed in advance that the document should inject and address what we are calling the intertemporal binding problem: whether an agent at one time can genuinely constrain the preferences of her future self without merely altering the choice architecture available to that future self.

Please draft the document. Begin with a numbered section of agreed claims: about the descriptive reality of hyperbolic discounting in human and animal behavior; about the existence of cases where agents act against their own acknowledged best judgment; about the efficacy of some commitment mechanisms; and about the inadequacy of purely exponential discounting to capture observed reversal patterns. Then provide a disagreements table: for each camp, state in wording that side would accept the precise claim that separates them—specifically whether there exists a distinct executive faculty whose failure constitutes akrasia; whether hyperbolic discounting is a property of the preference function or of the decision procedure; and whether the intertemporal binding problem is a genuine philosophical problem or a pseudo-problem generated by reifying the self across time. Next, identify the cruxes—particularly whether successful self-binding requires a change in the underlying discount rate or only in the decision environment, and whether phenomenological reports of "struggle" are best interpreted as conflict between preferences or as conflict between a preference and a stable executive plan. For each crux, specify one concrete case or experimental result both sides agree is relevant (for instance, the effectiveness of precommitment contracts in smoking cessation versus the failure of willpower alone in the same population), and record in advance what each camp predicts about that case—whether the unitary camp expects success only when future choice sets are physically restricted, and whether the bifurcated camp expects success only when an executive plan is actively maintained across time. Conclude by noting any part of the dispute that may be verbal rather than substantive, with precise reasoning about which formulations dissolve upon clarification.
