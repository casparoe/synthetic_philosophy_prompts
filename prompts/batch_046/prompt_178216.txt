You serve as a senior program evaluator for a municipal well-being board that applies a hedonist framework to score community wellness proposals: an intervention is treated as welfare-promoting only insofar as it produces pleasure, with pleasure functioning as the single common currency of value. Your board receives about a dozen applications each month—ranging from sensory-therapy studios to philosophical discussion circles to grief-counseling collectives—and you must issue a preliminary yes, revise, or reject recommendation within thirty minutes per file, relying on a reusable checklist rather than ad hoc intuition. The recurring conceptual obstacle is the heterogeneity problem for hedonism: the pleasures participants report share no obvious phenomenal feature. The relief of a chronic-pain patient, the visceral rush of a cold-plunge session, and the quiet satisfaction of solving an abstract puzzle all register as positive, yet they feel phenomenally unlike one another. Your task is to operationalize the hedonist claim—that there is nevertheless a single welfare-relevant kind these states instantiate—into a decision procedure that a non-specialist reviewer can apply repeatedly without philosophical training. The checklist should proceed in ordered steps: first, a screening test that distinguishes genuine pleasure from mere preference-satisfaction or the mere absence of discomfort; second, a cross-category comparison protocol that attempts to place heterogeneous experiences on one welfare scale and explicitly marks where incommensurability threatens to break the metric; third, designated judgment-call nodes—clearly flagged for the board chair’s sign-off—where phenomenological description alone cannot settle whether two experiences belong to the same kind; and fourth, a hard-case filter for “mixed-state” proposals, such as an intense endurance-training program that imposes acute present suffering in exchange for delayed euphoria, specifying whether present and future pleasures are to be weighted, summed, or treated as non-comparable. Apply the procedure step-by-step to three hypothetical applications: a community garden project valued primarily for meditative calm; a competitive debate league valued for intellectual exhilaration; and a high-intensity physical-conditioning program that you personally believe is beneficial but that the checklist might reject. For each case, record the evaluation path, flag any unavoidable judgment calls, and state the final recommendation. Then specify exactly what the procedure structurally cannot adjudicate—for instance, whether goods such as autonomous achievement or meaningful relationships can be fully reduced to pleasure without remainder, or whether negative states can be assigned precise cardinal weights—and explain why those boundaries are built into the theory rather than being merely practical time constraints. Please write your response in American English.
