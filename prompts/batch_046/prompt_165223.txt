A philosophy newsletter I follow recently summarized Frank Ramsey's view on the meaning of life. The summary claims Ramsey treats the question as a linguistic category error: the word meaning applies properly to symbols and sentences, not to a human life taken as a totality, so asking what life means is structurally confused—similar in form to asking what color Tuesday is. Once the grammatical illusion is dissolved, there is no remaining metaphysical fact to discover. The author further argues that Ramsey's dissolution is not merely therapeutic, in the style of later Wittgensteinian quietism, but actively constructive: with no hidden purpose to uncover, rational agency collapses into a subjective expected-utility framework in which an agent distributes belief and desire across finite outcomes according to personal probabilities. On this reading, values are not independent non-cognitive commitments but are fully reconstructed from betting dispositions, and philosophy's task is not to justify values but to clarify the decision structures in which they are embedded. The summary concludes that Ramsey therefore anticipated modern rationalist decision theory and that he explicitly rejected any conception of value that cannot be derived from an agent's probabilistic preferences. This sounds mostly right—Ramsey was indeed a pragmatist about belief, developed a subjective theory of probability tied to action, and was skeptical of metaphysical pseudo-questions—but I worry the account projects his mathematical economics onto his philosophy of language in a way that flattens his actual position. Is this a fair characterization? Please distinguish what is accurate in Ramsey's actual dissolution of the problem, what is a plausible but simplifying projection, and what he would likely deny—particularly regarding whether he really reduced ethics and value to decision-theoretic optimization.
