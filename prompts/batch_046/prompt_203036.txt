I've been thinking on my own about how groups can hold beliefs that none of the members fully share, and I came across the idea of the discursive dilemma—where a group votes on premises separately and gets one conclusion, but if they vote directly on that conclusion they get the opposite. I'm planning to present my own take on this next week at a philosophy discussion group I joined through the community center. The audience isn't academic philosophers; they're mostly engineers, a cognitive-science graduate student, and a logic instructor at the local college. They care about consistency and clear reasoning, they tend to see contradictions as mistakes to be fixed rather than deep structural problems, and they're skeptical of formal "social choice" machinery being applied to real human judgment because they think it ignores how people actually weigh reasons together.

My thesis is that the discursive dilemma isn't fundamentally about voting rules but about how we define a group's belief when individuals hold judgments that aren't fully consistent. I want to argue that if we move from binary yes-or-no judgments to graded confidence—say, "probably true to degree 0.7"—the dilemma collapses in one direction, because you can aggregate premises with weighted averaging. But I think it then reappears in a more subtle form: the aggregation of confidence levels across premises doesn't uniquely determine the confidence in the conclusion unless we impose an extra rule about how reasons combine, and different choices of that rule reproduce the same contradiction at the level of weights rather than votes. So the problem isn't solved; it's relocated from the conclusion to the architecture of reason-weighting.
