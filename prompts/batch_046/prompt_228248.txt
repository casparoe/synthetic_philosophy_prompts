I am currently taking an upper-level seminar on normative ethics, and we have spent several weeks on the problem of whether maximal moral goodness is genuinely desirable—the debate often framed around the figure of the moral saint. In our discussions, a recurring argumentative pattern has emerged that I cannot locate in the literature, and I want to know whether it has an established designation or whether it remains an unnamed comparative move.

The pattern works as follows. A speaker grants that moral value is genuinely good but denies that its unqualified maximization produces the best life. Rather than resting solely on the familiar claim that moral perfection crowds out personal projects, the speaker constructs parallel cases in adjacent domains of value—imagine a life dedicated with equal intensity to aesthetic appreciation or to epistemic acquisition, in which every resource of time and attention is optimized for beauty or for true belief, leaving no space for friendship, play, or rest. The speaker then asks whether we would regard those parallel lives as paradigms of human flourishing. If we hesitate, the speaker takes that hesitation as evidence that moral value, like aesthetic or epistemic value, possesses a kind of saturation point: beyond a certain threshold, its single-minded pursuit transforms from a constituent of a good life into a monotonic excess that undermines the plural structure of flourishing. The move is not merely an appeal to pluralism, because pluralism usually says that different values must be balanced; this move specifically uses cross-domain analogues to establish an internal ceiling on any one value.

I have tried to describe this without importing the technical vocabulary that might give the answer away, but I am unsure whether philosophers have named this particular analogical structure—perhaps as a form of domain-parity reasoning, or as an implicit appeal to the architecture of practical reason, or whether it is simply an unrecognized variant of the argument from personal projects. Could you help me identify the nearest established concepts, explain exactly how the pattern I have described differs from each—particularly from standard value pluralism and from the familiar critique of moral saints as lacking personal projects—and if no precise term exists, propose a clear definition and boundary conditions for what I am describing?
