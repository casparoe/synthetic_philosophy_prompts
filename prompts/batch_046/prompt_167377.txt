I'm working through the Hart-Devlin exchange for a jurisprudence seminar, and my study group keeps circling the same point without resolving it. The familiar framing is straightforward: Devlin argues that law may legitimately enforce a shared public morality because society's cohesion depends on it—he appeals to widespread feelings of intolerance, indignation, and disgust as evidence that a practice threatens social disintegration, much as the law suppresses treason or subversion. Hart counters with the harm principle, insisting that private immorality is not the state's business unless it causes tangible harm to others, and he accuses Devlin of conflating critical morality—what people ought to believe—with positive morality—the beliefs people actually hold. So far, so standard.

But here is the conceptual knot that keeps tripping us up. If Devlin genuinely takes the intensity of social feeling as his diagnostic for when law should intervene, then he appears to be treating morality as a social fact: whatever the majority finds intolerable is, for legal purposes, corrupting. That looks like a form of conventionalism. Yet Devlin also writes as though some practices are genuinely corrupt, not merely destabilizing—he seems to think that the law protects people from being corrupted by immorality, not just that it prevents social collapse. How can he maintain both that the relevant moral standard is defined by actual social sentiment and that this standard can be normatively correct? Conversely, Hart's distinction between critical and positive morality seems to require that we can evaluate social conventions against an independent critical standard. But if Hart needs such an independent standard to ground the harm principle—say, to decide what counts as harm—does that not smuggle in its own meta-ethical commitments? In other words, is the debate really about legal moralism versus liberal neutrality, or does it ultimately turn on whether either side can avoid collapsing into either conventionalism or an unstated moral realism?

Could you explain this conceptual tension carefully? I am not asking for a bibliography or for summaries of their books, but rather for a clear articulation of whether Devlin's disintegration argument can be separated from conventionalism, whether Hart's critical-morality framework avoids hidden metaphysical baggage, and whether there is a coherent intermediate position that preserves the sociological insight without surrendering normative judgment. If possible, use a concrete hypothetical—perhaps a society in which the majority's feelings of disgust toward a harmless private practice are intense but widely acknowledged to be irrational—to test whether each theory gives a consistent answer.
