I keep running into this decision-structure that feels familiar but I can't place. You make a choice now that only pays off if you'll later do something that, at the time, looks like a bad deal. The classic version is driving some stranger through the desert for a promised reward, except they only pay if you actually meant to do it for free; once you're home, taking the cash feels like the smart move, but if you'd take it then they never pay in the first place. I see the same loop in AI alignment talks about binding future versions of a system to costly actions. Is there a specific name for this two-stage trap where being rational now requires committing to be rationaly "irrational" later, and the whole thing collapses if that later step isn't credable? I know Parfit wrote about hitchhiking and rational irrationality, but I'm asking about the exact structure: is that the same thing, or is there a narrower concept that captures the dependancy between the credability of the future move and the payoff of the present one? If not, how should I think about its edges — like, does it apply when the later "irrationality" is just a preference change rather than a precommitment failure?
