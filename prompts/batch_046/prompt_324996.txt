Write opening remarks (approximately seven minutes, in American English) for an interdisciplinary workshop on genocide, modernity, and collective memory. The audience is split between historians versed in the Historikerstreit, sociologists familiar with Zygmunt Bauman’s argument that the Holocaust was enabled—not contradicted—by modern bureaucratic rationality, and non-specialist attendees, including descendants, who will not recognize technical terminology. Do not provide a bibliography, citations, or a reading list; do not name scholars beyond Bauman and the general uniqueness-versus-comparability debate. Build the speech around one conceptual distinction you introduce: “analytic comparability” versus “commemorative uniqueness.” Use Bauman’s idea of moral adiaphorization—the institutional separation of technical efficiency from ethical judgment—to show why comparing the Holocaust structurally with other modern state-organized atrocities need not violate its commemorative singularity, and why treating uniqueness solely as an analytic claim risks obscuring the bureaucratic mechanisms that made the event possible. The remarks must do real structural work: they should be undeliverable without holding both positions in tension; they must acknowledge to the historians that comparison can normalize; they must affirm to the family members that uniqueness is a moral posture rather than a historical taxonomy; and they must conclude that the workshop’s purpose is not to settle whether the Holocaust is unique, but to ask what kind of modern institutional logic produces events that require both comparison and incomparability to be fully understood.
