I read a summary of the alignment problem in a recent long-form essay by a prominent, unnamed AI researcher. The author writes: "Alignment is essentially a single technical problem with two failure modes. First is reward hacking, where the optimizer exploits a gap between the specified reward function and the intended goal. Second is instrumental convergence, where the system seeks power and resources as subgoals regardless of its terminal objective. If you bound the utility function and build in corrigibility, both modes are neutralized, and alignment is achieved. The separate worry that humans don't have coherent or unified values—what philosophers call value pluralism—is a problem for moral epistemology, not for alignment engineering. Once value learning is in place, the system infers preferences from human behavior, so pluralism doesn't create any special technical obstacle. Most alignment researchers treat these as settled points: there is one core problem, split into reward-design and corrigibility, and everything else belongs to ethics or sociology."

I am inclined to think this is mostly right but probably too tidy. I want you to audit the summary: go through it part by part and say what a researcher working on alignment foundations would accept, what they would wince at as a subtle misrepresentation or overstatement, and what is simply wrong. In particular, take a definite position—do not just lay out options—on whether value pluralism can be cleanly separated from the technical alignment problem in the way the author claims, and on whether bounding a utility function plus corrigibility actually addresses instrumental convergence. Argue for your specific reading of what the alignment problem includes and whether it is genuinely one problem or several distinct ones. Be self-critical: flag where your own interpretation might depend on contested assumptions about what counts as "technical" versus philosophical.
