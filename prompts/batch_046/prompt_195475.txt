I serve on the advisory board of a regional water and sanitation authority that must decide how to allocate its fixed annual maintenance budget. One path is to continue routine infrastructure upkeep, which will almost certainly prevent seasonal cholera outbreaks in three specific districts—approximately 12,000 people affected, with a well-documented reduction in mortality. The other path, proposed by an outside consultant, is to divert nearly the entire budget into a single, highly speculative geoengineering trial. The trial carries an estimated 0.002% probability of success, but if it succeeds it would permanently resolve freshwater scarcity for hundreds of millions of people globally. The consultant argues that multiplying the magnitude of the potential outcome by its probability yields a figure so large that it morally dominates the certain local benefits; he claims that treating such a small probability as disqualifying is merely a cognitive bias, and that any rational agent with the authority's resources must fund the trial. Several board members feel the recommendation is not just impractical but deeply wrong, yet they struggle to articulate why—particularly because they genuinely believe that saving more lives is preferable to saving fewer, and they do not wish to defend mere parochialism. I want a committed answer that takes a definite position and gives its reasons, not one that surveys theories and leaves the choice to me. Is the consultant correct that this kind of quantitative comparison obligates the board to choose the trial? Reconstruct the philosophical structure underlying the disagreement—specifically the conflict between treating near-certain, bounded welfare gains and treating astronomically large but probabilistically remote gains as decisive—without relying on generic practical advice. Say clearly whether the board's resistance can be philosophically justified, and on what grounds; if the resistance is indeed an error, explain precisely which intuitive judgment should be abandoned. In your response, please take a definite stance: either defend the board's right to reject the trial on principled grounds, or defend the consultant's conclusion that rational moral reasoning requires accepting it.
