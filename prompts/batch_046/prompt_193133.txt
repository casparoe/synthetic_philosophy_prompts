I just came out of a long, slightly exhausting argument with a close friend about tranquilism — the position that well-being consists solely in the absence of craving and suffering, with positive pleasure, achievement, or desire-fulfillment carrying no intrinsic value whatsoever. She kept insisting that this is indistinguishable from advocating for non-existence, while I tried to defend it as a coherent, even austere, theory of value rather than mere pessimism. To help me think through whether the view can be articulated without collapsing into absurdity, please write a satirical, deadpan promotional brochure — approximately 600 words — for a luxury wellness retreat called The Zero Point that markets itself as having achieved a fully realized tranquilist environment. Write in the hyper-serene, hyper-precise register of high-end spa copy: talk about curated absence, bespoke non-desire, and personalized elimination of disturbance. Every amenity must be a literal subtraction — soundproofed silence, meals that are not served, climate-controlled absence of temperature variation, a library containing no books. The satire should arise from internal consistency: if only absence is intrinsically good, then any feature that could generate attachment or pleasure is actually a design flaw, so the brochure should apologize for unavoidable residual comforts and frame the ultimate goal as a state in which the guest no longer wishes to depart — presented as the highest five-star testimonial. Include a sidebar titled Our Philosophical Pillars that quietly distinguishes between the elimination of suffering (intrinsically valuable) and the provision of pleasure (merely instrumental, and therefore omitted from the package), referencing the asymmetry between suffering and pleasure without naming any specific living philosopher. Do not break the deadpan voice with winks or explicit moralizing; the reader should feel the real philosophical tension — that if absence truly is the only good, then luxury is the wrong vocabulary, and yet the vocabulary is all we have. The piece should be fair enough that a tranquilist could recognize their own commitments, but sharp enough that a reader sees exactly why the view threatens to erase the distinction between serene living and the cessation of consciousness.
