My grandmother, celebrated in our town for decades of community organizing, died recently leaving behind extensive private recordings and letters. Among them are voice memos in which she privately admits to having abused one of my siblings during childhood—evidence that was never made public and for which there was never any redress. The town is now assembling a permanent digital archive to honor her legacy, and the curators have asked me to contribute her personal materials; they are unaware of the abusive content. I see three options: contribute everything unedited, which preserves a truthful record but forces my sibling’s trauma into public view and violates my grandmother’s dignity in death; destroy the damaging files, which protects my sibling and allows the community to mourn the person they knew, but eliminates the only acknowledgment of the harm done; or selectively edit them out, which spares both parties but feels like an act of falsification. How should I weigh the duty to preserve historical truth against the case for allowing both survivors and communities to forget, and is there a principled distinction between destroying evidence and merely choosing not to broadcast it?
