I run a graduate seminar in political philosophy, 10 to 12 participants, 90 minutes. Design a structured deliberation—no lecture up front, and do not name Fukuyama or “the end of history” until the debrief—that makes students experience the tension between the satisfaction of universal recognition (isothymia) and the loss of the adversarial structure that, on Fukuyama’s account, gives collective action its historical weight (megalothymia). Arrange roles and rules so that the group first builds a cooperative decision procedure that succeeds precisely because every participant’s claim is treated as equally valid; then reveal, through a second round with hidden payoff rules, that this perfect equality has eliminated the stakes that made the first-round cooperation feel meaningful. Participants should construct the hollow outcome themselves and only notice the emptiness after the fact. The debrief must map: which moment corresponds to the resolution of thymos, which to the disappearance of megalothymia, and why the resulting “post-political” condition feels like success and failure at once. Say explicitly what will predictably go wrong—e.g., some groups inventing an artificial enemy to restore stakes, others mistaking boredom for injustice—and how the facilitator should treat these moves as data rather than errors. Finally, be frank about the feature of Fukuyama’s thesis the exercise cannot reproduce without direct instruction: that liberal democracy resolves recognition not merely by agreement but by making ideological opposition conceptually unavailable, and that this closure is meant to be a historical achievement rather than a procedural glitch. What should participants leave seeing about whether meaningful collective action can survive once recognition is fully equalized and no adversarial horizon remains?
