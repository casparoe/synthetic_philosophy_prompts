I am a retired physicist who has recently begun studying philosophy in earnest, and I now serve repeatedly as an independent mediator for collaborative disputes in small interdisciplinary research groups—typically four to ten members, with shared equipment but separate funding lines. I want to turn Confucian role ethics into an operational decision procedure I can run in about fifteen minutes before each session, to determine whether a dispute is resolvable by clarifying relational obligations (yi and li within specific roles) or has crossed into a domain where role ethics offers no guidance and must be handed to institutional arbitration.

Treat the five cardinal relationships as analogues to modern research roles—principal investigator to junior researcher, peer to peer, senior to junior across labs, institution to researcher in the funding dimension, and a niche extension I propose: the maker-instrument relation, in which a shared dataset or apparatus derives a secondary moral status analogous to a family member because it embodies accumulated labor and constrains future action.

Structure the deliverable as follows. First, a three-step filter to classify the dispute, with the exact question at each step, what counts as a sufficient answer, and the precise stopping point where the procedure yields a no or hands the case away. Second, a checklist distinguishing role-obligation violations from mere preference conflicts, with explicit flags for judgment calls that cannot be eliminated by further fact-gathering. Third, apply the procedure to two hypothetical cases: (a) a senior researcher insists on corresponding authorship for a study in which she contributed only conceptual framing, against the junior researcher who performed all experiments; (b) a conflict over whether a shared spectrometer should be reserved for one group’s time-sensitive measurements during another group’s scheduled maintenance window. The second case should produce an answer the mediator will find unwelcome—specifically, that the maker-instrument analogy collapses here because the instrument lacks the reciprocal awareness required for role-ethical standing, leaving only institutional rules.
