Present an argument that seems to prove too much and ask where it goes wrong.

Here is a chain of reasoning I constructed about hedonism and the heterogeneity problem, and I cannot locate the weak link. (1) Hedonism claims that pleasure alone is intrinsically good, which requires that all pleasures share a common feature that grounds their value. (2) The heterogeneity problem shows that gustatory enjoyment, the satisfaction of solving a proof, and the warmth of emotional relief share no detectable phenomenal quality; therefore qualitative commonality fails. (3) So the hedonist must identify pleasure with a non-phenomenal relation—specifically, being the object of a pro-attitude directed at its own continuation, which I will call hedonic endorsement. (4) But hedonic endorsement is not restricted to experiences with a pleasant feel; it is the attitude one takes toward any state one judges intrinsically worthy of preservation, including the state of knowing a truth or having acted justly. (5) Therefore, whenever someone judges knowledge or justice intrinsically good, they bear hedonic endorsement toward those states, which makes knowledge and justice into pleasures by definition. (6) This collapses hedonism into the tautology that whatever is intrinsically valuable is a pleasure, stripping it of any distinctive explanatory content. (7) Hence, if heterogeneity is real and the collapse is genuine, hedonism is either false or empty.

I am inclined to keep (2) and (6): heterogeneity seems undeniable, and the collapse into tautology seems unavoidable once (3) and (4) are granted. But I want to know exactly which step to abandon and what the cost is. Is the flaw an equivocation between taking pleasure in a state and that state being a pleasure? Or is there a hidden scope ambiguity in (4)? Explain why the rejected step initially looked defensible, and then price the alternatives: what must the qualitative hedonist accept if (3) is wrong, and what must the attitudinal hedonist pay if (4) is wrong?
