I have no formal training in philosophy, but through independent reading I have become preoccupied with an old Chinese logical puzzle commonly referred to as the White Horse dialogue, in which a speaker argues that a white horse is not a horse on the grounds that horse denotes form alone while white horse denotes both form and color. I have also tried to grasp the wider concerns of the School of Names regarding whether language carves reality at its joints. I do not want a historical summary; I want an exam instrument that makes students think structurally.

Please construct one essay question suitable for an upper-level undergraduate exam, answerable in roughly 60 minutes. The question should center on the White Horse argument but extend it through a modern analog I have devised: the claim that a high-resolution digital scan of an oil painting is not an oil painting, defended with the same logic about compound designations versus simple ones. The prompt should require students to reconstruct the ancient syllogism, identify whether its weakness lies in an ambiguity between distributive and collective predication or in a conflation of intensional with extensional contexts, apply that diagnosis to the digital-scan case, and then assess whether the School of Names' broader semantic worry about names matching actual things supports or undermines the argument.

Provide the exam question, a model answer of about 500 words, and a marking scheme out of 20 marks. The scheme must list five required moves—accurate reconstruction of the original argument (4 marks), precise diagnosis of the logical error (5 marks), parallel analysis of the modern analog (4 marks), engagement with the School of Names semantic concern (4 marks), and a comparative verdict defended with reasons (3 marks)—with a brief note for each on the characteristic mistake that should reduce the score. The challenge should be analytical, not archival: students should be able to excel using general logical competence and familiarity with the dialogue's basic form, without needing to cite specific texts or scholars.
