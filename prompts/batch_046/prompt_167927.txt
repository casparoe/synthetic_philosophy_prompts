I'm working on a story about how museums and school textbooks chop Western history into those three big boxes — Medieval, Renaissance, Scientific Revolution — like they're seperate rooms with actual walls. You see it in floor plans: medieval armor, then Renaissance portraits, then suddenly telescopes and Newton's papers in the science wing, even though Newton was still messing with alchemy and Ficino was doing medicine and philology at the same time. Nobody builds a gallery for the Alchemical Enlightenment or the Long 16th Century; those stay in the footnotes.

What philosophical commitments about historical change does that layout presuppose? That eras have real essences? That science and art move on paralell tracks? Or is it just bureaucratic inertia? Reconstruct the implicit theory behind the walls. Is the three-box split strictly required by any coherent view of the past, or is it philosophically underdetermined — maybe even a constructivist about periods could defend it for teaching reasons? And if you actually dropped epochal essentialism, what would have to change: the architecture, the labels, the funding, or the whole story?
