I've been in this philosphy of extinction reading group for like two semesters now and we've gotten stuck on the same loop. We all agree human extinction is bad in a way that goes way beyond just counting the deaths of current people—Scheffler's afterlife conjecture comes up constantly, the idea that so much of what gives our lives meaning depends on there being an "afterlife" of humanity continuing after us. But then half the group says the real harm is distributive: you're robbing all those potential future people of existence, so it's just a massive failure of beneficence scaled across time. The other half insists thats missing the point, that the harm is relational or structural—extinction breaks the narrative thread that makes our present projects matter, not just because future people vanish but because the conditions for our own meaning collapse.

And yet nobody moves. I've started wondering if they're not actually disagreeing about extinction at all, but about whether value works like inheritance—each generation recieving value from the next in a chain—or like accumulation, where meaning pools independently and can survive a break if enough is stored. Maybe theres a hidden empirical question about whether people actually need that future "afterlife" psychologically, or maybe its just bedrock intuition about naturalness? Or perhaps one side is doing moral mathematics and the other is describing existential architecture, and they've been talking past each other for months without realizing it.

I want a diagnosis, not a verdict: is this a verbal dispute about "harm," a substantive disagreement about the metaphysics of value across generations, or are they answering different questions under the same heading? And given your diagnosis, is there anything that could actually shift it—a conceptual distinction, a modified thought experiment like comparing a "known last generation" who realizes they're the end versus one that never knows, or should we just accept that the impasse is permanent and move on?
