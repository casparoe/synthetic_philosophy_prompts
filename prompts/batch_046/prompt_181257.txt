I am a doctoral student in political philosophy and science studies, and I am developing a framework that applies Dewey's experimentalist conception of democracy—understood not merely as a fixed constitutional form but as a continuous, collective mode of inquiry into shared problems—to the governance of large-scale collaborative research infrastructures. In these settings, decision-making must remain open to revision by heterogeneous stakeholders, and the framing of the problem itself is often contested among experts, lay participants, and affected communities. Rather than asking for a summary of Dewey's view or a bibliography, please describe the most important implications of this experimentalist framework for how such infrastructures should handle problematic situations in which the very definition of the issue is in dispute. Specifically, how does treating democratic governance itself as an ongoing inquiry, rather than as a procedure for aggregating pre-formed preferences, reshape our understanding of authority, dissent, and institutional learning? Please avoid citations, reading lists, and references to specific secondary literature, and focus on the conceptual and practical stakes for interdisciplinary institutional design.
