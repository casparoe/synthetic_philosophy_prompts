I serve as the philosophical adviser to the ethics review panel of a national museum consortium; we meet monthly to approve permanent exhibition texts on twentieth-century state violence, and every claim attributing collective guilt requires our sign-off, with diplomatic risk if we misassign responsibility across generations. Please construct a reusable decision procedure—ordered questions, clear stop conditions, and explicit points where calculation yields to judgement—grounded in Karl Jaspers' distinctions among criminal, political, moral, and metaphysical guilt. The practitioner is a non-philosopher historian with two hours per proposal; the procedure must determine which mode of guilt is invoked, whether institutional continuity permits its ascription to present citizens, whether the claim is descriptive or normative, and precisely where it must halt and return to the panel. Provide brief test conditions for each mode, then apply the procedure to three proposals: (a) attributing political guilt to citizens of a post-authoritarian state for passive complicity in past surveillance; (b) attributing metaphysical guilt to descendants of perpetrators for an inherited failure of solidarity; and (c) attributing collective criminal guilt to an entire ethnic group for regime-led atrocities—which should be rejected, with the exact failure point shown and any residual ambiguity the procedure cannot resolve flagged. Include one case where the procedure yields an answer the board will dislike, state clearly what the procedure is incapable of settling, and present everything in British English.
