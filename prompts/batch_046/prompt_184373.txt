I spent thirty years as an experimental physicist before retiring, and over the past eighteen months I have developed a serious, self-directed interest in normative ethics; I am now preparing to lead a single forty-five-minute seminar for a small discussion group of fellow retired scientists and engineers who have no formal philosophy training but are thoroughly comfortable with formal reasoning and optimization models. The concept I want them to grasp is prioritarianism together with the leveling-down objection, but I do not want you to lecture me on the ethics itself; instead, I need a pedagogical plan for how to carry them through the idea in one session. Specifically, lay out the dependency structure: which conceptual pieces must be introduced first so that the objection operates as a genuine reductio rather than as a generic complaint about equality, and in what order the weight of the prioritarian curve, the distinction between benefiting people and benefiting states of affairs, and the structural logic of downward leveling must appear. For the carrying example, propose one concrete scenario—perhaps involving the rationing of a fixed stock of a life-extending therapy across patients with different baseline life expectancies—that can do the heavy lifting, and explain precisely what that example must possess and lack so that it illuminates why equalizing downward is structurally different from failing to raise everyone up, without collapsing into familiar utilitarian arithmetic about QALYs. Because my audience will instinctively reach for analogies to multi-objective optimization, map the misconceptions they are likely to import: treating prioritarian weights as tunable coefficients in an objective function, conflating the leveling-down objection with an equality-of-opportunity argument drawn from engineering fairness, or interpreting the objection as a practical rather than a normative failure, and specify exactly where in the sequence I should interrupt each analogy before it hardens into a false framing. Include one diagnostic question I can pose near the end to distinguish a participant who genuinely understands that the objection targets the shape of the prioritarian welfare function—specifically its willingness to sacrifice gains at the bottom to preserve equality—from someone who merely thinks leveling down is an unfortunate distributive side effect. Finally, be explicit about what subtle distinction—perhaps between telic and deontic formulations of the view, or between weak and strong prioritarianism—I should openly set aside as beyond this session’s scope and admit I am leaving out, and confirm that the entire plan can be executed without assigned readings, bibliographies, or citations that I could not verify from memory alone.
