I'm trying to stabilize my position on distributed selfhood for a philosophy-of-mind discussion, and I want a consistency audit before I defend it publicly. Here is what I currently hold:

(a) The self is best understood as a strange loop — a self-referential pattern realized in neural dynamics rather than a substance or soul — and its identity conditions are functional-causal, not substrate-bound.

(b) Because what matters is continuity of the self-modeling process, not spatial containment within one skull, there is no in-principle barrier to the loop extending into tightly coupled artificial prostheses or, under extreme conditions, into other brains.

(c) Split-brain cases show that a single substrate can host two partially decoupled strange loops, which suggests that loop-boundaries are fixed by integration density, not by anatomical boundaries.

(d) Nevertheless, I deny that two ordinary adult brains, communicating only through ordinary language and perception, could ever share a single distributed "I"; loop-integrity requires dense, continuous feedback among the processes that maintain the self-model.

(e) If two brains were linked by a sustained high-bandwidth neural interface that unified their self-modeling dynamics in real time, I think one loop might extend across both — though I also think the result would likely be an asymmetric absorption of one loop into the other, rather than a genuinely merged or bilateral self.

(f) I further hold that in extreme empathic identification — what I call transcranial loop-incorporation — one brain's self-model can literally include another person's first-person perspective as a represented subsystem; yet this creates only an asymmetric inclusion, never a shared "I."

Please audit these commitments. Which pairs are genuinely in tension, which tensions are only apparent and dissolve on closer inspection, and what is the least costly revision? Rank the tensions by severity rather than treating them all as equally damaging, and for each specify which commitment must weaken or be abandoned to restore consistency and what that costs the overall view. Separate what is relatively settled in the philosophy of mind and cognitive neuroscience from what remains genuinely contested among specialists, and do not present any disputed ontological claim about selfhood, or any contested interpretation of split-brain data or brain-interface coupling, as though it were the consensus view.
