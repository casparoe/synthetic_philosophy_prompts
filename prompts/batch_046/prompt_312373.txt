I have been discussing with a colleague in our philosophy of value reading group whether the view sometimes labelled tranquilism—that the only thing which genuinely matters for a life to go well is the absence of craving and of suffering, with no positive weight attaching to pleasure, achievement, or relational goods—can be articulated with enough precision to stand as a distinct theory rather than collapsing into negative hedonism, Stoic apatheia, or a particular interpretation of Buddhist liberation. To help me think through this, I should like you to set out the full list of desiderata that any philosophical account must satisfy in order to count as properly tranquilist, giving for each the motivating case or conceptual consideration behind it, marking clearly which requirements are non-negotiable for the view and which merely reflect the preferences of one camp within it, and stating explicitly if any subset of the requirements proves jointly unsatisfiable given standard objections in axiology. I am particularly keen that the list address what I shall call the coupling problem: whether craving and suffering are conceptually linked in a rational agent such that the absence of one necessarily entails the absence of the other, and whether the theory must therefore endorse a strict asymmetry according to which preventing suffering carries moral weight whereas failing to create pleasure carries none. Please also say what specific argument, counter-example, or empirical finding would be most likely to lead you to revise either the criteria themselves or your judgement about which are essential, and present the entire response as continuous prose without headings, bullet points, bold type, or tables, written in the manner of a formal letter.
