Having retired from experimental physics and only recently begun studying philosophy of science, I have adopted the view that Everettian quantum mechanics—taken as the claim that the universal wavefunction evolves unitarily and that apparent branching is an emergent feature of decoherence—should be our default ontology, not because it is parsimonious but because it refuses to add structure (hidden variables, collapses, or observer-dependence) that the formalism does not require. I regard Bohmian mechanics as ontologically redundant once one accepts that the wavefunction alone fixes all statistical predictions, and I treat Copenhagen not as a rival interpretation but as a methodological stance that abandons the interpretive task altogether. Yet I am troubled by what I take to be an under-discussed circularity: decoherence explains branching only by presupposing a division of the world into system and environment, but in a truly universal wavefunction there is no non-arbitrary way to draw that boundary without already assuming the classical structures that need explaining. I want to know what would genuinely defeat this Everettian commitment. Could there be a formal proof that no factorization of the universal state yields stable, approximately classical branches? Could Bohmian mechanics provide a non-circular account of subsystem individuation—say, through the quantum potential's role in defining trajectories—that Everett cannot replicate? And might a precisely formulated collapse dynamics, experimentally distinguishable from unitary evolution, render the entire branching picture moot? Please distinguish fatal defeaters from merely awkward ones, say which objections defenders of the view typically concede and which they dismiss, assess whether the position has been insulated by qualifications into unfalsifiability, and state how confident you are in your assessment, singling out the specific claim about which you are least certain.
