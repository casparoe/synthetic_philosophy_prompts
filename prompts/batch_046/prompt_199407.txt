I have recently been appointed trustee of a family foundation that administers capital accumulated over five generations, and this responsibility has made a conceptual problem in distributive justice urgently personal rather than merely academic. The standard philosophical justification for inheritance taxation appeals to equality of opportunity: when some individuals begin life with massive unearned financial endowments, competitive conditions are unfairly distorted in their favor. Yet I find this justification incomplete once one distinguishes between direct transmission—the legal bequest of monetary assets—and what I will call structural transmission, in which family wealth is converted into enduring institutional advantages that survive taxation, such as control over philanthropic boards, privileged access to regulatory networks, and the capacity to shape local educational and cultural environments. Please explain, with conceptual precision, whether the principle of equal opportunity can coherently justify taxing direct transmission without also committing us to regulate structural transmission. In particular, I want to understand whether there exists a defensible normative boundary between capital that the state may legitimately reclaim and influence that it must leave untouched, or whether conceding the impossibility of regulating the latter renders inheritance taxation a merely symbolic gesture—penalizing the visible form of dynastic advantage while leaving its functional equivalent intact. I am not asking for empirical predictions about tax avoidance; I am asking for an analysis of the conceptual architecture that connects equality of opportunity to intergenerational autonomy, and whether that architecture can survive the distinction between economic goods and social goods without collapsing into arbitrariness.
