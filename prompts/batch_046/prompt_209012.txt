I have been thinking on my own about Hannah Arendt's idea that evil can be ordinary and unthinking, particularly in her account of the Eichmann trial. One camp I encounter says she was essentially right: Eichmann was not a monstrous ideologue but a dull, career-minded bureaucrat who failed to think about what he was doing, and his evil was banal in the sense of being shallow and routine. Another camp insists she was deeply wrong: the trial record and later scholarship show he was actively antisemitic, ideologically committed, and fully aware of his actions, so calling his evil "banal" obscures real malice and wrongly shifts blame onto abstract systems.

These sides seem to talk past each other, and I suspect part of the problem is that they conflate two different questions—whether Eichmann's inner psychology was ordinary, and whether the bureaucratic structure he worked in made his actions routine regardless of his psychology. I would like you to draft the joint statement that an adversarial collaboration between these two camps should have produced.

Please name the two positions neutrally: call one the Psychological Banality camp and the other the Ideological Agency camp.

The document should contain:

1. A numbered list of claims both sides can genuinely sign—covering what the trial record clearly shows about Eichmann's own statements and conduct, about what Arendt actually argued versus common misreadings of her view, and about the conceptual distinction between describing an agent's mental state and assigning moral or legal responsibility.

2. A clearly labeled section stating the exact remaining disagreements, with each disagreement phrased in wording that the side holding it would accept for its own view, and the opposing position rendered in wording that side would find acceptable.

3. The cruxes—the specific sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one camp. I take likely cruxes to be: whether psychological thoughtlessness and ideological commitment can coexist in the same agent; whether the term banality should apply to the agent's inner life, to the institutional framing of the act, or to both separately; and whether operating within a modern bureaucratic role can diminish personal culpability without eliminating it.

For each crux, provide:
- A concrete case or piece of agreed-upon evidence from the Eichmann record that both sides accept bears directly on it.
- What the Psychological Banality camp would predict or conclude about that evidence.
- What the Ideological Agency camp would predict or conclude about the same evidence.

4. A brief concluding section identifying whether any portion of their dispute is verbal—where they agree on the underlying facts but use terms like banal, evil, or responsible differently—and explaining precisely which portion and why.

Write the result as a polished document both camps could put their names to, without asking either side to concede what it does not actually hold. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citation requests. Keep the focus on conceptual precision, the separation of psychological claims from institutional claims, and the specific predictions each side makes about the agreed evidence.
