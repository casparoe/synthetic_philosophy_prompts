After a long argument with a friend about whether our shared moral revulsion at “corruption” survives once we stop treating Western bureaucratic norms as the default frame, I want to stress-test the anti-universalist position I was pushing. That position holds that the standard legalistic definition—abuse of entrusted public power for private gain—commits a category error when applied to clientelist societies in which patronage, gift-circulation, and kin-based redistribution are not deviations from a public/private boundary but the primary infrastructure through which collective goods are allocated. On this view, labeling a patron’s distribution of state jobs to clients “corruption” is structurally analogous to calling kinship reciprocity “nepotism”: it exports a Weberian separation of roles into contexts where that separation never existed, thereby misclassifying functional obligations as extractive theft. Play it straight: what would have to be true for this position to be wrong? Which premises are genuinely load-bearing—the non-existence of a local public/private distinction, the functional equivalence of patronage to taxation or social insurance, the claim that “corruption” talk is a performative imposition rather than a description—and which are merely decorative? I want an honest map of its exposure: are there ethnographic patterns that would decisively undermine it, conceptual distinctions (say, between obligatory reciprocity and rent-seeking extraction) that it cannot accommodate without dissolving into triviality, or normative commitments it makes that turn out self-defeating? In particular, does the view risk anything empirically, or has it been insulated by definitional moves that render it unfalsifiable? And if you had to name the single observation, argument, or conceptual distinction that defenders of this anti-universalist stance should themselves concede as genuinely counting against it—not merely what external critics allege—what would that be?
