My philosophy mentor has offered informal judgments on whether particular agents or systems exhibit sphexishness—mechanical rigidity of the kind that undermines free will—versus genuine flexibility. Her rulings to date are: the digger wasp Sphex ichneumoneus, which repeats its burrow-inspection ritual even after the burrow is experimentally relocated, is sphexish; a human commuter who drives an identical route daily but can explain her choice and switch instantly when traffic demands is not; a reflex arc that withdraws a limb from heat with invariant latency is sphexish; a jazz pianist who varies her phrasing within a fixed harmonic structure is not; an industrial robotic arm that welds the same seam repeatedly yet pauses immediately upon a safety signal is not sphexish; and a clockwork automaton that executes an identical sequence regardless of obstacles is sphexish. This distinction has recently become personally urgent for me, since I now design adaptive control interfaces for assistive prosthetics and must decide whether flexibility of output or plasticity of mechanism is the decisive factor. The new case is a neural-network prosthetic controller whose feature-extraction layers are permanently frozen at manufacture, but whose final decision layer updates its weights in real time from sensory feedback, allowing the grip to adapt to novel objects, even though the core architecture can never be revised and the system possesses neither representational restructuring nor reason-responsive deliberation. Predict my mentor’s verdict—would she classify this controller as sphexish? State whether she is implicitly tracking behavioral variability, architectural modifiability, or responsiveness to reasons; cite which of her prior rulings best supports each interpretation; and specify the single contrasting case—perhaps a system with fully plastic internal architecture but behaviorally invariant output—that would settle which criterion she is actually using.
