Design a final-exam essay question for an upper-level seminar in comparative philosophy (advanced undergraduate or first-year graduate level). The course has covered classical Chinese philosophy, Western dream-skepticism from Plato through Descartes, and basic debates in personal identity and persistence. The exam is 90 minutes; students should write approximately 1,000–1,200 words. The question is worth 35 marks.

The question must center on Zhuangzi’s butterfly-dream passage, but it should be unanswerable by merely summarizing the narrative and stating that it raises skeptical doubts about reality. Instead, frame the problem around a specific conceptual crux: ask students to evaluate whether the scenario functions primarily as an epistemic argument (that we lack reliable criteria to distinguish waking experience from dreaming) or as a metaphysical argument about the conditions of diachronic personal identity across transformations (hua), and whether these two readings are genuinely compatible. In particular, require students to address the double-awakening structure—if Zhuang Zhou awakens and remains unsure whether he is now the genuine Zhuang Zhou or still a butterfly dreaming that it is Zhuang Zhou—does this generate skepticism about an external world, about the persistence conditions of the self, or about both simultaneously? They should also consider whether accepting the metaphysical reading, according to which self and other are transient phases of a single transformation without an underlying substrate, dissolves the skeptical problem or merely relocates it onto a different set of commitments about identity.

Provide exactly three components:

1. The exam question as students would receive it, including any necessary framing but without requesting citations, bibliographies, or references to specific secondary scholarship that a respondent without web access could not reliably verify.

2. A model answer that specifies the argumentative moves a top response must make—for example, distinguishing the epistemic reading from the ontological reading; explaining the role of hua and why it conflicts with substance-based or psychological-continuity accounts of persistence; identifying whether the skeptical force depends on assuming a stable self that Zhuangzi’s metaphysics may reject; and offering a reasoned judgment about whether the two readings collapse into one another or can be held apart.

3. A marking scheme out of 35 that allocates marks to specific moves and lists identifiable errors or omissions that should cost marks—such as conflating Zhuangzi’s position with Cartesian global skepticism; treating the butterfly solely as a metaphor for illusion without addressing transformation; failing to recognize that the challenge may be internal to a particular theory of the self rather than universally applicable; or neglecting the double-awakening structure entirely.

The difficulty should come from selecting this crux: a student who has only memorized generic labels about Chinese skepticism or dream arguments should not be able to score highly, whereas one who can navigate the interaction between epistemology and metaphysics of identity should be able to demonstrate mastery.
