Next month I present at a jurisprudence workshop defending a reconstructed legal-moralist thesis. I accept Hart’s distinction between positive and critical morality, but argue that Devlin was right that law may protect shared moral commitments yet wrong to ground this in collective disgust of the sort he invoked. Instead, I claim that certain private practices — specifically enforceable contracts for voluntary servitude or extreme bodily-risk waivers — should remain prohibited because their legal recognition produces what I term normative spillover: it destabilises the symbolic framework of personhood that makes other rights intelligible, without harming any identifiable third party in Mill’s sense. The audience is mostly Hartian liberals, including a prominent defender of Mill’s harm principle who has written on the Wolfenden report, plus a legal historian who knows Devlin’s judicial record in detail. Give me the objections in the order they are likely to arise, phrased as each of these critics would phrase them, with what I should concede, what I should hold firm, and where each reply is weakest. Identify the single objection — probably from the historian or the harm-principle defender — that I am hoping nobody raises, and draft the reply I must have ready should it come. Keep every entry brief enough for a two-minute response.
