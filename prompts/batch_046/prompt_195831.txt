I am preparing the syllabus for a graduate seminar on the conceptual architecture of political freedom—specifically the distinctions among Berlin’s negative and positive liberty and Pettit’s republican conception of freedom as non-domination—and in reviewing both recent student essays and contemporary policy debates I have noticed a recurring argumentative structure that I cannot place precisely within the existing taxonomy of fallacies or conceptual moves.

Consider two representative instances. First, a commentator defending extensive workplace regulation begins by framing the issue in terms of arbitrary power: an employer who retains the discretion to dismiss workers at whim dominates them, even if no dismissal ever occurs, so genuine freedom requires institutional checks. When an interlocutor objects that such checks diminish the employer’s negative liberty—the actual absence of obstacles—the defender pivots and insists that the regulations do not remove any meaningful option, since employers may still hire, workers may still resign, and the overall range of actions remains essentially intact. Once that objection subsides, the defender returns to the domination-based framing to justify further expansions—say, extending oversight to algorithmic scheduling—without ever reconciling the two justifications. Second, in classroom debate, a student advocates universal basic income on the grounds that economic dependence creates a master-slave relation, but when pressed on the coercive taxation required to fund it, responds that taxation merely alters background conditions without blocking any specific choice, then immediately reverts to the dependence-as-domination rhetoric to justify raising the benefit level. In both cases the speaker oscillates between two genuinely distinct conceptions of liberty—one concerned with status and arbitrary power, the other with the range of unimpeded actions—to defend a single policy position. The move is not quite the motte-and-bailey fallacy, because neither conception functions as a defensible core with an indefensible extension; they are equally substantive and share only a rhetorical umbrella. Nor is it straightforward equivocation, since the speaker is not smuggling one meaning into an argument that requires the other, but rather deploying whichever conception is locally immune to the present objection. Is there an established term for this specific pattern of defensive oscillation between republican and negative-liberty registers? If not, can you propose a name and a precise definition that distinguishes it from conceptual pluralism, from strategic ambiguity, and from the motte-and-bailey? I am particularly interested in where its boundaries lie: does it require that both conceptions be sincerely held, or can it be purely rhetorical? And does it apply only to liberty concepts, or is it a more general argumentative form?

Please structure your answer as follows: (1) Phenomenological mapping (approximately 250 words): describe the pattern precisely, identify the nearest established concepts—motte-and-bailey, strategic ambiguity, conceptual pluralism, perhaps Berlin’s own warnings about conflation—and explain how the described phenomenon differs from each in terms of speaker intent, logical structure, and normative stakes. (2) Taxonomy and naming (approximately 200 words): state whether an established term exists; if none does, coin a term with an explicit definition, specify necessary and sufficient conditions, and provide one clear boundary case that falls just outside it. (3) Generalization and limits (approximately 150 words): address whether the pattern is specific to liberty debates or extends to other normative domains, and clarify whether sincere belief in both conceptions is required or whether purely strategic deployment suffices.
