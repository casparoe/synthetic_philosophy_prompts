I sit on an interdisciplinary pilot-study review board for complex-systems biology, and nearly every proposal now claims its target—gut-microbiome effects on mood, tissue regeneration, collective behavior—is “emergent” and therefore immune to reductionist methods, demanding special holistic funding. Write a reusable evaluation procedure, in ordered steps with explicit pass/fail conditions, that a non-philosopher board member can run in about twenty minutes per proposal. Ground it in the actual philosophy of emergence: distinguish weak (epistemic or predictive) emergence, strong (ontological, downward-causal) emergence, and representational emergence (a feature of our models rather than nature), and include a preliminary filter that rejects uses of “emergence” as rhetorical cover for an unknown mechanism. For each classification, specify what evidence counts, whether the proposal proceeds under standard reductionist review, receives conditional funding, is rejected, or escalates to the full board because the issue is methodological values rather than empirical fact. Mark explicitly where judgment is unavoidable and where the procedure will misfire—especially where weak versus strong emergence turns on unresolved metaphysical commitments that evidence alone cannot settle. Then apply it to three proposals: (a) a study claiming depression is strongly emergent from gut bacteria, so targeting individual strains is futile; (b) a study arguing starling flocking is weakly emergent from local rules and proposing a predictive simulation; and (c) a study asserting consciousness is representationally emergent from neural correlates and requesting funds to construct a non-reductive ontology. Include at least one case where the procedure delivers an answer the applicant will strongly dislike, and close by stating the procedure’s hard limits.
