For a graduate seminar in logic and epistemology—10 students, one 75-minute session—design an in-class negotiation game that makes participants feel the force of Carroll’s tortoise problem: the difficulty of justifying a basic deductive rule such as modus ponens without either using that very rule (circularity) or invoking a further rule that itself demands justification (regress). Rather than staging Carroll’s dialogue, assign roles: two Proponents who must defend a specific inference drawn from a non-obvious premise set (a modal syllogism that you, the facilitator, supply in writing), one Tortoise who accepts every premise and explicit intermediate step but refuses to apply the final inference unless it is justified by a rule that does not presuppose the rule being defended, and two Arbiters who adjudicate whether a proposed justification is legitimate and award or deduct “credibility tokens.” The Proponents have a budget of exactly five justification moves; each move may cite a supplied premise, invoke an explicit meta-rule printed on a shared board, or appeal to a prior agreement reached in the round, but they may not quote, paraphrase, or implicitly rely on the target inference rule. If the Tortoise has not conceded by the end of the fifth move, the Proponents forfeit their tokens; if they secure the concession earlier, they keep the remainder. The Tortoise wins tokens for each successful refusal, the Arbiters for catching illicit smuggling of the rule, and all tokens translate into a small symbolic stake (e.g., ordering the debrief discussion). Structure the 75 minutes as: 10 minutes silent reading of the premise set and rules, 40 minutes of structured negotiation in rounds of 3 minutes, 15 minutes for Arbiters to render a formal verdict on whether circularity or regress occurred, and 10 minutes for transition. The debrief must map specific negotiation moments onto the two horns—identify exactly when a Proponent appealed to a meta-rule that contained the conclusion in its conditions (circularity) versus when they deferred to a higher-order principle that the Tortoise then challenged (regress)—and predict what will go wrong the first time you run it: Proponents will likely embed the inference rule inside a seemingly innocent “rule of reasoning” card, Arbiters will miss the embedding because it sounds like a procedural agreement, and the Tortoise will eventually accept an implicit premise that already contains the conclusion. Be explicit about how to handle each failure in the moment (pause, reveal the hidden premise, ask the room to vote). Finally, be frank about which feature of the philosophical problem the activity cannot reproduce: in actual reasoning we apply modus ponens without explicit token-based negotiation, so the artificial refusal is a pedagogical device; the facilitator must supply the acknowledgment that real justification is typically tacit, and the exercise shows only why an explicit, fully transparent justification chain collapses. Do not ask students to read any source text in advance; the problem should emerge entirely from the rules and the token economy.
