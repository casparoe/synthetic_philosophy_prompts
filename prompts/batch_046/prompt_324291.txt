Consider the institutional practice in Norway, Sweden, and Finland of maintaining fully public tax registers—where any citizen may look up another’s declared income and tax contribution—alongside steeply progressive taxation, extensive universal welfare provision funded largely through those revenues, and exceptionally high rates of voluntary compliance supported by relatively light enforcement. At the same time, policymakers and comparative sociologists routinely debate whether this specific configuration—high taxes paired with high interpersonal and institutional trust—can be “exported” to societies with lower baseline trust, weaker state legitimacy, or stronger norms of financial privacy.

What philosophical commitments about distributive justice, the moral status of privacy, the nature of social cooperation, and the ontology of trust does this pairing of practices presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory that makes transparent taxation, progressive extraction, and voluntary compliance appear mutually reinforcing rather than contradictory. Is the practice strictly committed to a particular view—for example, that fairness requires visibility of contribution, that trust is a non-instrumental social good, or that privacy must yield to democratic accountability—or is it philosophically underdetermined, compatible with several rival accounts? Finally, specify which institutional features would have to be redesigned if the underlying view were replaced by a purely transactional or coercive model of taxation, in which compliance is secured solely through audit risk and privacy is treated as a pre-political right.
