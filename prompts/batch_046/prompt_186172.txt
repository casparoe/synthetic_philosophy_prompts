I've never studied law or museum ethics formally; I've just followed news about returned artifacts—things like the Benin Bronzes and the debates over the Parthenon Marbles—and tried to work out the principles behind different decisions. Recently I read through the published rulings of a regional heritage restitution panel, and I think I've spotted a pattern, but I'm not sure which pattern it really is.

Here are four decisions they've made.

First, regarding ceremonial masks taken by soldiers during an 1898 raid on a village: they voted for full return to the descendants.

Second, regarding bronze statues acquired in 1923 through a contract signed by a local leader who later testified he was threatened with imprisonment if he refused: they voted for return, but with a shared-custody agreement allowing the museum to display them on loan every other year.

Third, regarding a collection of ritual textiles that had been buried for safekeeping during a war in 1943, then excavated with formal permission from the post-war government, and donated to the museum. The descendants want them back, but the objects are not used in any current practice, and the descendants are split—half want them in a local shrine, half want them to stay accessible globally. The panel voted to keep them, with a commitment to fund a local cultural center and provide open digital access.

Fourth, regarding carved wooden figures that left a community not through direct seizure, but because the community was relocated in 1920 by a government dam project that compensated families. Individual families sold some figures to a traveling buyer for cash during the move; the buyer donated them to the museum in 1925. The descendants now form a dispersed group across three countries, with no single unified religious use of the figures, though some regard them as ancestral. The panel voted to keep the figures in the museum, but required public acknowledgment of the relocation history and the sales.

I'm trying to figure out what rule the panel is actually running. It could be: (A) return only when the chain of ownership includes direct state violence or explicit coercion tied specifically to the taking; (B) return whenever the loss was an indirect result of state action that made retention impossible, even if individual transactions were documented; or (C) return only when there is a present, coherent community with an active, unified cultural or religious need for the physical objects, regardless of how they left.

Now the new case: the panel is about to rule on the Noro woven panels, made between 1880 and 1900 by a community that used them as instructional weavings—something like readable texts recording agricultural and ceremonial knowledge. In 1920 the community was displaced by the same government dam project mentioned above; families received compensation and relocated. During the move, several panels were sold to the same traveling buyer by individual families who needed cash. The buyer donated them to the museum. The panels are not used in any current religious ritual—the practice was disrupted—but a small group of descendants has recently revived interest in the weave-script and wants the panels back not for display but for hands-on teaching. Other descendants say the museum's high-resolution digitization lets people learn the script without risking damage to the fragile natural dyes, which degrade with handling. The revival group is small and not representative of all descendants.

If the panel wants to stay consistent with itself, what should it decide—return, shared custody, or keep? Which of the four earlier cases is the closest analog? If the four rulings genuinely don't determine the answer, what principle is the panel adopting with each possible choice?

Also, if my three rules don't capture the pattern, feel free to propose others—say, one that focuses on whether the object functions as a living instructional tool rather than as art or relic.

Finally, what single further case—one that hasn't happened yet—would best show whether the panel cares more about the state's role in displacement or about whether a living community currently needs the physical object?
