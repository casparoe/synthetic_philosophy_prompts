I am a high school junior preparing to facilitate a fifty-minute session for my school's philosophy discussion group—about eight peers, mostly seniors with no formal training in logic or social choice theory. Our topic is judgment aggregation and the discursive dilemma: specifically, how a group can consistently endorse each of several premises and yet, through majority voting, reach a collective conclusion that contradicts those premises, and why this reveals a structural feature of collective reasoning rather than a procedural error. I do not need the concept explained to me; I need a pedagogical plan. In what order must the components be introduced—should I begin with an intuitive case of group disagreement, or with the distinction between premise-based and conclusion-based aggregation—and what logical dependency makes that sequence necessary? What single concrete scenario should carry the entire explanation—I am considering a student council vote on whether to approve a new club, evaluated separately on budget, student interest, and fairness—and what properties must it possess, and what must it lack, so that the paradox actually arises and cannot be dismissed as a counting mistake? Which misconceptions should I anticipate—I expect they will suggest changing the voting method or appointing a single decider—and at precisely which point in the sequence should each be intercepted and defused? What final exercise or question can I pose to determine whether they understand the dilemma as inherent to collective judgment rather than as a solvable puzzle about voting rules? Finally, what should I deliberately exclude at this level—for instance, formal representations in propositional logic or the extension of these problems into legal reasoning—and how should I frame that boundary so it sounds like a deliberate limit rather than a gap?
