As a novelist who has long been suspicious of the narrative arcs I am paid to impose upon lived experience, I have drafted notes for an essay arguing that the conventional division of Western history into the Middle Ages, the Renaissance, and the Scientific Revolution is not a discovered taxonomy but a retrofitted narrative structure—essentially a three-act plot consolidated by nineteenth-century historiography and subsequently treated as geology. In the notes, I propose that the Middle Ages function as a dark exposition, the Renaissance as a rising action of recovered classical light, and the Scientific Revolution as a climactic rupture that resolves prior tension. I observe that if one examines the material and intellectual practices of the fourteenth through seventeenth centuries without this pre-imposed arc—looking at the persistence of manuscript culture well into the age of print, the survival of scholastic methods within early modern natural philosophy, and the incremental rather than catastrophic emergence of institutional science—the boundaries between these periods dissolve into continuous gradation. Nevertheless, I concede that certain discontinuities, particularly the formalization of peer review and the gradual replacement of patronage-based inquiry with state-funded research collectives, may resist full absorption into continuity. Before I develop these notes into a finished piece, I would value your comments on three specific points. First, does my central analogy to narrative construction risk being self-sealing—can any apparent counter-example be re-described as merely another plot point, and if so, does that render the thesis unfalsifiable in a way that should genuinely trouble me? Second, which institutional or material shifts, if any, genuinely fracture the continuity I describe, and do they invalidate the framing entirely or only demand a more nuanced narrative arc? Third, I have deliberately omitted the standard historiographical authorities—figures such as Burckhardt and Koyré—intending that the argument rest on conceptual logic rather than scholarly genealogy; does this omission make the essay appear under-informed, or does it successfully compel the reader to engage with the structure itself?
