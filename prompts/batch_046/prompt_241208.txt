I am preparing a feature on whether self-report remains a gold-standard method in psychology and philosophy of mind. In a recent long-form essay I encountered this characterization: "Introspection is essentially inner perception—like vision turned inward—and because consciousness is transparent to itself, errors are rare and usually come from outside interference rather than from the faculty itself. This is why first-person reports generally outmatch behavioral measures. Critics who cite confabulation experiments are missing the point: those cases are failures of memory, not failures of the introspective act itself. Meanwhile, predictive-processing models that treat the brain as constantly guessing its own states only describe sub-personal mechanisms, so they leave untouched the personal-level transparency that makes introspection reliable." Before I quote this framing, I want to audit its fairness. Which parts align with mainstream positions in philosophy of mind and cognitive science, which conflate transparency with infallibility or misplace the predictive-processing challenge at the wrong level, and does the conclusion—that confabulation is irrelevant to introspective reliability—survive once the personal versus sub-personal distinction is scrutinized? If introspection is not best understood as inner sight, what analogy, if any, better captures both its genuine strengths and its documented limits?
