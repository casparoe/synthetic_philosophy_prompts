I hold what I will call the mediation-isomorphism criterion for mechanistic sameness: two systems share the same mechanism just in case there exists a structure-preserving correspondence between their intermediate variables that preserves counterfactual dependency relations, regardless of differences in substrate, parameterization, or architectural implementation. On this view, an anomaly is not a statistical outlier but a failure of this mediation structure—an interruption or rerouting of causal influence that breaks the isomorphism with a reference mechanism. What should genuinely count against this position? I want the defeaters sorted by severity: empirical findings in mechanistic interpretability—say, about polysemanticity, distributed representations, or redundant causal pathways—that make substrate-independent mediation graphs unrecoverable or systematically misleading; formal results on causal identifiability showing that isolating these structures requires substrate-level knowledge we do not have; and conceptual objections, such as the charge that the criterion collapses mechanistic sameness into mere functional equivalence, or that it misses anomalies which preserve mediation topology but alter dynamics or thresholds. Which of these would a committed defender concede as damaging, and which would they dismiss as misapplying the framework? Finally, assess whether the view risks being qualified into unfalsifiability—if every apparent counterexample can be reinterpreted as a hidden mediation failure—and whether that insulation is a theoretical virtue or a practical defect for a criterion meant to guide real anomaly detection.
