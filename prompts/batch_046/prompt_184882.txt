Design a two-hour structured deliberation for a graduate moral-philosophy seminar of about ten students, working with only a whiteboard and no advance readings. The aim is to make them feel the force of indirect normativity: the idea that a norm can bind an agent not through the direct effects of the agent's particular act, but through what the act expresses about the agent's relation to an idealized deliberative standpoint that the agent does not currently occupy. To keep the exercise out of well-worn contractarian grooves, frame the idealized standpoint as the "Second-Order Observer" — not a person or a community, but a procedural filter requiring that every first-order reason an agent offers must be defensible without any indexical reference to the agent's own position, including not only "I want" or "this harms me" but even "this harms people in my situation." Structure the exercise in three rounds with real, small stakes: distribute a fixed pool of points that determine a concrete shared outcome, such as which two texts the seminar reads in the final session and in what order. In Round One, students deliberate in pairs and then as a full group using unrestricted first-order reasoning; in Round Two, they must reconvene and defend the same proposals through the Second-Order Observer filter, with a rule that any reason containing an indexical is struck and the speaker must either repair it or withdraw the claim; in Round Three, they vote by secret ballot on whether to adopt the outcome generated by Round One, Round Two, or a compromise, but they must justify their vote in writing using only one of the two permitted vocabularies, assigned randomly. The facilitator should not name the philosophical position until the debrief. In that debrief, map specific moments of resistance — for example, a student insisting that their direct harm-claim should count despite the filter, or a student accepting a counterintuitive allocation because the repaired reason survived — onto features of indirect normativity: the displacement of first-person authority, the separation of justification from motivation, and the possibility that a norm binds even when direct evaluation of outcomes recommends otherwise. Predict what will fail on the first run: students will likely smuggle indexicals into apparently neutral descriptions ("this is unfair to us"), will conflate the filter with mere impartiality, and will treat the Second-Order Observer as a hypothetical person rather than a structural constraint; advise the facilitator to interrupt only to flag smuggled indexicals, not to correct substance. Be explicit about what the exercise cannot reproduce: it can simulate the procedural pressure of justifying reasons from outside one's own standpoint, but it cannot reproduce the metaphysical claim that there genuinely is such a standpoint independent of the procedure, and it cannot capture the experience of being normatively bound by a reason one does not personally endorse — the facilitator must supply that gap in discussion. Do not assign named theories, do not ask students to cite authors, and do not include a bibliography or reading list.
