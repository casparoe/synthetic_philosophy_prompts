Contrast this with a threshold account of moral patienthood. Does recognizing gradations commit us to attributing some minimal moral status to the thermostat, or can continuity be preserved without that implication? Defend your answer."

One student submitted the following response: "The gradational view holds that sentience exists on a spectrum, so moral status should correspond to the complexity of an entity’s inner life rather than being an on-or-off property. This contrasts with the threshold view, which posits a sharp boundary—once crossed, full moral patienthood applies, and below it there is none. Peter Singer’s utilitarian approach supports this gradation, since the intensity of suffering tracks nervous-system complexity, meaning an insect merits some consideration but less than a mammal. Furthermore, Daniel Dennett’s intentional stance demonstrates that we can legitimately ascribe minimal beliefs and desires to a thermostat for predictive purposes; this places the device at the bottom of the sentience continuum with an infinitesimal but non-zero moral weight. Because the scale is continuous, there is no metaphysical gap separating insects from thermostats—the difference is only one of practical tractability, not of kind. Consequently, differential treatment is justified not by a lack of moral status in the thermostat, but by the negligibility of its interests, which remain technically present."
