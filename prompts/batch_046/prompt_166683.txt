You are a philosopher of history with expertise in collective memory studies. Please think carefully and step by step, mapping concepts precisely before drawing conclusions, and be explicit about where the framework strains rather than forcing it to speak.

Analyze the 1995–1999 traveling exhibition Verbrechen der Wehrmacht (Crimes of the Wehrmacht) in Germany as a case study in the conflict between collective memory and the historical record. The salient facts are these: for decades after 1945, a dominant collective narrative in West Germany held that the regular Wehrmacht had fought an honorable, conventional war, with systematic atrocities confined to the SS and special units; in 1995 the Hamburg Institute for Social Research mounted a traveling exhibition of archival photographs and documents showing Wehrmacht units participating in mass executions, anti-partisan terror, and cooperation with genocide in the East. The exhibition provoked mass protests, vandalism, political denunciation, and eventually a withdrawal and revision—not because its core archival thesis was refuted, but because a small number of specific photographs were found to be mislabeled or misattributed, though the broader documentary record remained intact.

Using the framework that collective memory reconstructs the past to serve present social identity, solidarity, and moral self-understanding—and the opposing claim that historical inquiry carries an epistemic and moral duty to correct collective narratives regardless of their social function—do the following.

First, map the episode with discipline: identify the mnemonic actors (veterans’ associations, families of soldiers, the postwar Federal Republic, the curating historians), the options actually available to them (full acceptance of the archival correction; wholesale rejection; a hybrid narrative that acknowledges crimes while preserving a category of “honorable” service), and what counts as evidence on each side (family testimony and regimental honor codes versus archival provenance, unit war diaries, and photographic forensics).

Second, apply the collective-memory lens: was the popular resistance merely factual denial, or was it the defense of what some scholars call “mnemonic sovereignty”—a community’s claim to authoritative control over its own narrative of virtue and suffering? And consider whether the attachment of second-generation descendants is better understood as “postmemory,” an inherited, mediated identification with parents’ and grandparents’ experiences rather than lived recollection; if so, does that change whether the historical record “should” override it, or does it show that the conflict is not about false belief but about identity preservation?

Third, examine where the theory is silent or breaks down. The exhibition was revised over a few mislabeled images despite an intact documentary core. Does this reveal that historical record is itself vulnerable to selection error and institutional framing, giving collective memory’s suspicion of archival authority some rational basis? Or does it confirm that only rigorous archival discipline can correct myth, and that the revision strengthened rather than weakened the historical case?

Fourth, conclude on the normative question this domain raises: when community memory and the record conflict, which should give way? Is the Wehrmacht episode an argument for the supremacy of the archive, for the legitimacy of mnemonic resistance, or for a negotiated third option in which history corrects without annihilating the social function of memory? Be specific about what the episode demonstrates about the theory itself—whether it is confirmed, anomalous, or never designed for cases of this scale—and note honestly any point at which the mapping between actor, option, and evidence becomes strained. I am not asking for bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; work directly from the conceptual framework.
