I'm preparing an eight-week independent study proposal on material constitution, centered on the statue and clay puzzle, but I want to push past the usual framing. The familiar trio of positions—strict identity, coincidence with differing modal or persistence conditions, and eliminativism about artifacts—treats the problem as a continuous spatiotemporal overlap: clay is gradually replaced, or suddenly reshaped, yet there is never a gap in which the substrate is wholly absent from the statue's location. My half-formed idea is that this misses a structurally different case: discontinuous constitution, in which the original clay is entirely dispersed into separate storage, leaving the statue destroyed or suspended, and then later reassembled—chemically altered perhaps, but with the artistic form and design intention fully restored—while the dispersed fragments remain available in storage. Does constitution then hold between the reassembled statue and the reunited clay, between the statue and none of the fragments, or between the statue and an entirely new aggregate? Existing answers leave this open because they conflate material origin with material presence: they explain whether a statue survives gradual part replacement, but they do not decide whether constitution requires continuous spatiotemporal presence of the substrate or can be sustained by genetic tracing and functional restoration across a temporal gap. The proposal should state this question precisely, explain why the standard replacement logic does not reduce the discontinuous case, and outline a three-phase method. In the first phase, formalize the discontinuous scenario and distinguish it rigorously from the ship of Theseus, noting that gradual replacement always preserves some overlapping material presence whereas the discontinuous case involves a period of zero substrate overlap. In the second phase, test each standard position against this scenario, tracking whether any must invoke an unmotivated asymmetry—say, allowing genetic constitution to ground persistence for the statue while denying that the dispersed fragments constituted it during storage, or treating the reassembled mass as a new object despite perfect formal continuity. In the third phase, defend a minimal ontological distinction, either that constitution is a three-place relation involving object, material, and temporal mode of presence, or that artifacts genuinely can have scattered material bases with counterintuitive counting consequences. State what a successful result looks like—ideally a taxonomy showing that at least one standard view collapses without this distinction, together with a precise statement of its ontological commitments—and state clearly what counts as failure: if the temporal gap proves philosophically irrelevant and the case collapses into standard replacement logic, or if all three standard views accommodate it identically, leaving no live disagreement. The scope should be realistic for eight weeks of conceptual work, with no empirical data collection required. Write the entire proposal as continuous prose only, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, in the style of a single formal letter. In describing the existing debate, separate what is broadly settled—such as the basic puzzle of apparently coincident objects and the standard taxonomy of responses—from what remains genuinely contested, particularly whether modal properties or persistence conditions can do the metaphysical work of distinguishing statue from clay, and do not present any contested interpretation as if it were the consensus.
