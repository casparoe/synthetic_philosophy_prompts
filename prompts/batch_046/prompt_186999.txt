The analogy is used to argue that such editing is intellectually dishonest, because the sentence was true (the figure genuinely influenced the city), and crossing it out pretends that influence never happened, even when the figure was morally flawed. Those who defend rapid removal are often portrayed as wanting to rewrite the autobiography so the city looks more virtuous than it actually was.

I'd like you to audit how far this analogy holds, with an explicit correspondence table: what plays the role of the author, the published memoir, the act of crossing out, the embarrassing sentence itself, the later reader, and the motive for revision? I am especially interested in whether the charge of dishonesty survives once we distinguish between indexical markers, which record that someone lived or acted in a particular place, and exemplary markers, which present someone as a model for citizens to imitate. Does the analogy quietly smuggle in the assumption that all commemorative practice is autobiographical self-praise rather than historical record-keeping?
