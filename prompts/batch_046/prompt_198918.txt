I was sitting in on a study session with Jake and Priya from my philosphy of science course, and they got into this surprisingly heated thing about causation, which I mostly followed but not totally. (1) Jake said the counterfactual account is basically the only coherent one: A causes B iff B wouldn't have happened without A, evaluated across nearby possible worlds. He used an example where a factory has two identical cooling pumps, Pump 1 and Pump 2, but Pump 2 is behind a locked panel with no key available. Pump 1 runs and keeps the reactor cool. Jake said if Pump 1 had failed, Pump 2 wouldn't have kicked in because nobody could open the panel, so Pump 1 genuinely caused the cooling—even though Pump 2 was physically present and functional. (2) Priya said that's exactly why counterfactualism fails: you're already smuggling in causal knowledge about what people would or wouldn't do to decide which worlds are "nearby." She defended the interventionist view—causation is about invariant relationships you can exploit by physically manipulating variables, not about imaginary worlds. She said Pump 2 isn't in the causal model at all because it's not manipulable without breaking the lock, so the counterfactual about it is irrelevant. (3) Jake fired back that interventionism secretly relies on counterfactuals anyway, because saying "if we intervene on X, Y changes" is literally just a counterfactual statement about what happens under manipulation. He said Priya hasn't eliminated counterfactuals, she's just restricted them to laboratory settings and called them "interventions." (4) Priya said that's a category mistake: an intervention is an actual physical operation, not a comparison between worlds, and that's precisely why interventionism can handle cases where counterfactualism gives weird answers—like if the key was actually in the manager's pocket all along, but nobody knew. In that hidden-key case, Jake's account might say Pump 2 could have saved the reactor, but intuitively Pump 1 still caused the cooling, and interventionism gets this right because the lock's actual state—not some hypothetical knowledge—determines what's in the model.
