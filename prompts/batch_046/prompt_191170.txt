Write the desiderata that an adequate conceptual theory of political corruption must satisfy if it aims to distinguish corruption from legitimate gift-exchange, culturally embedded clientelism, and patron-client reciprocity, without either collapsing into Western liberal parochialism or dissolving into pure cultural relativism. For each requirement, provide the motivating case or conceptual tension behind it — for example, the bureaucrat who accepts a ceremonial offering that creates social obligation but specifies no explicit quid pro quo; the patron who distributes public goods to clients in a manner that simultaneously constitutes democratic representation and vote-buying; or the leader of a rotating-credit association who diverts collective savings to secure a state contract that benefits the group, thereby blurring collective welfare and private appropriation of public resources. Mark which desiderata are non-negotiable and which belong to particular theoretical camps (e.g., whether corruption must necessarily involve private gain, whether it requires violation of formal-legal rules, whether it can occur outside public office, or whether it must be definable without reference to culturally specific role norms). Identify any subset of requirements that appears jointly unsatisfiable, especially the tension between cross-cultural applicability and the demand that corruption not be reducible to locally formal rules. Then, very briefly, assess how a legal-formalist account, a public-purpose account, and an anthropological role-logic account fare against the list. Keep the weight on the criteria and their conflicts rather than on the scoring. Please state how confident you are in what you claim and single out the point about which you are least sure.
