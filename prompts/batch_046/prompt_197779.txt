I’m a journalist who covers science and ideas—mostly neuroscience, philosophy of mind, and the occasional study on contemplative practice. I’m giving the opening remarks at this occassion, a small workshop called The Ethics of Absence, hosted by a university center I’ve written about before. The room will be quiet mixed: analytic philosophers who actually work on tranquilism—the view that only the absence of craving and suffering matters, not any positive pleasure-state—clinical researchers running trials on anhedonia, and a few contemplative teachers who’ll probably think we’re missing the point by framing stillness as a clinical outcome.

I want about seven minutes that actually hangs on the idea structurally, not just mentions it. Tranquilism treats the good life like noise-cancelling rather than composing music: it’s the removal of interference, not the addition of a track. So the remarks should shape what I can and can’t say—I shouldn’t congratulate anyone for pursuing happiness, and I shouldn’t treat absence as just another positive state we’re collecting. I’m planning to use the noise-cancelling comparison as a spine, and maybe contrast clinical anhedonia with contemplative absence so the distinction does real work. But I was arguing with a friend last week who said I was basically describing a void and calling it wisdom, so if that’s actually the confusion I’m smuggling in—if I’m treating the absence of suffering as secretly a positive achievement, or if the task can’t be done without that mistake—please call it out plainly instead of polishing over it, and draft the remarks with that constraint built in. Keep it casual; the graduate students in front should get it, but nobody should feel like they’re being lectured at.
