You are a professional philosopher of history specializing in comparative macro-history, explanatory pluralism, and the logic of counterfactual reasoning. Before constructing anything, take a few minutes to brainstorm the logical dimensions along which scholars and methodologists genuinely disagree about the well-posedness of contrastive historical questions—especially dimensions that standard surveys of the Great Divergence debate tend to collapse into a single axis.

Your task is to design a diagnostic taxonomy, structured as a decision tree or flowchart, for a graduate seminar in philosophy of history. The taxonomy must sort participants by what they actually believe about the classic framing “why Britain and not China?”—the so-called Needham question—rather than by which named scholar or school they prefer. The audience is conceptually literate but should be able to navigate the tree using only concrete judgments about cases and claims, with no prior knowledge of academic labels required.

Structure the tree with 5 to 7 branching nodes, yielding 7 to 9 leaf positions. Build it from the logical space first, then locate positions inside it: begin with the questions that split the space, map named and hypothetical positions onto the combinations of answers they allow, and explicitly mark any unoccupied combinations as either incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Where two positions collapse onto the same branch, flag them rather than papering over the failure.

The root split must address whether the contrast itself is well-formed. From there, include branches that separate:
(1) methodological objections—that the comparison is malformed because of unit incommensurability, teleological framing, or missing baseline conditions;
(2) empirical objections—that the comparison is well-formed in principle but false or overstated in fact;
(3) reformulation strategies—that the comparison survives only if the units or time-slices are redrawn, for instance replacing nation-states with regional sub-units or with functional regimes such as energy systems.

Crucially, inject one non-standard branch that asks whether the explanation-request depends on a hidden modal assumption: specifically, whether the comparison requires that China’s trajectory occupied the same counterfactual “modal space” as Britain’s (a thick baseline of shared possibilities), or whether it can stand with only a thin baseline in which no shared modal space is presupposed. Locate the position that accepts the comparison only under the thin baseline, identify its nearest neighbor that demands the thick baseline, and note whether anyone actually occupies the logical gap between them.

For each split, phrase the diagnostic question as a concrete case or claim—e.g., “If we substitute ‘Northwest Europe’ for ‘Britain’ and ‘East Asia’ for ‘China,’ does the explanatory puzzle dissolve, sharpen, or remain unchanged?”—not as “do you accept framework X?” Include at least one split that turns on a judgment about scale-dependency or measurement-unit sensitivity, since this is a point on which standard positions rarely come apart cleanly.

At the leaves, give each position a descriptive name (for example, “strict institutional divergence,” “anti-comparativist incommensurability,” “regional reformulation,” “counterfactual modal-pluralist”). Where no named view exists, label the leaf “unnamed” and provide exactly one sentence describing its most awkward commitment. Add a brief scoring guide that traces a path of answers to each leaf.

Do not include a bibliography, citation list, or reading recommendations; this instrument is meant to be fully usable without external lookup. Think carefully, work step by step, present the tree in a clean hierarchical format, and conclude by flagging any pair of positions your taxonomy cannot cleanly separate, with a one-sentence diagnosis of why they collapse together.
