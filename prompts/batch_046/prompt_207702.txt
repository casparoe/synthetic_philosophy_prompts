A person who never desired anything and never suffered disappointment lives in perfect neutrality; I judge this life excellent, perhaps optimal. Someone who achieved every goal—wealth, love, knowledge—but felt constant low-grade craving for more, with sharp suffering on failure, lives poorly, worse than the first. A being whose craving is eliminated not by satisfaction but by direct neural suppression of desire-formation, leaving rich sensory experience with zero motivation, is excellent and fully on par with the first case. A monk who meditates craving down to a faint, barely perceptible background hum—no suffering, just this trace—remains excellent, yet I feel this is slightly less perfect than pure neutrality, though I cannot justify the gap. A collective in which everyone achieves perfect tranquility via hierarchical suppression of individual desire causes mild suffering in the controllers; I say the collective state is not excellent because of this suffering, yet if the controllers too eventually reach zero craving—including any craving for control—the arrangement becomes excellent regardless of how it was produced. Finally, a dying person whose last moment is total craving-cessation with perfect clarity has supreme value in that instant, but I hesitate to call a life that contained only this moment at its end a good life overall.
