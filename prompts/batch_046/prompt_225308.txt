Two colleagues in my department, Maya and David, got into a sustained argument about whether our university should rename the Chen Library, which is named after Dr. Elena Chen, a biochemist who in the 1940s developed a life-saving synthetic process but also managed an industrial plant where poor ventilation led to dozens of worker deaths, conditions she knew about and actively suppressed. Maya argued that retaining Chen's name on a public library is not a neutral historical record but an active honor, and that honoring her in a space dedicated to knowledge and public welfare implicitly endorses her decision to prioritize production over human life. David replied that commemorative names are primarily markers of historical presence and contribution, not moral endorsements of every action taken by the namesake, and that removing Chen's name risks a kind of presentism in which we retroactively purge figures who fail current moral standards, leaving our public spaces sanitized and dishonest about the past. Maya countered that the selection of who receives a name is already a moral judgment — we do not name buildings after randomly selected citizens — so maintaining the name is not neutrality but a deliberate choice to elevate scientific achievement over the lives lost in her factory. David responded that if contribution is the standard, then consistency demands we audit every named building on campus, and that without a principled line between acceptable and unacceptable moral failures, renaming becomes merely reactive to whichever group is currently most vocal, making commemorative practice unstable and arbitrary. Maya then proposed what she called a recontextualization rather than an erasure: keep the name but redesign the library entrance as a counter-monument, with a permanent exhibition on the factory conditions and the suppressed reports, so that anyone entering the Chen Library is immediately confronted with the full record rather than an uncritical celebration. David objected that counter-monuments rarely work in practice because the original honorific framing dominates everyday use — people will still say they are meeting at the Chen Library with warmth and pride, while the critical installation fades into background architecture — which means the only intellectually honest positions are either full retention with full moral endorsement or complete removal and renaming after one of the victims or a neutral geographic descriptor. Maya said that this assumes public memory is static, but that over generations the critical framing can become primary, especially if the physical redesign makes the contradiction unavoidable rather than optional, and she added that symbolic acts matter even when they do not change material conditions because they shape what a community is willing to tolerate. They asked me to referee, and I honestly could not settle it. Please adjudicate their exchange point by point: for each of Maya's and David's moves, indicate whether it is clearly mistaken, partially right but incomplete, or clearly right, with brief justification. Then say who is more right overall, and identify what both of them are missing — for instance, whether they have adequately considered the standing of the workers' descendants in deciding the name's fate, whether symbolic change has any material effect on living people, whether there is a meaningful distinction between commemorative and documentary naming that neither has fully articulated, or whether the very concept of moral inheritance in public spaces needs more precise definition.
