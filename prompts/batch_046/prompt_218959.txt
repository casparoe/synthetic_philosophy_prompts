I'm on the conflict-resolution panel of a worker-owned cooperative that asks members to write "choice genealogies" for serious mistakes—tracing the values and prior decisions that led to them. One member, Alex, submitted a genealogy for deliberately withholding information that damaged a shared project. He traces the secretive, competitive mindset back through deliberate steps: he chose to study negotiation tactics, chose mentors who prized winning over transparency, and even chose to reject his parents' cooperative ethos after what he describes as genuine reflection. He asks us to treat this as partial mitigation, since every link in the chain was an active choice rather than passive inheritance. Another panelist, Jordan, says the genealogy only proves Jordan's point—who chose the standards by which Alex found those mentors and books appealing in the first place? Jordan argues that unless Alex is literally the uncaused source of his own nature, we are never truly justified in blaming him for being the kind of person he turned out to be, and therefore our panel should reject moral fault altogether and speak only of practical repair. I feel Jordan's conclusion is extreme, yet Alex's chain never reaches a point where he authored himself from nothing. Should we accept Alex's partial excuse, cut the chain off at some specific prior choice and hold him responsible for that, or follow Jordan to the end and eliminate blame entirely? And what practically changes if we say someone is responsible for a particular decision versus responsible for being the kind of person who makes such decisions? Please reply in continuous prose only, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, as if writing a letter.
