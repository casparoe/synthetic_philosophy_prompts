I'm preparing a long-form feature for a science-and-ideas magazine on whether "big history" — the genre that narrates human experience across evolutionary and civilizational scales, as in Yuval Noah Harari's cognitive-revolution framing and Jared Diamond's ecological determinism — survives the recent anti-teleological challenge advanced by David Graeber and David Wengrow. Rather than a conventional literature review, I need a diagnostic taxonomy: a decision tree that sorts actual intellectual positions on the legitimacy of grand narrative, built from the logical space of the debate rather than from a checklist of author names.

Construct the tree as a sequence of branching nodes, each phrased as a concrete claim that an educated general reader could evaluate without already knowing which academic label it maps onto. Begin with the root split: does explaining societies over millennia require a distinct explanatory scale with emergent properties, or is every macro-pattern ultimately reducible to local, face-to-face interactions? From there, branch through the following diagnostic questions: whether evolutionary or technological "thresholds" (the emergence of writing, agriculture, or symbolic cognition) mark genuine regime changes or are retrospective impositions; whether gaps in the archaeological record for non-state societies reflect a real structural absence of hierarchy or an "archival parallax" — the systematic distortion introduced by institutions that generate durable records; and whether the predictive failure typical of grand narratives is an epistemic disqualifier or merely a practical limitation.

The leaves must include at least the following positions, named precisely: a Harari-style cognitive-revolution narrative; a Diamond-style ecological determinism; the Graeber-Wengrow anti-teleological view; a strict microhistorical reductionism that denies scalar autonomy altogether; and at least one unnamed combination of answers that no prominent author occupies, which you should flag and briefly diagnose — is the combination incoherent, conceptually unstable, or merely unexplored? Where your tree genuinely cannot separate two named positions, say so explicitly and explain why the distinction collapses at the level of these diagnostic claims.

This should not be a neutral cartography. Take a committed position: state which branches you regard as conceptually sound, identify any answer-combinations you consider self-undermining, and defend your classification choices with explicit reasoning rather than presenting every path as equally viable. Write in formal, polished prose suitable for a journalist's research notes; do not include bibliographies, citation lists, requests for page numbers, or appeals to texts the reader cannot access without searching. The audience is an intelligent non-specialist who has not studied these primary works in depth.
