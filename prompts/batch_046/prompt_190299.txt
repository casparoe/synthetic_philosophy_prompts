In public debates over assisted dying, proponents frequently invoke the analogy to veterinary euthanasia: just as a caring owner and a clinically detached veterinarian may jointly decide that ending a suffering animal's life is merciful when recovery is impossible, so a consenting patient and physician may reach a parallel decision for irremediable human suffering. The move is meant to reframe killing as an act of compassion rather than harm, licensing the conclusion that legalization should follow. I want a rigorous audit of this analogy. Begin with an explicit correspondence table: identify what plays the role of the owner, the veterinarian, the animal's inability to project future interests, the irreversibility of the act, and the absence of legal personhood. Next, separate the disanalogies that undermine the normative conclusion—such as the patient's autonomous capacity to refuse or revoke consent, the risk that family members in the "owner" position have conflicts of interest, and the institutional fact that veterinary decisions are often shaped by economic constraints absent from human medical ethics—from merely incidental differences like species membership. Then assess whether the conclusion that assisted dying should be permitted survives once these structural breaks are visible, or whether the analogy was doing all the moral work. If the latter, propose a more apt analogy for the physician-patient relationship in end-of-life decision-making.
