I'm putting together a feature on collective minds for my magazine—specifically that weird corner people call Aunt Hillary and the Ant Fugue, where ant colonies get treated like they might actually think. I walked into this argument that my fact-checker says proves way too much, but I can't find the exit.

(1) If a system has distributed representations that guide behavior and no single part holds them, then that system is a cognitive subject in its own right.
(2) Ant colonies run on pheremone fugues—counterpoint loops where trail-laying ants reply to prior chemical signals with modified ones—to solve navigation and labor problems no single ant can manage.
(3) So the colony is a cognitive subject with mental states.
(4) The human immune system does the same thing: cytokine networks distribute pathogen representations, no one cell knows the full profile, and the network guides response.
(5) So immune systems are cognitive subjects too.
(6) But that can't be right—nobody thinks your spleen has beliefs or intentions.
(7) Therefore (1) is false, which means ant colonies don't have minds either.

I really want to keep (3) alive because the fugue framing is the whole hook for my piece, but I feel like something slips between (4) and (5). Which step should I actually reject, and what does it cost me? Please answer in American English—my editor gets cranky about British spellings.
