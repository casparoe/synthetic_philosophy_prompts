I've been thinking about conspiracy theories not as a psychologist but just as an epistemology problem, and I've landed on this view: the standard 'epistemic closure' story is mostly wrong for real-world conspiracy beliefs. By that I mean people usually don't hold them in a way that's structurally immune to counterevidence — it's not that every refutation gets swallowed into the plot. Instead, they tend to operate with an extremely high prior on institutional deception and a low trust in authoritative testimony, which means they update like anyone else but from a very different base rate. So calling them 'unfalsifiable' misses the point; they're falsifable in principle, just not by the kinds of evidence that convince people with normal priors.

What would actually count against this position? I want the arguments, empirical results, or conceptual distinctions that should move me, sorted by how decicive they'd be. Specifically, is there evidence that conspiracy believers genuinely don't update on counter-evidence even when it's presented in ways that should matter given their own framework? And has anyone shown that the 'high prior on deception' model collapses into the closure model in practice? I'm also wondering whether this view gets qualified into unfalsifiability itself — like if I can always say 'they'd update if the right evidence came,' does that make my claim empty? Would appreciate an honest assessment of whether I'm just describing rational disagreement or actually letting conspiracy theories off the hook, and whether there are defeaters that hit the view at the root rather than just being awkward.
