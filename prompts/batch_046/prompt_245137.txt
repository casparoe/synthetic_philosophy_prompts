In archaeological inference there is a durable disagreement between those who treat settlement patterns and mortuary assemblages as direct indices of institutional hierarchy—so that standardized measures, storage architectures, or differentiated grave goods license immediate inferences to centralized bureaucracy or stratified property rights—and those who hold that material culture is mediated by ritual logic that systematically obscures social reality, making direct inference circular without independent textual or ethnographic calibration. Despite extensive case studies neither camp has shifted; new excavations are consistently absorbed by both frameworks. Is this stall best explained as a hidden empirical claim about the universality of ritual mediation, a substantive disagreement about what counts as an explanation—predictive coverage of material distributions versus interpretive access to producers' intentions—or a verbal dispute about whether 'institution' denotes observable behavioral regularity or unobservable normative structure? Give me a diagnosis anchored in what each side actually demands of an inference, not a survey of positions, and say honestly whether any archaeological finding, formal model, or conceptual distinction could resolve it, or whether the impasse indicates the question itself is ill-posed.
