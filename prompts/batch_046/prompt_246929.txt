I have been arguing with a close friend since late last night about how to explain the participation of ordinary Germans in the Holocaust, and I want to check whether the position I have landed on is actually coherent before I present it in a graduate workshop. Here are my commitments: (a) Browning's study of Reserve Police Battalion 101 shows that the men were psychologically unremarkable, largely unideologized, and that peer dynamics and bureaucratic distancing were sufficient to secure mass compliance; (b) Goldhagen's claim that a pervasive eliminationist antisemitism shaped German culture is also correct, but I treat it as a background enabling condition rather than a proximate motivational cause; (c) moral responsibility requires only that an agent retain reflective awareness that an action is wrong, a capacity I believe these men possessed even under strong social pressure; (d) if two factors are each sufficient for the same outcome, they are overdetermined rather than competing, so situational and cultural explanations should be integrated rather than ranked; (e) nevertheless, I want to apply a criterion of counterfactual robustness—preferring the factor that would still have produced the behavior if the other had been absent—which leads me to say situational pressure was the decisive cause, while cultural hatred was redundant. Do these hang together? Specifically, does my acceptance of (d) make my preference in (e) arbitrary, and does (c)'s emphasis on individual reflective capacity undermine (b)'s collective cultural explanation without also undermining (a)'s situational account? Please rank the tensions by severity, identify whether there is a hidden contradiction between treating cultural hatred as both background-enabling and redundant, and propose the least costly revision that preserves my core intuition that ordinary people can do extraordinary evil without being extraordinary people.
