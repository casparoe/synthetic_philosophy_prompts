I have recently been diagnosed with early-stage Parkinson’s disease and am now weighing whether to pursue deep brain stimulation. This clinical decision has made the neuroscience of voluntary action suddenly personal: if an implanted device can modulate basal ganglia circuits involved in initiating movement, what exactly remains “mine” about the movements I make? I have tried to work through this by revisiting Benjamin Libet’s experiments on the readiness potential, and below are notes for an essay I am drafting. I would welcome your comments, particularly on three points: first, whether I have interpreted Libet’s distinction between conscious initiation and conscious veto with reasonable accuracy; second, whether applying this framework to DBS-mediated agency is philosophically coherent, or whether it illicitly conflates endogenous pre-conscious preparation with exogenous electrical modulation; and third, whether the argument succeeds in being genuinely interesting rather than merely repeating familiar determinism-versus-free-will debates.

The standard account of Libet’s findings—EEG studies conducted in the 1980s measuring the Bereitschaftspotential—is that unconscious neural activity precedes the conscious experience of deciding to move by several hundred milliseconds. The usual inference drawn in popular philosophy is that conscious will is an epiphenomenal post-hoc narration. I want to resist this inference by emphasizing a feature of Libet’s own interpretation that is often underdeveloped: the conscious veto. Even if the brain initiates a motor program pre-consciously, Libet suggested that consciousness retains the capacity to suppress or allow the action in the final moments before execution. On this view, agency is not authorial—consciousness does not write the first draft of action—but editorial: it reviews, authorizes, or cancels drafts generated by subcortical processes.

My proposed extension—perhaps the core claim of the essay—is that this editorial model offers a more nuanced way to think about agency in the context of neurointervention. Deep brain stimulation does not replace the patient as an agent; rather, it alters the quality or intensity of the pre-conscious drafts that reach conscious review. The patient with DBS still exercises veto and authorization, but over a modified pool of impulses. If this is right, then the threat to free will posed by neuroscience is not that consciousness is causally irrelevant, but that the boundary between “my” drafts and “externally modified” drafts becomes philosophically ambiguous. The essay then asks whether we need a new category—something like mediated agency—to capture cases where the editorial function remains intact but the provenance of the drafts is distributed between brain and device.

What I need is not a bibliography, a reading list, or citations I could not verify without access to a library, but targeted commentary: where does this line of reasoning collapse into confusion? Does the editorial model genuinely accommodate DBS, or am I stretching Libet’s framework beyond its empirical support? And is there an obvious objection—perhaps from accounts of therapy versus enhancement, or from compatibilist theories of agency—that I have ignored?
