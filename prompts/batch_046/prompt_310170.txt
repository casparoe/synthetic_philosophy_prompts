hey, i'm working through this idea about free-floating rationales — reasons that exist even when there's no reasoner around to hold them. i jotted down some rough notes for a discussion group and i'm not totally sure the distinction i'm making holds water. here's the gist:

i want to seperate them into three kinds. first, derivative rationales: you have reason to keep a promise becuase you're embedded in a social practice that generates obligations. second, immanent rationales: a thermostat tracks temperature and in some functional sense has a "reason" to cool the room, but there's no mind or representation doing the work. third, fully orphaned rationales: normative pressures that apply even when no system tracks them — maybe there's genuinely a reason not to cause suffering even if every sentient creature dies and no record remains.

then i try to apply this to a weird case. imagine a genetic algorithm running in a sealed server with no human oversight. it optimizes for energy efficiency, doesn't represent its goals, and eventually the server dies with no observer. is there an orphaned rationale saying it should have optimized differently, or is that just us projecting? my worry is that if we answer yes, we're treating the algorithm like it has immanent rationales, but then i can't see what makes immanent different from orphaned except that one is easier to spot.

can you look over this and tell me if the three-way split makes sense, whether the server case actually pulls immanent and orphaned apart, and if i'm smuggling a reasoner back in through the word "should"? also, is this actually an interesting line of thought or just a taxonomy for taxonomy's sake? any suggestions on where it collapses would be great.
