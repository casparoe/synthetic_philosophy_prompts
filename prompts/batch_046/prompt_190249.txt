Read Steven Spielberg’s Schindler’s List (1993) through the lens of Plutarch’s practice in the Parallel Lives, where biography serves moral education not by presenting flawless exemplars but by juxtaposing lives so that virtue and vice illuminate each other — a method always threatened by hagiography, which flattens complexity into edifying myth. The film traces Oskar Schindler from profiteer and womaniser to rescuer, pairing him implicitly with Amon Göth and with Itzhak Stern. Examine whether this structure operates like a Plutarchan parallel: does comparing Schindler with Göth (both men of appetite and power, one destructive, one redemptive) teach calibrated moral judgement, or does the comparison collapse into a simple morality tale?

Consider in particular the idea of ‘moral chiaroscuro’ — the deliberate retention of darkness around a luminous figure so that the viewer learns to judge virtue in context rather than imitate an impossible ideal. Spielberg preserves Schindler’s flaws (his infidelity, his initial exploitation of Jewish labour for profit, his ambiguous motives) and uses black-and-white cinematography to literalise this tonal contrast. Does this serve Plutarch’s pedagogical aim, or does the narrative’s redemptive arc — especially the girl in red, the list itself, and Schindler’s final breakdown — resolve the darkness into hagiography?
