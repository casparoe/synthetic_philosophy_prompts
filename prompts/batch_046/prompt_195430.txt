I'm preparing a 90-minute session for an upper-level philosophy of religion seminar—about twelve graduate students with some background in Indian philosophy—and I want them to feel the cognitive pressure of Jain anekāntavāda rather than treating it as a polite pluralist slogan. The activity should center on syādvāda and its sevenfold predication schema, saptabhaṅgī, arranged so that participants experience logical friction between assertion-modes in real time: not merely that different people hold different views, but that a single judgment must be held in conditional form, and that the seventh mode—avaktavya, the inexpressible—operates as an active epistemic brake rather than a diplomatic retreat.

Set up a structured deliberation around a concrete, morally charged policy case with no explicit religious content—say, whether a municipality should permit selective logging in an old-growth watershed to fund climate-adaptive infrastructure. Assign each student one of the seven syādvāda modes as a binding speech constraint for the first 30 minutes: they may only advance arguments that conform to their mode's logical form (syād asti; syād nāsti; syād asti ca nāsti ca; syād avaktavya; and the three compound forms combining inexpressibility with affirmation, negation, or both). Layer on an additional naya card that fixes whether they reason from substance-conditions (dravyārthika) or from modal, temporal conditions (paryāyārthika), so that two students with the same syādvāda mode still talk past each other on the merits.

Design explicit rules, materials, and a peer-evaluation mechanism—without real money, but with a status payoff tied to coherence within one's assigned constraints rather than to persuasive victory. Provide a timeline (approximately 30 minutes of constrained deliberation, 20 minutes of reassessment with modes lifted, 15 minutes of debrief), and in the debrief map specific breakdown moments—especially the deadlock between pure affirmation and pure negation, and the disruptive effect when avaktavya is invoked not as silence but as a substantive claim about predicate-limits—onto the Jain distinction between epistemic partiality (each naya is genuinely true within its domain) and metaphysical pluralism (reality itself is multiform, anekānta). Be frank about what this classroom setup cannot reproduce, likely the long soteriological discipline of holding all seven modes as an internalized practice rather than an assigned role, and warn me about the predictable first-run failure mode in which students treat the seven modes as rhetorical costumes rather than as logical constraints on what can be said. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citation requests; the design should be answerable from conceptual familiarity with anekāntavāda, but inject enough specificity about the naya categories and the compound saptabhaṅgī forms that the answer cannot collapse into generic perspective-taking exercises.
