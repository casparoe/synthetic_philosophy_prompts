Two members of a philosophy of physics reading group, Mara and Julian, have reached an impasse over Kurt Gödel's philosophical conclusions about time in his rotating-universe solution to Einstein's field equations. I want you to adjudicate their exchange point by point.

Mara's position: Gödel demonstrated that in a rotating universe, closed timelike curves exist, making it impossible to define a global cosmic time. From this he concluded that objective temporal becoming—the A-series of past, present, and future—is physically unreal, and that only a four-dimensional block ontology survives. She insists that local temporal ordering along worldlines does not save becoming, because Gödel's point is precisely that without global synchronization the intuitive present has no physical anchor.

Julian's response: Mara misreads the argument's logical structure. Gödel used the failure of global time to show that the concept of a universal present is coordinate-dependent, not that time itself is illusory. Local causality and temporal relations remain intact; the ontology should therefore be a form of local relationalism about temporal properties, not eternalism. Julian further claims that Mara implicitly relies on an unstated chronometric accessibility principle—namely, that only globally measurable temporal parameters are ontologically real—which Gödel never explicitly defended. Without that principle, the rotating universe merely yields a non-standard temporal topology.

Mara's counter: Even if Gödel never named the principle, his inference requires it. If local temporal relations remain fully intact, there would be no reason to deny objective becoming globally unless one already assumes that global definability is necessary for reality. So the principle is not smuggled in; it is the hidden premise that makes Gödel's move from physics to ontology valid.

Julian's final reply: If that principle is truly implicit, then Gödel's argument is circular—he assumes global synchronization is necessary for real time, proves it is absent in rotating models, and then declares time unreal. Without that controversial premise, all we have is a cosmological model with closed loops and local temporal order, which does not force eternalism at all.

Please go through their exchange and say who is right about each specific point—the logical structure of Gödel's argument, the status of local temporal relations, whether the chronometric accessibility principle is present or required, and whether the conclusion supports eternalism or local relationalism. Then say who is more right overall, and identify anything both are missing—perhaps about whether Gödel's own metaphysical commitments affect the interpretation, or about whether modern cosmological data renders the rotating-universe model moot regardless of its philosophical implications.
