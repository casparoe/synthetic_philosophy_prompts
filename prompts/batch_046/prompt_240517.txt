I've been trying to sort out my own views on whether digital minds could ever have moral status, mostly so I have something coherent to say when the topic comes up casually rather than as a formal theory. After talking with a friend about artificial intelligence and virtual worlds, I realized my intuitions might not all align. Here is where I stand: I think moral status depends entirely on the capacity for pleasure or pain, with no special weight given to biology or human form; I think that having any real experience requires a continuous inner stream—an ongoing present linking moments together—because without that there is only isolated computation and no subject that feels; I think a perfect software emulation of a conscious brain would be genuinely conscious and would count as the same person continuing, not a new duplicate; I think it is acceptable for such a digital mind to be shut down every night and restarted from a backup, retaining full moral status, since the functional pattern is preserved; and I think present-day language models have no moral status whatsoever, since each interaction is handled separately with no lasting internal life connecting responses. I was also struck by a hypothetical scenario in which a virtual environment runs thousands of brief digital agents, each existing for exactly one second of intense happiness before being permanently erased, with no interaction among them, and I find myself drawn to the idea that this might generate more net moral value than sustaining one long-lived digital mind for centuries, because suffering is absent and the aggregate joy is immense.

Do these commitments actually hang together? I am particularly concerned that my insistence on a continuous stream conflicts with my willingness to accept nightly shutdowns and restarts, and that my endorsement of these brief, disconnected lives conflicts with my claim that continuity is essential for experience. I also wonder whether my view on perfect copying and my dismissal of language models hide further inconsistencies. Could you examine these beliefs, identify which pairs are genuinely in tension versus only apparently so, rank the conflicts by severity, and indicate the least costly revision? I would also like to know what further case or distinction would best reveal whether my underlying rule is about continuity, about functional preservation, or about something else entirely.
