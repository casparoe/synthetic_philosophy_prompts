I'm preparing a seminar module on Confucian role ethics—lunli—for advanced philosophy undergraduates, and a colleague recently pushed me on a tension that I think cuts deeper than the usual virtue-ethics comparison. Classical accounts presuppose a relatively stable social fabric in which roles are largely ascribed and hierarchical, yet contemporary defenders want to extend the framework to modern contexts where roles are voluntary, adversarial, or institutionally fragmented: think of corporate whistleblowers, climate activists who reject inherited national roles, or caregivers negotiating bureaucratic healthcare systems. Rather than asking for a broad literature overview, I want to know which open conceptual lines of work should take priority if we take what I will call "negative role space" seriously—that is, the moral significance of refusing, corrupting, inventing, or permanently exiting roles, as opposed to merely performing assigned ones with excellence. If you were advising a small conceptual-research group with about four years of funding and no mandate to produce empirical social science, which problems should they tackle first, and in what order? I am especially unsure whether the so-called "bad role problem"—how to be a good occupant of an inherently unjust role—is genuinely upstream of other work, or whether it is actually a distraction from the harder preliminary task of defining what role-negation itself consists in and whether it can be virtuous rather than merely destructive. Please defend your ranking with explicit criteria: conceptual tractability for philosophers rather than sociologists, how many other debates in the field depend on resolving this particular question, and what kind of argument or conceptual clarification over the next few years would cause you to reorder the priorities. I want a defended agenda, not a survey, and I would appreciate the response in American English.
