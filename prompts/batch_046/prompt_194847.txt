I am working on a paper for my philosophy of mind course, and my instructor said my reasoning about other minds contains a step that proves far more than I intend, though I cannot see where the slide occurs. I have tried to construct the argument without leaning on standard textbook formulations. Here is the chain. (1) Whenever I attribute a mental state to another human being, my only direct evidence is observed behavior and its correlation with physical conditions; I never have perceptual access to their consciousness itself. (2) The same evidential structure holds for my own mental life: my knowledge of past beliefs and present subconscious processes is mediated by memory traces, behavioral outputs, and theoretical inference, not by an unmediated direct pipeline. (3) Therefore, by what I call epistemic parity—if two cases of attribution share an identical evidential structure, they ought to share the same ontological status—there is no privileged boundary separating self-knowledge from other-knowledge, or present-self from past-self. (4) Now consider an artificial predictive system trained solely on my behavioral data, which forecasts my mental reports with near-perfect accuracy despite possessing no internal experience of its own. By (3), if correlational access suffices to justify attributing minds to other humans, it must also justify attributing a mind to this system, or justify the system attributing one to me. (5) But this seems to dissolve the problem of other minds into pure behaviorism and implies that any sufficiently accurate statistical model counts as a bearer of mental states, which is clearly absurd. I want to preserve (1) and (3) if possible, because they express my naturalistic commitments, but I am willing to revise (4) or (5). Which step is the error? And if the mistake lies in (3), does abandoning it force me to accept a mysterious, non-inferential form of self-access that violates the very naturalism I hoped to defend?
