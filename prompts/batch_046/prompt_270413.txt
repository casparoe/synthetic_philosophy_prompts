I'm currently taking an advanced seminar in distributive justice and applied ethics, and over the past few weeks we've been working through the anti-aggregation view associated with John Taurek—specifically the claim that each individual facing harm makes an equal moral claim on rescue resources, and that the mere fact that five people are threatened rather than one does not, by itself, generate a stronger reason to aid the larger group. I find the theoretical argument compelling, but I keep returning to a practical worry: if I'm interning with a regional disaster-response coordination office that must review extraction proposals under severe capacity constraints—say, a single helicopter can reach either one stranded party or another within a weather window, but not both—the coordinators need a reusable decision procedure, not a philosophical essay. They have about fifteen minutes per proposal, no formal ethics training, and must produce an auditable justification signed off by a supervisor; they also operate under a charter that explicitly rejects head-count as a decisive factor but offers no substitute mechanism. I want you to translate this anti-aggregation principle into an operational protocol my team could actually run: specify the ordered questions, what counts as a sufficient answer at each step, where the procedure stops with a documented "no" or defers to a designated moral authority, and—crucially—where judgment cannot be eliminated and must be marked as such. The protocol must include a tie-breaking mechanism for cases where no non-numerical distinction applies, and it must incorporate a "moral residue" documentation step that records the unfulfilled claim of those not extracted without treating that record as a utilitarian ledger entry or allowing it to be smuggled back in as an aggregation proxy. Apply the procedure to three scenarios: (a) one injured climber versus four injured climbers with identical injuries, no special relationships, and equal accessibility; (b) a single parent with two young children versus three unrelated adults, where proximity, vulnerability, and relational bonds differ in non-quantifiable ways; and (c) one scenario where the procedure yields an answer the coordinator will strongly dislike—for instance, selecting a smaller group when a larger group is equally reachable and there are no distinguishing non-numerical features. Be explicit about what the procedure cannot do: for example, does it permit probabilistic estimates of future harm, or must such reasoning be strictly excluded? Finally, identify the precise point where the protocol is most vulnerable to being misused by someone who wants to reintroduce aggregation through the documentation or tie-breaking steps, and suggest one structural safeguard against that misuse.
