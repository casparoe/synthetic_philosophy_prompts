I am a novelist constructing the philosophical scaffolding for a multi-generational saga that moves from the post-war period through to the present. Before I allow my characters to inhabit generational identities—Baby Boomers, Generation X, Millennials, Gen Z—as if they were natural kinds, I have drafted a brief working essay tentatively entitled "The APC Problem and the Ontology of Cohorts: Generations as Narrative Fictions with Institutional Weight". The core argument is that the age-period-cohort identification problem in epidemiology and demography demonstrates that cohort, age, and historical period are linearly dependent variables; consequently, any claim that a generation exists as a metaphysically discrete entity collapses into an arbitrary modelling choice. From this epistemic underdetermination, I draw an ontological conclusion: generations are not natural kinds. Nevertheless, I propose that they function as what I call "performative institutional kinds"—entities constituted not by shared biological essence but by census classifications, marketing segmentation, and, significantly, the narrative architectures (family chronicles, historical fiction, political memoirs) that retroactively impose coherence upon continuous demographic flow. This performative ontology, I suggest, explains the phenomenological reality of generational belonging without granting generations independent causal powers; they are self-fulfilling prophecies mediated through patterns of cultural consumption.

I should be grateful for rigorous critical comments on this sketch, particularly on three matters. First, does the APC identification problem genuinely warrant an ontological conclusion, or am I illicitly sliding from epistemic indistinguishability to metaphysical non-existence? Second, is the category of "performative institutional kinds" conceptually coherent, or does it reduce to an unilluminating form of social constructionism that cannot account for the subjective weight of cohort identity? Third, I have deliberately eschewed conventional sociological frameworks in favour of this epidemiological-narrative hybrid; does this lend the argument a productive originality, or does it risk being merely idiosyncratic and insufficiently grounded? I seek not reassurance but precise conceptual criticism that might strengthen the philosophical backbone of the novel.
