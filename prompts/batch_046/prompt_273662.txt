I'm trying to refine a principle about when trading accuracy for other values becomes corruption rather than justified simplification. My tentative framing is that an inaccuracy is permissible only when it functions as epistemic scaffolding: (i) the audience genuinely could not reach the correct belief through accurate presentation at their current capacity; (ii) it does not misattribute agency, credit, or causal responsibility to anyone; and (iii) it carries an implicit or explicit debt to be corrected once the audience is capable. Here are my verdicts. (1) A climate scientist rounds down confidence intervals in a public policy brief to prevent decision paralysis, while the full ranges remain in the technical appendix: acceptable—(i) and (ii) hold, and (iii) is satisfied by the published record. (2) A therapist allows a grieving patient's false belief that they caused a parent's death to stand unchallenged for several sessions, planning gentle correction only after stabilization: acceptable—no agent is misrepresented, and the debt is scheduled. (3) A physics textbook presents the Bohr model as literal planetary orbits, knowing it is physically false, to build intuition before quantum mechanics is introduced: acceptable—classic (i), (ii) is unviolated, and (iii) is paid by the subsequent curriculum. (4) A historian writing for general readers collapses a multi-factor causal explanation into one dominant narrative, omitting a genuinely minor contributing factor that does not involve a marginalized group being erased: acceptable—(i) holds, (ii) holds, and (iii) is met by specialist literature. (5) A clinical-trial report rounds a p-value of 0.06 to 0.05 to cross a publication threshold, even though the drug is genuinely safe and the effect is real but noisy: not acceptable—(i) fails because the accurate report is available to the intended scientific audience, and (ii) fails because it misrepresents the statistical process itself rather than simplifying it. (6) A journalist paraphrases a leaked quotation, preserving its propositional content but altering its rhetorical force and emotional valence, to protect a vulnerable source: I keep going back and forth—it seems to satisfy (i) and (ii), but (iii) may be permanently unpayable if the original recording is never released, which makes it feel closer to (5) than to (1)-(4). Does the scaffolding principle survive intact, or must it be repaired or abandoned? In particular, where does (6) fall, and what does the principle predict for an unlisted case: a philosopher writing for non-specialists who attributes to Kant a streamlined version of the categorical imperative that most Kant scholars reject, on the grounds that the nuanced original is cognitively inaccessible to lay readers and the streamlined version reliably guides moral action? Please lead with your bottom-line assessment of the principle in the first sentence, then apply it case by case with explicit reference to clauses (i)-(iii), state honestly which of my verdicts it must overturn if any, and avoid preamble or restatement of the question.
