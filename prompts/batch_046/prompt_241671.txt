I'm a doctoral student in philosophy of science working at the intersection of evolutionary theory and philosophy of mind, and I am drafting a short research proposal—approximately two pages, suitable for a summer workshop or as a dissertation prospectus chapter—on a conceptual question that I think has been obscured by the way existing literatures stay in their separate silos. The problem concerns cases where high-level functional competence systematically outstrips any corresponding comprehension in the system that produces it. Darwin's theory of natural selection provides the canonical biological instance: evolutionary processes generate exquisitely adapted organisms without any understanding, intention, or representational grasp of the design problems being solved. Turing's conception of machine intelligence offers a structurally parallel inversion: a computing device can exhibit behaviors we classify as intelligent without possessing any internal comprehension of the meanings or purposes it processes. Although each inversion is well studied on its own—Darwin's as an ontological claim about design without a designer, Turing's as a methodological claim about behavior without mental representation—almost no one has asked whether they share a single logical form that I want to call competence decoupling, or whether that shared form jointly undermines the standard philosophical justification for epistemic authority, which typically assumes that reliable expertise must be grounded in some form of understanding.

The precise question I need the proposal to frame is this: do Darwin's and Turing's inversions instantiate an identical structure of decoupling between functional success and representational comprehension, and if so, does that structure jointly challenge the view that comprehension is a necessary condition for justified epistemic deference, or do the two cases diverge in ways that preserve that condition in evolutionary contexts but dissolve it in computational ones? The proposal should explain why existing answers leave this open: philosophers of science have treated Darwin's inversion primarily as an argument against natural theology, while philosophers of AI have treated Turing's inversion mainly as a claim about the sufficiency of behavioral criteria for intelligence, but neither tradition has systematically mapped whether the gap between competent performance and cognitive understanding operates identically across biological and artificial systems, especially with respect to normative questions about when we are justified in deferring to processes we do not ourselves comprehend.

The approach should avoid both historical textual scholarship and empirical case studies of contemporary machine learning. Instead, I want a conceptual taxonomy that distinguishes causal, functional, and normative dimensions of decoupling, applied symmetrically to evolutionary adaptation and to computation without cognition. I have chosen this framing over the obvious alternatives because only by separating these dimensions can we determine whether the challenge to comprehension-based authority is genuinely unified or merely analogical. The proposal should describe what a useful result would look like—perhaps a demonstration that both inversions collapse the distinction between reliability and authority in the same way, or perhaps a sharper argument that evolutionary decoupling is teleologically neutral while computational decoupling carries a specific normative deficit, which would mean the implications for epistemic deference differ between the domains.
