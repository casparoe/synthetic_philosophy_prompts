I have been reading through Hume's Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding, focusing on the passages where he examines the basis of our inferences about cause and effect. He argues that all reasoning concerning matters of fact depends on the relation of cause and effect, that this relation is discovered only through experience, and that experience can never establish the necessary connection required to justify projecting past patterns onto the future. Pressed for the principle that actually produces this expectation, he denies that it is reason and instead identifies custom or habit, describing it as a principle of human nature that determines the mind to form beliefs about the unobserved. I am trying to understand whether this is meant as a purely descriptive psychological claim—an account of how belief is formed—or whether it carries a normative implication that no rational justification for induction is possible at all.

This seems to bear on a problem I recently tried to think through in connection with Goodman's formulation of the new riddle of induction. Goodman asks why we are entitled to project predicates like green rather than contrived alternatives such as grue, where an object is grue if it is green before some future time and blue thereafter, given that both predicates have been satisfied by every emerald we have observed. Some discussions I have seen treat Goodman's puzzle as essentially a formalization of Hume's skeptical conclusion: if induction is nothing but custom, then our preference for green is merely an accident of psychological history, and there is no rational distinction between well-behaved and gerrymandered predicates. But I am unsure whether this assimilation is fair. Goodman appears to be asking a normative question about which predicates are projectible, whereas Hume seems to be explaining the causal mechanism by which we come to expect uniformity. I want to know whether Hume's text actually licenses the strong skeptical reading—namely, that induction is arbitrary except for inertia—or whether his appeal to custom is compatible with there being implicit constraints on which regularities we can stably project.

Could someone walk me through the relevant passage with this distinction in mind? I am particularly interested in whether Hume's reduction of causal inference to custom entails that all inductive projections are on a par except for psychological entrenchment, or whether there is something in his account—perhaps in the presupposition of a uniform course of nature—that allows us to distinguish legitimate projections from Goodman-style contrivances without appealing to an independent rational justification. And if Hume genuinely offers no normative resources, does that mean Goodman's riddle is not a deeper version of Hume's problem but rather a different kind of question altogether—one that only arises when we try to rationalize induction rather than merely explain it?
