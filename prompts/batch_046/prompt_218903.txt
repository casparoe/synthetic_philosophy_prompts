I'm trying to work out what actually distinguishes a real "imagined community" in Anderson's sense from a merely invented tradition in Hobsbawm's, especially when both involve recent symbols and reconstructed pasts. In a course discussion we kept circling back to whether the direction of construction—bottom-up cultural solidarity versus top-down state fabrication—matters, and whether statehood is necessary. Here are my gut verdicts on concrete cases, which mostly cohere but contain one pair that pulls hard in opposite directions.

The Scottish Highland tartan and clan-kilt system, largely formalized in the 1800s by Victorian antiquarians and then commercialized, is now treated worldwide as ancient clan heritage; I say it's a clear invented tradition, yet Scottish national feeling within Britain feels genuinely older, so the invention is ornamental rather than foundational. The Meiji state's imperial Shinto rituals and the narrative of an unbroken imperial line reaching to antiquity were reconstructed to serve modern centralization; I take this as classic top-down invention, with Japanese nationhood modern in its political form even if the linguistic substrate is old. The European Union's flag and anthem, adopted by institutional decision in the mid-twentieth century with little pre-modern popular "European" consciousness, now appear in schools and sports; my verdict is that the EU is not a nation, because its symbols feel administratively imposed and lack the anonymous horizontal comradeship Anderson emphasizes, despite some elite identification. Kurdistan lacks continuous statehood, adopted a flag and national narrative largely crystallized in the twentieth century, and is spread across four existing states, yet rests on a shared Kurdish language, territory, and collective memory of displacement; I judge it a real imagined community—a nation without a state—because its symbols express a pre-existing solidarity rather than manufacturing one. The Iroquois (Haudenosaunee) Confederacy's Great Law of Peace is traditionally dated to the twelfth through fifteenth centuries, but its written constitutional form was largely reconstructed and translated in the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, partly to assert sovereignty against colonial powers; I think the political community is genuinely ancient and continuous, yet the specific "constitutional tradition" framing is partly modern invention—though this feels different from the tartan case because the invention serves communal self-defense rather than state-building or tourism. The tension that breaks my pattern is the EU versus Kurdistan: both lack sovereign states, both adopted modern flags and anthems, both encompass diverse internal populations, and both depend on institutional framing, yet I want to call Kurdistan a nation and the EU not one, without simply saying "it feels organic."

My tentative principle is that a community counts as a nation when it exhibits Anderson-style horizontal comradeship among people who will never meet, and invented traditions undermine nationhood only when they are elite-imposed substitutes for that comradeship rather than expressions of it. On this view the tartan is ornament, the Meiji rituals are substitutive but sit atop real substrate, and Kurdistan's symbols are expressive; but the principle struggles with the Iroquois case—was the constitutional framing substitutive or expressive?—and it doesn't clearly explain the EU, since some Europeans do report cross-border solidarity. The case I keep going back and forth on is Liberland, declared in 2015 on a disputed stretch of the Danube, with citizens recruited mostly online, its own flag and constitution, sovereignty recognized by almost no one, and territory that remains under another state's practical control: is this an imagined community that is merely tiny and unsuccessful, or does it lack the necessary substrate altogether?
