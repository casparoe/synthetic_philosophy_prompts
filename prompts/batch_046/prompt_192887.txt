I am preparing a handout for a graduate metaphysics seminar on modal ontology and want a branching taxonomy—structured as a decision tree with yes/no or either/or nodes—that sorts live positions on whether concrete objects have transworld identity or are represented by counterparts. Begin the tree from a single concrete modal judgment, such as whether this particular table could have been made of different wood, and branch through commitments about whether modal predication requires literally the same object in the other world; whether cross-world identity is primitive or analysis-dependent; whether counterpart relations are grounded in qualitative similarity, in shared causal origins, or in essential properties of origin; and whether accepting counterparts forces the rejection of origin essentialism. Include a hidden branch that addresses the rarely discussed “mereological counterpart” problem: whether a counterpart relation can hold between an object and a fusion of parts in another world, and place this branch so that it separates similarity-based accounts that permit cross-category counterparts from origin-based accounts that deny them. Phrase every node as a question a student could answer by considering a specific case, without knowing the position labels in advance. Flag any pair of leaves the tree genuinely cannot separate, explaining whether the ambiguity comes from an unaddressed case or from an internal tension in a view. Finally, conclude with a brief note that separates what is widely agreed about the logical architecture of the debate from what remains genuinely contested—such as whether counterpart theorists must treat modal properties as non-intrinsic, or whether origin essentialism can be sustained without primitive transworld identity—and do not present any single reading of Lewis or Kripke as the undisputed consensus.
