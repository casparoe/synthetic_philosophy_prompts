In my reading group and in recent political theory, the debate over whether "left" and "right" still name real divisions has stalled in a way that feels structural rather than evidential. One camp cites cross-cutting coalitions—environmentalists aligning with libertarians on some axes, social authoritarians with economic socialists on others—and concludes the distinction is an obsolete spatial metaphor lacking essential content. The other camp insists that something enduring binds each side, whether an orientation toward equality versus hierarchy or a shared enemy structure, and that apparent incoherence is merely surface-level coalition politics. Both sides invoke the same electoral realignments, the same 1789 seating genealogy, and the same conceptual history, yet neither has shifted in years. My working hypothesis is that they are answering different questions under one name: one treats left/right as a sociological claim about current group alignment, the other as a normative-psychological claim about which moral axes organize judgment, so empirical counterexamples to one never reach the other. Is that diagnosis right? Or is the stall driven by a hidden disagreement—about whether coalitions need shared positive commitments or can be held together by opposition alone, about whether "realness" requires a single axis or permits family resemblance, or about what counts as a political reason versus a strategic alliance—supported by what each side actually says? I want the diagnosis, not a survey: do they differ on facts, on standards of evidence for conceptual reality, on which cases count as data, or on bedrock intuitions about coherence and naturalness? And given that diagnosis, what would count as progress—a formal model of coalition dynamics, a distinction between positional and substantive opposition, a reframing of the question—or should we expect permanent impasse because the concept is irreducibly plural?
