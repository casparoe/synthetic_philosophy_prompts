In a workshop on historical methodology, a colleague defended a progressive narrative of constitutional rights by invoking the saying that "the past is prologue"—treating earlier institutions as mere anticipations of present liberal arrangements and dismissing discontinuities as historiographical noise. I want to formulate a precise objection, but first I need to understand what work that slogan is actually doing. Could you lay out the distinct theses it might express—say, (i) a weak hermeneutic claim about narrative coherence, (ii) a causal claim about institutional path dependence, (iii) a teleological claim that history carries an immanent direction, and (iv) a normative claim that present arrangements inherit legitimacy from their antecedents? For each reading, indicate whether it genuinely licenses writing the past as progress toward the present, whether it commits the Whig fallacy, and whether it can accommodate radical conceptual rupture—specifically, cases where a society abandons a central normative vocabulary entirely, with no transitional bridge that would make the earlier vocabulary a meaningful "prologue" to the later one.
