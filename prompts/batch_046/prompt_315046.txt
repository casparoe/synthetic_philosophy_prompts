I just finished a long argument with a friend who studies Jain logic, and she was adamant that anekāntavāda is not casual pluralism but a disciplined method: every assertion must carry the syat qualifier (in some respect), every standpoint is a naya that reveals only one facet of a manifold object, and contradictions dissolve once predicates are properly relativized. I chair an interdisciplinary review board at a university that adjudicates, roughly once a month, conflicts in which two departments claim exclusive conceptual authority—say, over justice, consciousness, or human rights—with no philosophers on staff, a forty-five-minute decision window, and a requirement to sign off personally. I want you to operationalize anekāntavāda into a reusable decision procedure: an ordered sequence of tests my board can run, specifying what counts as a sufficient answer at each step, where the procedure issues a clear rejection of an exclusivity claim, where it halts and defers to a human values judgment rather than a factual one, and where it is likely to misfire. Apply the procedure to three submissions: (1) a neuroscience unit claiming consciousness is identically neural activation and other frameworks are irrelevant; (2) a comparative-religion unit claiming consciousness is identically ātman and empirical methods are inapplicable; and (3) an environmental-studies proposal I personally support, arguing that ecological interdependence is the sole correct ontology for human rights—show me precisely where the procedure yields an answer I will dislike. End by stating what this procedure cannot do, especially when a dispute is not about ontology but about institutional power or resource allocation.
