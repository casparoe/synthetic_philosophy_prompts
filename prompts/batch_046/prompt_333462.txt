I am drafting a position sketch for an advanced seminar on moral psychology and want a rigorous consistency check before I circulate it to colleagues. My framework is built around Adam Smith's account of sympathy and the impartial spectator, but I have tried to synthesize it with some less standard claims about cultural embeddedness, narrative cultivation, and the epistemic effects of politeness norms. Here is the package I am holding together. First, I understand sympathy as an imaginative projection into another's situation that depends on relatively accurate emotional data—both observed circumstances and expressed sentiments—without which the projection misfires. Second, I take the impartial spectator to be an internalized social construct rather than an innate faculty, shaped by the particular norms of propriety and emotional display that prevail in one's own community. Third, despite this deep cultural embeddedness, I maintain that the spectator can legitimately adjudicate moral disagreements between members of radically different social structures—say, between a hierarchical honor culture and a commercial egalitarian society—without merely imposing its home norms, because Smith's framework permits a universal baseline of human sentiment that survives local variation. Fourth, I treat sympathy not as a spontaneous emotional reflex but as a cultivated skill that improves through disciplined narrative exposure and deliberate attention to others' circumstances. Fifth, I argue that conventions of politeness and indirectness—particularly those that require subordinates or interlocutors to mask genuine sentiment behind formulas of deference—corrupt the spectator's judgments by systematically obscuring the emotional data on which sympathy relies. Sixth, and in apparent tension with the previous point, I also hold that societies with stronger norms of indirectness and social reserve actually produce more reliable spectators over the long term, because the stability and predictability of interaction allow for deeper calibration of sympathetic judgment across time. Seventh, I deny that evolutionary debunking arguments undermine sympathy's moral authority, since I treat it as a socially transmitted technology of coordination rather than as an innate moral intuition.

I am unsure which of these commitments genuinely conflict and which only appear to do so. Could you audit the set and rank the tensions by severity, specifying for each whether it is a real incompatibility or a dissolvable appearance? For the serious conflicts, please identify the weakest link and propose the least costly revision—whether that requires abandoning my universalist claim about cross-cultural adjudication, weakening the corruption thesis about politeness, qualifying the cultivation claim, or revising my denial of evolutionary debunking. I am especially uncertain whether my view of indirectness undermines my defense of cross-cultural judgment, and whether my cultivation thesis sits comfortably with my claim that sympathy requires accurate emotional data. Please treat this as an audit of my own position, not as a general exposition of Smith.
