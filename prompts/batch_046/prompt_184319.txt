Following a recent diagnosis that has forced me to reconsider what a good life requires, I have become convinced that Moritz Schlick’s account of life’s meaning—centered not on transcendent purpose or future-oriented achievement but on present, self-contained absorption in activity for its own sake—offers the most humane framework for end-of-life reflection. Next Thursday I am presenting this to a joint seminar of philosophy-of-religion faculty, existentialist scholars, and clinical ethicists from palliative care. The thesis I defend is that Schlick’s distinction between Zweck (external purpose) and Sinn (intrinsic meaning), particularly his claim that Spiel—activity pursued without instrumental justification—is the sole locus of genuine meaning, not only survives standard existentialist and theological objections but also provides a more adequate conceptual basis for what I am calling micro-meaning in terminal care than either narrative-integration models or theistic teleology.

The audience is precisely divided: two philosophers of religion who regard any non-theistic account of meaning as implicitly nihilistic and who know Schlick’s Vienna Circle context well; one existentialist who will treat Schlick’s youthful-play ideal as an evasion of death-anxiety and an unacknowledged privilege; two clinical ethicists sympathetic to patient autonomy but worried that framing dying as an opportunity for playful absorption risks romanticizing suffering and ignores structural inequities in access to palliative comfort; and several philosophy-of-medicine graduates who are agnostic but alert to ableist assumptions in idealized accounts of agency.

Prepare me for the objections I will actually face, in roughly the order they will arise, phrased as each faction would phrase them. For each objection, indicate whether I should concede, hold firm, or partially qualify; provide a concise reply appropriate to a twenty-minute Q&A; and flag the weakest point in that reply. Include the objection I am most hoping nobody raises—specifically, the one that connects Schlick’s biographical context, including the circumstances of his death, to the alleged failure of his philosophy to confront mortality seriously. Finally, identify the single reply that must land if the proposal is to survive the clinical ethicists’ skepticism, and note where even that reply remains soft.
