I am drafting a novel in which a physicist volunteers for an experimental teletransporter that scans her neural and psychological state, destroys the original body, and reconstructs her at a remote station. To close the chapter, I need a villanelle—nineteen lines, five tercets followed by a quatrain, with two refrains weaving through an ABA rhyme scheme—spoken from her point of view at the instant the reconstruction completes. One refrain should voice the psychological-continuity claim that what matters is the preservation of the pattern, not the continuity of flesh; the other should voice the bodily-continuity objection that without the same living body there is only a duplicate, not survival. The poem's structure should enact the philosophy: the refrains should circle and miss each other through the tercets, then stand adjacent in the final couplet without synthesis, echoing Parfit's separation of identity and survival. To avoid mere illustration, I want the verse itself to suffer the discontinuity it describes: maintain a regular iambic pentameter through the first two tercets, then fracture the meter at the point of transmission—break the line lengths and stress—before resuming the syllable count with a disrupted rhythm, so that the formal body of the poem is reconstructed but not identical. The speaker should remain uncertain whether she is the original or the copy, and the ambiguity should stay open at the end. Please do not cite any philosopher by name, do not include a bibliography, and do not resolve the tension with a moral about technology.
