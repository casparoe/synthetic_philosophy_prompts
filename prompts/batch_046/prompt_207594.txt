Analyze the August 1991 Soviet coup attempt as a case study in historical contingency and chaos theory—the “for want of a nail” problem applied to regime collapse. The salient facts: the State Emergency Committee detained Gorbachev and declared emergency rule; tanks entered Moscow but the plotters failed to arrest Yeltsin at the White House; Yeltsin addressed crowds from a tank and called for resistance; radio Echo of Moscow and informal fax networks broke the information blockade; and the coup dissolved within three days. Treat the crisis not as a linear causal chain but as a nonlinear dynamical system: identify the likely bifurcation point on the morning of August 19, and ask whether the coup’s failure was genuinely sensitive to initial conditions—so that a single corrected decision, such as cutting communication lines or decapitating the resistance, might have pushed the system into a stable authoritarian basin—or whether macro-structural conditions (the military’s refusal to fire, the republics’ secessionist momentum) made collapse inevitable regardless of those micro-dynamics. Then say where chaos theory genuinely illuminates the plotters’ paralysis and hesitation, where it merely redescribes familiar political failure in mathematical language, and whether the episode supports a strong contingency thesis (path-dependence on micro-decisions) or reveals that structural forces dominated the August dynamics.
