I'm a novelist working on a book set in a fictional country that's just come out of a civil war it lost, and I'm trying to understand how "lost cause" myths actually work so I don't just rewrite the American South badly. I scribbled some rough notes comparing the Confederacy, Masada, Kosovo, and Dunkirk, and I'd like your comments — especially on whether I've spotted a real pattern or just forced one onto four different stories.

Here's the gist. I keep noticing that a lost cause only becomes proper national glue if the original reason people fought gets quietly replaced after the fact. The Confederacy fought to preserve slavery and state sovereignty, but the Lost Cause turned them into noble defenders of home against an industrial invader. Masada started as a failed revolt ending in mass suicide, but later became a symbol of heroic refusal to surrender. Kosovo in 1389 was a defeat that killed the king, yet poets turned it into an eternal vow of resistance. Even Dunkirk — which had no moral stain — was rewritten as a moral victory of British pluck, though that happened while survivors were still alive to steer the narrative.

So my question for the essay is this: is there an established concept for that specific inversion, where the actual cause is laundered through time until it's unrecognisable? I know about invented traditions and political myth, but those feel too broad. I'm after the precise mechanism — the way defeat is reframed so thoroughly that the original claim disappears. Does that have a sharper name?

I also wonder if Dunkirk breaks the pattern because the survivors controlled the story immediately, whereas with the others the myth-makers were generations removed. Is that distinction useful, or am I just forcing a taxonomy?
