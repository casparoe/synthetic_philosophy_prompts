Write a short story that takes as its conceptual backbone Daniel Dennett's brain-transplant puzzle—the thought experiment in which a person's brain is surgically removed, sustained in a life-support vat or synthetic chassis, and then remotely linked to a body that may or may not still house the original neural architecture—while introducing one specific twist not found in the canonical formulation. In your version, the link between the extracted brain and its original body is not merely a one-way sensory feed or a remote-control interface, but a fully bidirectional, synchronous neural bridge: every intentional state, every proprioceptive adjustment, and every conscious experience is simultaneously instantiated in both the sustained brain and the body left behind, with perfect temporal coherence. The protagonist therefore lives as a genuinely distributed entity, unable to locate a privileged point of presence in either locus. The narrative should center on the moment when a catastrophic system failure forces an emergency partition of the bridge, leaving each fragment with only a partial, discontinuous memory trace. Rather than treating this as a simple cloning dilemma, explore the phenomenology of separation: does either fragment experience the split as death, as amputation, or as something conceptually stranger? Ground the story in the precise metaphysical tension Dennett raises—whether personal identity tracks the brain, the body, or the functional pattern—while using your distributed-bridge twist to ask whether a self can be non-locally extended and still remain one thing. Avoid generic cyberpunk imagery; keep the focus on the first-person experience of being literally in two places at once, and on the philosophical horror of discovering that one's identity was never anchored to a single point in space.
