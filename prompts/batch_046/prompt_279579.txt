I'm delivering the keynote address at the dedication of the Millbrook Memorial Plaza, a new public space in an unnamed mid-sized Midwestern city. The plaza commemorates a state-sanctioned displacement of local families that occurred roughly seventy-five years ago—three generations removed from those present today. The audience is intentionally mixed: descendants of the displaced families, descendants of the civil servants who administered the policy, members of the current city council and mayor's office, local historians, and a group of high school students who participated in designing the plaza's physical layout. The design includes a wall inscribed with the names of those displaced, and—by deliberate choice—an empty plinth where the names of the local administrators were initially proposed but ultimately left absent.

I need this speech to do genuine philosophical work rather than serve as ceremonial decoration. Please structure it around Karl Jaspers' distinction among criminal guilt, political guilt, moral guilt, and metaphysical guilt, applied specifically to whether this city—now, generations later—can meaningfully bear guilt for what occurred. The argument should determine what can and cannot be said from the podium: for example, it must distinguish expired legal liability from enduring institutional responsibility, and it must show why treating metaphysical solidarity as a collective, assignable debt would be a category mistake. Use the empty plinth as the central concrete image—the speech should explain why inscribing names there would conflate one form of guilt with another, and why the blank space is not an act of erasure but a philosophical boundary made visible.

Write the speech for delivery in about eight to ten minutes—approximately 1,200 to 1,500 words. It must be accessible to the teenagers and family members in the room without losing the historians and philosophers present. Do not include bibliographies, citations, or reading lists; the reasoning should stand entirely on its own. You may adapt or invent details about the plaza's architecture or the local history, but do not put any philosopher's words in direct quotation marks—paraphrase Jaspers' ideas in your own language.
