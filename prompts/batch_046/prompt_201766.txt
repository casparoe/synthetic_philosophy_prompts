Draft the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between Contextualists and Universalists on the legitimacy of the "man of his time" defence in moral assessments of Jefferson, Churchill, and Columbus. The Contextualists maintain that anachronistic moral judgment conflates explanation with excuse, that culpability requires genuinely available alternatives, and that assessing historical agents demands weighing context-sensitive capacities for moral knowledge; the Universalists maintain that basic moral truths about human dignity and suffering were accessible to contemporaries, that the defence systematically obscures internal dissent and individual agency within the same epoch, and that evaluating character requires applying stable moral standards regardless of historical context.

Structure the document exactly as follows. First, a numbered agreed-claims section: list propositions both camps can genuinely sign regarding the historical record (including that Jefferson held enslaved people, that Churchill endorsed imperial policies with lethal consequences for subject populations, and that Columbus's voyages initiated systems of enslavement and demographic collapse), regarding the logical structure of moral evaluation, and regarding what the defence is and is not intended to establish. Second, a disagreements table with parallel columns: present the precise formulations of each camp's position, with wording each side accepts for its own view, addressing at minimum (i) whether culpable moral ignorance negates excuse; (ii) whether contemporaneous moral dissent demonstrates that the relevant moral knowledge was genuinely available to the agent; (iii) whether evaluating a historical figure requires integrating moral failures and achievements into a single metric or allows compartmentalized assessment; and (iv) whether the defence applies uniformly across wrongs of different kinds—specifically chattel slavery, imperial administration, and wartime strategic bombing. Third, identify the cruxes—sub-questions whose resolution would move at least one side—explicitly including whether the naturalness or widespread acceptance of a moral belief carries intrinsic normative weight, and how first-person testimony of moral uncertainty from historical actors should be weighted when assessing culpability. Fourth, for each crux specify one concrete historical case, argument, or evidentiary pattern both sides agree is relevant, and record in advance what each camp predicts that case demonstrates.

Keep all formulations rigorously neutral: neither column should be rejectable by its own side as a strawman. Explicitly distinguish any purely verbal disagreements—such as whether "judge" means evaluate, blame, or rank—from substantive ones, and identify which part of the dispute, if any, you conclude is merely verbal and why. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations. End with a single line beginning "Summary:" followed by one sentence stating the exact residual disagreement where the two camps still stand irreconcilably apart after this collaboration.
