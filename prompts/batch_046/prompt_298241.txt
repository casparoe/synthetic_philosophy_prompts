Having retired from a career in experimental physics—where every observation is treated as an interaction with its own noise floor and bandwidth limitations—I have recently begun studying the philosophy of mind and have formed a qualified position on the reliability of introspection. Rather than treating introspection as a single faculty that is either globally trustworthy or systematically deceptive, I propose that it should be understood as a family of measurement channels, each with distinct calibration properties. Specifically, I maintain that introspection operates with relatively high fidelity for occurrent propositional attitudes—explicit beliefs, intentions, and judgments—while functioning as a noisy, low-bandwidth channel for affective states, motivational magnitudes, and sub-personal dynamics. The unreliability documented in much of the psychological literature, I suggest, is largely confined to the latter category and is misinterpreted when generalized to all first-person access.

What would count against this position? I want a ranked map of its exposure, from fatal to merely awkward. Which empirical findings—such as systematic confabulation about occurrent beliefs in split-brain or anosognosia cases, or predictive-processing models that treat self-modeling as uniformly reconstructive—would genuinely threaten the claim that propositional-attitude introspection is a high-fidelity channel? Which conceptual distinctions—perhaps between access consciousness and phenomenal consciousness, or between first-order registration and second-order attribution—would force me to concede that my proposed boundary is unsustainable?

Please distinguish between defeaters that defenders of this view would themselves concede as damaging and those they would dismiss as category errors. I also want an honest assessment of whether my instrumental-measurement qualification has insulated the position into unfalsifiability: if every counterexample can be accommodated by reclassifying the mental state in question as belonging to the low-fidelity category, does that render the view empirically empty? And is that a fatal flaw, or merely an awkward feature, for a hypothesis that presents itself as describing the actual architecture of self-knowledge?

Assess this without bibliographies, reading lists, or citations to specific works; I am interested in the conceptual structure and empirical plausibility of the position itself.
