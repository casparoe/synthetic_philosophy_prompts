As a novelist who has spent the past decade constructing a multivolume fiction about the slow erosion of civic memory in modern democracies, I find myself returning to a conceptual problem I cannot resolve through narrative alone, and I wish to see it dramatized with philosophical precision. The problem concerns what I have come to call the narrative debt of representation: the implicit theory of the polity encoded in any method of selecting rulers—whether the state is best understood as a continuous story requiring experienced narrators, or as an episodic anthology that benefits from constant recasting. I would like a dialogue between two interlocutors who take this framing seriously but draw opposite conclusions. The first should defend career politicians within a system of long tenure, arguing that sortition combined with strict term limits produces a form of dramaturgical discontinuity—a perpetual resetting of the plot that prevents complex policy narratives from reaching resolution, and that institutional memory is itself a kind of narrative capital that cannot be replaced by procedural fairness alone. The second should defend democratic lottery paired with firm term limits, contending that career legislators accumulate performative authority—what she might term casting familiarity—whereby theatrical continuity is mistaken for genuine mandate, and that only constant recasting through sortition can restore casting authenticity and prevent the polity from being held captive to a single, aging ensemble. Both must be equally competent; neither may be reduced to a straw figure. Their exchange must contain genuine back-and-forth: each must respond directly to the other's actual points about institutional memory, dramaturgical discontinuity, narrative capital, and casting authenticity, rather than talking past one another. I do not want a bibliography, a reading list, or citations to named scholars—my interest lies entirely in the conceptual architecture. Please write this in a polished, formal register appropriate to an essay by a novelist venturing into political theory, and inject whatever niche conceptual distinctions are necessary to keep the argument from sounding like a well-worn rehearsal.
