I have been recording the verdicts of a literary critic who applies Leo Tolstoy's aesthetic framework — particularly as developed in his essay What Is Art? — without altering its terms. Her rulings on whether a given work counts as genuine art or as counterfeit are these:

- A village hymn sung by untrained parishioners in a small church: genuine art.
- Beethoven's Ninth Symphony performed by a professional orchestra in a ticketed concert hall: counterfeit.
- An anonymous religious manuscript illuminated by monastery workers without individual attribution: genuine art.
- A well-known playwright's tragedy staged in a national theatre by trained actors: counterfeit.
- A folk song about harvest and loss, sung collectively in the fields by the people who composed it: genuine art.

The new case is this: an anonymous collective of displaced persons has built a continuous low-frequency sound installation using salvaged metal and solar generators, placed in temporary public shelters. It is designed to foster shared emotional release and solidarity across language barriers; it requires no musical literacy, carries no religious symbols or narrative, and has no named author, composer, or performer.

Predict her verdict. Identify at least two plausible rules that fit her earlier judgments, show how they diverge on this installation, name the closest analogue in her record, and give your best-guess prediction with reasons. Be explicit about whether her precedents genuinely underdetermine the case; if so, state the principle she would be adopting with each possible verdict. Finally, propose one further hypothetical case that would best distinguish the rules you have identified.

In answering, separate what is settled about Tolstoy's own framework from interpretations that remain genuinely contested among scholars of his aesthetics, and do not present a disputed reading as the consensus. Please respond in British English.
