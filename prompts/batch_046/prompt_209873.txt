I am preparing the interview outline for a podcast episode with Dr. Elena Vasquez, a moral philosopher who has recently advanced what she terms an “ecological” defense of virtue ethics. Vasquez accepts the central empirical findings of the situationist critique—specifically, that broad cross-situational consistency for traits such as honesty or compassion is weak in ordinary subjects—but argues that this undermines only a “psychological essentialist” interpretation of virtue, not virtue ethics itself. On her account, virtues are not global, context-independent dispositions but “ecologically indexed regulatory capacities”: they manifest with high reliability within specific moral ecologies—structured configurations of social roles, institutional expectations, and relational norms—while remaining genuinely variable or dormant outside those configurations. She maintains that this reading is closer to Aristotelian phronesis, which was always embedded in particular practices and political contexts, than to the modern personality-psychology framework that situationists like Doris and Harman attack.

Please draft eight to ten interview questions that avoid asking her merely to restate the view, and instead press on its internal architecture and its most vulnerable joints. At least one question should ask whether her framework collapses into a form of moral particularism or role-based relativism, with a follow-up keyed to her likely reply that phronesis supplies the unifying rational structure across ecologies. At least one should probe how her account treats moral ecologies that are themselves corrupt or unjust—such as an institution that systematically rewards loyalty over honesty—asking whether virtue can be indexed to a defective ecology without forfeiting normative force, and include a follow-up anticipating her appeal to some meta-ecological or second-order criterion. Another should examine empirical distinguishability: ask what behavioral or observational evidence could separate a genuinely ecologically virtuous agent from someone who merely conforms to local norms through social pressure or strategic adaptation, with a follow-up that presses her if the distinction ultimately turns on first-personal phenomenology rather than third-person observation. Finally, include a question about whether her view saves virtue ethics from situationism only by changing the subject—transforming “virtue” into something closer to professional competence or social role-performance—and a follow-up asking whether, if that is so, she retains enough of the traditional moral weight and universal aspirational force of virtue to justify preserving the term at all.
