I'm drafting materials for an advanced seminar on speech ethics, and I want to move past the usual stalemate over whether lying is intrinsically worse than misleading. Please draft the joint statement that two opposing camps could both sign after a rigorous adversarial collaboration. Call the first camp the Assertoric Fidelity side: they hold that lying carries a distinctive moral wrongness—specifically, a direct breach of propositional fidelity—that is not present in mere misleading, even when both acts are equally intentional and harmful. Call the second camp the Uniform Deception side: they hold that lying and misleading are morally equivalent expressions of intentional deception, and that any intuitive difference tracks only conventional expectations about grammatical form, not a deeper normative distinction.

Structure the document as follows. First, a numbered section of claims both sides can accept: cover what counts as an assertion versus an implicature, the possibility of misleading without uttering a falsehood, the role of speaker intention in deception, and shared judgments about canonical cases (for example, a speaker who says "I have never been to Rome" after only connecting through its airport, versus a speaker who selectively withholds exculpatory documents without uttering anything false). Second, present a disagreements table in which each remaining divergence is formulated precisely and neutrally, with each camp's own formulation phrased so that its members would accept it as accurate; I want the table to separate disagreements about the listener's epistemic entitlement from disagreements about whether the speaker's control over belief-formation is what matters morally. Third, identify the cruxes—those sub-questions such that settling them would genuinely move at least one side. I take two likely cruxes to be whether deception requires what we might call an indexical transparency condition—that is, whether a lie requires the speaker to present themselves as the direct warrant for the false proposition, whereas misleading permits the listener to construct the false belief through independent inference—and whether moral evaluation should track the path of belief-formation rather than the surface truth-value of the uttered sentence. For each crux, provide a concrete case or argument both sides agree bears on it, together with each side's explicit prediction. Use, for instance, the encrypted-testimony case: a speaker provides a listener with a technically true message and a decoding key arranged so that the listener will derive a false belief, but the speaker never explicitly endorses the decoded content; both sides should agree this case is relevant, and each should state in advance whether it counts as lying, as misleading, or as neither, and why. If any portion of the dispute turns out to be verbal—say, over whether "lying" should be defined by psychological state, by grammatical surface form, or by the listener's justified expectation of direct warrant—identify exactly which portion and explain why it is verbal rather than substantive. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; the goal is a precise conceptual map that can be tested against shared case intuitions without reliance on external authorities.
