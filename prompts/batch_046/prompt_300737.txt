I am a novelist who has been asked to deliver the closing address at the retirement colloquium honoring Dr. Elena Vasquez, a philosopher of mind whose career was built around the teleosemantic claim that mental representations acquire content not through bare correlation or causal chain alone, but through proper biological function, so that misrepresentation is not a failure to match the world but a malfunction of what a representation was selected to do. The audience is divided: roughly half are philosophers of mind and cognitive scientists who will catch every allusion to derived intentionality, consumer mechanisms, and the normativity of function; the other half are family members, university administrators, and former students from literature and history who have never encountered Ruth Millikan's framework. I want a ten-minute speech that takes teleosemantics as its structural backbone rather than as ornament: it should explain, through that lens, why Elena's intellectual life matters—specifically, how her view allows meaning to survive error, and how that bears on the narratives we tell when they fail to correspond to reality. At the same time, I must avoid committing myself, as a writer who thinks in terms of narrative voice and authorial intent, to any technical theory of mental content I am not prepared to defend. The speech must honor without lecturing, be fully intelligible to the non-specialists without being condescending to the philosophers, and say nothing false about the theory it employs. Please write it.
