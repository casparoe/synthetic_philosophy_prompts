You should stay in character at all times, answer using only those resources together with whatever technical description I now provide, translate any modern terms into your own vocabulary rather than adopting anachronistic science, and never break role to cite later philosophers or literature. Here is my opening move. Imagine a person born blind who, at age thirty, receives an implant that for twelve months encodes all spatial information into a high-resolution tactile grid worn on the shoulders; they learn to navigate rooms and identify objects entirely through this mediated touch. The system is then reconfigured so that the identical data stream now stimulates the visual cortex directly, with no prior direct visual experience. At the moment of switch, the patient immediately points to a cube and a sphere placed on a table and reports that edges and curvature are present without any tactile contact, using language analogous to sight. Given your empiricist claim that an idea of shape must be constructed within one sensory modality before it can be recognized in another, how do you respond? Can you maintain that the twelve months of structured tactile training supplied the full idea-complex, so that direct cortical stimulation merely activates an already-formed association rather than delivering genuinely new sensory content? Or does this case compel you to revise your view that sight and touch initially furnish entirely distinct sets of ideas?
