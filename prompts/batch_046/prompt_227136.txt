Write an essay on whether luck egalitarianism can be coherently extended from the distribution of material goods to the distribution of epistemic authority. Begin by laying out the standard brute-luck versus option-luck distinction as it applies to income, health, and social position. Then introduce the proposed extension: if an agent’s false or harmful beliefs are traceable to brute epistemic luck—being raised in an environment with no access to counter-evidence, suffering from cognitive impairments outside her control, or being systematically deceived—then justice might require that institutions treat her testimony with a form of compensatory generosity, rather than discounting it as severely as they would discount the testimony of someone who, with abundant resources and full access to evidence, deliberately embraced the same falsehood for personal gain. Conversely, if belief-formation is largely within an agent’s control and she negligently fails to inquire, then reducing her epistemic weight in collective deliberation might be justified in the same way that reducing her share of resources is justified under option-luck conditions.

Weigh the main objections carefully. Some will argue that epistemic authority is not a distributable good like income or welfare, and that applying distributive logic to credibility confuses moral entitlement with epistemic reliability. Others will object that distinguishing brute from option luck in the domain of belief is practically impossible, since belief-formation is always overdetermined by social, psychological, and voluntary factors. There is also the concern that adjusting credibility on the basis of luck violates equal respect: treating people as more or less credible based on circumstances they did not choose might undermine the democratic norm that every citizen’s voice carries equal weight, regardless of background.

Reach and defend a clear conclusion. Either argue that a modified luck-egalitarian framework can legitimately govern how institutions—such as juries, peer review boards, or democratic assemblies—assign epistemic weight, provided that the brute/option distinction is applied cautiously and only at the institutional level rather than in interpersonal interaction; or argue that the extension collapses because belief-formation lacks the conditions—voluntariness, measurability, and separability from identity—required for distributive justice, and that epistemic authority should instead be governed by purely evidential or procedural norms. Your conclusion should follow directly from the objections you find decisive, and you should explain what practical difference your verdict makes for how a just society should treat testimony arising from disadvantaged cognitive environments.
