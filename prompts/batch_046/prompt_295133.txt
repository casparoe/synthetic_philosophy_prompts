I'm preparing a feature for a science-ideas magazine on what I call "inverted reasoning"—the kind of argument Darwin makes when he shows that natural selection produces exquisitely adapted structures without any designer who understands biology, and the kind Turing makes when he proposes that we can judge intelligence entirely from external behavior without ever asking what is going on inside. Over coffee last week, a colleague in organizational psychology joked that modern HR analytics has accidentally operationalized both inversions at once: we score people on outputs and survival metrics while treating their inner lives as epistemically off-limits. That struck me as a perfect target for satire.

Please write me a deadpan parody—around 600 to 700 words, in the form of a fictional institutional press release and brief methodological appendix from a consultancy named "The Darwin-Turing Institute for Behavioral Optimization"—announcing the rollout of "Blind Selection Protocol 7" (BSP-7). The framework should apply evolutionary selection and Turing-style behavioral benchmarking to workplace evaluation, explicitly forbidding any reference to employee comprehension, intentionality, or consciousness on the grounds that these are "untestable metaphysical variables." The voice should be that of a serious technology-journal announcement, not a cartoon; the humor must arise from pushing the actual logic of competence-without-comprehension to its bureaucratic extreme—performance reviews as phenotypic selection, promotion as fitness maximization, termination as environmental pressure, and the evaluation algorithm itself caught in a "Recursive Competence Loop" where it validates itself solely through predictive accuracy, with no external ground in understanding. The piece should remain readable to a non-philosopher, but a specialist should recognize that the satire is fair: it exaggerates a real feature of both Darwin's and Turing's arguments rather than inventing a straw man.

Keep the internal consistency strict—no speaker in the piece should slip into compatibilism or admit that knowing what employees think might matter—so that the reader feels the genuine tension of whether a system that never asks "why" can still be said to manage people justly. Do not include citations, reading lists, or quotations from actual texts; write entirely from conceptual knowledge, and avoid referencing any specific page or edition so that the prompt remains meaningful without web access.
