I'm working on a feature about moral philosophy for my magazine — we're doing a series on big ideas people misunderstand — and I sat in on a philosophy dept seminar where the speaker summarized the seperateness-of-persons objection against utilitarianism. His version went something like: Rawls argued that utilitarianism violates this principle because it treats individuals like fungible inputs in a social welfare function, adding up preferences or pleasures across different lives as if they belonged to one super-person. So if you could save five people by sacrificing one, the math works out and utilitarianism says go ahead, which is why Rawls said this proves utilitarianism collapses the distinction between persons. He then said modern utilitarians tried to patch this with prioritarianism — weighting the well-being of the worst-off more heavily — but that doesn't actually fix the core problem because you're still aggregating across seperate lives, just with unequal weights.

I'm trying to write this up for readers who aren't philosophers, but I want to be careful. Is this summary basically fair? Specifically, does prioritarianism actually address the seperateness objection, or is it solving a different problem altogether? And is it accurate to say Rawls thought utilitarianism treats people like inputs in a social welfare function — that sounds more like an economist's framing than Rawls's own language, honestly. I don't want to misrepresent either side, especially since my editor thinks the torture-one-save-five framing is overused and might be a strawman itself. Can you help me untangle what's accurate, what's a common simplification, and what a defender of utilitarianism would say is being missed?
