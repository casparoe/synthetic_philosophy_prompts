My father was recently diagnosed with a progressive neurodegenerative disorder, and the remote possibility of synthetic neural replacement has become personally urgent. In thinking through whether such a procedure could preserve him, I have settled on these commitments:

(1) Personal identity is sustained not by narrative coherence or bare substrate, but by psychological continuity specifically in a first-person anticipatory structure—the particular way an agent projects itself into future states with concern.

(2) Gradual replacement of biological neurons with functionally equivalent synthetic substrates can preserve this structure, provided the transition is continuous and each intermediate stage is causally integrated with the next.

(3) Yet if the synthetic architecture introduces imperceptible micro-divergences in preference weightings—what I call value drift—so that the endpoint ranks goods in ways the original agent would reject, then continuity is broken, regardless of explicit memory preservation.

(4) Moral obligations to a future self are scalar, tracking the degree of psychological continuity rather than being all-or-nothing.

(5) There is no further fact of identity over and above these relations; no hidden essence must survive.

Do these hang together? In particular, (2) seems to license a gradual path through imperceptible drift at each step, yielding an endpoint continuous with every intermediate stage but radically different from the origin—seemingly violating (3). Meanwhile (4) implies the endpoint might still warrant partial moral concern, which sits uneasily with (3)’s claim that identity is broken. Are these genuine tensions or do they dissolve once stage-to-stage continuity is distinguished from origin-to-endpoint comparison? Rank the conflicts by severity, identify the least costly single revision—perhaps weakening (3), or abandoning the scalar moral view in (4)—and say whether one change cleans up the most. Include any tensions that only appear on the surface.
