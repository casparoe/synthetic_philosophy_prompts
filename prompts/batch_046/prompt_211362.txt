I was observing a straight rod partially submerged in water, and due to refraction it genuinely looked bent to me—though I knew it was straight. I told a friend that in this case I wasn't really seeing the rod, but rather seeing something bent that didn't exist in the pool, which made me doubt whether "seeing the rod" describes both situations. She replied that this commits me to implausible objects in my visual field; instead, I'm seeing the same rod in both cases, and in the submerged case my visual system merely misrepresents one of its properties, analogous to misjudging color under tinted light. She also noted that neuroimaging reveals nearly identical activation patterns for submerged and unsubmerged viewing, so treating the two experiences as belonging to different fundamental kinds seems biologically unmotivated. I remain unsure whether her neuroscience point answers the philosophical worry, or whether there's a coherent view on which illusion involves perceiving something genuinely different rather than perceiving one thing wrongly. What exactly is at stake between these two descriptions, and is my intuition—that I see something other than the rod when it looks bent—philosophically defensible?
