I am working on a paper for my biology and ethics class about what heritability actually means, and I think I have talked myself into a confusion that I cannot resolve on my own. I understand that a heritability coefficient of, say, 0.75 for a cognitive trait in a particular population only describes how much of the variance in that specific environment is associated with genetic differences, and that it does not apply to individuals or to other populations with different environments. I also came across the concept of reaction norms, which I understand as the idea that each genotype produces a specific curve of phenotypic outcomes across a range of environments, so that plasticity itself can vary genetically. From these two premises, I built the following argument, and I want to know exactly where it breaks down. If heritability is 0.75, then 75 percent of the variance is genetic and 25 percent is environmental. But if the shape and slope of every reaction norm are themselves genetically determined, then the 25 percent environmental portion is not really an independent source of variance; it is just the expression of genetic differences in how people respond to environment. In other words, some genotypes have steep norms and others have flat norms, so changing the environment does not affect everyone uniformly but instead amplifies or suppresses differences according to genetically specified rules of plasticity. This seems to imply that heritability estimates actually underestimate total genetic influence, because they treat environmentally induced variance as non-genetic even when the capacity to be affected by environment is inherited. Following this logic, I concluded that large-scale educational interventions could never substantially reduce achievement gaps, since any change in environment would be filtered through genetically distinct reaction norms and therefore preserve or even widen rank-order differences. I realize this sounds like genetic determinism, which I have been told heritability does not entail, but my reasoning does not rely on genes fixing a single outcome; it relies on genes fixing the rules of environmental responsiveness. Could you explain, in continuous prose without headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, where this line of thinking goes wrong? Specifically, I want to know whether I am conflating variance in plasticity with variance in the trait itself, whether reaction norms are indeed always genotype-specific in the way I assumed, and whether there are conditions under which a uniform environmental improvement could reduce phenotypic variance despite differing reaction norms. Please treat my argument charitably but show me precisely which step is mistaken.
