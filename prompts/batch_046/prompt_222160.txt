As a novelist constructing a philosophical interlude for a literary novel about an ethics council in a near-future polity, I require a dialogue—approximately five hundred words—between two equally formidable interlocutors, neither a strawman. Dr. Elara Voss defends indirect normativity through idealized deliberation; Dr. Marcus Chen contends that such idealization necessarily presupposes direct normative commitments it claims to derive. Their debate should center on an invented but concrete scenario: a governing intelligence must choose between direct, adaptive welfare-maximizing algorithms and rigid indirect constitutional rules that forbid real-time adaptation even when local harm is evident. Voss should argue that the rules derive authority from endorsement by an ideally rational agent—one stripped of motivational drift, cognitive bias, and informational asymmetry—operating behind a procedural veil of ignorance about her particular position. Chen should reply not by misrepresenting her but by pressing that the very specification of this ideal agent—what counts as drift, what counts as bias, which values autonomy and predictability—already embeds substantive moral premises, so the deliberation generates nothing that was not smuggled in. They must respond to one another’s actual moves: when Voss claims stability is the point, Chen should ask whether stability remains a virtue under radical contingency; when Chen accuses her of concealed deontology, she should concede the structural resemblance but defend it as a necessary framework rather than deny it. The dialogue should be rendered in polished American English, in the voice of literary fiction—spoken lines with minimal attribution, no stage directions—so that a reader unfamiliar with the technical literature grasps the human stakes, while a specialist recognizes a genuine philosophical tension. Do not include bibliographies, citations, or appeals to named authorities beyond incidental first-name address within the speech itself.
