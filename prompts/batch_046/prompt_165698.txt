In popular expositions of the occasionalist position associated with Al-Ghazālī, the relation between God and nature is often likened to a video game rendered frame by frame: the game engine and its internal rules appear to generate events, yet in reality the programmer writes and displays each frame independently, so that no in-game object truly causes another. The analogy is usually invoked to make occasionalism intuitive—if a virtual blade does not genuinely cut a virtual branch, then fire does not genuinely burn cotton—and thereby to license the conclusion that secondary causes are metaphysically inert and only God acts. Audit this analogy systematically. First, provide an explicit correspondence table mapping programmer, rendering engine, individual frames, player inputs, hard-coded physics rules, and in-game bugs onto God, natural causes, discrete temporal events, human actions, laws of nature, and apparent causal anomalies. Second, identify precisely where the mapping fractures: does the analogy smuggle in a deterministic source code that occasionalism need not accept? Does player agency correspond to human free will, to a mere illusion of choice, or to something the analogy cannot accommodate? Does the discrete frame-rate impose a temporal atomism that the view does not require? Third, distinguish the disanalogies that genuinely undermine the intuitive pull of the comparison from those that are merely ornamental. Fourth, determine whether the conclusion the analogy was meant to support—that secondary causes lack all causal efficacy and God alone is the true agent—survives once these fractures are visible, and if it does not, propose a better analogy that preserves occasionalism’s core claim without importing the misleading structural features of digital simulation.
