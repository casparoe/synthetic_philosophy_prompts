I serve on the acquisitions committee for a small municipal museum that receives donated historical artifacts. Every quarter we must select exactly one object to place in our rotating central display case, and we do this by comparing donation proposals pairwise across four dimensions: authenticity of provenance, emotional resonance for a typical visitor, physical durability over a ten-year loan, and representativeness of local social history. Recently I have noticed that when advantages are slight and spread across different dimensions, our pairwise rankings loop: Artifact A barely edges out B on durability, B barely edges out C on resonance, C barely edges out D on provenance, and eventually the chain loops back so that the final comparison favors A again on conservation cost. I have been wondering on my own whether "better suited for the central display" is genuinely transitive when differences are this small and incommensurable, especially given the philosophical puzzles about tiny differences accumulating across a long chain—what I have heard called spectrum-style reasoning.

Because our board meets only once per quarter and we have no budget for outside consultants or formal decision-analysis tools, I need a reusable decision procedure that my committee—four volunteers with no specialized training—can actually run in about ninety minutes using only the proposal files, a whiteboard, and a printed worksheet. The procedure should specify the exact sequence of questions to ask when comparing any two proposals, what counts as a sufficient answer to move to the next step, where the procedure issues a hard recommendation of "select," "reject," or "defer," and where it must stop and hand the decision to the committee as an unavoidable judgment call about values rather than facts. It must be explicit about which distinctions survive simplification—such as whether authenticity can ever be traded off against durability—and which are dropped for usability. It should also flag the specific conditions under which the loop I described cannot be broken by further factual inquiry.

Please apply this procedure to three actual donation proposals we are considering this quarter: a well-preserved factory time-clock from the 1930s with clear provenance but weak emotional narrative and moderate durability; a handwritten letter from an unknown resident describing an ordinary Tuesday in 1952, which is fragile and hard to display but deeply resonant and locally representative; and a professionally restored replica of a lost town welcome sign that scores very high on durability, visual impact, and representativeness, but is not an original object and has no direct provenance. Include one case where the procedure delivers a ranking or deferral that I will find genuinely unsatisfactory—likely either selecting the replica despite its inauthenticity, or leaving us with no decision after exhausting all steps—and explain precisely why the procedure produces that unwelcome result given its own rules. Finally, state clearly what the procedure cannot do: for instance, whether it can settle disputes about which of the four dimensions ought to dominate, whether it assumes that "better" operates on a single scale rather than a cluster of irreducible values, and whether it can prevent the circular ranking problem altogether or only manage it once it appears.
