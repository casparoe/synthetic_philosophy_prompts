I am a doctoral student in philosophy of mind and the history of cognitive science, drafting an essay tentatively titled “Von Neumann’s Statistical Brain: Between Computation and Self-Observation.” The standard reception of John von Neumann’s posthumous The Computer and the Brain (Yale University Press, 1958) reads it as an early defense of computationalism: the brain is a computing machine, only slower, noisier, and more parallel than the electronic devices of the 1950s. I want to argue that this reading overlooks a deeper tension in the text.

My essay advances three claims. First, von Neumann repeatedly stresses that neural computation is fundamentally statistical and analog, operating through redundant, error-correcting mechanisms rather than through the deterministic logic of digital circuits. Second, in the unfinished sections on self-reproducing automata, he implies that any system capable of modeling its own operations introduces an irreducible self-observational loop—one that cannot be captured by a purely external, input-output functional description. Third, applying this loop to the brain suggests that its representational content is partly constituted by approximate, self-correcting dynamics rather than by explicit symbol manipulation.

From this I draw a stronger conclusion: von Neumann does not actually support a strict language-of-thought computationalism, but instead anticipates something closer to an enactive or predictive-processing view, in which cognition emerges from statistical, embodied dynamics rather than from rule-governed computation.

These are the notes and the line of argument. I am not asking for a bibliography or reading list; I want to know whether the interpretation holds together conceptually and textually. Please comment on the following:

1. Is my counter-reading of The Computer and the Brain defensible, or does it overread von Neumann’s remarks on self-reproducing automata?
2. Does the statistical/analog distinction genuinely undermine strict computationalism, or can it be fully accommodated within a more sophisticated computational framework?
3. Is the move from self-observation to an enactive conclusion coherent, given that von Neumann explicitly sought a formal mathematical theory of brain function?

Please do not merely list strengths and weaknesses or survey the options. Take a committed position: state clearly whether the essay’s central claim succeeds or fails, identify the single weakest link in the argument, and propose one concrete revision—either to salvage the interpretation or to abandon it for a more defensible reading. I want a definite judgment with explicit reasons, not a balanced outline of possibilities.
