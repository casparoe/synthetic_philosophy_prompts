Read Othello through the lens of the moral difference between lying and misleading — specifcally whether deceving someone with technically true statements arranged malicously is less wrong than flat-out falsehood, and whether the victim's own epistemic state changes the blame. Iago almost never asserts something literally false; he lets Othello draw the wrong conclusions from real sightings of the handkerchief and Cassio's laughter. Does Shakespeare treat Iago's method as worse because it's insidious, or as less culpable because he can claim he never lied? Look at the scene where Iago warns Othello about jealousy, framing a true warning as a weapon. Also consider Othello's own false accusations: are they lies, or is he misleading himself?
