My father was recently diagnosed with early-stage frontotemporal dementia, and in helping him revise his advance directive I have become preoccupied with a question I cannot answer for myself: under what conditions does an earlier self retain the authority to override a later one? Rather than legal guidance, I want to understand the moral logic behind my own judgments. Below are my verdicts on five concrete cases. In each, an earlier self (S1) executed a binding advance directive; a later self (S2) exists in the specified condition and expresses a contrary preference.

Case 1. S1 directs refusal of artificial nutrition if she reaches severe dementia in which she no longer recognizes family but smiles frequently and appears content. S2 is in exactly that state, communicates through simplified yes/no signals, and indicates she wants to continue tube feeding. Verdict: S2’s present wish should prevail; S1’s directive is not binding here.

Case 2. S1 directs refusal of life-extending intervention if brain injury eliminates her philosophical and religious commitments while leaving capacity for simple pleasures. After an accident, S2 lacks all prior values, enjoys music and food, and actively seeks continued care. Verdict: S1’s directive should be honored; S2’s preferences do not carry normative authority.

Case 3. S1, following a religious conversion, directs refusal of blood products under any circumstances. Years later she deconverts, abandons the faith, then suffers an accident requiring transfusion and begs for it. Verdict: Honor S2’s request; the directive’s authority was tied to the religious identity that has been repudiated.

Case 4. S1 directs refusal of treatment if she enters a persistent vegetative state. Instead she enters a minimally conscious state with a radically altered personality—no autobiographical memory of S1, new artistic capacities, and an explicit desire to live. A neuroprosthetic intervention could restore partial autobiographical memory but would erase the new personality. S2 refuses the prosthesis and asks to live as she is. Verdict: The directive’s triggering condition is unmet, and S2’s refusal of restoration should be respected.

Case 5. S1 directs that treatment be withdrawn if “my core values have been replaced by alien ones,” specifying assessment by a clinical ethicist using a standardized values inventory. Over fifteen years of gradual Alzheimer’s, divergence reaches 60 percent, yet S2 still claims continuity with her “essential self” and wants treatment continued. Verdict: I am genuinely unsettled; I lean toward honoring the directive once divergence exceeds a threshold near 50 percent, but I feel that cutoff is arbitrary.

These verdicts strain against one another—especially Case 1 and Case 2, since in both S2 is content, lacks prior values, and seeks care, yet I treat them oppositely. I suspect there is a principle, or a small family of rival principles, governing when diachronic authority survives transformative change, but I cannot articulate it without either saving every verdict at the cost of ad hoc complexity or overturning one.

Please provide:
1. The best single principle, or up to two rival candidates, that captures my verdicts, with each clause tied to the case that motivates it.
2. For each principle, state precisely which of my five verdicts it cannot accommodate, and whether saving consistency requires overturning Case 1 or Case 2.
3. A prediction for a case I have not listed: a patient who, after a transformative psychedelic experience, writes an advance directive refusing future psychiatric medication; five years later she has returned to her pre-experience personality but retains a clear, emotionally neutral memory of the experience, and now asks for medication during a relapse.
4. An honest assessment of whether a non-ad-hoc principle forces me to drop at least one verdict, and if so which repair is least damaging.

Present your answer as a JSON object with the following schema:
- principle_candidates: array of objects, each with fields name (string), core_claim (string), motivating_cases (array of strings), overturned_verdicts (array of strings), prediction_for_new_case (string).
- tension_assessment: string describing whether Case 1 and Case 2 are reconcilable without ad hoc adjustments.
- recommended_repair: string stating which verdict to overturn and what the repaired principle becomes.

Then include a table with these columns: Case, Actual_Verdict, Verdict_under_Principle_A, Verdict_under_Principle_B, Divergence_Notes. The rows should cover Case 1 through Case 5 and the New Case. After the JSON, place the table.
