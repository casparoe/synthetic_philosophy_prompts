I recently joined an interdisciplinary working group at my institution that is trying to map explanatory strategies in evolutionary cognitive science, and I find myself stuck in a disagreement that has now persisted through six meetings without any convergence. The specific case is the evolutionary origin of recursive syntax: one camp treats proposed mechanisms—such as social coordination pressures being decomposed into incremental neural-reuse steps—as exemplary crane-building, where each stage is supported by simpler structures already in place. The opposing camp argues that invoking a singular adaptive breakthrough for syntax, even if dressed up in selectionist language, functions as a skyhook: it introduces an unexplained explainer that does no incremental work and merely relocates the mystery. Strikingly, both sides accept the same comparative primate data and the same absence of direct fossil evidence for syntactic structures; they disagree not about the empirical record but about whether a given decomposition is genuinely incremental or just a rhetorical redescription of a leap.

What kind of disagreement is this, actually? Is there a hidden empirical question about the minimal granularity of evolutionary steps that we have not yet tested, a conceptual ambiguity in what distinguishes greedy reductionism from legitimate reductive explanation, or a bedrock divergence about whether cognitive-science explanations must eventually bottom out in mechanisms that look suspiciously like skyhooks? And if the impasse stems from the crane/skyhook metaphor itself breaking down in domains where the baseline—primate social cognition—is already complex, is there a formal or methodological distinction that could restructure the debate, or should we treat this as a sign that the framing is ill-posed for language origins?
