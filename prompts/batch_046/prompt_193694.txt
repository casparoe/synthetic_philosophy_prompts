In my comparative work on classical Chinese logical metaphysics and contemporary philosophy of language, I keep returning to Gongsun Long’s argument—famously summarized as the claim that a white horse is not a horse—and to a frustration that modern scholarship on it has barely moved in decades despite broad agreement on the textual basis. One camp reads the dialogue as an early exercise in extensional logic: horse and white horse have different extensions because the latter includes whiteness, so identity fails in roughly the way that the set of equines is not identical to the set of white equines. A second camp treats it as metaphysical: horseness is a pure form, and adding whiteness produces a compound that lacks the unmixed essence, so white horse denotes something that is literally not a horse in the ontologically relevant sense. A third camp, drawing on the broader Confucian context of rectifying names for social governance, argues the point is normative and institutional: basic categorical names stabilize order, and allowing perceptual compounds to override them dissolves the distinctions required for political life.

What strikes me is that these interpretations do not actually disagree about any recoverable fact in the text, yet they also do not seem to be talking past each other in a purely verbal way—each side can reconstruct the others’ positions and still find them irrelevant to what is at stake. I have begun to suspect the stall comes from a shared, unexamined assumption: all three camps treat name and actuality as mapping onto a single ontological layer, whether semantic, essential, or institutional. But perhaps Gongsun Long’s argument requires a two-layer framework, in which horse designates a normative-functional kind sustained by social practice and white horse designates a perceptual compound, with is not expressing not a failure of identity or essence but a failure of category-preservation across layers. On this reading, the dispute is not really about universals versus extensions at all, but about whether linguistic categories can survive decomposition into sensory features without losing their regulative force.

If that framing captures something real, is the long-standing disagreement substantive—a hidden disagreement about whether ontology must be layered—or is it a case of three different disciplines answering different questions under a single heading? More specifically, do the logical analysts, metaphysicians, and political hermeneuticians differ in their standards for what counts as data (the text alone, ontological intuition, or institutional context), or do they share standards and simply reach bedrock intuitions about whether language should track perception or practice? And if the impasse is indeed structural in the way I suggest, what would count as progress: a formal model distinguishing layer-preservation from identity, a historical argument showing the two-layer reading is anachronistic, or a change of question that abandons whether a white horse is a horse in favor of what work that distinction performs in the dialogue’s argumentative architecture? I am less interested in adjudicating between interpretations than in diagnosing why this debate has stalled and whether it should be expected to move at all.
