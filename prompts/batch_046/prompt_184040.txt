As a doctoral researcher working on distributive ethics and contractualist moral theory, I have come to defend a particular resolution to the aggregation problem within Scanlon's framework. On my reading, contractualism does not merely reject quantitative aggregation; it substitutes a pairwise justification procedure in which the wrongness of an act is determined by whether it could be justified to each affected individual taken separately. I argue that this procedure implicitly requires what I call a pairwise transitivity constraint: if an agent is justified in prioritising aid to person A over person B in isolation, that priority ordering must be preserved when a third party C is introduced, even if C's presence changes the total cost of intervention. This constraint explains why contractualists can sometimes save the greater number without invoking a social welfare function — the transitivity of pairwise justifications blocks the cyclical preferences that generate aggregation paradoxes — yet it also forbids reversing priorities purely because the scale of harm has increased.

What would have to be true for this position to be wrong? I am asking for an honest assessment of its exposure, not a catalogue of generic objections to contractualism. Specifically: does the pairwise transitivity constraint collapse into a disguised lexical ordering that Scanlon's account explicitly rejects? Are there multi-party cases in which the constraint generates its own intransitivities, or in which it forces us to aid the relatively better-off? Which of these defeaters would be decisive, and which merely awkward? Furthermore, what should a committed defender of this view concede as genuinely counting against it — for instance, empirical intuitions about emergency triage, or formal results about preference aggregation — and which objections could be deflected by refining the notion of excessive cost? Finally, I want to know whether this position risks being qualified into unfalsifiability: if every counterexample can be accommodated by redrawing the boundaries of the pairwise comparison, is that insulation a theoretical virtue or a damaging cost?
