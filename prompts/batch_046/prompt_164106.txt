I am a journalist covering science and ideas, currently drafting a long feature on relational ethics and the governance of artificial companionship, and over the past months of interviews with care ethicists, African philosophers, and engineers of social robotics I have arrived at a working framework that I want to stress-test before it goes into print. My commitments are these. First, I take Ubuntu seriously as an ontological claim: personhood is not an intrinsic metaphysical property but a relational achievement—what is sometimes rendered as the idea that one becomes a person through other persons—so that moral obligations are fundamentally obligations to maintain, repair, and recognize interdependent webs rather than to protect atomistic autonomy. Second, I think this relational view accommodates marginal human cases—severely disabled infants, patients in permanent vegetative states—without contradiction, because kinship and care can extend asymmetrically; the web remains constitutive even when reciprocity is absent or one-sided. Third, by parallel reasoning, I am inclined to allow that an artificial system embedded in sustained reciprocal care practices—say, a long-term synthetic companion for an isolated elder—might acquire a derivative and revocable form of relational standing, though never full unconditional personhood. Fourth, I nevertheless deny that collective entities such as corporations or nation-states can be Ubuntu-persons, on the grounds that they lack embodied vulnerability and are instrumental aggregations rather than nodes in a web of mutual becoming. Fifth, I hold that justice requires institutional mechanisms—constitutional courts, individual rights-claim procedures—that allow a single person to press a claim against the collective, even though this appears to import the very liberal, atomistic subject that relational ontology should reject. Sixth, and this is the framing I have begun to use to stabilize the previous points, I now think full personhood requires what I call ancestral continuity: being situated in a generational chain of biological and cultural transmission; this explains why marginal humans retain unconditional status despite asymmetry, while synthetic companions, however reciprocal, do not.

Before I finalize the article, I need an audit of whether this package is jointly tenable. Which of these commitments genuinely pull against one another—particularly between the derivation of synthetic standing in (3), the denial of collective personhood in (4), and the ancestral-continuity condition in (6)—and which tensions are only apparent, such as the one between relational ontology in (1) and institutional rights in (5)? Please rank the conflicts by severity, indicate for each what would have to give and at what cost, and identify the least expensive revision that restores consistency without collapsing the framework into either pure liberal individualism or a majoritarian collectivism. I am not asking for a bibliography or a survey of the literature; I want to know whether my own position, as it stands, commits me to verdicts I have not yet accepted.
