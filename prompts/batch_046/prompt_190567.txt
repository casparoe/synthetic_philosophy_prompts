I'm preparing a 90-minute seminar for advanced undergraduates who have completed introductory courses in ethics and political philosophy but have not studied comparative intellectual history. The session asks whether "Western values" is a coherent category, what it includes, and how the phrase operates in argument. I need a teaching plan rather than the explanation itself. Specify the order of introduction: what distinction—such as genealogical inheritance versus functional convergence—must be established before the coherence question can even be posed, and what dependency governs the move from descriptive clustering to normative universalism. Choose one carrying example or concrete case that should anchor the entire session; it should be specific enough to show how identical practices get framed as either particularist or universalist, and it should lack the abstraction of a simple list. Map the misconceptions the students will likely bring—perhaps that "Western" is purely geographic, that critics reject the values rather than the framing, or that coherence means only internal consistency—and identify the precise point in the sequence where each should be defused. Design one closing check that reveals whether students can distinguish using the phrase descriptively from deploying it as an argumentative move, and state what you will openly refuse to cover—such as citation-heavy national constitutional histories or detailed genealogies of individual thinkers—and why that omission is necessary for the conceptual goal at this level.
