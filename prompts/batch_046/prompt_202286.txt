I have just come away from a long and somewhat unresolved argument with a close friend—an historian who specializes in twentieth-century labor movements—about whether generational categories such as Boomers, Millennials, and Gen Z denote anything ontologically substantial, or whether they are merely reified marketing constructs that obscure more than they reveal. Our disagreement has immediate practical stakes: in two days I must deliver the opening remarks at an interdisciplinary university symposium titled "Cohort, Culture, and Institutional Memory," where the audience will include quantitative sociologists who treat generations as measurable cohorts shaped by shared period effects, critical historians who reject generational essentialism as a form of false universalism, and a large contingent of students for whom generational identity functions as a primary moral and political vocabulary. I need the address to navigate a precise philosophical tension. It must acknowledge that tracking people by shared historical location—exposure to specific technological regimes, economic shocks, or political events—yields genuine predictive and explanatory power in social science, without thereby committing to the stronger claim that generations are natural kinds possessing essential properties or collective agency. Conversely, it must avoid dismissing generational experience as pure fiction, since that move would alienate the students present and ignore the institutional reality that cohort membership actually structures access to resources, language, and political expectation. Could you write a five-minute opening address that uses this ontological ambiguity as its organizing principle? The speech should frame the symposium's purpose—understanding how shared historical location shapes belief and practice—while explicitly refusing both the essentialist and the eliminativist positions, and it should speak comfortably to experts without ever becoming a lecture that loses the non-specialists in the room.
