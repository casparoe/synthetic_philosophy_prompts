Please act as a professional philosopher of politics with expertise in democratic theory, and approach this methodically: begin with a brief brainstorm of the central conceptual tensions, then examine the chain of reasoning step by step, identifying the weakest link and pricing the alternatives carefully. I am not an academic — I have simply been trying to work out, in my own head, who genuinely belongs to the demos — so I would value a patient, clear explanation in British English.

Here is the argument I have constructed, and its conclusion feels intolerable to me. (1) If democracy is rule by the people, then the people ought to include everyone genuinely subject to collective decisions — call this the all-subjected principle. (2) Subjection is not confined to citizens: visitors live under the law, future generations inherit its long-term consequences, and even non-human entities are shaped by present environmental regulations. (3) Applying (1) consistently therefore pushes the boundary of the demos outward without any obvious stopping point, threatening an open-ended or even infinitely expandable membership. (4) To halt this expansion, one might restrict membership to those with ‘standing’ — the actual capacity to take part in decision-making. (5) Yet that capacity depends on education, language, and socialisation, all of which are products of prior collective decisions to which those very individuals were not yet party. (6) Hence standing itself derives from prior subjection, which means defining the demos by standing tacitly presupposes an earlier demos that conferred it. (7) Breaking this circle requires an initial, arbitrary exclusion — a founding group that fixes the boundary before anyone can legitimately claim entry. (8) But if the boundary is ultimately arbitrary, democratic equality (treating like cases alike) is violated at the foundation, and democratic legitimacy collapses altogether. (9) Therefore either there is no legitimate demos, or one of these premises must be abandoned. I would rather keep (1) and (8), since they capture my core democratic intuitions, but I need to know precisely which step to reject and what conceptual price I pay for giving it up. Where does the reasoning slip, and what does repairing it cost?
