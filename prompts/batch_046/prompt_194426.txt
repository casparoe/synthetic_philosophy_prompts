The gender-equality paradox shows that when structural barriers fall, gender gaps in career choice actually widen. In Nordic countries with strong egalitarian policies, segregation is often sharper than in places with weaker protections, with more men in engineering and more women in nursing and care work. This suggests that equality doesn't just reveal pre-existing preferences — it actively produces gendered outcomes by freeing people to follow thier innate tendencies.

The best explanation comes from Catherine Hakim's preference theory, which argues that in low-constraint societies career choice becomes a form of preference externalization: once economic pressure and legal restriction are lifted, people pick jobs that communicate who they are. So the segregation we see isn't leftover discrimination; it's authentic preference made visible through identity signaling.

If these preferences emerge under conditions of real freedom, they carry special moral weight. Justice should therefore leave the distribution alone rather than forcing parity, since engineering equality of outcome would override genuinely autonomous choices. The paradox thus supports a hands-off approach to career distribution, because interfering with freely formed preferences violates autonomy.
