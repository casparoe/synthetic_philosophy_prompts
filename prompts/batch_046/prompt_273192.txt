I'm a backend engineer with no formal philosophy training, and I'm stuck on a design choice that feels conceptually murky rather than technically difficult. Our team built an adaptive task-routing system for freelance contractors. Over several weeks, the engine observes each contractor's completion patterns, then begins presenting a pre-filtered queue of "recommended next tasks" immediately at login. We recently added a toggle that reveals the full, unfiltered assignment marketplace, but analytics show it is used in fewer than two percent of sessions. The contractors still technically choose from the complete pool, yet in practice they accept the pre-filtered recommendations with almost no deviation.

My product lead argues that this design clearly respects autonomy: the barrier to viewing all options has been removed, every acceptance requires an explicit click, and the contractors retain full veto power. I think a different kind of loss is occurring. When the system has spent weeks not only hiding options but actively calibrating the very criteria a contractor uses to judge an option worthy of their time—speed, difficulty, pay structure—then the toggle becomes a formality. The contractors haven't lost access; they've lost the deliberative framework that would make access meaningful.

I'm asking whether there's a precise conceptual distinction between these two conditions—having options technically available versus having the internal architecture for evaluating options remain intact and self-directed. More specifically, if a person's preference-formation process has been gradually shaped by an external system they do not fully understand or control, does retaining a veto mechanism restore what actually matters, or is the relevant damage located in the calibration of preferences itself?

Please take a definite, committed position. Either defend the product lead's view that retained veto power and full visibility are sufficient for the kind of self-direction that should concern us, or defend my intuition that preference-formation integrity is an independent, necessary condition and that our current architecture violates it. If you believe both sides capture part of the truth, specify exactly which design feature addresses which concern and identify the precise gap that remains. Apply one coherent framework concretely to this pipeline—do not survey competing philosophical views in the abstract—and derive a specific recommendation: approve the feature as-is, reject the pre-filtering mechanism entirely, or modify it in an exact way (for instance, by introducing periodic counterfactual exposure to unfiltered queues, or by rendering the calibration algorithm transparent and contestable to the user).
