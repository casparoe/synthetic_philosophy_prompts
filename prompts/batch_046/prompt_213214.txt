A philosophy blog I read recently offered the following summary of Buddhist ethics in relation to consequentialist reasoning. The author argues that because the First and Second Noble Truths frame suffering (dukkha) and its origin in craving and aversion as the central problem of practice, Buddhism is fundamentally oriented toward reducing suffering across all sentient beings, which appears consequentialist. The author then adds three qualifications. First, Buddhist ethics is not aggregative: because karma operates on individual mental continuums and there is no collective welfare function, each agent is responsible only for the trajectory of their own consciousness, not for maximizing a summed utility across beings. Second, intention (cetanā) operates not merely as one factor in moral evaluation but as a hard constraint—an action that produces excellent outcomes but arises from greed, hatred, or delusion generates negative karma regardless of its results, so good ends never justify corrupt motives. Third, in Mahāyāna traditions the doctrine of upāya (skillful means) transforms the five precepts from absolute prohibitions into defeasible heuristics: a bodhisattva may violate a precept—for instance, deceiving a murderer about a victim’s location—provided the act is motivated purely by compassion and the long-term reduction of mental defilements (kleśas) in all affected parties outweighs the short-term harm of breaking the rule. From these premises the author concludes that Buddhist ethics is best understood as an agent-centered, non-aggregative form of long-run consequentialism, governed by deontic side-constraints that can be overridden by a compassion-based calculus of defilement-reduction.

I suspect this captures something real about the tradition, but I also worry that it subtly imports Western moral categories—perhaps by treating upāya as a general license for outcome-optimizing rule-breaking, or by framing cetanā as a deontic side-constraint rather than as the constitutive core of action itself. Could you audit the summary for accuracy? Specifically: which of these three qualifications are broadly accepted in scholarly treatments of Buddhist ethics, which conflate Buddhist concepts (karma, cetanā, upāya, kleśa) with modern consequentialist or deontological machinery, and does the author’s conclusion—that Buddhist ethics is a hybrid agent-centered consequentialism with overridable constraints—actually survive once the subtle misrepresentations are corrected? I am not looking for a bibliography, but for a conceptual diagnosis I can use to evaluate the blog post’s reliability.
