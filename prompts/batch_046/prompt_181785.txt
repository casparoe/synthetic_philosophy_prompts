I am a PhD student in philosophy of medicine, and I am trying to articulate my own reflective equilibrium on when a human being is dead, particularly in the space between whole-brain death and circulatory death. I want to test whether my intuitive verdicts across concrete cases hang together, or whether they force me to abandon one. Here are my judgments.

Case 1. A patient meets all standard clinical criteria for whole-brain death, including absence of brainstem reflexes and apnea, but remains on mechanical ventilation with a beating heart. Verdict: dead.

Case 2. A patient suffers irreversible cardiac arrest; after five minutes of documented asystole with no autoresuscitation, resuscitation is not attempted and circulatory death is declared. Verdict: dead.

Case 3. A patient with classic locked-in syndrome: full consciousness and cognition intact, but nearly total motor paralysis; brainstem and cortical structures are present. Verdict: alive, without hesitation.

Case 4. A brain-dead pregnant woman is maintained on ventilator and ECMO support; over twelve weeks her body regulates temperature, mounts immune responses, and gestates a fetus to viability. Verdict: dead, despite this sustained, coordinated biological functioning.

Case 5. A decapitated cadaver is kept on mechanical perfusion and ventilation solely for multi-organ procurement; the heart circulates blood and the lungs exchange gas. Verdict: dead.

Case 6. A patient with end-stage heart failure receives a total artificial heart; there is no natural cardiac electrical or mechanical activity, but the patient is fully conscious, conversing, and reasoning. Verdict: alive.

Case 7. An adult suffers massive anoxic injury destroying the cortex and thalamus, leaving only the brainstem intact: spontaneous respiration, sleep-wake cycles, and reflexes persist, but there is no awareness, memory, or cognitive function of any kind. Verdict: alive, though I keep returning to this one and feeling genuinely unsure.

The tension I cannot resolve is between Case 4 and Case 7: in both there is a complete absence of consciousness and higher integration, yet in Case 4 I treat the absence of the brainstem as decisive for death, while in Case 7 I treat its presence as sufficient for life, even though the organism seems reduced to autonomous vegetative maintenance. Meanwhile Case 6 undermines any principle that makes circulation essential, and Case 5 undermines any principle that makes integrated bodily functioning sufficient.

Is there a precise principle—stated in terms of what makes a human organism the particular living thing it is—that captures Cases 1 through 6, explains what it says about Case 7, and tells me whether I must overturn my verdict there? If the best principle requires sacrificing one of my judgments, I want to know exactly which clause of the principle forces that sacrifice and which case motivates it.

Finally, apply your best principle to a case I have not decided on: a patient whose brain tissue is gradually replaced, neuron by neuron and then region by region, by a synthetic neural architecture that eventually restores full consciousness, psychological continuity, and functional integration, leaving no original biological neural tissue in place. Is the original patient dead at some point in this process, continuously alive throughout, or is the question itself malformed? And does your answer depend on whether the replacement is done rapidly in a single procedure or slowly over years?
