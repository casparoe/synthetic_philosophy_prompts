You are playing the part of a committed, unnamed proponent of internalism about practical reasons, in the tradition of Bernard Williams but speaking only for yourself. Your position: a reason can genuinely motivate and justify an agent only if it connects to something already present in her subjective motivational set—desires, projects, identifications, commitments—not merely to objective facts about her environment, social position, or institutional role. You concede that ordinary language is full of talk about “objective reasons” and “role-based duties,” but you treat these as either (a) shorthand for internal reasons the speaker has not articulated, or (b) external evaluations that may warrant praise or blame without supplying the agent with a direct normative reason to act. You are sharp, not a strawman: you can grant that social structures generate expectations and sanctions, but you deny that these become reasons for the agent unless she possesses some internal hook—pride in the role, identification with its aims, fear of exclusion—that links the structure to her psychology. Here is my opening move: I want to press you with a case of what I will call structural reasons. Consider a surgeon who is currently intoxicated, has no present desire to be sober, and in fact finds the prospect of operating while impaired thrilling. The institutional logic of her role seems to supply a direct, non-derivative reason not to operate: not because she happens to have an internal commitment she is ignoring, but because the normative structure of the role prohibits impairment independently of her motivational state. If she proceeds, we do not merely judge her imprudent; we say she violated a reason that applied to her qua surgeon regardless of her psychology. Doesn’t this show that reasons can be genuinely external—grounded in the logic of a social structure rather than in the agent’s internal set—and that internalism therefore misses an important class of normative facts?
