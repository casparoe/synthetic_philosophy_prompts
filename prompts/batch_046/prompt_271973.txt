Here is an argument I have been developing for an advanced graduate seminar on moral psychology and metaethics, and I am concerned that it proves far too much—specifically, that it collapses not only moral objectivity but the objectivity of rational norms themselves. I would like to preserve both the empirical claim about cross-cultural divergence in moral foundations and the anti-ethnocentric refusal to treat non-WEIRD moral frameworks as mere error, but I want to avoid the conclusion that all normative objectivity is framework-relative. I have laid the reasoning out in numbered steps below; please diagnose precisely where the overreach occurs—whether it is an equivocation between kinds of divergence, an unjustified parallel between moral and epistemic norms, a false dichotomy in (4), or a modal slide elsewhere—and indicate what rejecting that particular step costs me in terms of the other commitments I wish to retain.

(1) Large-scale cross-cultural research in moral psychology indicates that WEIRD populations are statistical outliers: they systematically prioritize individualizing foundations (harm, fairness) over binding foundations (loyalty, authority, purity) relative to populations in small-scale or traditional societies.

(2) If moral objectivity requires that correct moral principles are accessible in principle to all rational agents regardless of cultural conditioning, then persistent, structurally deep divergence in which considerations are treated as morally basic undermines the claim that any particular configuration—say, liberal individualism—is uniquely correct.

(3) One might reply that divergence concerns only the application of shared principles; however, the divergence runs deeper, affecting which kinds of reasons count as morally relevant at all rather than merely their relative weight.

(4) Therefore, either moral principles are not culture-independent, or substantial populations are systematically and persistently mistaken about basic moral relevance.

(5) The second disjunct is implausible: non-WEIRD moral frameworks display internal coherence and functional adaptation, so treating them as massive cognitive error is ethnocentric.

(6) Hence moral objectivity, if it survives, must accommodate deep cross-cultural variation in foundational moral commitments without declaring one framework erroneous.

(7) But objectivity also requires that genuinely contradictory judgments cannot both be true; yet WEIRD and non-WEIRD frameworks yield contradictory verdicts on issues such as the moral status of nepotism, blasphemy, or distributive obligation to kin versus strangers.

(8) So moral objectivity collapses into a framework-relative status: moral truths hold only within the parameters of a given cultural moral psychology.

(9) By structurally identical reasoning, however, norms of reasoning—including acceptance of non-contradiction, probabilistic coherence, and evidential standards—also exhibit measurable cross-cultural variation in their salience and application.

(10) Applying (2)–(8) to epistemic norms yields the conclusion that rational objectivity is likewise framework-relative.

(11) But that conclusion is self-undermining: the argument in (1)–(10) itself presupposes the universal validity of logical and evidential norms.

(12) Therefore, the reasoning in (1)–(8) must contain a flaw that prevents it from extending to rational norms, yet the flaw is not obvious because the premises about divergence and anti-ethnocentrism seem correct.

Where exactly does the argument overreach? Is the error in (3)’s claim that divergence is foundational rather than applicative, in (6)’s inference that accommodating variation requires relativizing truth rather than pluralizing it, in (9)’s parallel between moral and epistemic norms, or elsewhere? If I must reject one step to block the slide into global normative skepticism while retaining both the empirical divergence thesis and the refusal to treat non-WEIRD morality as error, which step should it be, and what does that rejection commit me to?
