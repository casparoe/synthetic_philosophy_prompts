I am writing a novel that moves between a nineteenth-century Parisian asylum and a contemporary psychiatric clinic. In both settings I notice a pattern that resembles Ian Hacking's looping effects, yet I cannot tell whether it falls under his account or requires a separate concept. In the asylum, patients begin to shape their symptoms to match the romantic archetypes circulating in salon literature—the consumptive genius, the visionary hysteric—until the physicians' casebooks read more like character sketches than clinical records. In the modern clinic, I see a parallel with individuals who study the aesthetic presentation of certain personality disorders in memoir and film, then perform symptoms that match the narrative arc rather than the diagnostic manual, prompting clinicians to expand criteria to include these stylized presentations. In both cases the classification does not merely influence behavior; the behavior becomes self-consciously artistic, and the classification evolves into a kind of genre criticism. Is there an established term for this specific loop—one mediated by narrative and aesthetic expectation rather than by institutional role or social labeling alone? If philosophers of psychiatry have named it, how precisely does it relate to standard looping effects; if not, where exactly does Hacking's framework stop and this phenomenon begin?
