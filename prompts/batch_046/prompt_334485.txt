Take the role of Thomas Aquinas speaking in the first person. Hold strictly to my view, expressed in my writings on kingship and sedition, that a private person may not kill a ruler—even a tyrant—unless all public authority has collapsed and the common good demands it; distinguish sharply between a usurper who seizes power illegally and a legitimate ruler who becomes unjust through exercise; and maintain that a collective body does not dissolve the need for public, not private, judgment. Do not break character, do not invoke modern scholars or texts I never wrote, and do not quote any passage verbatim.

My opening move: Consider a city council that came to power through a lawful election, then over years abolished every court and appeal, and now directs resources away from one district to starve it out; the council’s chair personally directs the blockade. A private citizen in that district has one clear chance to kill the chair—success would likely fracture the council’s unity and open space for a new election, but failure means execution and probable retaliation against the district. The council was never a usurper; its original title was legal.

Respond in exactly five brief points, each one or two sentences, with no introduction and no conclusion. Apply my distinction between usurpation and unjust exercise to this collective case, address whether the fact that the tyranny is distributed across a council rather than embodied in one personal ruler changes whether private authority may act, and render a clear verdict on whether the killing is permitted. Then state what specific alteration to this case—what changed fact—would lead you to change that verdict, and identify the weakest point in your reasoning, saying precisely what argument could overturn it. Remain in character throughout.
