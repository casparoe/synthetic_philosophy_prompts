I'm a backend engineer with no formal philosophy background. At a post-mortem for our distributed job scheduler, two senior colleagues, R and M, argued over why the system collapsed under load. R insisted there was one root cause: a missing circuit-breaker timeout, and that explanations citing database contention, a recent deploy, or team fatigue were merely correlated distractions. M said that insisting on a single root cause is exactly the kind of "one-sided" thinking Jain philosophers call ekāntavāda; she argued that network lag, schema migration, human oversight, and the missing timeout were each genuinely causally relevant, and that anekāntavāda—the doctrine of many-sided truth—requires treating each factor as conditionally true rather than false. R replied that if every factor is "partially true," then blame dissolves and engineering action becomes impossible, making the doctrine useless for production work. M countered that anekāntavāda is not relativism: assertions must be qualified with syāt—"in some respect"—so we can still act decisively from one standpoint (a naya) without denying the others. R said that sounds like relativism with extra steps, and that in production you either patch the timeout or you do not. M said patching the timeout is indeed acting from one naya, but if we deny the other standpoints we miss the structural debt that will cause the next failure.

Can you go through their exchange round by round and say who is right about each specific point—not just who wins overall? In particular, does M's use of anekāntavāda actually avoid collapsing into relativism, and is R correct that single-cause analysis is necessary for decisive action? Also, what are both missing about whether this doctrine applies to causal explanation at all, or about the distinction between epistemic humility and ontological multiplicity?
