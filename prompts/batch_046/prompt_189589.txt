As a retired astrophysicist who has recently turned to philosophy of science, I am preparing a session for an interdisciplinary reading group of about fourteen graduate students—some in physics, some in philosophy—meeting for eighty minutes. I want them not merely to understand but to feel the specific pressure that deterministic chaos exerts on our notions of predictability and ontological determinism. To that end, please design a structured classroom game or simulation in which the participants themselves generate and then confront this pressure, with rules and payoffs arranged so that the problem emerges from their actions before anyone names it.

The idea I want staged is somewhat technical, so I state it plainly for your use: in hyperbolic dynamical systems there exists a shadowing property—roughly, that near any approximate trajectory generated by noisy measurements there lies a true deterministic orbit that stays close to it forever, yet there is no measurement procedure within the system's own dynamics that can distinguish the true orbit from its shadows. I do not want the participants to study this theorem; I want them to live inside a simplified analog of it. Design roles—perhaps Observers who may take measurements only at discrete intervals with a fixed quantization error, and Predictors who must commit to a forecast trajectory based solely on those measurements—together with rules governing information exchange, a timeline divided into measurement rounds and prediction rounds, and a debrief that maps each phase onto the distinction between epistemic limitation and ontological determinism. The debrief should also be honest: identify exactly which feature of real shadowing dynamics—perhaps the infinite-time asymptotic behavior or the specific metric structure—this classroom analog necessarily fails to reproduce, and tell me how to supply that missing element verbally without undermining the experiential point.

Please include a brief note on what will predictably go wrong when the activity is run—whether participants will conflate practical unpredictability with indeterminism, whether the quantization rule will feel artificial, or whether the role division will collapse—and how a facilitator should handle each failure mode. Keep the framing appropriate to a retired scientist addressing peers: precise about the physics analog but philosophical about what is at stake. Do not ask for bibliographies, citations, or reading lists; I want a self-contained design that relies on no external texts beyond what the participants bring to the room.
