Write a short dialogue between two philosophers of mind, Elena and Marcus, who disagree about whether panpsychism can solve the combination problem. Elena defends a version of constitutive micropsychism according to which macro-consciousness arises when micro-subjects enter asymmetrical phenomenal binding relations—relations in which one micro-subject's qualitative character structurally determines another's access conditions without merging their first-person perspectives. Marcus defends a cosmopsychist alternative: macro-consciousness is fundamental, and apparent micro-subjects are merely modal divisions of a single cosmic subject, so the combination problem is a pseudo-problem generated by assuming micro-subjects are metaphysically prior.

Center the dialogue on a specific hypothetical case they construct together: a synthetic neural architecture in which subsystem A has only chromatic qualia (pure redness), subsystem B has only geometric qualia (pure circularity), and the link between them permits only one-way causal influence from A to B, with no reciprocal access. Elena should argue that this architecture could still generate a unified macro-experience of a red circle, because B's phenomenal field contains a structural slot that A's redness directly occupies, without requiring A and B to become a single subject. Marcus should respond that one-way occupation is phenomenally indistinguishable from mere representation—B represents A's redness without co-experiencing it—so the architecture yields either two isolated subjects or one cosmic subject that temporarily partitions itself, not a genuinely combined macro-subject.

Require that the exchange be genuinely interactive: each speaker must directly address the specific point the other just made, not deliver independent speeches. Neither should be a strawman. By the end, the disagreement should be sharply defined—whether asymmetrical phenomenal binding can preserve both macro-unity and micro-distinctness—without either side conceding. Do not include citations, bibliographies, references to specific texts, or requests for further reading; keep the discussion grounded in conceptual analysis and the hypothetical architecture.
