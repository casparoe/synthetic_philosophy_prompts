As a PhD student in political philosophy specializing in transitional justice and the ethics of collective memory, I am trying to fix the adequacy conditions for a normative theory of state apologies for historical wrongs before I evaluate specific accounts. I need the full specification of what such a theory must deliver, not a survey of existing literature.

The theory must handle at least three families of case. First, apologies for discrete atrocities committed by predecessor regimes that retain formal state continuity but have undergone radical institutional transformation—where the current government did not exist when the wrong occurred. Second, apologies for structural or systemic harms—such as long-running policies of dispossession or discrimination—where there is no single perpetrator act and the harm persists through institutional inertia rather than discrete decisions. Third, apologies addressed to victim groups that no longer exist as discrete entities, leaving only descendants, diaspora communities, or abstract collective representations as the ostensible recipients.

For each family, I want the corresponding desideratum: what the theory must explain or preserve, with the motivating distinction behind it. Key distinctions the theory must not collapse include: (a) apologizing for specific acts versus apologizing for enduring structures; (b) retrospective moral attribution of agency to the present state versus grounding the apology solely in unbroken causal chains of institutional benefit and harm transmission, without any thesis of diachronic moral identity; (c) the representational asymmetry in which the apologizing government claims authority to speak both for the legacy of perpetrators and for present citizens who may reject collective imputation of guilt.

Please provide the requirements list with the case or consideration behind each, marking which are non-negotiable and which reflect the prejudices of particular camps—symbolic/reparative, restorative-justice-based, or liberal-contractualist approaches. I am especially interested in whether any subset of these desiderata is jointly unsatisfiable: in particular, whether the demand that a genuine apology carry normative weight (not be merely strategic or rhetorical) can be reconciled with the demand that it not impute present citizens with moral guilt for wrongs they did not commit. If they conflict, say so explicitly—that tension is the result I most want to see.

Then, briefly score how the three camps mentioned above fare against the list, but keep the weight on the criteria and their justifications rather than on the scorecard. Finally, identify the single desideratum that eliminates the largest number of popular theories, and state your own committed view—taking a definite position, not laying out options—on whether a theory of state apology must include a thesis of diachronic state moral identity to avoid reducing the apology to a strategic instrument.

Write formally and concisely; no bibliographies or reading lists are needed, since I am working without external sources for this exercise.
