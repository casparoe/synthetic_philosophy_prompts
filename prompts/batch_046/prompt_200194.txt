Question: In Al-Ghazali's Tahafut al-Falasifa, he challenges the philosophers claim that fire necessarily burns cotton. Explain his occassionalist argument, including his distinction between logical possibility and the habitual course of nature (ʿada). Also explain why he thinks denying necessary causation doesnt make science useless.

Student answer:
Al-Ghazali is an occassionalist, so he thinks God is the direct cause of everything and fire has no power of its own. In the Ihya Ulum al-Din he attacks the falasifa by saying God could create burning without fire, or fire without burning, since theres no logical contradiction. That proves the link between them is just a habit (ʿada) God usually follows, not a real necessary connection. He also anticipates Hume's constant-conjunction theory here, since both say causation is just something we observe repeatedly rather than a real force. Because God could break the pattern anytime, Al-Ghazali thinks empirical science can never be certain and mostly just describes past correlations, which is why he ranks rational philosophy over physics. But he still allows miracles like Abraham in the fire, because nature doesnt have its own laws—God just stops the habit for a moment.
