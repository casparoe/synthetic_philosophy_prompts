I work as a software engineer in applied machine learning, with no formal background in philosophy. In my field there is an institutional practice I find conceptually puzzling. We routinely deploy predictive systems—recommender engines, risk scorers, demand forecasts—built on statistical assumptions that everyone knows are false in deployment: observations are treated as independent and identically distributed, distributions are assumed stationary, and strategic adaptation by users is ignored. The standard institutional defense for these unrealistic assumptions is not that they approximate reality, but that the model performs well on held-out validation data and improves a downstream business metric. Yet within the same organization, if a linear regression exhibits heteroskedasticity or multicollinearity—violations that do not necessarily reduce predictive accuracy—we often reject the model or demand structural remedies. At the same time, we deploy deep neural networks whose internal representations bear no transparent relationship to the causal structure of the domain, accepting them solely on the basis of A/B test lift.

What philosophical commitments does this pattern of acceptance and rejection presuppose? Does it implicitly endorse Friedman's instrumentalist view—that unrealistic assumptions are harmless provided predictions are accurate? Or does the insistence on fixing statistical violations despite unchanged predictive performance commit us to a different position, one on which some forms of unrealism undermine explanatory legitimacy, statistical coherence, or institutional accountability? Reconstruct the implicit theory of model evaluation that makes heteroskedasticity unacceptable but black-box structural unrealism acceptable. Is the practice consistent with a single philosophical view, or does it combine incompatible commitments—perhaps instrumentalism for predictive architectures and some form of structural realism or auditability for interpretable ones? And if the real underlying commitment is not about prediction at all, but about blame allocation or transparency, what would that imply about how we ought to validate and justify these systems?
