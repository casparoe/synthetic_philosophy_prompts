After spending the last eight months navigating my partner’s diagnostic workup for an unexplained neurological condition, I have become preoccupied with a question that the attending physicians kept circling but never named: how do we know that something is not the case when our belief depends on ruling out alternatives rather than tracking a present fact? I am drafting a proposal for a six-month independent study fellowship—roughly the scope of one dissertation chapter—to use this clinical intuition to press a problem inside Nozick’s tracking theory of knowledge.

The precise question: Does Nozick’s tracking framework preserve epistemic closure for negative knowledge (knowing that p is false) in the same way it does for positive knowledge, or does closure for negative beliefs require a distinct “exclusionary tracking” condition that Nozick never articulates and that, once made explicit, undermines his standard response to skepticism?

Why existing answers leave it open: The literature on tracking and closure generally treats entailment symmetrically—if S knows P and P entails Q, then in the nearest worlds where ~Q, ~P, so S does not believe Q is false. But this assumes that knowing ~P (for example, knowing it is not a tumor) functions like knowing P (knowing it is inflammation). In clinical reasoning, negative knowledge often works differently: the physician tracks the absence of a tumor not by tracking tumor-worlds and seeing she lacks belief there, but by tracking a battery of exclusionary indicators. Nozick’s framework does not distinguish these mechanisms, and critics of closure have not pressed whether their counterexamples operate differently for negative beliefs. Without that distinction, we cannot tell whether closure fails uniformly or only for one kind of knowledge.

Approach, and why not the obvious alternatives: Rather than surveying all closure debates or constructing another brain-in-a-vat variant, I will formalize two tracking conditions—direct counterfactual tracking and exclusionary counterfactual tracking—and apply them to structured conceptual models drawn from diagnostic reasoning (not empirical case studies, but normative analogies). Month one: formalize both conditions with precise modal clauses. Month two: apply them to Nozick’s canonical examples—lottery, grandmother, skeptical scenarios—rewritten specifically for negative belief. Month three: determine whether exclusionary tracking either saves closure for negative knowledge or collapses into a form of contextualism about evidential thresholds. Month four: draft the chapter, state explicit failure conditions, and bracket extensions to social or collective epistemology that the timeline cannot cover. A historical study of Nozick’s texts would miss the conceptual distinction; an empirical study of physician behavior would miss the normative structure. Only this conceptual-engineering approach can yield a determinate answer.

What a result looks like and what counts as failure: Either (a) exclusionary tracking is reducible to direct tracking, in which case closure holds uniformly but skeptical arguments regain unexpected force for negative beliefs, or (b) it is irreducible, in which case closure fails selectively and we need a bifurcated epistemology of absence. The project fails if the distinction collapses into mere pragmatics about how much evidence one gathers, leaving the tracking framework entirely untouched and providing no reason to revise closure.

Please take a definite position: argue for either (a) or (b) as the more plausible outcome, defend the specific formalization of tracking you think best captures negative knowledge, and explain precisely what that choice costs for Nozick’s anti-skeptical strategy. Do not merely map the options and leave the choice to me; commit to one, show why the costs of rejecting it are worse, and indicate where your own argument is most vulnerable to objection.
