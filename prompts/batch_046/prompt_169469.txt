After a long argument with a friend about aggregation and personal identity, I have tried to write down my actual philosophical profile on the separateness of persons, and I want an audit before I defend it further. Here are my commitments: (1) I accept the separateness of persons objection in its standard form—severe burdens on one individual cannot be justified merely by summing smaller benefits across many others, because persons are not fungible repositories of utility. (2) I am convinced by Parfit’s reductionist account of personal identity: there is no further fact of being the same person over time beyond psychological continuity and connectedness, so the boundary between persons is metaphysically no deeper than the boundary between temporal stages of one life. (3) Despite (2), I insist on preserving a sharp moral asymmetry between intrapersonal and interpersonal trade-offs: I regard it as rational and permissible to impose present costs on myself for future gain, but I reject parallel sacrifices imposed across distinct individuals. (4) I ground that asymmetry in agent-relative reasons—the reason I have not to harm you is directed specifically at me in virtue of my relation to you, not merely a general reason for the world to contain less harm. (5) I take reflective equilibrium to license holding (1)–(4) together, on the grounds that (4) supplies the normative structure that (2) removes at the metaphysical level. Is this package jointly tenable? Which pairs are in genuine tension, which only appear to conflict, and what is the least costly revision? In particular, does (2) undermine the very distinction (3) tries to preserve, or does (4) genuinely save it? Rank the tensions by severity, specify for each what would have to give, and then identify which single commitment, if dropped, would resolve the most conflict at the lowest philosophical cost.
