I serve on a clinical review board that evaluates long-term neural-prosthesis protocols in which a patient's biological neurons are replaced incrementally with synthetic analogues over years, with no discrete moment of "uploading." We need a reusable decision procedure—usable in a thirty-minute board meeting by members without philosophical training—to determine at each milestone whether the undergoing patient remains numerically identical to the pre-procedure person for purposes of consent continuity, liability, and rights. Ground the procedure in the conceptual structure of gradual-replacement identity debates, but do not require citation of named theorists; instead, translate the relevant distinctions into ordered clinical and phenomenological checkpoints. The procedure should specify: the sequence of questions to ask, what counts as a sufficient answer at each stage, where it must halt with a finding of discontinuity, and which nodes are irreducible value judgments rather than empirical determinations. Explicitly address how it handles the "interstitial gap"—the period during which neither the remaining biological substrate nor the installed synthetic substrate fully realizes the mental states in question—and whether the procedure permits branching, division, or co-existence of identity candidates. Apply it to two hypothetical review cases: first, a patient in whom replacements preserve functional connectivity, autobiographical access, and affective coherence; second, a patient in whom replacements gradually alter emotional response profiles and autobiographical coherence, producing an outcome the board will find ethically unwelcome. Include clear markers of where the procedure smuggles in philosophical commitments—for instance, whether psychological continuity is treated as necessary, whether bodily continuity carries independent weight, and whether identity is treated as all-or-nothing or as admitting degrees. Make it honest about where it will misfire and what it cannot settle.
