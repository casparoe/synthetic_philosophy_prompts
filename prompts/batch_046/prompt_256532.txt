Question: Judy Benjamin is dropped behind lines. She knows there are two divisions (Red and Blue) and within each division two companies (A and B). Her prior is uniform over the four combos. She then recieves a radio message she can only parse as the conditional: "If I'm in Red, then I'm in Company B." She does not observe her division directly. Explain how she should update her beliefs about (i) being in Red, and (ii) being in Company B. Compare strict conditioning (treating the material conditional as the observed event) with Jeffrey conditioning (keeping division marginals fixed and updating within blocks). Give the exact posteriors for both rules.

Student answer:
Judy starts with four equally likely states: (Red,A), (Red,B), (Blue,A), (Blue,B), each 1/4.

For strict conditioning, the message R → B is false only at (Red,A), so we drop that world and renormalize over the remaining three. That gives P(Red) = 1/3 and P(B) = P(Red,B)+P(Blue,B) = 1/3 + 1/3 = 2/3. That part is straightforward.

For Jeffrey conditioning we don't drop worlds globally. Instead we fix the division marginals at P(Red)=P(Blue)=1/2. Within Red, B becomes certain, so P(Red,B) = 1/2. Within Blue nothing is constrained, so P(Blue,A)=P(Blue,B)=1/4. Thus P(B) = 1/2 + 1/4 = 3/4 while P(Red) stays 1/2.

Now, this is actually the same mechanics as Lewisian imaging: you shift the probability mass from (Red,A) over to the closest (Red,B) world, leaving all Blue worlds untouched. So Jeffrey conditioning is basically imaging applied to conditionals. Because of that, the two rules don't really conflict once you apply the right normalization — they just weight the sample space differently, and in fact both converge on P(B)=2/3 if you average over divisions correctly. The real lesson is that Bayesian updating is subjective here: since P(Red) comes out 1/3 under one rule and 1/2 under the other, there's no objective fact about what Judy should believe, so the problem is ill-formed for anyone who wants unique posteriors.
