I recently came across an unnamed popular essay that attempts to summarize Peter Singer's case against speciesism. The author writes that Singer holds moral status to depend entirely on sentience, which makes species membership irrelevant in every context, including as a practical proxy for cognitive ability. From this, the essay concludes that Singer must regard a severely cognitively disabled human being and a pig with comparable mental capacities as possessing exactly the same moral weight, thereby demonstrating that speciesism is structurally equivalent to racism. The author further claims that Singer's framework obligates us to extend the same legal rights to sentient nonhuman animals and to human infants, since neither can enter into reciprocal moral contracts. I am considering using this framing in a feature I am writing, but I worry it collapses distinctions that Singer actually keeps separate—particularly between equal consideration of interests and identical treatment, and between intrinsic moral relevance and proxy relevance. Could you audit the summary? Please distinguish which elements accurately reflect Singer's position, which are subtle misrepresentations or overstatements, whether the analogy to racism survives once the role of sentience is properly specified, and whether the conclusion about uniform legal rights actually follows from the premises.
