I'm delivering the opening remarks at a one-day symposium, Speech, Repair, and Institutional Memory, convened jointly by the philosophy department and the university's Center for Civic Dialogue. The occasion marks the twentieth anniversary of the state's formal apology for the forced assimilation and removal policies directed at indigenous communities in the northern provinces between the 1950s and 1970s. The audience is divided: on one side are philosophers, legal theorists, and linguists who work on performative utterance theory; on the other are community historians, advocates, and descendants of those affected, many of whom regard the 2004 apology as a hollow performative—formally valid, yet failing to repair the relation it claimed to restore.

Write a ten-minute address in which speech-act theory is structural, not decorative. You should use the framework of performative utterances, felicity conditions, and misfires to explain how an institution can utter an apology with full procedural authority and still fail to achieve repair. Distinguish clearly between performative success—the correct speaker, correct procedure, correct institutional audience—and perlocutionary failure, the absence of any actual change in the relation between the institution and the affected communities. Introduce and develop the concept of cumulative misfire: the risk that elaborating, repeating, or refining an apology can compound the original injury by treating an irreparable harm as if it were correctable through speech alone. The argument should shape what you can and cannot say as the speaker: do not claim that words alone can complete the work of repair; do not promise concrete institutional actions that lie outside your authority; and explicitly identify at least one moment where further utterance would be not merely inadequate but actively harmful. The philosophers in the room should recognize the conceptual machinery—felicity conditions, the performative-constative distinction, perlocutionary effects—without the need for citations or named texts; the non-philosophers should be able to follow the reasoning without prior familiarity with that literature. Avoid bibliographies, reading lists, or references to specific works or scholars. The tone should be formal and accessible, and the rhetorical arc should be driven by the philosophical framing rather than ornamented with it.
