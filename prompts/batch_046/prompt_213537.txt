In my philosophy of language seminar we have been working through Davidson's account of radical interpretation and the principle of charity, specifically the claim that attributing mostly true beliefs and broad rational coherence is not merely a practical heuristic but a constitutive condition of interpreting another speaker at all. I want to examine whether this claim survives if we extend the interpretive scenario to entities that lack biological evolutionary histories—imagine a synthetic system whose representational states are distributed across non-symbolic architectures and do not map neatly onto propositional attitudes. Before I defend this extension in my essay, I need to understand the exposure of the underlying Davidsonian position itself: what would have to be true for the constitutive link between belief-attribution and global rationality to break? I am looking for defeaters that go beyond the familiar paranoid-interlocutor counterexample—perhaps modular or domain-specific irrationalities documented in cognitive science, or formal models of interpretation convergence that do not require global charity assumptions. For each, please indicate whether a committed Davidsonian should concede it as damaging or can dismiss it by restricting the domain of 'interpretation'; rank them from fatal to merely awkward; and say whether the position risks being qualified into unfalsifiability if every apparent counterexample is absorbed by such restrictions.
