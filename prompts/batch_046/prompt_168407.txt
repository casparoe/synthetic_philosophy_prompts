Two colleagues, Sam and Riley, have been arguing about whether moral compromise between incompatible value systems can ever be genuinely rational—specifically, whether it can produce something analogous to gains from trade. They discuss a hypothetical joint institution: a conservation-health consortium in which one partner, guided by a strict deontological environmental ethic that treats certain ecosystems as inviolable, agrees to relax absolute prohibitions on land use in exchange for the other partner, guided by a utilitarian public-health framework that prioritises aggregate welfare, adopting unprecedented safeguards that reduce net harm across both domains.

Sam opens by arguing that this is structurally like Ricardian comparative advantage: each side exports what it values less (absolute purity, or pure welfare-maximisation) and imports what it values more, leaving both better off according to their own metrics. Riley objects that moral values are not fungible commodities; relaxing an inviolable prohibition is not like trading surplus grain, because the cost is not merely a reduction in value but a corruption of the evaluative framework itself. Sam replies that this assumes all value systems are lexically ordered, whereas real pluralistic ethics involve weighted considerations allowing marginal adjustments without collapse. Riley counters that even weighted pluralism contains thresholds of integrity—certain compromises are not marginal adjustments but category mistakes, analogous to exchanging methodological honesty for research funding, which destroys rather than reallocates value. Sam responds that calling something a category mistake is often rhetorical refusal to recognise an unfavourable exchange rate, and that revealed preference—both partners genuinely endorsing the consortium—shows the values satisfied at the margin. Riley retorts that revealed preference is unreliable in moral domains because agents can be systematically mistaken about what their values require, and that moral compromise involves second-order identity: one becomes the kind of agent who permits the relaxation, so there is no stable self whose preferences validate the trade. Sam concludes that identity is itself constructed through practical negotiation, so there is no pre-compromised authentic self whose corruption should be feared.

Please adjudicate their exchange move by move: assess the strength of each argument as actually made, indicate where each speaker is clearly right, clearly mistaken, or partly correct, and explain what, if anything, both are missing. Then reach an overall verdict on whether moral compromise can genuinely yield gains analogous to economic trade, with your reasoning. Write your answer in British English.
