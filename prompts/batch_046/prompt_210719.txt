Consider a permanently observation-inaccessible domain—call it the substrate—about which two scientific communities develop complete, empirically equivalent theories. One describes the substrate as composed of discrete, point-like entities interacting through local forces; the other describes it as a continuous field-based manifold with no discrete parts whatsoever. The two theories are mathematically isomorphic: every predictive and explanatory success of one can be mapped onto the other without remainder. Crucially, there is no deeper structural feature that could adjudicate between them, because the substrate is defined solely through its relational effects, and those relations are identical in both descriptions. I contend that this scenario does not vindicate your position but dissolves it. If ontology is determined entirely by structure, and both theories share exactly the same structure, then there is no ontological distinction left to be made—not even a structural one—between “particles” and “fields.” The question “which ontology is correct?” becomes not answered by structural realism, but shown to be malformed, analogous to asking whether a set is “really” its members or its subset lattice.
