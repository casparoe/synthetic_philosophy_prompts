I have just finished drafting the central chapter of my dissertation, in which I defend a literal reading of Simone Weil's argument that political parties ought to be abolished — specifically, her claim that party loyalty operates as a form of spiritual gravity, corrupting the soul's capacity for justice-oriented attention — and I apply this to propose that modern citizens' assemblies replace party discipline with structured rituals of collective attention. Next month I present this to my university's political theory workshop: the audience includes a democratic-aggregation theorist who treats parties as indispensable mechanisms for preference aggregation, a comparative-politics scholar who studies secession movements and regards parties as the only organizational vehicles for self-determination claims, and a historian of ideas who considers Weil's mystical-religious vocabulary biographically interesting but politically inert. Prepare my Q&A defense: list the objections they will actually raise, ordered from the most damaging to the least, phrased in the vocabulary each would use. For each, provide a concise reply suitable for a two-minute response, flag the weakest point in that reply, and state definitively — without hedging — which objections I should concede outright, which I must hold firm, and which reply must land for the argument to survive. Include the one question I am hoping nobody asks. Keep the focus on Weil's specific claim that party loyalty damages the soul's orientation toward justice, not on generic anti-party institutional arguments, and take a committed position on whether that metaphysical claim can be translated into secular institutional design without losing its normative force.
