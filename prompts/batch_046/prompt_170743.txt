After a long argument with a friend about whether democratic backsliding is an analytically coherent concept or merely a partisan label for governments we happen to oppose, I am drafting the final examination for my upper-level comparative politics and democratic theory seminar. I want an essay question that students cannot answer by reciting country profiles of Hungary, Poland, Turkey, or the United States. Instead, the prompt should compel them to build and defend a conceptual architecture. To that end, please embed in the question a hypothetical criterion I have been calling the “inviolate-level thesis”: that backsliding is best diagnosed not by tallying institutional violations, but by identifying whether a regime has breached an internal democratic “floor”—a norm that the regime itself previously treated as non-negotiable—such that the breach reveals erosion even when formal constitutional structures remain technically intact. The question should require students to apply this thesis to distinguish genuine backsliding from ordinary conservative reform or intense democratic contestation in at least two of the four cases mentioned, and then to evaluate whether the thesis successfully escapes the charge of being irreducibly subjective or partisan. The exam is timed for 90 minutes; indicate an expected answer length of roughly 900 words. Please provide: (1) the exam question itself, phrased so that it cannot be answered without engaging the thesis; (2) a model answer of about 800 words that demonstrates the specific analytical moves required; and (3) a marking scheme totaling 25 points, built around five must-hit analytical moves, with the point value for each move specified, plus a one-line note on the characteristic failure mode that should cost marks for each. The scheme should clearly separate an A-level answer, which must explicitly address the subjectivity objection and show how the thesis interacts with at least one concrete institutional feature from two countries, from a B-level answer that merely applies the label without analyzing the floor mechanism. Do not ask for bibliographies, citations, or reading lists; the evaluation should depend entirely on conceptual reasoning.
