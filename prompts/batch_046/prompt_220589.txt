You sit on a city's Commission on Public Memory and Spatial Heritage, tasked with recommending to the city council whether to retain, rename, or reconceive Venn Square. The square was named in 1948 for Dr. Elias Venn (1871–1939), a sanitary engineer whose filtration system ended a local cholera outbreak in 1894, but whose recently digitized private correspondence from 1926–1928 endorses compulsory sterilization of those he calls the hereditarily unfit and proposes using public-health infrastructure to monitor racial-hygiene metrics. The council has narrowed the options to three: retain the name with an added contextual plaque; rename the square after a figure nominated by the affected communities; or adopt a model of palimpsestic retention, in which the original name remains physically present but is enclosed in a transparent interpretive frame displaying excerpts from the correspondence and a critical historical narrative, so that the site functions as reflexive commemoration rather than uncritical honor. Draft a formal decision memo recommending one option, with explicit criteria that the commission could apply to analogous cases—such as whether the harm was private conviction or institutional practice, whether the public achievement is conceptually inseparable from the harmful ideology, and whether removal risks erasing a necessary historical warning. The memo must include an honest treatment of the strongest consideration on the losing side, especially the objection that palimpsestic retention transforms functional civic space into a permanent exhibition of shame and thereby imposes an unavoidable moral burden on everyday users of the square.
