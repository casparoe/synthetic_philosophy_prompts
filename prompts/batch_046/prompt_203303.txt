After a long argument with a friend about whether the Enlightenment should be read as an emancipatory project or as the hidden origin of modern domination, I want to test a more precise framing on a familiar text. Read Mary Shelley’s Frankenstein through the lens of the Enlightenment’s internal paradox—not the usual Promethean hubris of Victor, but the pedagogical arc of the creature, who teaches himself reason, history, and moral philosophy from Volney, Plutarch, and Milton, only to discover that the same rational framework that lets him name his suffering also gives him the conceptual tools to justify vengeance. I am interested in whether Shelley dramatizes a version of the dialectic in which Enlightenment reason, when extended to a subject denied social recognition, does not liberate but becomes instrumental in a new form: self-domination and retaliatory violence. Focus on the scenes in which the creature discovers the books, compares himself to both Adam and Satan, demands a companion, and pursues Victor to the Arctic. Where does the philosophical idea illuminate these moments—for instance, in the creature’s claim that he was benevolent until misery made him a fiend—and where does the novel resist reducing his violence to a failure of reason alone? Does Shelley’s portrayal of the creature’s education add something to the debate about Enlightenment and domination that abstract argument misses, particularly regarding the role of recognition and affect in rational self-formation? I want a reading that does not treat the novel as mere illustration, but takes both the text and the conceptual problem seriously, and that ends by saying what the work contributes that the philosophy, on its own, cannot.
