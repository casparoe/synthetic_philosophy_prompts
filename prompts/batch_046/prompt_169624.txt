For an upper-level undergraduate philosophy of science final exam, write one essay question that students can answer in 75 minutes (target length: approximately 800 words). The topic is Hempel's raven paradox, but the question must not be answerable by reciting the standard formulation, the logical equivalence of "all ravens are black" with "all non-black things are non-ravens," and Hempel's resolution. Instead, frame the problem as follows: ask students to consider the hypothesis reformulated as a statistical claim—that the base rate of blackness among ravens exceeds 0.99—rather than as a universal generalization. They must assess whether observing a red apple (a non-black non-raven) provides evidential support for this statistical claim, and whether observing a black shoe (a black non-raven) provides counter-evidence by parallel reasoning. They must then determine whether shifting from logical to probabilistic confirmation dissolves the paradox or merely relocates it, and they must evaluate whether Nicod's criterion—which privileges direct positive instances—can be reconstructed within a Bayesian framework without collapsing once the backing of logical equivalence is removed. Provide a model answer of roughly 600 words that takes a clear position and explicitly works through the apple/shoe asymmetry. Also provide a marking scheme totaling 20 points, divided into five specific analytical moves that an excellent answer must contain; for each move, state its point value and a one-line note on the characteristic mistake that should cost marks. The prompt and all materials should be written in American English. Do not request bibliographies, reading lists, citations, or references to specific editions or page numbers.
