I was discussing Strawson's Basic Argument with a colleague over coffee yesterday — in particular, the regress that arises when we ask whether an agent is responsible for being the kind of agent she is — and it struck me that students often grasp the logical structure more clearly when they see it translated into an institutional vocabulary they already navigate. For my advanced metaphysics seminar, I should like you to compose a sustained piece of bureaucratic satire.

Please write a deadpan parody of a university disciplinary code — specifically, the "Regulations for Conduct, Sanctions, and Exculpatory Review" issued by a fictional institution, the University of Infinite Regress — in which the administration has formally adopted Galen Strawson's Basic Argument against moral responsibility as the governing metaphysics of institutional agency. The document should be formatted as numbered clauses with nested sub-clauses and cross-references, written in the voice of an excessively formal university secretary. Its governing principle must be that, since no agent can be the ultimate cause of the way she is, no student, lecturer, or administrator can ever be genuinely culpable for plagiarism, dereliction of duty, or contractual breach; consequently, the disciplinary machinery must be rebuilt to accommodate an infinite regress of exculpation. You should exaggerate genuine features of Strawson's reasoning — the distinction between being the way one is and doing what one does, the impossibility of true self-origination, the collapse of desert-based sanctions — without distorting them into caricature. Include, for example, a mandatory "Causal Self-Origination Exemption Form", a standing sub-committee charged with investigating whether an investigating committee could have acted otherwise, and a retroactive refund scheme for fines levied upon agents now recognised as lacking ultimate responsibility. The humour should emerge from the bureaucratic logic pushed to its reductio, not from invented absurdities unrelated to the argument; it should be legible to a reader with no prior exposure to the free-will debate, yet recognisably fair to someone familiar with the literature. Aim for approximately 700 words, maintain sustained deadpan irony without winking, and write in British English.
