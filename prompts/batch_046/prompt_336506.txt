I've noticed a recurring pattern in practical reasoning about long-range policies—particularly in climate adaptation and macroeconomic forecasting—that I don't have a precise term for, and I'd like to know whether philosophy of probability or decision theory has named it, or whether I should coin one.

The pattern works like this. A planning committee faces a decision under uncertainty—say, whether to design a coastal defense for a 1-in-100 or a 1-in-500 storm risk. The initial evidence supports a specific probability range, and the committee is cautious. Over time, they commission more studies: broader historical reconstructions, additional sensitivity analyses, richer socioeconomic projections. Crucially, none of these new studies change the estimated likelihood of the storm; they are consistent with both risk levels and do not discriminate between them. The balance of evidence remains exactly where it was. Yet the committee reports feeling better informed, describes the evidential base as much denser, and gradually shifts toward the riskier design—without ever revising the probability figures upward. They seem to treat the sheer accumulation of non-discriminating background knowledge as a license for bolder action, as if the volume of relevant information itself reduced the danger of being wrong, even though the reasons for and against the riskier option are in the same proportion.

This is not ordinary overconfidence, because they do not claim the probabilities have changed. It is not ambiguity aversion in the usual sense, since they are not avoiding unknown probabilities but embracing risk despite unchanged ones. And while it touches Keynes's distinction between the weight of argument and the balance of probabilities, it is not quite that distinction itself: Keynes discussed weight as a feature of the argument's completeness, not as a behavioral driver that overrides unchanged probabilities in practical choice.

Is there an established concept—perhaps in Keynesian scholarship, decision theory, or the philosophy of risk—that captures exactly this move: treating an increase in non-discriminating evidential bulk as justification for stronger commitment, despite an unchanged probabilistic balance? If so, how does it differ from related notions like evidential weight, robustness analysis, or the precautionary principle? If there is no precise term, please propose a name and a definition that distinguishes it clearly from Keynes's weight, from ambiguity aversion, and from simple cognitive bias, including boundary cases where the pattern does not apply.
