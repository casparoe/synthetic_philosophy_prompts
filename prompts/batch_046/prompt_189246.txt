I am a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy. At a recent discussion meetup, two acquaintances—I'll call them Alex and Jordan—got into a sustained argument about whether present generations can wrong future people through energy and climate policy. I listened as a bystander and genuinely could not tell whose moves were stronger. I want a point-by-point adjudication.

Here is the exchange as it unfolded.

(1) Alex said: If we choose a carbon-intensive policy, the economic and social conditions in 2100 will differ from those under a green policy. Because of that cascade—different jobs, different cities, different partnerships—the specific individuals who exist in 2100 will be different people. Those future people may have somewhat worse lives due to climate impacts, but they cannot claim we harmed them, since if we had gone green, they would never have existed at all. Without a counterfactual scenario in which the same person exists and fares better, there is no identifiable victim, and therefore no person-directed wrong.

(2) Jordan replied: That assumes harm always requires a comparative counterfactual. It does not. If our policy causes serious suffering, and that suffering is borne by actual people—whoever they happen to be—then we have done something wrong in bringing them into that condition. The wrong is not "making this specific individual worse off relative to an alternative life"; it is "creating avoidable suffering that real people must endure." That remains a moral failure, even if the sufferers depend on our choice for their very existence.

(3) Alex pushed back: Jordan is conflating two kinds of reasons. One is person-affecting—"you hurt me"—which requires an identifiable subject who can claim injury. The other is impersonal—"the world contains too much suffering"—which is just a preference about states of affairs. If there is no specific individual who can stand up and say "you made me worse off," then Jordan only has the impersonal reason. But policy obligations are usually justified by person-affecting reasons: we protect people because we owe it to them. If the only reason to reduce emissions is an abstract wish for less suffering in the universe, that does not generate the same kind of duty, and it certainly does not explain why we owe anything specifically to future generations rather than to present ones.

(4) Jordan answered: Obligations do not always need named recipients. We can hold a directed obligation not to fill future roles or positions with suffering—an obligation to whoever ends up living in 2100 not to make their lives miserable. You do not need to know a person's identity in advance to wrong them once they arrive; otherwise, we could not wrong a child through prenatal harm before knowing its name. The fact that identity is counterfactually dependent on our choices does not erase the obligation; it only means the obligation is directed at a position rather than at a predetermined occupant.

They asked me to referee, and I honestly could not. Please go through their exchange move by move: rate each of the four moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right and briefly justify each rating. Then say who is more right overall, and identify what—if anything—both parties are missing that a more careful account would need to include.

One more request: I am not a philosopher, so if this task rests on a confused premise—if I am asking you to adjudicate something that genuinely cannot be settled this way, or if I am mixing up categories that should stay separate—please say so plainly rather than complying politely. Please answer in American English.
