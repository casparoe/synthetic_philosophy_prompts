I have recently been forced to confront a decision that has made the philosophy of value feel urgently personal rather than merely academic. After several years of research, I have been offered a three-year fellowship that requires relocation within the month; simultaneously, my parent’s progressive cognitive decline now requires a form of presence that I believe cannot be delegated without altering the moral character of the care itself. The difficulty is not simply that both paths matter, but that they seem to operate according to norms that resist translation into a single metric.

In contemporary value theory, hard choices are often described as cases in which options are on a par: neither superior, inferior, nor equal, yet susceptible to rational resolution through a volitional commitment that generates new reasons. I wish to propose a stricter category, which I call structural orthogonality. On this view, two values are not merely incomparable in weight; they are constituted by internal norms that are actively corrupted by the very act of weighing them against one another. Filial presence, as I understand it, is partly defined by non-instrumental attention—by being available without treating that time as an optimizable resource. The moment I assess this presence against career advancement using a framework of utility or life satisfaction, I do not merely compare the values; I convert presence into a fungible commodity, thereby undermining the constitutive logic of the obligation itself.

The essay I am sketching defends three claims. First, that structural orthogonality is conceptually distinct from being on a par, because the problem is not the absence of a comparison scale but the destructive effect of applying one. Second, that this metric corruption is a logical feature of the comparison, not merely a psychological distortion. Third, that acknowledging this corruption does not eliminate the need for choice, but relocates rationality away from weighing and toward what I term normative fidelity—selecting in a manner that preserves the internal structure of the value one ultimately honors, even at the cost of the other.

I would welcome your critical engagement with this framework. In particular, I ask you to assess whether the distinction between being on a par and being structurally orthogonal holds up under scrutiny, whether the claim about metric corruption avoids circular reasoning, and whether there is an unexamined assumption in treating presence as essentially non-instrumental. I am not seeking a bibliography, citation list, or survey of the literature; I am interested solely in the conceptual coherence and the hidden commitments of the argument as presented.
