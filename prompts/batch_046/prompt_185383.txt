For a piece I'm writing on conceptual structure and perception, I have been working through a position drawn from the Letter Spirit tradition but pushed in a more specific direction: that what makes every a an a is not a geometric prototype or a set of necessary stroke-level invariants, but a relational grammar of negative-space topology—the particular configuration of enclosed, semi-enclosed, and open regions that persists across typefaces, handwriting distortions, and even partial occlusion. On this account, recognizing an unfamiliar blackletter or severely distorted a is not template matching but the construction of a temporary analogical mapping between the unfamiliar form's negative-space relations and the relational roles defined by one's familiar alphabet. I want to stress-test this view honestly. What would count against it? Please rank the defeaters by severity: empirical findings in visual cognition, formal limitations in analogical-mapping architectures, and conceptual tensions—especially regarding letter recognition in degraded, three-dimensional, or cross-script contexts. Which of these would defenders of the relational-topology view themselves concede as genuinely damaging rather than dismissible, and which escape routes remain live? I also want an assessment of whether the position has been qualified into unfalsifiability, and if so, whether that insulation is a problem for a claim of this kind.
