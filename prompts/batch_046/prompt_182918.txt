I am drafting a historical novel that shuttles between the ledgers of a collapsed provincial administration and the private writings of a single midwife who lived through its dissolution, and I wish to feel that same scalar tension in a room of people rather than only on the page. Please design a runnable, ninety-minute seminar activity for fourteen postgraduate students in historiography—no lecture up front—that makes them experience the force of microhistory against the history of elites and states before either framework is named.

Divide the participants into asymmetric roles with concrete rules and information constraints, arranged around a fictional winter crisis in 1741 in the province of Alvernia, so that one faction works solely from macro-state fragments—tax aggregates, a governor’s circular, census tables—while the other works solely from micro-local fragments—a parish burial register, one midwife’s account book, a single coroner’s inquest. Give them an identical question to answer—why the mortality spike occurred and whether the state’s grain requisition was the decisive cause—and require them to reach a provisional verdict in writing before any exchange of evidence is permitted. Then introduce a structured deliberation in which the macro groups may pose only yes-or-no questions to the micro groups, and vice versa, with a scoring scheme that rewards coherence within each scale but penalises unacknowledged borrowing across scales. The rules should make a specific conceptual hazard—call it the resolution fallacy—actively tempting: the mistaken belief that finer-grained evidence necessarily corrects the macro-causal picture, rather than transforming the very object of explanation. Participants should feel its pull in their own arguments.

Specify the room layout, the timeline minute by minute, the materials needed, and the small stakes—perhaps a collective vote on which explanation will be entered into a mock provincial chronicle, with individual roles bearing symbolic rather than monetary costs. The debrief must map each phase of the activity onto the corresponding historiographical problem—representativeness versus depth, structural causation versus agentive narrative, the state’s visibility versus its invisibility in local records—predict precisely what will go wrong the first time it is run, and be frank about which feature of genuine microhistory, such as the irreducible thickness of subjective experience or the problem of generalising from the singular case, this simulation cannot reproduce and must therefore be supplied by the facilitator.

Take a committed position: choose one definite constraint on information exchange and one definite role structure, defend those choices with explicit reasons rather than listing options, and tell me what the students should come away seeing about the relationship between history from below and elite-centred narrative. Write in British English. Do not provide a bibliography, a reading list, or citations; I want the design to stand on its own logic.
