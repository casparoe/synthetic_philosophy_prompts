I've been retired from field ecology for about three years and lately I've gotten wierdly into moral philosophy, mostly arguing with my old grad students over beer about whether there are actually real moral dilemmas or if every conflict just has a hidden right answer that we're too lazy to find. I keep thinking about those degenerate energy states in physics, you know, where two totally different setups have the exact same energy level and you can't reduce one to the other? Maybe some moral conflicts are genuinely like that - degenerate in the values, with no deeper principle that breaks the tie.

So here are my gut verdicts, and I can't make them line up. Case one: an ecologist managing a wildfire has to choose between letting it burn to regenerate an old-growth system (killing hundreds of deer and small mammals) or suppressing it to save the animals but destroy the regeneration cycle. I say that's a real dilemma - both obligations stay live and they don't cancel each other out. Case two: a lab director has preliminary climate data that could influence an immediate policy vote but hasn't finished peer review; she can publish now and risk being wrong, or wait and miss the window. I say that's NOT a real dilemma - there's just a correct procedure, and urgency doesn't turn a practical problem into a moral contradiction. Case three: that same director has to miss her kid's emergency surgery to present verified results at a summit that might actually shift carbon policy. Here I want to say it's a real dilemma, but I can't see why family involvement changes the structure except that I feel it in my gut, which makes me suspicious. Case four: a doctor with one ventilator, her own child versus a stranger with a slightly better prognosis. I feel like that's a dilemma too, but I also think using social-value triage is wrong, so I'm definatly contradicting myself. Case five: a farmer can spray a neonicotinoid that saves a crop feeding a village but wipes out local pollinaters, or let the crop fail and preserve the bees. I keep flipping on this one - sometimes it feels like a true value conflict, sometimes like I'm just bad at weighing outcomes and there must be a correct answer I'm missing.

So is there a principle that captures which ones are genuinely degenerate and which are just hard calculations with one right answer? Maybe something about incommensurability of the norms involved? Or do I have to drop some of these verdicts to keep things coherent? If your principle saves case one and case three but kills case four, I'm ok with that, but tell me exactly which clause does what and why. Also, what does it predict for a case I haven't listed - a researcher who finds her own old papers used fabricated data, and must choose between retracting (ruining junior co-authors' careers) and staying silent (polluting the literature)? Does your principle call that a real moral dilemma, or just a painful but clear duty?
