A philosophy blog I follow summarized Avicenna’s flying-man argument like this: imagine a person created fully formed in empty space, suspended in air, with eyes covered and limbs separated so that he never feels any bodily sensation. Even in this state, he would immediately affirm his own existence. From this, the blogger concludes that Avicenna proves the soul is an immaterial substance that can exist completely apart from the body, that self-awareness is a non-embodied intellectual intuition requiring no sensory or corporeal basis whatsoever, and that the body is therefore only an accidental instrument that the soul happens to use. The blogger then claims this makes Avicenna an early Cartesian dualist and that modern neuroscience, which links self-awareness to specific brain regions, therefore refutes the argument directly. How fair is this summary? Please go claim by claim: which parts capture Avicenna’s actual reasoning in his psychological writings, which parts import Cartesian categories he would reject, whether his view of the soul-body relation is really one of pure accidental attachment or retains an Aristotelian hylomorphic element, and whether a neuroscience-based objection actually engages with what Avicenna is trying to establish about the immediacy of self-awareness.
