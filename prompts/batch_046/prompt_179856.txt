I'm preparing a lecture on whether a culture can deliberately select which moral emotion governs transgression, and I'm using Hawthorne's The Scarlet Letter as the central case. To frame the problem precisely, I'm working with a distinction I haven't seen fully developed in the standard literature: "autarchic guilt," in which the self is simultaneously agent, tribunal, and witness of judgment, requiring no external audience; and "theatrical shame," in which moral failure is constituted by exposure to a collective gaze and the offender's identity is reconstituted by the spectators' interpretation. The guiding question is whether Hawthorne depicts Puritan Boston as a society that has collectively engineered a shift from autarchic guilt to theatrical shame—through institutions like the public scaffold and the embroidered letter that turns private transgression into visible spectacle—and whether Hester Prynne's psychological arc demonstrates that such engineering is stable or ultimately self-defeating.

Specifically: does Hester's private reinterpretation of the letter—transforming it from a mandated badge of disgrace into an artistic, almost defiant symbol—represent a reactivation of autarchic guilt, an internal tribunal reclaiming moral authority from the crowd? Or is her reinterpretation still performed for an imagined audience, which would mean she remains trapped within theatrical shame despite her isolation? Furthermore, does her eventual voluntary return to Boston, where she resumes wearing the letter without being compelled, indicate that the culture's choice of shame has become irreversibly internalized, or does it reveal the limits of top-down cultural selection in shaping moral psychology?

Please read the novel's major scenes—the scaffold, the embroidery, Hester's relationship to Dimmesdale's hidden guilt, and her final return—through this lens. Say where the framework illuminates Hawthorne's symbolism and where the novel resists it, perhaps by showing that Hester's state is irreducible to either category. I want to know what Hawthorne adds to the philosophical debate about whether guilt or shame is the better moral emotion, and whether a culture can genuinely choose between them, that conceptual argument alone cannot establish.
