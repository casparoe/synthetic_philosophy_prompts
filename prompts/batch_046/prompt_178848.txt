After a protracted discussion with a friend about whether practical rationality can survive imperfect recall, I am trying to isolate the precise mistake in my argument concerning the absent-minded driver problem. I had claimed that the paradox dissolves once we recognise it as a variant of the Sleeping Beauty problem: the driver faces self-locating uncertainty about which exit he occupies, so he ought to apply a half-er weighting—treating the first and second nodes as equiprobable belief states—and then maximise expected utility relative to those weights, which, I argued, yields a consistent recommendation to continue at both nodes. My friend objected that this illegitimately imports an epistemic framework into a practical context where causal feedback matters: taking the exit at the first node precludes reaching the second, so the decision nodes are not symmetric belief states but sequential actions with irreversible consequences, and evidential weighting therefore misdescribes the choice situation. I want to examine whether her objection holds. Specifically, does the absent-minded driver problem genuinely share the logical structure of a self-locating belief puzzle, or does the presence of causal dependency between nodes mean that ex ante plan selection—rather than node-by-node credence weighting—is the only appropriate framework? And if one rejects diachronic coherence in favour of an ex ante evaluation of constant plans, does this commit one to resolving Newcomb’s problem in a particular way, and should that be seen as a theoretical cost? Please indicate what argument or result would cause you to abandon the view that these puzzles are structurally analogous and that a unified evidential treatment applies, and be explicit about where your reasoning is most vulnerable—particularly regarding the distinction between updating beliefs about one’s location and selecting actions under imperfect recall. I should emphasise that I am not seeking a bibliography, a reading list, or appeals to authority, but a direct analysis of the conceptual confusion.
