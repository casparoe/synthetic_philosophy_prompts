I'm taking a course on decision theory and find bounded rationality super confusng, especially when people try to extend it past practical choice. My study partner came up with this argument and now we're stuck. (1) Satisficing is rational only when the cost of searching for a better option exceeds the expected gain. (2) Forming a belief is just a search process — you're scanning evidence for the view with the best fit. (3) So if satisficing makes sense for jobs or apartments, it should also apply to beliefs: you stop investigating once your current view hits some internal "good enough" threshold. (4) But once you've crossed that threshold, reopening the search is irrational by the very cost-benefit logic that justified stopping in the first place, since you'd be paying search costs for no guaranteed improvement. (5) That means a rational agent should become permanently dogmatic about anything they've already settled, because any new evidence that might change their mind is just a reason to reopen an irrational search. (6) Obviously people do rationally update their beliefs, so something's wrong. (7) Therefore either premise (1) fails for belief, or satisficing doesn't extend to epistemic norms at all.

I think the slip is somewhere between practical choice and belief — maybe beliefs aim at truth, not utility, so the cost-benefit framing doesn't carry over — but I can't tell if that's enough to block (4) or if I'm just special-pleading. Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost for the broader satisficing framework? If I have to keep something, I'd rather save (1) and drop the belief-extension, but I want to know the price tag. Please answer in American English. Also, be explicit about what would change your mind: is there a clear case where belief revision is genuinely irrational due to search costs, or does the analogy break down somewhere specific like the absence of a voluntary "stop" mechanism in perception? I'm especially suspicious of step (4) but can't defend that intuition well.
