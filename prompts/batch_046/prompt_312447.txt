I was discussing welfare aggregation with a friend who works in health economics after a long thread on lexical priorities in suffering-focused ethics, and I realized I want to run a classroom simulation rather than just debate the point. I'm facilitating a philosophy workshop for about 14 graduate students—one 90-minute session—and I want them to feel the structural failure of cardinal aggregation between pleasure and pain before anyone names lexical negative utilitarianism. The niche mechanism I want to inject is a masked-profile resource game: each student receives a quantitative scorecard describing an intervention's net welfare effect on a -50 to +70 scale, but hidden behind each number is a qualitative experience profile—e.g., the -20 entry describes sustained severe chronic pain, while the +60 entry describes profound aesthetic rapture. In the first phase they must design and sign a binding micro-pledge (a real, small charitable commitment that activates if their aggregation rule is adopted) using only the numbers, working in pairs with explicit payoff rules: if both partners agree on a distribution rule, they pool their pledges; if they deadlock, both forfeit half. Only in the second phase are the profiles revealed, and they must defend whether the positive scores can cancel the negative ones under their own rule. I need full rules, materials, the deliberation protocol, and a debrief that maps the moment of collapse or refusal onto the formal claim that pleasure and pain may be incommensurable. Please also tell me what will predictably go wrong—likely that students will smuggle qualitative judgments back into the metric, or that lexical-threshold defenders will reject the framing as a category error rather than adjusting weights—and be frank about what this simulation cannot reproduce, namely the first-person phenomenology of severe suffering, which the facilitator must supply. They should leave seeing that aggregation breaks structurally, not just quantitatively, and they should never hear the term lexical negative utilitarianism until the final five minutes.
