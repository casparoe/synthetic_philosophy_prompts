I was chatting with my colleagues over lunch about Whitehead's rhythm of education — romance, precision, generalisation — and realised we've been using it in our maritime apprenticeship scheme without actually knowing what it means. I'm the curriculum coordinator now, six-week blocks, about forty apprentices per cohort, high stakes, and I have to signn off on every modul proposal before the funding board sees it. We need a reusable decision proceedure — something my team can run in about twenty minutes, no philosophy degree required — that takes a proposed course and tells us whether it belongs in the romance phase, the precision phase, or the generalisation phase for the current cohort, or whether it's out of sequence and should be binned.

The checks should come in a fixed order, with clear stop-points: a firm 'no' when a module skips a rhythm or collapses two phases together, and an explicit hand-back to me when the issue isn't sequencing but values — like whether these particular apprentices ought to be pushed toward abstraction at all. You should build in one niche conceptual twist: treat 'romance' not as excitement but as productive disorientation, meaning the apprentice can clearly name a problem but cannot yet solve it with tools they already possess; and treat 'generalisation' not as abstract theory but as transfer under constraint, meaning applying a precision-learned skill to a novel situation with incomplete information. Keep precision and generalisation seperate in the procedure, because in compressed six-week courses they bleed together constantly and the board keeps approving modules that do both badly.

Apply the proceedure to three proposals: (a) a navigation-chart module that teaches dead-reckoning through repeated drill and correction; (b) a 'leadership and storytelling at sea' module mixing historical anecdotes with ethical reflection, proposed for week two; and (c) a damage-control simulation that requires apprentices to adapt their technical skills to a scenario they have never seen, proposed for week five. One verdict should be one the funding board will hate — almost certainly the storytelling module — and you should say exactly why the procedure rejects it, what restructuring would fix it, and whether the rejection is a feature or a bug. Finish by being honest about where the proceedure genuinely can't do the job: which kinds of modules it will systematically misclassify, why no checklist can determine whether a cohort is actually ready to shift rhythms, and which judgment calls must stay with a person rather than being dressed up as technical steps.
