I'm working through my framework for how strategic reasoning should relate to historical study, and I want a consistency audit before I develop it into a longer essay. Here are the commitments I've settled on:

(1) Analogical reasoning is cognitively unavoidable. Any attempt to forecast or decide in genuinely novel strategic situations necessarily imports structural templates from prior cases; the live question is never whether to analogize, but which analogies survive critical scrutiny.

(2) The Munich (appeasement) and Vietnam (quagmire) analogies have become deprecated heuristics in my view. They function as rhetorical Schelling points that collapse complex strategic landscapes into binary emotional frames, and responsible decision-making should treat them as deprecated rather than live predictive models.

(3) The Thucydides-trap framing for rising-power dynamics is empirically false for nuclear-armed states—the cost structure of direct great-power conflict is so different that the ancient Greek base rate is inapplicable—yet I still preserve the framing as conceptually useful, specifically as a warning about commitment escalation and misperception spirals.

(4) The correct replacement for narrative analogy is reference-class forecasting: instead of asking whether a crisis is "like Munich," we should identify the reference class of past crises that share the relevant causal and institutional features and compute base rates, even though this is rarely done in practice.

(5) Historical study remains valuable for decision-makers, but not because it yields predictive analogies. Its value lies in building counterfactual robustness—the disciplined ability to distinguish portable causal mechanisms from context-bound institutional artifacts.

(6) I take these five positions to form a coherent package: we reject bad analogies, retain one conceptual warning, substitute base-rate reasoning, and ground the enterprise in causal training rather than prediction.

Go through these commitments and audit them: which pairs are in genuine tension, which only appear to conflict, and for each real tension rank its severity and identify the least costly revision that would restore consistency. Check especially whether the move in (4) and (5) actually eliminates analogical reasoning or merely renames it—since selecting "relevant" reference-class features and distinguishing "portable" mechanisms both seem to require the same structural mapping I'm criticizing in (2). Also check whether preserving the Thucydides framing in (3) while deprecating Munich and Vietnam in (2) is principled or ad hoc. If the request itself rests on a confused premise—for instance, if there is no stable distinction between a deprecated heuristic and a preserved conceptual warning, or if counterfactual robustness collapses into sophisticated analogizing—say so plainly rather than politely accommodating the contradiction. I don't need citations, bibliographies, or a lecture on historiography; I want to know whether my own commitments hang together.
