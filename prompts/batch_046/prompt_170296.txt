A philosophy of language seminar handout summarizes the debate between epistemicism and supervaluationism about vagueness as follows. Epistemicists hold that every vague predicate has a sharp boundary; in a borderline case of baldness, the person is determinately bald or determinately not bald, and our ignorance is merely epistemic. Supervaluationists deny this, claiming that vagueness is semantic underdetermination: there is no single correct extension for "bald," but rather many admissible precisifications. A sentence is "supertrue" only if true on all precisifications, and false only if false on all; otherwise it lacks a truth value. The handout then claims that supervaluationism's chief advantage is preserving classical logic without positing mysterious unknown sharp boundaries, whereas epistemicism must treat vagueness as a defect in our cognitive access rather than in language itself. It concludes that both views agree vagueness is ultimately a linguistic defect, but disagree on whether the defect is epistemic or semantic. It also notes that epistemicism neatly avoids the problem of higher-order vagueness, since if the boundary is sharp there is no further indeterminacy about where the indeterminacy lies, while supervaluationism must face an infinite hierarchy of admissible precisifications.

This summary sounds authoritative, but several of its claims strike me as suspiciously neat. Is it a fair characterization? Please go through the summary point by point and tell me: (1) whether the epistemicist's claim is correctly described as mere psychological ignorance, or whether something stronger—such as in-principle unknowability—is essential; (2) whether supervaluationism really preserves classical logic in the straightforward way the handout suggests, given its treatment of bivalence for borderline cases; (3) whether it is accurate to say that both views treat vagueness as a "defect" of language, or whether one or both see it as a feature or as an epistemic limitation without linguistic failure; (4) whether the handout's remark about higher-order vagueness correctly assigns the advantage to epistemicism; and (5) whether there are any other subtle conflations—such as between lacking a unique truth value and lacking any truth value at all, or between semantic underdetermination and speaker indifference—that a defender of either view would object to. Separate what is broadly correct from what is a standard simplification and from what is genuinely misleading.
