I run a philosophy seminar with 14 graduate students for one 90-minute session. Design a structured classroom game that makes participants feel the specific force of Moore's paradox—not as a logical contradiction but as a breakdown in the very authority that makes assertion possible—before anyone names the paradox. Inject the framing idea of assertoric transparency: the principle that in first-person present-tense contexts, to assert P is implicitly to guarantee belief in P, not as an added clause but as part of the speech act's own conditions.

The deliverable should include explicit rules, assigned roles (speaker, auditor, referee), a minute-by-minute timeline, and a payoff or privilege scheme with no real money. Arrange the incentives so that participants must evaluate testimony for both content-accuracy and speaker-belief, and discover for themselves that issuing or certifying a Moorean statement—asserting that P holds while simultaneously disclaiming belief that P holds—breaks the evaluation protocol in a way that mere falsehood or third-person reporting does not. Include a debrief that maps each stage onto parts of the paradox (logical consistency versus pragmatic absurdity versus transparency failure), predicts what will predictably go wrong the first time (for example, participants treating belief-disclaiming as simple lying or as detached observation), and states frankly which feature—the first-person present-tense phenomenology of avowal—the game cannot reproduce and the facilitator must supply directly.

Lead with the bottom line—the core design principle and the precise feeling participants should have— in the first sentence, then present the rules, roles, timeline, stakes, debrief, failure predictions, and honest limitation, with no preamble and no restating of the prompt.
