I am drafting a contribution for an interdisciplinary workshop on communicative ethics, and before I proceed I should like a rigorous audit of my present commitments. I have been thinking about Dutch directness and Japanese indirectness, about whether white lies are ever permissible, and about how to frame these issues without collapsing into mere cultural relativism. Here, then, is my current position, which I present not as a settled doctrine but as a cluster of claims whose joint tenability I doubt.

First, I am a moral realist about communicative ethics: I take politeness and directness to possess objective moral properties that are not reducible to local convention or preference.

Second, I hold that white lies are pro tanto wrong, and indeed wrong in all but genuinely catastrophic circumstances, because they treat the interlocutor’s rational agency as dispensable for the sake of social comfort.

Third, I regard Dutch directness as generally more virtuous than Japanese indirectness, on the ground that directness respects the listener by refusing to manipulate her epistemic environment—delivering the propositional content without strategic ambiguity or emotional cushioning.

Fourth, I draw a sharp distinction between semantic directness and pragmatic directness. An utterance may be semantically indirect—stating, for example, "It is rather late" in order to prompt departure—while remaining pragmatically transparent, inasmuch as the listener can recover the intended illocutionary force without cognitive distortion. I maintain that such pragmatic indirectness does not violate the duty of honesty, since no false proposition is asserted and no deceptive inference is required.

Fifth, I endorse a virtue of epistemic modesty in discourse: one ought not to presume that one’s own assessment of a situation is authoritative enough to warrant its blunt delivery, particularly where asymmetries of status or knowledge obtain.

Sixth, I claim that the moral wrongness of white lies is culturally invariant, whereas the threshold that distinguishes permissible directness from offensive bluntness is fixed by local conversational norms.

Please examine this set for internal coherence. Which pairs are in genuine tension, and with what severity? In particular, does my preference for Dutch directness survive the distinction I draw in the fourth claim, or does pragmatic transparency undermine the charge that indirectness manipulates the listener’s epistemic state? Does my commitment to epistemic modesty conflict with my claim that directness is generally more virtuous, and if so, what is the cheapest revision—restricting the scope of directness, weakening the prohibition on white lies, or qualifying the virtue of modesty? Are there conflicts that appear serious but dissolve once one distinguishes moral realism about communicative ethics from the cultural variability of normative thresholds? Order the real tensions by severity, specify for each what must give and at what cost, and conclude by identifying the single revision that would restore the greatest coherence with the least damage to the overall framework. I should prefer a precise, formal audit to a general lecture, and I ask that your response be written in British English.
