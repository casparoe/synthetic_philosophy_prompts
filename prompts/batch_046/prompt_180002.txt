I'm delivering the opening remarks at a two-day workshop titled "Stoicism and Its Uses," convened jointly by our classics department and our clinical psychology program. The room is genuinely split: half are scholars of Hellenistic philosophy who know the Enchiridion intimately, and half are therapists and counselors who have begun integrating Stoic-inspired techniques into cognitive-behavioral practice. I want to avoid the usual self-help platitude about focusing only on what lies within one's power, since both audiences have heard it flattened into a slogan and neither is served by repetition. Instead, I want the remarks to turn on a precise structural tension in the modern revival: the classical dichotomy of control was inseparable from the Stoic reserve clause—the mental reservation, often called hypexairesis—whereby even one's own virtuous actions are pursued with an implicit qualification that external outcomes may still be thwarted by fate. In the ancient framework this clause was coherent because the cosmos itself was held to be rational and providential; the contemporary therapeutic and popular revival largely strips away that metaphysics, leaving the reserve clause either invisible or conceptually orphaned. The philosophical point should do real structural work in what I can and cannot say: I must not offer a neat synthesis that papers over the absence of providence, I must honor the therapists by acknowledging that clinical efficacy may not depend on cosmological belief, and I must honor the classicists by showing exactly what is lost when the dichotomy is reduced to emotional regulation. Please write about ten minutes of spoken remarks, suitable for delivery without notes, that lets this missing reserve clause shape the arc of the speech and closes with a genuine framing question for the workshop—perhaps whether a technique can survive the death of the cosmology that originally made it coherent—rather than with a resolution.
