In my typography readings, people often compare recognizing every variant of the letter 'a' to recognizing chairs: just as there is an abstract chairness that lets us identify chairs regardless of material or design, there is supposedly an essential a-ness that survives differences in serif, weight, and script. The analogy is used to claim that reading is essentially the extraction of an invariant mental form rather than pattern-matching to familiar shapes. I want to know precisely how far this holds. Could you lay out the correspondence table—what plays the role of function, substrate, designer intent, and prior exposure? Then show where it breaks: do letters have a purpose independent of communication the way chairs have sitting? Is there a single correct prototype, or only family resemblances? Does the analogy quietly import a false Platonic essentialism that ignores how surrounding letters and context reshape what we actually see? And if the chair comparison collapses, would a different analogy—say, to spoken accents or musical notation—better capture how we read letterforms without pretending there is one hidden archetype behind them all?
