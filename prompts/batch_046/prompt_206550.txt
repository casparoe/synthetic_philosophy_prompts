You serve as the standing ethics adviser to a university research council that revises its staff conduct guidance every quarter. The board meets for ninety minutes and must rule, item by item, on whether to list specific everyday behaviours—embedded in large-scale collective harms but individually causally negligible—as "discouraged" in the guidance. The behaviours fall into three domains: air travel for academic purposes (climate), procurement of beef for institutional catering (animal agriculture), and staff voting in large-scale democratic elections where margins are vast. For each item you have roughly fifteen minutes; you have no access to live data or statistical modelling, your recommendation must be defensible in a two-paragraph written rationale, and the board—not you—signs off. Staff objections invariably invoke the inefficacy objection: one flight, one steak, or one ballot makes no discernible difference.

Translate the philosophical treatment of individual contribution to collective harm—particularly the inefficacy objection and the distinctions between token-causal impotence, practice-constitutive participation, and normative signalling—into a reusable decision procedure the board can apply repeatedly without requiring philosophical training. Your procedure should take the form of an ordered set of tests or filters (presented as a flowchart in words), with explicit decision nodes, a clear stopping rule, and marked "judgement call" points where the procedure cannot eliminate discretion.

The procedure must handle hard cases, including: actions near a collective tipping point; actions that are symbolically central to a harmful practice despite being replaceable; actions where the available substitute displaces rather than eliminates harm; and actions protected by other moral claims (for example, democratic duty). Apply the procedure to three worked cases: (i) a staff member booking one domestic flight to present a paper; (ii) the council procuring beef for a single departmental dinner for forty people; and (iii) staff voting in a safe-seat national election. Include at least one case where the procedure yields a "discourage" recommendation that conflicts with common intuition—state the conflict explicitly and show how the rationale is constructed. Conclude by stating precisely what the procedure cannot do: which categories of decision it refuses to settle, which empirical inputs it assumes but cannot verify, and where it is likely to misfire if applied mechanically.

Write your response in British English, with no bibliography, citation, or reading list, and without relying on named theoretical frameworks that a well-read but non-specialist model could not reconstruct from general knowledge.
