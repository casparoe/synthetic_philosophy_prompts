My colleague in metaphysics, Dr. Elena Vasquez, has offered firm verdicts across a range of identity and persistence cases involving artifacts. On the classic Ship of Theseus—planks replaced gradually in situ—she holds that the ship persists and is identical to the original; material continuity is not required. She reaches the same verdict for the overnight version, in which every plank is replaced simultaneously in dry dock before relaunch: the brief functional interruption does not break identity, provided the organizational form and institutional recognition remain intact. On the warehouse reconstruction—original planks stored and reassembled elsewhere after the in situ vessel has been fully replaced—she judges that the continuously operating vessel is the original and the warehouse assembly a mere replica, however perfect. She rejects the blueprint-rebuild case (original destroyed, new one constructed elsewhere from the design) as not identical, and she also rejects atomic-scale teleportation (scanned and rebuilt atom-for-atom elsewhere with the original destroyed) on the grounds that the causal chain is derivation rather than continuation. Notably, she allows that replacement planks carved from the timber of other destroyed ships do not affect identity: provenance of material is irrelevant.

Next week we are discussing a constructed case for the seminar. The continuously operating ship—now composed entirely of replacement planks—is destroyed in a storm. The original planks, which had been preserved in storage and gradually assembled according to the original design in an adjacent dry dock, are now launched; there is no competing vessel in operation. Would Vasquez say this launched vessel is the original Ship of Theseus?
