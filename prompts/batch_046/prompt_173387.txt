Consider the following argument about Davidson's radical interpretation, which seems to prove more than it should. (1) Radical interpretation fixes belief and meaning simultaneously through the principle of charity: to understand a speaker, an interpreter must attribute mostly true beliefs and rational connections to her utterances. (2) Imagine encountering a community that systematically asserts that heavy objects fall upward and organizes its practices around this claim. (3) By charity, the interpreter should avoid attributing widespread false belief; instead she should reinterpret the community's vocabulary—perhaps "fall" means "rise," or "heavy" tracks a different property—so their utterances come out true. (4) But if such reinterpretation is always available, then no interpreter can ever genuinely recognize that another group holds false beliefs about the world; every apparent disagreement dissolves into semantic divergence. (5) Davidson, however, treats radical interpretation as a framework that should make disagreement and error intelligible, not eliminate them, and he explicitly rejects conceptual relativism. (6) Therefore applying charity consistently collapses radical interpretation into a relativism in which no one can be substantively wrong, undermining Davidson's own commitments. Where does the argument fail? Identify the weakest step, explain why it seemed plausible, and assess the cost of rejecting it. In particular, discuss whether charity operates globally or only locally, whether triangulation against a shared physical environment prevents wholesale reinterpretation, and whether Davidson can permit massive peripheral error while preserving core agreement. Reach a clear verdict: does the argument reveal a genuine tension in Davidson's view, or does it rest on a misreading of charity's scope? Answer in American English.
