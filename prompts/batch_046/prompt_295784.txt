As a novelist, I am interested in the narrative architecture of meaning—how a life’s coherence can depend less on personal survival than on an implicit contract with the future. Write a short story that dramatizes Scheffler’s afterlife conjecture: the claim that human extinction is catastrophic not chiefly because of the deaths it involves, but because it severs the future generations whose continued existence lends significance to many present endeavors. Avoid conventional apocalyptic imagery. Instead, invent a niche, concrete intergenerational project—perhaps a lexicographer compiling a dictionary for a language with no remaining native speakers, or a horologist constructing a mechanism that requires three centuries of uninterrupted maintenance before it can strike a single chime—whose protagonist gradually, through psychologically earned sensory detail, recognizes that the project’s value is contingent on an unexamined assumption that humanity will persist to inherit it. Use formal narrative structure and precise scene-work to translate the philosophical tension into character and action; do not rely on exposition or spectacle. The story should explore what is lost when that implicit audience disappears, not through argument, but through the collapse of purpose in a single, meticulously observed life.
