I am a third-year undergraduate studying Ancient Philosophy and Ethics. During a recent seminar on Plato's moral psychology, two of my peers — Sam and Riley — became locked in a disagreement about Plato's attitude to drunkenness across Laws Book I, the Symposium, and the Phaedrus. I was present only as an observer and have no settled view myself. I should be grateful for a careful adjudication.

Sam opened by arguing that Plato is essentially a prohibitionist about genuine intoxication. In Laws I, the Athenian Stranger treats wine as a pharmakon — dangerous in excess and permissible only within a strictly regulated civic framework whose purpose is always the cultivation of enkrateia. Sam insisted that alcohol literally dissolves the rational part of the soul, so any real loss of reason is vicious. The drinking permitted by law is therefore only a simulated stress-test, never an endorsement of actual drunkenness.

Riley replied that this flattens Plato's psychology. She noted that in the Symposium and Phaedrus, divine madness (mania) is praised as a higher state, and that in Laws I wine is said to soften the soul and reveal its true nature. For Riley, this is not mere containment but an epistemic and pedagogical tool. She pushed further, proposing that Plato's view anticipates a frame-dependent ethics of intoxication: the moral and cognitive status of drinking depends on the institutional setting — the supervised symposion versus private excess — rather than on the biochemical state alone.

Sam countered that Riley conflates chemical intoxication with genuinely divine mania. In Phaedrus, prophetic and erotic madness are direct gifts from the gods, not pharmacological effects. To treat alcohol as a causal vehicle for divine influence imports modern biochemistry into a text that distinguishes bodily causes from divine origins. The elaborate legal framework in Laws II, Sam added, demonstrates that Plato regards wine primarily as a threat requiring state control, not as a medium for spiritual insight.

Riley responded that Plato lacks our modern dualism between natural and supernatural causation. A physical substance may serve as the instrument through which divine influence operates; the mechanism differs, but the compatibility is not anachronistic. She also observed that if Plato sought pure prohibition, he would not require such a detailed regulatory apparatus — he would simply ban wine, as he excludes certain corrupting musical modes. The complexity of the law suggests that Plato judges the risk worth the gain in self-knowledge.

Neither persuaded the other. Please analyse their exchange and deliver a point-by-point verdict.
