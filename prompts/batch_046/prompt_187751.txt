I'm defending the following thesis in a philosophy department colloquium attended by metaethicists, comparative philosophers, and cognitive anthropologists familiar with the WEIRD critique: cross-cultural convergence on harm and fairness norms does not support a universal moral grammar, but instead reflects independent institutional outsourcing—societies that developed state-level legal codes converged on harm-based prohibitions because they solve large-scale coordination problems, not because they track objective moral facts. Deep moral intuitions (purity, loyalty, authority) vary widely across populations, while the apparent universality of harm and fairness is an artifact of bureaucratic scaffolding that offloads moral cognition onto procedural systems. From this I conclude that moral objectivity, if defensible at all, must be restricted to thin procedural coordination norms rather than substantive moral truths.

The audience is largely sympathetic to moral realism but skeptical of strong relativism; they care about whether cognitive science produces an explanatory debunking that slips into a justificatory one, and they will press on whether my model collapses into functionalism, eliminates first-person moral authority, or fails to account for pre-institutional harm judgments in small-scale societies.

Prepare me for the objections they will actually raise.
