I am preparing a graduate seminar on the limits of expected-utility theory, and I want to present the Pasadena game without misrepresenting its structure. In this gamble, a fair coin is tossed until heads appears; the payoff grows exponentially with the number of tosses, but is arranged so that the positive and negative contributions both diverge, yielding an expectation that is undefined—not infinite, but genuinely absent because the series lacks absolute convergence. One student argued that this is essentially a division-by-zero error: a malformed computation that can be repaired by adopting a symmetric averaging rule over partial expectations, thereby recovering a definite value that should govern rational choice. I think this smuggles in an arbitrary ordering and mistakes mathematical silence for a correctable mistake, but I want to be precise: does the Pasadena paradox show that rational preference can be genuinely indeterminate when the underlying measure is not absolutely convergent, or is the undefined expectation merely an artifact that a well-chosen summation convention can legitimately dissolve? And if it is genuinely indeterminate, should we conclude that the agent has no reason to play, or is that inference itself an unwarranted step from the absence of a mean?
