After a long, circular argument with a friend about Aristotle’s sea-battle scenario and whether tomorrow’s naval clash is already fixed as true or false, I want a joint adversarial-collaboration document drafted between two camps. Camp one is the open-future non-bivalents: they hold that some future-contingent propositions currently lack a truth-value because the future is metaphysically unsettled, and that bivalence fails for statements indexed to genuinely open times. Camp two is the thin-red-line bivalents: they hold that every future-tensed proposition is already determinately true or false along the single actual timeline, but that contingency is preserved modally—through counterfactual sensitivity and branching nearby worlds—rather than through any failure of bivalence.

The document should have these sections in this order:

A. Joint Agreement (about 200 words): numbered claims both sides can sign—covering the formal structure of the sea-battle case, the distinction between causal determinism and logical fatalism, what fatalism would actually require (inevitability regardless of present causal structure), and the temporal-logic principles both accept (e.g., that past-tensed propositions have settled truth-values). Keep wording neutral enough that neither camp feels it concedes its core view.

B. Residual Disagreements (table format, about 250 words): state precisely, in wording each camp would accept for its own position, where they part ways—specifically on whether present truth-makers are required for future-tensed statements, whether bivalence applies across all temporal indices, whether “it is now true that there will be a battle” entails present inevitability, and whether contingency is an ontological feature of time or only a modal feature of worlds. Then include a brief, explicit judgment: is any part of this dispute verbal rather than substantive, and if so, which formulation dissolves it?

C. Cruxes and Predictive Tests (about 300 words): identify the sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one side—e.g., whether an ontology of branching worlds can preserve contingency without multiplying actualities; whether epistemic asymmetry between past and future requires an ontological asymmetry; and whether a completed deterministic physics still leaves logical space for non-bivalence. For each crux, specify a concrete case, argument, or conceptual result both sides agree bears on it, and record in advance what each camp predicts about that case or result.

D. Reasoning Audit (about 150 words): state explicitly what argument or empirical finding would change your verdict on which camp is closer to correct, and identify the point in your reasoning most likely to fail—particularly where you might be conflating our epistemic openness about the future with a metaphysical openness in the future itself.

Keep the whole response formal, polished, and under about 900 words. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations—write as if neither of us can look anything up. In section D, be specific about your vulnerabilities rather than offering generic humility.
