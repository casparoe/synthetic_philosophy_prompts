Exam Question (20 points): Analysts of AI development economics distinguish the "competitiveness constraint"—the strategic pressure on individual labs to reduce safety investment in order to avoid losing capability leadership to rivals—from the "alignment tax," the direct resource cost (additional compute for oversight, researcher time for interpretability, deployment delays for red-teaming) incurred by firms that choose to build aligned systems. A recent framing in governance discussions proposes treating the alignment tax not as a uniform public burden but as a positional good: its value to a particular firm depends on rivals bearing it less, since relative capability advantages can overwhelm absolute safety gains. Evaluate whether this positional-good framing actually integrates the two phenomena or collapses them into one, and assess whether socializing the tax through mandatory, collectively funded safety standards would dissolve the competitiveness constraint or merely displace it onto other competitive dimensions.

Student Answer:
The competitiveness constraint is best understood as a multi-actor prisoner's dilemma: each lab prefers that all labs invest in safety, but each individual lab gains a strategic advantage by reducing its own investment, since safety work consumes resources that could go toward scaling capabilities. The alignment tax is the direct price of that investment—extra training runs for value alignment, auditing procedures, and delayed release schedules. If we treat the tax as a positional good, then a firm's benefit from paying it depends on competitors not paying it; being relatively safer offers little strategic value if one is relatively less capable. Under this view, the tax and the constraint are not separate phenomena but two levels of the same strategic structure—individual cost versus collective outcome.

If a regulator socializes the tax by imposing uniform safety standards and funding them collectively, the positional advantage of defection is eliminated, because every firm bears an identical burden. Once the cost is equalized, the prisoner's dilemma that generates the competitiveness constraint dissolves: no lab can improve its relative position by cutting safety spending. The only remaining risk is implementation failure—standards might become performative rather than substantive. Therefore, provided enforcement is credible and uniform, socialization resolves the constraint, though regulators must remain vigilant against hidden defection through accelerated capability timelines that fall outside the scope of the safety budget.
