The scale argument for insect sentience holds that, because insects exist in populations orders of magnitude larger than all vertebrates combined, even a very low per-individual probability of phenomenal consciousness generates an overwhelming expected number of moral patients. If that reasoning is sound, however, it seems to apply with equal force to any vast population of complex non-biological systems—say, the aggregate state-transitions in global computing infrastructure—provided we grant them an arbitrarily small but non-zero base rate of sentience. Defenders typically reply that insects possess evolutionary continuity and behavioral markers that justify the initial probability assignment, but that reply appears to smuggle in a specific functional or neural criterion that the scale argument was meant to render unnecessary. Is the argument therefore incoherent unless it abandons its purely probabilistic form and admits a hidden architectural condition? Or is there a principled way to block the extension to artificial aggregates without undermining the argument's original moral force?
