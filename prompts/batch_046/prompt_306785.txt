You serve as the senior ethics and compliance officer for a regional public-works authority that approves dozens of small-scale infrastructure, procurement, and community-partnership proposals each quarter. Your board has adopted a standing commitment to avoid contributing to collective harms—specifically climate damage, industrial animal suffering, and democratic dysfunction—yet it recognizes the classic inefficacy objection: in large-scale collective problems, any single approved project makes no perceptible difference to the outcome, so conventional causal reasoning seems to license almost anything. You need a reusable decision procedure—not a one-time memo—that your three-person review team can run in under two hours per proposal, using only internal expertise and no external literature searches, to determine whether a given proposal should be approved, rejected, or escalated to the board for a values judgment. The procedure must translate the philosophical distinction between direct causation, contribution to a harmful practice, and symbolic or institutional signaling into ordered questions with explicit sufficiency conditions; it must flag the point at which further factual inquiry stops and normative weighing begins; and it must be honest about cases where the inefficacy objection genuinely applies versus cases where it is misapplied, such as threshold effects, overdetermination scenarios, or cases where the authority's action is part of a coordinated institutional pattern rather than an isolated individual choice.

Construct the procedure as a word flowchart: state the questions in sequence, specify what answer is required to proceed, identify the decision node where the procedure outputs reject, approve, or escalate, and mark the nodes where professional judgment cannot be eliminated. Then apply it to three concrete proposals that will come before you next quarter: (A) a contract to supply beef and dairy for eight hundred employees at municipal facilities, where eliminating the contract would shift only a trivial fraction of regional demand; (B) a road-widening project that will increase vehicle capacity by three percent on one corridor, with a carbon increment that is measurable but dwarfed by regional totals; and (C) a partnership to fund nonpartisan voter-registration drives in low-turnout districts, where the authority's contribution is one of hundreds of similar efforts and a single election outcome is not determinative, but the pattern of institutional engagement may affect democratic norms. Include one application where the procedure yields an answer that conflicts with your board's likely intuitive preference, and explain what the procedure cannot do—specifically, where it fails to resolve disputes about moral threshold, comparative institutional responsibility, or the weight of symbolic action relative to direct harm.
