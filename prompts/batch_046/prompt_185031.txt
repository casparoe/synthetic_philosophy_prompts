A recent podcast offered the following summary of Daniel Dennett's brain-transplant thought experiment, in which he imagines asking where he is once his brain has been surgically removed and sustained in a vat, with his body then operated remotely; he later discovers that the brain in the vat has been copied, so that both the original vat-brain and its duplicate are each controlling separate bodies. The presenter claimed that Dennett takes this to show that personal identity is not tied to any particular physical brain but is instead a purely functional, replicable pattern of information processing. Consequently, the summary asserts, Dennett concludes that asking where he is has no meaningful answer, that moral and legal responsibility ought to be divided equally among all functioning copies of oneself, and that stepping into a teletransporter should inspire no fear of death, since the arriving copy is genuinely continuous with the original self—merely instantiated elsewhere. The presenter further suggested that Dennett regards the self as a useful fiction with no metaphysical weight, comparable to a centre of narrative gravity rather than a real entity. I suspect this account mixes Dennett's actual reasoning with ideas drawn from other philosophers, and that some of its conclusions overreach the argument. Please respond in British English, and analyse which elements of this summary are accurate to Dennett's view, which are subtle misrepresentations or conflations with other positions—particularly regarding duplication, teletransportation, and the metaphysical status of the self—and whether the conclusions about distributed responsibility and the meaninglessness of location actually follow from the premises as presented.
