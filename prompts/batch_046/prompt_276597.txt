After a long and somewhat frustrating argument with a friend about whether any account of meaning in life can avoid collapsing into either sentimental self-deception or cold nihilism, I have settled on a specific view I want to stress-test. I hold that meaning is not a property of lives considered in isolation—whether measured by subjective fulfillment or by objective value—but is instead constituted by a particular structural relation: the sustained, unresolvable tension between an agent's lucid awareness of cosmic insignificance and their continued, wholehearted investment in local, finite projects. On this view, which sits between the classical absurdist tradition and contemporary hybrid theories, meaning does not emerge despite the absurd but is literally the shape of the gap between the recognition that nothing ultimately matters and the refusal to stop caring. Resolve the tension—either by achieving a false transcendence that denies the insignificance, or by succumbing to nihilistic withdrawal—and meaning dissolves; maintain it without resolution, and meaning persists precisely as the activity of holding both poles together.

What would count against this view? I want more than the standard objections: not merely that people find meaning in religious transcendence or in purely subjective flow, since my view predicts those are either misdescribed or structurally unstable. I want to know which intuitions about particular lives, which empirical findings about human psychology, which formal results in value theory, or which conceptual distinctions in recent philosophy of meaning would genuinely damage it. Specifically: could there be a meaningful life that entirely lacks awareness of cosmic scale, or one that achieves a stable, resolved integration without tension? Would a demonstration that human motivation is structurally incapable of sustaining contradictory evaluative frames undermine the view? And is there a risk that this position has been qualified into unfalsifiability—if any apparent counterexample can be redescribed as hidden tension or unrecognized resolution—and if so, is that insulation a philosophical virtue or a vice for a theory of this kind? Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and tell me which ones a committed defender of this tension-based view should concede as genuinely counting against it.
