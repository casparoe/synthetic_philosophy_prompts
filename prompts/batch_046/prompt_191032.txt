I'm on the university's international partnerships review board, and we're deadlocked on a five-year research deal with the Meridian Institute. They run what they call "stewardship governance" — senior scholars hold veto power over joint publications and budget splits — which clashes with our flat peer-review norms. But they control unique field sites and funding streams we can't replicate. Half the board says accepting thier hierarchical veto over our faculty's work is a flat-out betrayal of institutional values; the other half says we're being parochial, that moral frameworks can have "gains from trade" just like economies — they value hierarchical accountability more than we do, we value distributed authority more than they do, so we ought to specialize and exchange.

My co-chair asked for a formal memo recommending approval, rejection, or conditional approval with a concrete "compromise architecture" — maybe designating "sovereign domains" where each institution's norms rule absolutely and "exchange zones" where hybrid procedures apply. She wants the memo to actually wrestle with whether value systems can be traded this way without collapsing into relativism, and to treat seriously the strongest objection: that some commitments like academic autonomy aren't fungible, so treating them as tradeable doesn't compromise them but corrupts them. She also wants it written for people who could vote either way, not just preaching to the choir.

Please draft that memo for circulation before Thursday's meeting. Use American English, give a clear bottom-line recommendation with reasoning both sides have to answer to, and don't just list pros and cons — engage whether inter-institutional moral compromise is genuinely like economic exchange or if there's a category mistake in applying "gains from trade" to value systems at all. No bibliography or citations needed; just a rigorous internal argument. Also, I'm kinda tired of memos that sound like legal briefs, so keep it readable but still serious.
