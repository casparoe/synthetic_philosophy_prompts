You are delivering the closing remarks at a symposium held to mark the retirement of Dr. Elena Vasquez, who directed our university's archive of administrative and testimonial records from a period of state violence for over three decades. The audience is divided: on one side sit historians, archivists, and legal scholars who treat preservation as an ethical imperative; on the other sit survivors, descendants, and community representatives who have formally petitioned to destroy or permanently restrict access to specific perpetrator-record sets, arguing that their continued existence causes ongoing harm. You have ten minutes. Do not resolve the petition. Instead, use the philosophical tension between Avishai Margalit's claim that collective remembrance is a form of solidarity with victims — such that willful forgetting can amount to a second injury — and David Rieff's argument that enforced remembrance can become a form of moral coercion that binds communities to a past they need to transcend, in order to show that both sides are advancing genuine ethical claims rather than merely emotional or political demands. Crucially, your remarks should identify and make explicit a shared premise that both positions often conceal: that memory is itself a mode of care. Then show how this shared premise intensifies rather than resolves the conflict, because it means preservationists are demanding care for the dead at the expense of the living, while advocates for restriction are demanding care for the living at the expense of historical accountability. The speech must work for the specialists in the room, who will recognize both authors, and for the family members present, who will not. Do not provide citations, page references, bibliographies, or verbatim quotations. Let the conceptual architecture determine what you can and cannot say about Dr. Vasquez's legacy, about the petition itself, and about the future of the archive.
