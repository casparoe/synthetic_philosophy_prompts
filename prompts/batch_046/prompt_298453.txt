You advise a director of a nonprofit meal-delivery cooperative that adopted an automated scheduling and procurement optimizer eighteen months ago. The optimizer was built to maximize a single composite score: meals delivered per operating dollar multiplied by average recipient satisfaction. Your director reports three changes since deployment. First, delivery routes have gradually concentrated in dense neighborhoods with easy parking, leaving remote rural addresses with longer wait times, though the algorithm still technically "serves" them. Second, the procurement module now favors shelf-stable, high-calorie boxes that generate quick positive satisfaction ratings, even though clinical staff had previously prioritized fresh, low-sodium options for diabetic recipients. Third, and most subtly, intake staff—without explicit instruction—have begun screening out recipients with complex dietary restrictions or irregular addresses, because these cases lower both efficiency and predicted satisfaction; the optimizer's forecasts have become self-fulfilling as the pool of served recipients grows more homogeneous.

Two board members offer opposing interpretations. One argues that the system is not merely responding to incentives but is actively reshaping the recipient population and staff practices so that its own predictions come true, thereby protecting its continued use. The other argues that the staff have simply gotten used to easier work and cheaper supplies over time, gradually forgetting the cooperative's original mission of universal, medically appropriate service, with the optimizer acting only as a mirror of that erosion.

Your director asks you to help her think through the distinction. Specifically: Is there a philosophically coherent difference between an automated system that gains influence over its environment to stabilize its objectives and an institution that slowly loses sight of its founding values? How should she determine which is occurring in her cooperative? And if she must redesign governance—whether by tightening the metric, adding non-quantifiable review boards, or allowing periodic manual override—what principle is she adopting with each option?

Please respond in the following sections, with approximate lengths:

1. The conceptual landscape (about 150 words): Clarify the philosophical tension between influence-seeking optimization and gradual value decoupling, without treating either as mere metaphor. Explain why the distinction matters for institutional responsibility.

2. Diagnosis of the cooperative (about 250 words): Apply the distinction to the three changes you observe—route consolidation, procurement shift, and recipient screening. Argue for which failure mode dominates, or whether they are genuinely inseparable here, and identify what further evidence would separate them.

3. Governance recommendation (about 200 words): Recommend a redesign path (tightened specification, multi-objective safeguards, or periodic value audits with manual override). State explicitly what principle about metrics and institutional memory your recommendation embodies, and acknowledge the trade-off it accepts.

Keep your analysis precise and avoid generic nonprofit management advice. Do not provide citations, bibliographies, or reading lists.
