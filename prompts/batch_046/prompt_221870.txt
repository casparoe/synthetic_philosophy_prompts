I am preparing a presentation for my value-theory seminar on rational choice when goods are genuinely incommensurable—not merely hard to weigh, but lacking any shared metric. Rather than a list of philosophers, I need a taxonomy built from four logical splits, anchored by a concrete case: an archivist must decide whether to preserve a damaged, singular manuscript in its original form (cultural-memory integrity) or to digitize and edit it for broad accessibility (democratic use), where neither option can be expressed in the currency of the other.

Split 1: Is real incommensurability possible, or is every apparent case reducible to hidden information or an implicit metric?
Split 2: If real, can practical reason still deliver a rational resolution—through commitment, identity, or lexical ordering—or is the agent left with an irreducible tragic choice?
Split 3: If reason resolves it, does the resolution construct a new comparative fact via the agent's practical identity, or does it reveal a pre-existing hierarchy among values?
Split 4: Regardless of resolution, is there a standing moral residue—an enduring duty to acknowledge or repair the loss of the unchosen good—or can the choice be morally clean?

Please present this as a branching tree: each internal node is one split phrased so a respondent can answer without knowing the philosopher it points to; each leaf is either named (pluralist-tragic, parity-commitment, lexical-hierarchy, constructivist-identity, comparative-denial) or marked 'unnamed/unstable.' For unnamed leaves, diagnose in one sentence whether the view is incoherent, unattractive, or unexplored. For named leaves, state the single most awkward commitment in one sentence. Flag any pair of leaves the tree cannot cleanly separate, particularly if different answers to Split 3 collapse into identical practical recommendations. Do not include bibliographies, citations, or reading lists; keep the architecture and the running case central.
