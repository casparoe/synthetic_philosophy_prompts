The debate over Nelson Goodman's formulation of the new riddle of induction—centered on the predicate grue—has persisted for decades without clear resolution, and I am increasingly convinced that we have mischaracterized its structure. On one side, philosophers treat grue as exposing a genuine asymmetry in inductive practice that demands an ontological account of projectibility, often appealing to natural kinds, dispositional properties, or the causal structure of the world to explain why green is projectible and grue is not. On the other side, critics dismiss the riddle as a linguistic or conventional artifact: they argue that grue is defined via a hidden temporal index, making its non-projectibility a matter of syntactic form or semantic stipulation rather than a discovery about nature, and that once we recognize this, the paradox dissolves without metaphysical residue. My working hypothesis is that the disagreement is not primarily about induction at all, but about two deeper issues that rarely get separated: whether induction is fundamentally an epistemic norm governing rational belief-formation or a descriptive feature of human cognitive architecture, and whether projectibility is discovered in nature or imposed by our representational scheme. If that is right, then the two sides may be answering different questions under the single heading of the grue problem—one asking how rational agents ought to generalize, the other asking how human minds actually do, with the ontological and conventionalist readings serving as proxy positions for these underlying commitments. I want a diagnosis rather than a survey: using what each side actually says about the status of predicates, the role of temporal gerrymandering, and the need or lack thereof for a metaphysics of properties, determine whether this is a substantive disagreement about facts, a verbal disagreement about the word induction, or an ill-posed question that conflates normative and descriptive inquiries. Be explicit about whether a hidden empirical question—say, about the psychology of predicate learning or the neuroscience of categorization—is doing the philosophical work in disguise. Finally, tell me what, if anything, would count as genuine progress given your diagnosis: a formal result in the logic of projectibility, a conceptual distinction that cleanly separates the epistemic from the descriptive problem, an empirical finding about concept acquisition, or an argument that we should abandon the framing altogether and why.
