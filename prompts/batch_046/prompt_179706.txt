The standard solution holds that the proposition expressed involves these senses, so the two statements are not identical in thought-content despite being identical in truth-conditions.

But suppose we introduce a hypothetical representational system in which proper names are not merely paired with descriptive senses, but are syntactically indexed by what we might call structural coordinates—formal markers that determine a name's logical position within a framework of relations, without themselves contributing to the semantic content or reference of the name. These coordinates are not hidden descriptions (they carry no descriptive content about Venus), yet they are cognitively accessible: learning that 'a' and 'b' share a coordinate allows a thinker to merge their representational files. If such coordinates exist, then the discovery that Hesperus is Phosphorus might be explained not by grasping that two distinct senses converge on one object, but by recognizing that two syntactically distinct anchors point to the same node in the structural framework.

Please explain the conceptual issue this raises for Frege's original puzzle. Specifically: does the introduction of these non-semantic, syntactic anchors create a genuine third category between sense and reference—one that carries cognitive value and explains informativeness—thereby undermining Frege's binary framework? Or can the Fregean maintain that these coordinates are merely part of the psychological mechanism of grasping sense, leaving the philosophical distinction intact? I want an intuitive but conceptually precise explanation of where the pressure lies, particularly regarding whether identity statements can be informative solely through the restructuring of formal anchors rather than through any change in propositional content.
