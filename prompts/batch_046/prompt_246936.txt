I serve on the Allocation and Display Ethics Committee of the International Memorial Consortium, a body that oversees both preservation funding and the design standards for our shared digital archive of twentieth- and twenty-first-century mass violence. At next week's session we must vote on the proposed Comparative Harm Framework, which its designers present as a middle path between pure narrative display and quantitative comparison. The framework scores documented atrocity events across four weighted dimensions—mortality scale, institutional intent, temporal duration, and intergenerational trauma transmission—while embedding an irreducibility veto that allows any affected community to suppress comparative visualizations of their event, leaving the data present but unranked. Supporters, including our quantitative history working group, argue that this lets researchers study patterns of violence without reducing unique horrors to a single metric; critics on the board, led by a philosopher of history and a survivor-advocate representative, insist that assigning numerical weights to suffering is itself an obscenity, that the veto merely displaces rather than dissolves the moral problem, and that funding allocations should never be influenced—even indirectly—by any calculus of comparative harm.

I need a formal written opinion, suitable for circulation before Thursday's meeting, recommending whether the Committee should approve the framework outright, approve it with binding amendments, or reject it. Your recommendation must be concrete and actionable, supported by reasoning that the proponents and critics could both recognize as fair, and it must include an intellectually honest engagement with the strongest argument on the side you do not take. Please also address specifically whether the distinction the framework draws between permitted comparative analysis and prohibited hierarchical ranking is philosophically stable once the underlying dimensions are quantified, and whether an institutional body like ours possesses any legitimate authority to structure how suffering is compared, even when no explicit ordering is displayed. I was discussing this with a colleague who suggested that the veto makes the framework merely decorative, and I want the memo to take that objection seriously rather than treating it as a rhetorical flourish.
