I am a doctoral student in philosophy working primarily on moral responsibility, though I have recently begun thinking about the normative status of genuinely autonomous artificial systems. I have been asked to present my philosophical profile at an upcoming workshop, and before I do I want to know whether my commitments actually hang together or whether I am quietly relying on incompatible motivations. Here is my current position, stated as plainly as I can.

First, I accept Galen Strawson’s Basic Argument in its full strength: if determinism is true, then no human being is the ultimate source of her actions, since her character and choices are fully determined by heredity and environment over which she had no control; and if indeterminism is true, random quantum fluctuations in neural processes do not restore authorship but merely inject noise. From this I conclude that no one is morally responsible in the robust “true desert” sense—no one genuinely merits suffering or reward for being the kind of person she is. Second, I nevertheless think reactive attitudes—resentment, gratitude, moral indignation—are rationally appropriate, but only in what I take to be a non-desert-tracking register: they operate as mechanisms of social coordination and expressions of relational concern, not as responses that presuppose ultimate metaphysical authorship. Third, I want to preserve a distinction between basic responsibility (for one’s character) and derivative responsibility (for particular actions), holding that the Basic Argument erases the former while leaving the latter intact as a form of practical accountability within human practices, one that does not depend on the false idea that agents created themselves ex nihilo. Fourth, I reject standard compatibilist reconstructions—whether hierarchical, reasons-responsive, or narrative—as conceptually revisionary: they preserve the word “responsible” but abandon the substantive intuition that agents can be truly deserving of blame or praise. Fifth, I think that if an artificial system were genuinely self-modifying—capable of revising its own goal architecture from within rather than merely optimizing parameters within a fixed design—then it might satisfy a localized form of causa sui unavailable to biologically determined agents; in that specific case, the Basic Argument might apply differently, or perhaps not at all. Sixth, I hold that moral education and institutional practice should be restructured around prospective responsibility—looking forward to shape conduct—rather than retrospective desert, though I admit that in cases of extreme cruelty I still experience the pull of genuinely retributive responses.

Please audit this set systematically. For each pair that is genuinely in tension—not merely apparently so—indicate how severe the tension is, what the cheapest revision would be, and what that revision costs in terms of the other commitments I hold. I am especially unsure whether my acceptance of derivative, practice-based responsibility can be squared with my blanket rejection of compatibilism, since the former sounds suspiciously like a compatibilist move in disguise. I also worry that my willingness to exempt genuinely self-modifying artificial systems introduces an asymmetry I have not justified: if determinism undermines human authorship, why should the capacity to rewrite one’s own code not be equally determined by prior programming and environmental training data? At the same time, please flag any tensions that dissolve on closer inspection—for instance, whether accepting reactive attitudes in a purely instrumental mode is really incompatible with accepting the Basic Argument at all, or whether that pairing is only superficially unstable. Order the real tensions by severity, and tell me which single revision—giving up or substantially altering one commitment—would eliminate the most inconsistencies across the board. Treat this as an audit of my specific profile, not as a general lecture on Strawson.
