In climate-policy debates I keep encountering the proverb that we do not inherit the earth from our ancestors but borrow it from our children, most recently deployed to reject long-term discounting by insisting that a borrower must return the principal intact. Before I report on this, I need the sentence disambiguated. Lay out the precise theses it could be expressing—distinguishing, for example, a legal-fiduciary reading (identifiable future beneficiaries with enforceable claims), a non-identity moral reading (no specific individual is harmed by present emissions), a stationary-state ecological reading (natural capital must be preserved in kind), and a purely rhetorical reading—indicating for each whether it is a platitude, a substantive and defensible claim, or false. Then address whether any of these readings actually supports dismissing intergenerational discounting, and note which interpretation the speaker needed for his argument to hold, particularly given that climate damage is largely irreversible on human timescales. Present the options as a menu rather than selecting one, and reach a verdict on whether the proverb, on any defensible reading, does the work it was asked to do.
