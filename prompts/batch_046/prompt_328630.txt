I am preparing an opening piece for my school's philosophy society on whether honouring sunk costs is always irrational. I should like a Petrarchan sonnet that treats the fallacy through the lens of "narrative-identity costs" — the idea that long-held commitments can become constitutive of the self, so that abandoning them is not mere waste but a form of self-erasure. Please respond in American English. The octave should present the rationalist case for cutting losses, using maritime imagery of jettisoned cargo. The volta at line nine should introduce the counter-thought that some investments are not external goods but parts of one's narrative identity. The closing couplet should leave the tension open, asking whether practical rationality must be reconceived when the sunk cost is who one has become. It should be precise enough that a student of practical reason and personal identity recognises the argument, yet work as verse in its own right.
