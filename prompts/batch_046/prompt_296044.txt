Write a deadpan satirical internal memo, approximately 650 words, from the Office of Theological Compliance at a fictional multinational conglomerate named Axiologos Holdings, which has adopted strict divine-command theory as the governing framework for its ESG and ethics auditing. The memo should announce the rollout of a new “Piety Metrics Dashboard” intended to resolve whether corporate actions are morally correct because the board’s patron deities command them, or whether the deities command them because the actions are independently correct. The satire should come from getting the Euthyphro dilemma precisely right: build in the circular dependency between divine approval and moral correctness, the arbitrariness problem if the deities’ preferences are brute, the independence horn’s challenge to omnipotence, and the practical impossibility of auditing “piety” as a measurable KPI without begging the question. Format it as a numbered corporate memo with sections for Executive Summary, The Dashboard Architecture, The Circularity Protocol, Risk Disclosure, and Next Quarter’s Objectives. The target is the institutional practice of operationalizing divine command theory into auditable policy—not a named living person. Maintain a sustained, deadpan bureaucratic voice; the humor should emerge from the logic rather than from winking asides. Take a definite position: the memo’s implicit argument should be that once divine command theory is treated as a non-circular governance framework, it collapses into either arbitrary fiat or concealed moral realism, and the piece should lean clearly toward one of these conclusions rather than balancing them neutrally. After the memo, include a brief author’s note of about 150 words that takes a committed stance on which horn of the dilemma is more institutionally devastating, and that explicitly identifies where the satire takes liberties—such as treating polytheistic patronage as a corporate board structure, exaggerating the quantifiability of piety, or assuming that institutional ethics requires publicly justifiable, non-circular reasoning. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; do not invoke named contemporary scholars or obscure secondary literature. The framing should be unfamiliar enough that a model cannot rely on a memorized template, yet grounded entirely in the standard structure of the Euthyphro problem so that no external search is required to answer.
