I'm in a phil of religion course and we're working through the hiddenness problem, but I tried to formalize the argument myself and now it feels like it eats everything. Here's my chain: (1) If God exists and is perfectly loving, then God wants every rational agent to be in a conscious, reciprocal relationship with God. (2) That kind of relationship requires the agent to beleive God exists with enough confidence to actually trust, not just treat it as an abstract possibility. (3) If God wants that for agent S and can bring it about without overriding S's freedom, God will do it. (4) God can do it—say through non-coercive phenomenal presence, like how I just know my roommate is in the room. (5) So every rational agent should have that kind of direct, confident awareness. (6) But lots of rational people don't; they have reasonable non-belief that occured without resistance. (7) So God either doesn't exist, isn't loving, or can't do it. (8) But apply the exact same reasoning to other minds. I want real friendship with you, yet I don't have direct phenomenal access to your consciousness—only inference and behavior. By (3), if I want friendship and can make my inner life transparent to you, I should. (9) Obviously I can't fully do that, and you don't think that means I don't want friendship. So (3) must be false, or needs a hidden clause about what's metaphysically possible. (10) But if I weaken (3) for humans because of epistemic privacy, why can't God invoke a parallel divine epistemic distance? Then hiddenness stops being a defeater entirely. I feel like I'm either throwing away the hiddenness argument or committing to some weird view that parents who stay anonymous don't love their kids. Which step do I actually reject, and what does it cost? I'd rather keep (1) and (6) if I can, but I need to know what I'm paying.
