As a journalist covering the philosophy and engineering of automated decision-making, I am drafting a long-form feature on whether algorithmic fairness is structurally impossible or merely technically hard. To ground the piece, I need a formal adversarial-collaboration document that two camps—call them the Statistical Incompatibilists and the Dynamic Reconstructivists—could both sign after a good-faith exchange. The Incompatibilists take formal impossibility results (showing that calibration within groups and equalized error rates are jointly unsatisfiable except in trivial cases) to reveal an in-principle tension in predictive justice across static populations. The Reconstructivists hold that these results apply only to single-shot classifiers evaluated on fixed datasets, and that the tension dissolves once fairness is reconceived as a causal, temporal, or compositional property of sociotechnical systems.

Please produce the joint statement in the following form.

First, a numbered section of agreed claims: specify what both sides accept about the formal conditions under which the impossibility holds; about the mathematical scope of the proofs; about the empirical prevalence of accuracy-fairness trade-offs in audited real-world systems; about the limitations of current benchmarking datasets; and about the difference between a model’s predictions and the institutional processes that deploy them.

Second, present the precise residual disagreements in wording each camp would accept for its own position. Structure this as either a table or as clearly separated sections. The disagreements should cover, at minimum: whether the impossibility is ontological or an artifact of metric choice; whether fairness should be evaluated at the level of individual predictions, institutional procedures, or long-run equilibria; and whether the presence of unmeasured confounders together with proxy-variable reconstruction in causal graphs undermines, preserves, or transforms the core results.

Third, identify the cruxes—the sub-questions whose resolution would move at least one side. For each crux, provide a concrete case or synthetic scenario both sides agree is relevant: for example, a multi-stage hiring pipeline with feedback loops between screening and promotion, or a risk-scoring system in which protected-class membership is not observed but reconstructed via geographically correlated proxies within a causal graph containing hidden confounders. For each scenario, state in advance what the Incompatibilists predict will be observed and what the Reconstructivists predict will be observed.

Fourth, include a brief, neutral assessment of whether any portion of the dispute is verbal—particularly around the term fairness itself, or around whether unfairness can be properly attributed to an algorithm when its source lies in upstream social dynamics rather than in model weights—and explain why resolving that verbal component still carries practical weight for governance and design.

Write in a polished, formal register suitable for a science-and-ideas magazine. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, citation requests, or appeals to external sources. Where formal results are mentioned, describe them precisely without attributing them to specific authors unless the attribution is common knowledge. Inject one framing that is not standard in popular discussions: specifically, treat the interaction between proxy reconstruction, unmeasured confounding, and sequential decision stages as a central crux rather than a peripheral technicality, and require explicit predictive divergence on this point. Keep the focus on conceptual structure, the conditions under which each camp would revise its view, and the separation of genuine disagreement from terminological confusion.
