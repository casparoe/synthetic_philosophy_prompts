I oversee allocation reviews for a municipal housing authority. Several times a quarter, my staff face ties: two or more applicants have identical priority scores, family composition, accessibility requirements, and verified wait-time, and the available units are functionally identical in layout, rent, and location. We need a reusable decision procedure — a step-by-step checklist with ordered tests, documentation rules, and explicit hand-off points — that staff with no philosophy background can complete in under twenty minutes and defend in an administrative audit. The procedure should be grounded in the philosophical problem of choice under perfect indifference (Buridan's ass and related symmetry-breaking arguments), but translated into operational rules. Specifically, it must: (1) distinguish verified absence of preference from hidden, unstated, or manufactured preferences; (2) specify the exact conditions under which randomization is mandatory, the conditions under which a substantive distinction may be admitted, and the conditions under which the decision must be escalated to a human reviewer because it involves values rather than facts; (3) include pass/fail criteria for each step and say what counts as sufficient documentation; (4) flag which steps are judgment calls disguised as procedural rules; and (5) include a "misfire warning" describing when the procedure will yield an outcome that conflicts with intuitive fairness and what staff should record when that happens. Apply the procedure to three tied-allocation proposals: (a) two applicants with identical verified profiles for one unit, where staff propose breaking the tie by "who submitted paperwork three hours earlier," a distinction never codified in our rules; (b) three applicants for two identical units, where one applicant asserts a weak, unverifiable preference for a north-facing window based on personal taste; and (c) a tie in which an applicant introduces a post-hoc distinction — proximity to a specific bus route — that is technically true but was never part of the eligibility or preference framework. For each, show the step at which the procedure stops, the output (randomization, admission of distinction, or escalation), and the required audit entry. Do not ask for bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; the deliverable must be self-contained and usable by staff without external research.
