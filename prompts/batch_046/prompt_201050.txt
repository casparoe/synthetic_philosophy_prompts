I have been discussing with friends the ethical comparison between beef and chicken consumption, and I have developed a somewhat contrarian position that I treat as a working hypothesis rather than a settled conviction. The conventional argument holds that replacing beef with chicken reduces harm because chickens suffer in factory conditions and beef requires vastly more land and direct killing per calorie. Against this, I propose what I call the crop-intensity asymmetry: poultry production depends on highly concentrated feed inputs—primarily soy and maize—grown in intensive monocultures where mechanized planting and harvesting, combined with systematic rodenticide and pesticide application, generate substantial incidental mortality among small wild mammals, ground-nesting birds, and invertebrates per unit of feed energy. By contrast, beef can be raised on perennial grasslands with far lower mechanized soil disturbance per hectare and with rotational grazing patterns that, while covering more total area, do not replicate the high-frequency mechanical killing events of row-crop agriculture. Furthermore, if we weight wild-animal suffering not by body count but by approximate neural complexity—so that the death of a field mouse or a sparrow registers less severely than the displacement or injury of larger wild mammals in pasture ecosystems—the indirect suffering footprint of chicken feed may exceed that of beef production even after adjusting for beef's larger spatial footprint. I do not claim this settles the question, but I want to understand what would genuinely undermine it.

What would count against this view, sorted by how decisive each objection would be? I want empirical evidence that could overturn the asymmetry—say, data showing that grassland grazing actually produces higher per-calorie wildlife mortality than I have assumed, or that feed-conversion ratios for chicken are so efficient that total crop acreage per calorie is lower than for grass-fed beef—distinguished from objections that merely restate the direct-slaughter disparity without addressing the indirect-crop mechanism. I also want to know which ethical premises, if rejected, would collapse the position: for instance, if suffering-weighting by neural complexity is shown to be ethically arbitrary or if the boundary between "direct" and "indirect" harm is drawn differently than I have drawn it. Crucially, I want an assessment of whether this position risks unfalsifiability—whether its defenders could always salvage it by adjusting the sentience-weighting function, redefining which agricultural stages count as "indirect," or appealing to regional differences in farming practice—and whether that insulation is a genuine flaw for a view intended to guide practical dietary choices. Finally, please say what a fair defender of the asymmetry should concede regardless: which partial corrections, scope restrictions, or empirical uncertainties ought to be acknowledged even if the core claim survives?
