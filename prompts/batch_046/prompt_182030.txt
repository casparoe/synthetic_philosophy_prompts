In my retirement from experimental physics I have begun attending a philosophy seminar on epistemology. Our leader has ruled consistently on the following cases: in the classic Gettier situation—Smith reasoning about Jones and the ten coins—she denies knowledge; in the stopped-clock case she denies knowledge; in a museum analog of the fake-barn scenario, where a visitor correctly identifies the sole authentic specimen among convincing replicas, she denies knowledge; in ordinary direct perception, such as seeing a cup on one's desk, she grants knowledge; in accepting the word of a generally reliable expert on a matter within her competence, she grants knowledge; and in a case where a belief is true, formed by a correct reasoning chain containing no false premises, but succeeds only because an unknown environmental factor happens to cancel an otherwise fatal error—so that in slightly different conditions it would have been false—she nevertheless grants knowledge.

Next week we discuss a case from instrumental measurement. A researcher reads a thermometer that she correctly believes to be calibrated, but it in fact carries a systematic +2 °C bias. Unbeknownst to her, the chamber she measures always contains a hidden heat source that raises the true temperature by exactly 2 °C. Her belief about the chamber temperature is therefore true, and it remains true across nearby conditions in which both the bias and the hidden source are present. Yet her belief depends on the false premise that her instrument is unbiased.

If she wishes to remain consistent with herself, what should her verdict be? Identify the competing epistemic principles—such as a no-false-lemmas condition, a modal-safety condition, and a sensitivity condition—that could accommodate her track record; say which earlier ruling is the closest analog; state what each principle predicts for this thermometer case; offer your best-guess verdict with reasons; specify what further principle she would be adopting with each choice; and propose one further hypothetical case that would definitively distinguish the principle she actually employs.
