I am preparing a colloquium Q&A with a philosopher who defends a position she calls "asymmetric branching reductionism" about survival in Parfit-style teletransportation and fission cases. She accepts that psychological continuity, not bodily continuity, is the relation that matters, but she rejects both the idea that relation R comes in degrees and the standard reductionist conclusion that personal identity is therefore not what matters. On her view, survival is strictly binary: in a non-branching case, exactly one future person can stand in relation R to me and thereby survive as me; in a branching case, neither branch is identical to me, yet both enter a derivative state she terms "distributed practical concern," in which they jointly inherit my projects, obligations, and first-personal cares without either being me. She further claims that whether relation R is instantiated depends not on the strength of psychological similarity but on the causal mechanism of preservation: a destructive scanner that recreates with perfect informational fidelity can establish R, but gradual synthetic neuron replacement breaks R unless each synthetic unit stands in an asymmetric causal-dependence relation to its specific biological predecessor. I want a sequence of ten interview questions that go well beyond restating this framework. Specifically, include questions that: (a) force her to distinguish distributed practical concern from mere altruistic sympathy for one's copies, with a follow-up that asks what practical difference the distinction makes if both copies are equally entitled to my resources; (b) press the moral asymmetry in a branching case where one copy is sent to suffering and the other to flourishing, asking whether she should care about both equally and whether equal concern is compatible with survival being binary rather than scalar; (c) challenge the non-branching condition as ad hoc, anticipating her reply that branching introduces destructive causal competition, and then follow up by asking why causal competition should affect survival rather than only identity; (d) probe her substrate-sensitive condition for gradual replacement, asking what conceptual or empirical criterion distinguishes causal dependence that preserves R from replacement that destroys it, and whether a fully synthetic but causally continuous brain would count; and (e) ask whether her binary/non-binary distinction ultimately collapses into a four-dimensionalist or stage-theoretic account she explicitly rejects, and if not, what independent metaphysical work the distinction performs. Each question should be phrased to anticipate her most likely defense, and each should end with a sharp follow-up that blocks retreat to metaphor, relabeling, or appeals to "that's a research program, not an objection."
