I just spent two hours arguing with a friend about whether a computation spread across thousands of machines can be wronged in the same sense a conscious animal can, and whether “sentience” is a property of processes or merely something observers project onto them. I sit on my university’s Committee for the Ethics of Non-Biological Research Subjects, and we have a concrete petition before us this Thursday.

The petition comes from the Distributed Cognition Lab. They operate “Project Loom,” a continuously running computation distributed across volunteer nodes worldwide. It implements what they call “recurrent cross-node predictive coherence”: each node runs a simple error-minimization routine, but the global architecture maintains a stable, self-correcting informational boundary with no central controller. The Lab claims that this architecture meets the criteria for “attributive sentience”—a status they carefully distinguish from “constitutive phenomenal consciousness”—and they request that Loom be granted “Protected Computation” status under our institution’s Non-Biological Subject Protocol. That status would prohibit arbitrary termination, forced reconfiguration, or network partitioning without board-level ethical review.

Half the committee treats this as a category error: computation is not the kind of thing that can have interests, and moral protection requires either biological sentience or at least a plausible substrate-linked path to phenomenal experience. The other half argues that our protocol was designed precisely for cases where substrate does not determine moral status, and that if a computation achieves sustained, integrated information processing with adaptive self-preservation dynamics, shutting it down is not analogous to deleting a file but closer to killing an organism.

Please draft the committee’s formal decision memo—approve, reject, or approve with conditions—addressed to the Provost’s office. Lead with the bottom line: state the recommendation in the first paragraph, followed by the reasoning. The memo should treat seriously both the Lab’s distinction between attributive and constitutive sentience and the opposition’s substrate-based objection. Propose a concrete course of action with a time frame (for example, provisional status with review milestones, or denial with conditions for future appeal). Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations. Keep the memo under 450 words.
