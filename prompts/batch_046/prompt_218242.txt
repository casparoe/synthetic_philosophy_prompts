I will be giving a seminar presentation at the interdisciplinary workshop "Forms and Functions: Computation, Perception, and Design," attended by cognitive psychologists specializing in visual object recognition, practicing graphic designers with expertise in typeface taxonomy, and philosophers of art concerned with category formation and essentialism. My argument extends the Letter Spirit framework—particularly its claim that letter recognition arises through analogical mapping across gridfont representations rather than through matching against a stored geometric prototype—to a stronger ontological thesis: stylistic variation is not a superficial layer added to an essential letterform, but is constitutive of the category itself. I maintain that there is no pure geometric essence of an 'a' that remains invariant across blackletter, sans-serif, and script instantiations; rather, the category is sustained by a family-resemblance network of functional roles within writing systems, and the gridfont-style analogical mechanisms explain how perceivers navigate this variation without relying on invariant feature detection. The psychologists in attendance are likely to accept analogical reasoning but will resist abandoning invariant-feature accounts of object recognition; the designers may object that my view collapses meaningful taxonomic distinctions between display and text typefaces; and the philosophers will likely press whether I am conflating conditions of recognition with conditions of ontological membership, or whether I am dissolving the token-type distinction altogether. Please prepare me for the Q&A: identify roughly eight objections I will actually face from these three subgroups, ordered by how severely they threaten the proposal, phrased in the vocabulary and commitments characteristic of each subgroup, with a concise reply I can deliver in about two minutes, a brief note on where each reply is weakest, and the specific concession I should make upfront to defuse the most damaging line of attack. Be sure to include the objection I most hope is not raised—namely, that if style is constitutive then a severely distorted or partially occluded 'a' ceases to be an 'a' altogether rather than being a degraded token—and advise me on how to respond should it arise.
