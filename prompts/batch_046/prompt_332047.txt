I serve as legislative counsel for a jurisdiction that permits citizen-initiated constitutional amendments by referendum, and my office is seeing a sustained increase in compound proposals that combine a high-salience normative commitment with a low-salience technical implementation mechanism. I need a reusable decision procedure that my team can apply to any incoming proposal, using only the text of the proposal and standard legal-reference materials, to determine whether the measure must appear on the ballot as a single question or should be split into separate votes. The procedure must be grounded in democratic-theoretic conditions for legitimate collective choice—specifically the distinction between value commitments and instrumental specifications—rather than in local statutory drafting rules. Translate that distinction into an ordered sequence of operational tests that a non-philosopher can run, defining at each stage what counts as a sufficient answer, where the procedure stops with a mandatory split, where it issues a discretionary recommendation, and where it reaches an explicit normative boundary that requires human judgment rather than mechanical application. Include a specific check for what I call semantic drift risk: the danger that voters will assent to a broadly framed value statement while failing to recognize that it activates a precise technical mechanism with consequences they have not evaluated. Apply the procedure to three hypothetical proposals: first, a measure that pairs a constitutional guarantee of access to clean water with a fixed tax-rate formula and administrative board structure for funding it; second, a measure that bans a specific agricultural practice statewide while simultaneously preempting all existing local ordinances on the same subject; third, a measure that combines a symbolic declaration of national heritage with a binding alteration to the process by which senior judges are appointed. In at least one of these applications, the procedure should yield a recommendation to split the proposal that the initiative's sponsors would strongly oppose, and you should identify precisely where the procedure ends and an irreducibly political judgment about democratic sovereignty must take over. Conclude by stating what the procedure cannot do—whether it can resolve conflicts between voter autonomy and administrative efficiency, or whether it merely structures the choice without settling it.
