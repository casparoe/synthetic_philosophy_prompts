Here is the examination question:

Compare Judith Jarvis Thomson's violinist argument and Don Marquis's future-like-ours (FLO) argument concerning the moral evaluation of abortion. First, explain whether the two arguments are genuinely in tension or whether they address orthogonal moral questions (bodily autonomy versus the wrongness of killing). Then analyse the following constructed case: a foetus affected by a specific neurological condition that permits full biological maturation into an adult human organism capable of homeostasis and reproduction, but that permanently precludes the development of phenomenal consciousness, autobiographical memory, or any first-person perspective—what we might call a biological human without subjective continuity. Would the FLO argument condemn the termination of such an organism? Would Thomson's argument permit disconnection or termination? Defend your view, and state whether the two arguments converge or diverge in this instance.

Here is the student's answer:

I think Thomson and Marquis are largely talking past each other, so there is no genuine tension. Thomson grants, for argument's sake, that the foetus is a person with a full right to life; she then asks whether that right entails a claim on another person's body. The violinist shows it does not—even a fully actual person may not be compelled to provide life support. Marquis, meanwhile, is not concerned with bodily autonomy at all; he argues that killing is wrong because it deprives the victim of a future like ours—experiences, projects, and activities that are valuable. So the arguments address different moral questions: one asks whether you may be forced to sustain life, the other asks whether killing itself wrongs the victim. They can both be correct: abortion might wrong the foetus by depriving it of a future, yet still be permissible because no one can be compelled to provide bodily support.

Applying this to the constructed case—a biological human without subjective continuity—I think Marquis's argument clearly fails. The phrase future like ours requires a subject who can actually live that future; without phenomenal consciousness or autobiographical memory, there is no ours in the relevant sense to be deprived. So termination does not wrong the organism in Marquis's terms. Thomson's argument, however, applies with full force. The core question for her is always whether one individual can be required to use their body to sustain another. Since this organism is a living human being—possibly even a person, depending on one's criteria—the right to disconnect remains intact. In fact, because it lacks subjective continuity, it may not qualify as a person at all, but that only strengthens the autonomy claim: bodily rights do not depend on the moral status of the dependent party.

Consequently, both arguments converge on permitting termination, though for different reasons. Marquis offers no protection, and Thomson provides a positive liberty to withdraw support. The usual distinction between killing and letting die also collapses here, because once there is no valuable future at stake, direct termination is morally equivalent to withdrawal. There is therefore no residual objection from either framework.

Finally, Marquis's position is best understood as a potentiality argument: he values the foetus not because it already has a future, but because it possesses the potential to develop one. In our constructed case, that potential is absent—there can be no development of subjective continuity—so the potentiality reading also fails. This reveals that both philosophers are ultimately tracking the same underlying feature, namely the potential for a valuable human life, expressed through different conceptual frameworks. Hence the autonomy-based and deprivation-based distinctions collapse in this edge case.
