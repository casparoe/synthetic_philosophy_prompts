In discussions of indirect normativity and idealized deliberation, colleagues keep citing Bernard Williams's phrase that a husband who saves his drowning wife by consulting moral permissibility has had "one thought too many". They invoke it to reject any model of practical reasoning—whether consequentialist, contractualist, or virtue-theoretic—that asks an agent to run an idealized normative check before acting on partial attachments. But the slogan is doing ambiguous work. Could you separate the precise theses it might express: (a) a claim about the correct content of first-person deliberation (what thoughts should be present or absent), (b) a claim about the causal psychology of moral motivation (whether explicit principle-invocation disrupts appropriate affect), (c) a claim about the distinction between justifying reasons and motivating reasons, and (d) a claim about the limits of idealized rational agency in normative theory? For each, say whether it is trivial, defensible as a thesis about indirect normativity, or a false empirical generalization about human psychology, and indicate which reading actually licenses the conclusion that idealized deliberation is normatively inappropriate rather than merely psychologically unrealistic.
