I'm trying to identify a pattern I've noticed in applied debates about distributive justice, especially around prioritarianism and reactions to the leveling-down objection. A thinker begins with a continuous weighting function—welfare gains count more the worse off the recipient is. Then the classic leveling-down case is raised: taking resources from the well-off to make them equally poor, with no benefit to anyone. The prioritarian rightly rejects this. But in subsequent practical arguments—climate burden-sharing, healthcare rationing, progressive taxation—the same thinker rejects any policy that significantly lowers the welfare of people in the middle or upper ranges unless the corresponding gain for the worst-off is large enough to satisfy the original weight ratio multiplied by the size of the loss. Over several cases, an implicit "no net degradation above a socially defined adequate threshold" rule accumulates around the continuous curve. The result behaves like a hybrid: absolute priority for the severely disadvantaged, but a protected baseline status for the moderately well-off that the original weighting function did not entail. I see concrete instances of this in climate ethics, where prioritarian reasoning is used to justify heavier obligations for wealthy nations, yet the same framework is invoked to block carbon taxes that would reduce wealthy-nation welfare without massive, quantifiable gains for the poorest nations—treating current wealthy-nation welfare as a protected floor. I see it in healthcare allocation, where a prioritarian might fund treatments for the severely disabled, yet resist reallocating resources from moderately well-off patients to severely ill ones if the well-off would fall below an "adequate care" threshold, even though a pure prioritarian curve should permit such trade-offs.

Does this specific drift—the gradual accretion of discrete protective thresholds for the better-off onto a continuous prioritarian weighting—have an established name? How exactly does it differ from explicit sufficientarianism, from a deliberately defended two-tier distributive view, and from the standard leveling-down objection itself? If there is no precise term, how should we distinguish it from ordinary political risk aversion or moderation, and what boundary cases—say, a policy that slightly lowers middle-class welfare for large bottom-decile gains, versus one that lowers top-decile welfare for moderate bottom-decile gains—would reveal whether a thinker is genuinely running the weight function with added insulation versus having quietly replaced prioritarianism with a different distributive principle?
