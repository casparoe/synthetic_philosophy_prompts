I am preparing material for an advanced philosophy-of-mind seminar focused on the debate between Norman Malcolm—who argued in his 1959 work that dreams are not experiences at all—and Daniel Dennett, who treats dreams as narratives confabulated upon waking rather than as ongoing conscious episodes during sleep. Given Descartes’s reliance on clear and distinct perception as the criterion for knowledge, his substance dualism about mind and body, and his specific skeptical move in the Meditations that no internal mark distinguishes dreaming from waking, please trace step by step how he would likely respond. Would he side with Malcolm and deny that lucid-dream protocols reveal genuine experiential content, arguing instead that measurable cortical activity is merely bodily motion compatible with an unconscious soul? Would he gravitate toward Dennett and treat both human and AI-generated dream narratives as post-hoc constructions lacking intrinsic phenomenology? Or would his Cartesian framework push him toward a third option—perhaps that dreams are real mental acts of an immaterial substance, but ones so obscured by the body’s mechanisms that neither modern account captures them? Please develop the reasoning in detail, flagging any points where Descartes’s historical commitments make him unable to accommodate a distinction that Malcolm or Dennett takes for granted, and do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; I need only the reconstructed philosophical argument.
