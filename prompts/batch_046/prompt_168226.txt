hey, i'm writing a chapter on whether social conventions carry actual moral weight or are just etiquette with extra steps, and my intuitions are kind of all over the place.

case 1: in the US, leaving less than 20% at a sit-down restaurant feels like real harm — like i'm freeloading on a wage structure. moral failure, not just rudeness.

case 2: in berlin, crossing against a red with empty streets while locals wait — i feel like a clueless tourist, but not like i've violated anyone's rights. not moral.

case 3: cutting the queue for the tube in london — wrong, and wrong in a heavier way than jaywalking, though i can't point to a direct victim.

case 4: in japan, tipping is awkward and sometimes insulting. insisting on leaving cash feels wrong, but wrong like being ignornant, not like cheating someone.

so my verdicts are: US-tipping yes, berlin-jaywalk no, queue-yes, japan-tipping no. my hunch is obligations arise when a convention does hidden distributive work (tipping substituting wages) or protects a shared resource people depend on (the queue), but not when it's pure coordination with no dependancy (traffic lights) or when compliance itself causes offense.

but queueing doesn't seem distributive, and jaywalking does rely on others stopping for me. so is there a cleaner split — maybe protocol-obligations vs outcome-obligations? or do i just concede one verdict is wrong?

what principle captures all four? if none does, which should i overturn and why? and what does it predict for removing shoes in a japanese home — moral failure or bad manners? also, is there a framing better than distributive-vs-coordination that i'm missing?
