I just had a long, somewhat heated argument with a friend about George Orwell’s 1984 and Simone Weil’s critique of political parties, and I want to work through it with real precision rather than repeating our positions. My friend insists that 1984 is fundamentally about totalitarian state power, not about parties in any philosophically interesting sense—after all, Ingsoc is a monopoly regime with no rivals, and Weil’s essay arguing for the abolition of all parties targets plural democratic systems where citizens voluntarily surrender judgment to partisan loyalty. I think the novel is more specific than that, but I need a sharper reading.

Read 1984 through the lens of Weil’s claim that a party is defined not by its program but by its demand for total intellectual adhesion: members must treat disagreement with the line as a personal failure rather than an argument to be answered, and the party functions less as an instrument for policy than as a machine for manufacturing collective passion that replaces individual moral judgment. Pay particular attention to the difference between the Party itself and the Brotherhood—Weil distinguishes parties from other associations precisely by this suppression of independent reasoning, so does Orwell present the Brotherhood as a genuine counter-example, or does O’Brien’s interrogation reveal that even resistance organizes itself through the same mechanism?

I would like you to examine the Two Minutes Hate, Winston’s attempt to keep a private diary, and the final scene in the Ministry of Love, asking in each case whether what is at stake is generic authoritarian fear or specifically the kind of self-induced blindness Weil describes, in which loyalty requires one to distrust one’s own perceptions before they can conflict with the collective line. Where does the lens illuminate a scene or a character’s choice that a standard political reading misses? Where does it break—perhaps because Ingsoc’s control is enforced by violence and surveillance rather than by the voluntary surrender of judgment that Weil emphasizes, or because the Brotherhood is shown to be a fabrication rather than a real association? Do not reduce the novel to an illustration of Weil, and do not distort her view to make it fit; if the work shows something about collective loyalty, the corruption of individual conscience, or the impossibility of dissent within a structured collective that her essay does not capture, say so explicitly. I am preparing this for a reading group that knows the novel’s plot and major scenes well but has not read Weil, so ground the interpretation in moments they can recall without requiring verbatim quotation, and conclude by saying what 1984 adds to the philosophical debate about parties and judgment that argument alone cannot provide.
