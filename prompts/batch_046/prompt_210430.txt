I'm not a philosopher, just someone who reads a lot, but I've noticed a pattern in how people talk about moral credit and I can't tell if it has a proper name. Here's what I mean: people usually say an act has more moral worth when the agent had to overcome some internal resistance — like a naturally selfish person forcing themselves to share. That's familiar enough. But then there's this other layer. If the resistance comes from something that feels outside the person's 'real' self — say they're sleep-deprived or on medication that makes them irritable — and they still do the right thing, people seem to discount the praise. It's like the struggle doesn't count because it wasn't part of there actual character. Meanwhile if there's no resistance at all, credit also drops. So the peak praise requires resistance that is internal and authentic, not imported from circumstances. Is there an established concept for this specific filter on moral worth? I know Kant distinguishes acting from duty versus inclination, but that doesn't seem to track whether the inclination traces back to your stable personality or to being tired. How does this differ from moral luck, and if there's no name, what'd you call it — and where exactly does the boundary sit, like with brainwashing or temporary mood states?
