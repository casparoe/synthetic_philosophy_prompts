I serve on my university’s disciplinary appeals and institutional-culture panel, and we are revising the framework for serious breaches of academic integrity—specifically, whether to retain our current confidential-remediation model or adopt a proposed community-accountability protocol. The current model is designed to elicit what philosophers distinguish as guilt: it treats the violation as a discrete wrong action, requires private acknowledgment of harm to specific affected parties, mandates concrete restitution, and aims at reintegration without public stigma. The proposed protocol, by contrast, would require students found responsible to participate in a facilitated, peer-attended session in which they must articulate not merely what rule was broken but the kind of scholar they failed to become, with the explicit goal of activating shame as a collective-binding emotion rather than allowing guilt to be privatized and rationalized away.

A substantial minority of the panel argues that shame is the more morally serious emotion because it attaches to character and thereby protects communal identity, and that an institution has both the right and the duty to design its practices so as to cultivate the emotional ecology it deems appropriate. The majority worries that shame is structurally resistant to repair—once internalized as a global self-assessment rather than a localized action-assessment, it tends to produce withdrawal or defensiveness rather than reform—and that attempting to choose a culture’s dominant moral emotion oversteps institutional authority, reducing students to instruments of collective sentiment rather than treating them as agents capable of targeted moral repair. There is also a practical concern: our office lacks the clinical and cultural competence to manage shame-based processes without risking disproportionate psychological harm, particularly for students from backgrounds where public honor and face-loss carry heavier weight.

Please draft the written opinion I will circulate before Thursday’s meeting. It should recommend either retaining the guilt-oriented framework, adopting the shame-oriented protocol, or instituting a structured hybrid with clear conditions, together with a concrete implementation timeline if your recommendation involves change. Your reasoning should engage seriously with the strongest argument for the position you reject—specifically addressing whether an institution can legitimately engineer emotional culture, and whether guilt’s action-specificity genuinely makes it the better moral emotion for institutional repair—rather than simply restating legal or procedural norms. The opinion should be written as a formal memo that the panel could vote on, with an explicit bottom line and conditions if applicable.
