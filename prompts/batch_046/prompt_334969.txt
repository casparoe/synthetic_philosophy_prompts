Consider the institutional practice now common at large AI laboratories: internal safety evaluations routinely score models for “belief consistency” across counterfactual prompt variations—testing whether the system assigns the same truth-value to a claim regardless of framing or language—while the same organizations mandate that all user-facing documentation replace mental-state verbs with purely behavioral descriptions (“outputs X under condition Y”). Meanwhile, their risk-review boards rely on intentional-language summaries (“the model falsely believes that...”) to flag dangerous capabilities, yet prohibit anthropomorphic phrasing in product interfaces. What philosophical commitments about mental-state attribution does this bifurcated practice presuppose? Must it assume that belief-talk is instrumentally indispensable for risk assessment but literally false of the system, or can it be sustained by a contextualist view on which the same model genuinely possesses beliefs in evaluation contexts but not in consumer-facing ones? Reconstruct the implicit theory, distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what merely fits it well, and say what would have to change—both in evaluation protocols and in interface design—if the lab adopted a unified stance, whether full intentional realism about the model or strict eliminativism across both domains.
