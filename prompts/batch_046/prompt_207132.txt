I am a doctoral student in philosophy of language. At our reading group last week, a colleague offered the following summary of Saul Kripke's account of rigid designators and necessary a posteriori truths, but she appended a framing I had not encountered in the standard literature. She argued that proper names and natural-kind terms are rigid designators, designating the same entity or kind in every possible world in which it exists; on this basis, the identity of water with H2O is necessary, even though it is known only through empirical investigation. She then claimed that this result refutes descriptivism altogether, on the grounds that if reference is fixed by contingent descriptions, those descriptions cannot guarantee the cross-world stability rigidity requires; hence the descriptivist must covertly rely on necessary descriptions, which are unavailable for empirical terms. She further insisted that the causal-historical chain of reference transmission requires every competent speaker to maintain some epistemic link—however indirect—to the original baptismal event, which makes theoretical terms such as electron particularly problematic, since no speaker enjoys perceptual access to that event. Finally, she proposed that in cases of split reference, where causally disconnected communities independently baptise the same phonetic form, the rigidity thesis forces us to treat the form as ambiguous between homophonous terms rather than as genuinely ambiguous, an ontologically costly rescue.

I suspect that parts of this are accurate but that several distinctions are being collapsed. Could you audit the summary?
