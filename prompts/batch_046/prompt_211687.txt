Analyze the dissolution of the Soviet Union between 1989 and 1991 as a case study in reference-class forecasting and the outside view of imperial collapse. Frame the problem as it would have appeared to a well-calibrated forecaster in 1985: which reference class applies to the USSR—continental empires, ideological one-party states, or nuclear-armed federations—and what base rates for dissolution, violent fragmentation, or reform-led survival does each class supply? Map the theory onto the episode: does the outside view predict the speed and peacefulness of the collapse, or does it predict either stability or violent breakup? Identify the specific inside-view factors—the party apparatus, the nuclear command structure, Gorbachev's refusal to deploy force against secession, the Baltic independence movements—that either correct an erroneous reference-class assignment or constitute anomalies the outside view cannot absorb without ad hoc adjustments. Then reverse the analysis: does the Soviet case reveal that reference-class forecasting systematically misweights regimes that combine totalitarian party control with nuclear weapons, or does it instead show that forecasters in 1985 should have weighted the "multi-ethnic empire" class more heavily despite the ideological overlay? Be disciplined about where the theory is silent—for instance, on whether a forecaster should have updated on the August 1991 coup attempt differently than on earlier crises, or on how to weight the absence of a violent center-periphery conflict—and say whether the episode ultimately strengthens or weakens the claim that the outside view should dominate for geopolitical forecasting when class membership itself is contested.
