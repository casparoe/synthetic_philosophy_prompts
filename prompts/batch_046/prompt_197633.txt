For the purpose of specifying an adequate theory of collective moral responsibility, I want the full desiderata for an account of blaming a nation-state for initiating a war, taking the First World War and the Fischer controversy—over whether Imperial Germany’s leadership deliberately pursued a continental war in 1914—as the central test case. Write the requirements list: the phenomena the theory must explain, the distinctions it must preserve, the cases it must get right, and the results from political theory and philosophy of action with which it must remain consistent, with the motivating consideration behind each.

The theory must distinguish, without collapse, between (a) causal-historical responsibility attaching to a state’s bureaucratic-military apparatus, (b) moral blame attaching to its decision-making elite, and (c) inherited liability or stigma attaching to its present citizenry, and it must specify the conditions under which these come apart. It must handle the Fischer scenario: if the archival record indicates that a narrow circle of officials advanced expansionist aims while democratic institutions and the broader public were structurally excluded from war planning, does blame remain with the state as a corporate agent, dissolve into purely individual culpability, or distribute across successive generations? It must also address the temporal displacement problem—those who bear reparative burdens or diplomatic stigma are not identical to those who authorized aggression—without reducing national blame to a purely forward-looking mechanism of institutional repair.

Further requirements: the account must be consistent with the fact that nation-states are not unitary minds but are treated as agents in international law; it must explain the asymmetry between blaming Germany for 1914 and withholding equivalent blame from present-day Germany for the same historical act; it must specify whether democratic opacity—citizens’ lack of access to classified war aims—functions as a full excuse, a dilution factor, or an irrelevance to collective attribution; and it must accommodate the intuition that bureaucratic fragmentation can dissipate individual intent without thereby dissolving collective agency.

Flag which desiderata are non-negotiable—such as consistency with the non-identity of present citizens, the non-unitary nature of the state, and the distinction between backward-looking moral judgment and forward-looking institutional correction—and which are camp-specific preferences, such as the demand for retributive satisfaction, the liberal-neutrality prohibition on attributing collective guilt, or the realist insistence that only state power, not moral status, matters. If any subset of the list is jointly unsatisfiable—for instance, full backward-looking blame plus democratic innocence of the citizenry plus temporal continuity of the state—identify that tension explicitly, state which single requirement does the most work in excluding candidates, and briefly indicate how collective-intentionality accounts, structural-injustice frameworks, and state-realist models fare against the criteria, keeping the weight on the specification itself.
