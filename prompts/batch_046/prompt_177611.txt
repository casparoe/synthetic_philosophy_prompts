Consider the institutional mechanics by which scientific literature and legal precedent correct themselves over time. In academic publishing, a journal may issue an erratum that updates the original article in place—the citation, the DOI, and the canonical text remain numerically identical, with only an appended notice of revision—while in other cases it issues a formal retraction paired with a replacement article bearing a new identifier, leaving the original intact but marked withdrawn. In common-law jurisdictions, a higher court may overrule a prior decision, leaving it in the reporters but stripping it of binding force, or it may vacate a judgment, treating it as void ab initio and expunging its legal existence. These practices appear to treat the paper or the case now as the same entity modified, now as a sequence of distinct temporal stages only loosely unified.

What ontology of persistence—endurantist or perdurantist, or some hybrid—do these correction mechanisms implicitly presuppose? Reconstruct the view of diachronic identity for institutional artifacts that makes each practice intelligible. Are the publishing and legal systems operating with consistent metaphysics, or does one treat the article as an enduring object that survives alteration of its properties while the other treats it as a four-dimensional worm whose temporal parts include an erroneous slice and a corrected slice? Distinguish what each practice strictly requires from what merely coheres with it, and identify where the institution is philosophically underdetermined—where rival theories of persistence would license the same procedural rule for different reasons. Finally, if we replaced the implicit ontology with its rival, what concrete changes would have to occur in citation norms, database architecture, or judicial reasoning?
