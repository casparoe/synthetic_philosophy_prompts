As a journalist who covers the philosophy of democratic institutions, I recently sat in on an upper-division seminar discussing the boundary problem—who properly belongs to the demos when territorial jurisdiction and affected interests diverge. The instructor wants a final-exam essay that resists rote summary of the familiar all-affected-interests versus all-subjected-persons framing and instead forces students to think through an injected, unconventional scenario. Please design a single 90-minute essay question for this seminar. The prompt should present a concrete case: a transboundary river-basin authority must approve a long-term irrigation protocol that will alter downstream salinity, biodiversity, and settlement patterns for decades. The authority is considering adopting a corporate-style stakeholder framework—giving formal voice to current residents, downstream agricultural cooperatives outside the voting jurisdiction, projected future populations represented through demographic modeling, and ecological proxies speaking for non-human river-system interests. The exam question should ask students whether importing this stakeholder logic solves the boundary problem, or whether democratic legitimacy requires a stricter demos defined by co-authorship of binding law, and whether ecological or future-generation proxies commit a category mistake by treating non-voting entities as members of a political community rather than as objects of present protection.

Along with the question, provide a model answer of roughly 650 to 800 words and a marking scheme suitable for grading in a closed-book exam where no bibliographic citations are expected or rewarded. The marking scheme should assign specific marks to at least four conceptual moves—such as distinguishing democratic authorship from affected-interest inclusion, diagnosing whether the stakeholder analogy collapses that distinction, assessing whether representation of future or ecological interests extends membership or merely expands the scope of present duty, and identifying the specific error in assuming that broader inclusion automatically strengthens democratic legitimacy. Include a one-line note on how students typically miss each point, and clearly state what separates an A-range answer from a B-range answer: specifically, that the former must show that the stakeholder framework does not resolve but rather dissolves the boundary problem by conflating two distinct normative relations, whereas the latter merely describes both positions without taking that critical step.
