I am working through Immanuel Kant's Critique of Pure Reason (1781), specifically the section in which he denies that existence is a real predicate. He asks the reader to compare one hundred actual thalers with one hundred merely possible thalers, observing that the concept of the latter lacks nothing contained in the concept of the former; existence, he concludes, does not enlarge the concept of a thing but only posits the object together with all of its predicates. I want to understand whether this argument supports a metaontological claim—namely, that disputes about whether entities of a given kind exist are in part verbal disputes about which conceptual scheme or linguistic framework we adopt, rather than substantive disagreements about an independent reality. At the same time, I worry that this reading is anachronistic: perhaps Kant's point is strictly logical, concerning the form of existential judgments, and carries no implications for semantics, ontology, or whether existential questions can be dissolved by clarifying our commitments. Could you help me see how Kant's comparison bears on the modern idea that some ontological disagreements are resolvable through framework clarification rather than discovery, and where the boundary lies between his logical claim and any stronger metaontological interpretation?
