Geological bodies that govern epoch classification require a Global Boundary Stratotype Section and Point—a physical marker in rock, ice, or sediment—to ratify a new geological period. That procedural rule explains why recent Anthropocene proposals center on radioisotope layers around 1950 at Crawford Lake, even though climate historians and earth-system scientists often date the epoch to 1610 (the Columbian Exchange) or 1784 (industrial coal combustion) on grounds of socio-ecological transformation rather than synchronous lithostratigraphic signal. Meanwhile, museums, textbooks, and climate-policy frameworks routinely display "Anthropocene" timelines anchored to one of these dates without explaining why a geological ratification procedure built for uniform global rock traces should govern a concept defined by uneven, socially distributed ecological change. What philosophical commitments about historical periodization, the ontology of institutional authority, and the relation between physical traces and social reality does this pattern presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory, distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what it merely accommodates, and say what an institutional framework built on socio-ecological rather than stratigraphic criteria would have to change—especially regarding who may declare a new epoch and what evidence counts. If my premise that these heterogeneous practices share a single reconstructible implicit theory is confused, or if the task can't be done well, say so plainly instead of forcing coherence.
