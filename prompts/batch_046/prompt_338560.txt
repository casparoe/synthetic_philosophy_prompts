I was arguing with a friend about whether neural nets have real representions, and I think I've settled on this weird middle ground. I want to say that in deep vision models, intermediate layer activations genuinely represent specific visual features — edges, textures, parts of objects — in a way that's counterfactualy robust and causally drives the final classification. But they don't have propositional content, they aren't compositional like language, and they definitely lack anything like Brentano-style intentionality. So they're representations in a mechanistic, functional sense, but not mental representations. Call it weak representational realism, if that doesn't sound too pretentious.

Anyway, what would actually count against this? Not the generic it's-just-correlation objection, but specific things: maybe results from mechanistic interpretability showing that these activations are too distributed or context-dependent to map onto specific features; maybe formal results showing that any distributed representation collapses into either full symbol-hood or pure statistical correlation; or philosophical arguments that representation-ascription requires some normative or semantic condition that these activations can't meet. Rank the defeaters from fatal to just awkward, and say which ones a committed weak realist should honestly concede hurt the view, versus which they'd just brush off. Also, please tell me how confident you are in this assessment and single out the point you're least sure about — I have a hunch that the line between functional representation and fancy correlation is way blurrier than I'm letting on, and I want to know if that's the weak spot.
