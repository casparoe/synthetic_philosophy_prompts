I'm running a small workshop for about 10-12 grad students in history of science, 90 minutes, and I want them to actually feel hindsight bias and the narrative fallacy in their bones, not just nod at it. Lately I've been obsessed with this idea I'm calling counterfactual narrative compression — the way once you know an endpoint you don't just misremember what you predicted, you literally shrink the space of live alternatives that existed beforehand and treat the path you got as the only path that could have been there. I caught myself doing it with these old 1970s polymer lab notebooks I found; I had built this neat story about why the experiment "had to" fail that totally erased three other failure routes that were clearly visible in the contemporaneous notes.

Can you design a structured simulation or role-play where participants have to make real small-stakes choices or predictions based on genuinely ambiguous information, then learn a specific outcome, and then are forced to reconstruct the "history" of why it happened — but with rules that make them physically feel the branching possibilities getting crushed into one line? I want roles or rules that make them commit to predictions in writing before the reveal, then defend those predictions afterwards, and then do a second reconstruction where they have to account for the erased branches. The debrief should map specific moments onto hindsight bias versus the narrative fallacy separately, and you should be frank about which part of the bias (the "I knew it all along" feeling) the activity can reproduce and which part has to come from facilitator discussion. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time — I'm guessing people will refuse to erase their first predictions or will fake remembering them differently — and how to handle it. Keep it runnable without any outside reading, since I want them to generate the problem themselves before I name it.
