I am defending a strengthened version of the political-crime thesis of famine, building on Sen's entitlement approach but extending it to a specific claim about high-capacity authoritarian regimes: that where state surveillance and distributive control are extensive, famine mortality should not occur without either intentional entitlement suppression or structurally negligent information blockage, since aggregate food availability is rarely the binding constraint. I take the canonical cases—Bengal 1943, the Irish famine of the 1840s, and the Great Leap Forward—to illustrate distributive rather than meteorological failure. What would count against this stronger position? I am not asking for the standard reply that drought or crop disease can initiate crisis, but for the evidence, conceptual distinctions, and cross-domain results that should actually move a defender. Which empirical patterns—say, documented availability-decline famines under high state capacity, or bureaucratic rationality that prevents information blockage—would be fatal versus merely awkward? How do distinctions between negligence and intent, or between availability decline and entitlement collapse, function as potential defeaters? Would results from state-capacity theory or the ontology of collective harm undermine the framing? Rank these from decisive to cosmetic. I also want an honest assessment: has the thesis been qualified into unfalsifiability by redefining every apparent counterexample as hidden entitlement failure, and if so, is that insulation a problem for a view of this kind?
