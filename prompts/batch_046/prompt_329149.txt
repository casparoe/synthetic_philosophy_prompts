I write about philosophy and religion for a magazine covering science and ideas. At a recent workshop on comparative ethics, I witnessed a debate between a philosopher of Indo-Tibetan Buddhism and a comparative ethicist over whether Buddhist ethics is structurally consequentialist. The exchange went through four moves.

Scholar A argued that karma functions as a causal law linking action to future suffering or well-being across lifetimes, with mental intention (cetana) serving as an intermediate causal variable rather than a deontological constraint. She added that upaya, or skillful means, licenses breaking precepts when this minimizes aggregate suffering, which looks like outcome-based reasoning.

Scholar B replied that sila (moral discipline) has non-instrumental value as an expression of an enlightened mind, not as a tool for producing states; treating it as optimizable commits the grasping (upadana) the tradition seeks to dissolve. He noted that karma includes mental factors like compassion and wisdom that are not reducible to welfare metrics.

Scholar A countered that in the sutras sila is consistently justified soteriologically: it is valued because it leads to the cessation of suffering (nirodha). Thus the expressive reading is compatible with a consequentialist framework that includes mental transformation in its outcome set.

Scholar B invoked the two-truths doctrine: at the conventional level (samvriti-satya) we speak of actions and results, but at the ultimate level (paramartha-satya) there is no agent, act, or consequence to maximize. Applying consequentialist calculus to Buddhist ethics, he said, is category-mistaken, treating conventional constructions as if they had the ontological weight for quantitative optimization.

Please adjudicate each move: rate Scholar A's opening, Scholar B's reply, Scholar A's counter, and Scholar B's final move from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with brief justification. Then say what both miss—specifically, whether there is a coherent intermediate position that respects the soteriological structure without reducing it to welfare maximization, and whether the two-truths move blocks the consequentialist reading or merely relocates it.
