I’m a journalist covering philosophy of science and metaphysics, and I’m drafting a feature on whether recent debates in computational ontology have accidentally revived Russell’s old regress about universals. In my notes I reconstructed an argument—part standard realism, part inference to parsimony—that seems to prove too much: it ends by showing that scientific taxonomy is impossible whether or not we accept universals. I’d like you to act as an expert metaphysician with training in philosophy of science, think carefully, and begin with brief brainstorming on which premises are actually carrying the weight. Then diagnose the chain.

Here is the argument laid out step by step:

(1) Any ontology adequate to scientific taxonomy must explain why numerically distinct particular objects fall under the same kind-label.
(2) The only explanation that avoids treating resemblance as brute and inexplicable is that those objects literally share a universal property.
(3) Therefore, scientific taxonomy commits us to first-order universals.
(4) But any universal—say, being-redness—has a nature that determines which particulars can instantiate it.
(5) That nature must either be a particular feature of the universal itself or another universal.
(6) If it is a particular, it cannot play an explanatory role across different universals; hence it must be a second-order universal.
(7) By strict parity of reasoning, every universal requires a higher-order universal to ground its own nature, generating an infinite ascending hierarchy.
(8) An ontology that contains an actually infinite hierarchy of entities violates the principle of ontological parsimony that scientific realism itself endorses.
(9) So the commitment in step (3) is incompatible with the methodological constraints of science.
(10) But if we reject step (2) and allow brute resemblance, we lose the shared-property basis required for inductive inference, thereby undermining scientific reasoning altogether.
(11) Hence we are caught: either we accept an infinitely regressive ontology that science cannot tolerate, or we abandon the explanatory foundation of taxonomy and induction.

The reconstruction claims this shows realism about universals is self-refuting in any scientific context. I feel something slips between the steps—perhaps an equivocation on what it means for a universal to have a “nature,” a hidden modal assumption about explanation, or a false dichotomy in step (5)—but I cannot name the exact fault.
