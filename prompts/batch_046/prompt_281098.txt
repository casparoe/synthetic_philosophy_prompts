I have recently been appointed to a long-term advisory board tasked with allocating resources across research programs that pursue incommensurable values—epistemic rigor, social equity, and aesthetic innovation—over successive funding cycles. In preparing my framework for how the board ought to revise its priorities over time without falling into strategic exploitation or incoherence, I have arrived at the following commitments. I want to know whether they form a consistent package, or whether I am helping myself to incompatible motivations.

Here is where I stand:

(1) Rational preference over multi-dimensional goods can be genuinely non-transitive: there are coherent cycles of the form A preferred to B, B to C, and C to A, arising not from error but from the irreducible plurality of evaluative dimensions.

(2) Vulnerability to a money pump—being led through a sequence of pairwise trades that returns one to the initial option with a net loss—is always and necessarily a mark of diachronic irrationality, regardless of whether the preferences are held by an individual or an institutional agent.

(3) Diachronic rationality requires dynamic consistency: at time t1 an agent should not adopt a plan that they can foresee they will systematically abandon at t2 in the absence of new information or evidence.

(4) Rational institutional agents should be open to revising their evaluative weights over successive cycles in response to genuine value learning—discovering, for instance, that a previously weighted dimension was mischaracterized—without this revision counting as a failure of rationality.

(5) The canonical argument that non-transitive preferences are irrational because they license money pumps assumes that the agent's preference profile remains fixed across the sequence of trades; this assumption is appropriate for commodity exchange but inappropriate for evaluative bodies whose explicit mandate includes normative development over time.

Please audit this set. For each pair, indicate whether the tension is real or only apparent; for the real ones, rank them by severity, identify the specific commitment or inference that creates the conflict, and say what the least costly revision would be and what it sacrifices. Then tell me which single revision—giving up or modifying one of these five commitments—would restore the greatest overall consistency, and whether that revision undermines the institutional purpose I described. In particular, examine whether the critique of fixed-profile assumptions in (5) undermines (2) or merely qualifies it, and whether (3) can be preserved intact alongside (4) once the distinction between information-driven and learning-driven change is made precise. Be specific about which commitments pull against each other and which only look as if they do.
