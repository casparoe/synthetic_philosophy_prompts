I'm working through material for a seminar on decision theory, and my study group recently had a prolonged argument about whether honoring sunk costs is always irrational or whether there are defensible contexts—such as identity continuity, credible commitment, or moral consistency—where past investment can legitimately shape present choice. To push this question into sharper relief, I want you to write a deadpan travel-guide entry in the precise, practical, and cheerfully neutral voice of a Lonely Planet page, describing a fictional nation—call it Permanensia—where the rejection of sunk-cost reasoning has been elevated into an absolute civic and metaphysical doctrine, and therefore every citizen is legally and ethically required to honor every prior investment without exception. The satire should work by exaggerating the hard rationalist claim that past expenditure can never justify continued action, but it should do so through inversion: in Permanensia, abandoning anything you have started is the capital crime, and the bureaucracy, culture, and daily rituals are all organized around mandatory continuation regardless of changed circumstances, new evidence, or disastrous outcomes.

Please include the standard guidebook sections: an overview of local customs (for example, the dining protocol that requires finishing any meal once ordered, the tour rules that obligate visitors to complete an itinerary even after the destination has been destroyed by flood or erosion, and the etiquette for politely refusing to help a stranger who has accidentally begun an undesirable task); practical travel tips, including how to pass the entry visa exam that tests one's willingness to persist in error and what penalties apply to tourists who attempt to "cut their losses" and leave early; a sidebar on the national holiday honoring the chief engineer who completed a massive bridge to an island that had disappeared beneath the sea thirty years before construction finished; and a brief note on the local currency, which is non-transferable and can only be spent on goods and services related to projects one has already begun. The piece should run about 600 words, remain legible to someone who has never studied behavioral economics, and never wink at the reader or break the encyclopedic tone; the humor should arise entirely from the deadpan presentation of a society that has turned one side of a philosophical debate into an absurd total system. Do not include citations, bibliographies, named living figures, or references to specific academic texts.
