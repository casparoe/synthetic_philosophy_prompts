After a long argument with a friend following our philosophy of mind seminar, I find myself stuck on whether teleosemantics actually resolves the problem of mental content or merely displaces it into evolutionary biology. She defends a selectionist view—roughly in the tradition of Ruth Millikan—according to which misrepresentation requires a history of selection for a specific tracking relation, and she insists that this creates an immediate problem for synthetic organisms: imagine a bacterium engineered last Tuesday with chemoreceptors that reliably swim up glucose gradients, but which has never undergone natural selection for that capacity. If its receptors can misrepresent—say, by tracking a toxic analog—there is no evolutionary proper function to ground the error, so selectionist teleosemantics seems unable to assign content at all. I replied that this objection confuses the source of function with the conditions for content, suggesting that a systemic or organizational account of function—where contribution to current homeostatic organization fixes the norm—can handle the synthetic case without abandoning naturalism. Yet after two hours we had not moved: we agreed on the microbiology, on the possibility of misrepresentation in artificial life, and on the need for a non-circular naturalistic account, but we could not agree on whether the synthetic-bacterium example reveals a substantive gap in selectionism or just a boundary intuition about whether mental content requires an etiological pedigree.

So my working theory is that the broader stall between teleosemantic and rival naturalistic approaches—causal-informational, conceptual-role, and some enactivist variants—is not mainly about empirical biology but about what kind of disagreement it is. Is it a hidden empirical dispute about whether evolutionary history is necessary for functional norms? Is it verbal, with representation meaning one thing for those who tie content to selection history and another for those who tie it to present causal covariance or organizational closure? Or are the two sides actually answering different questions—one about the metaphysics of biological function, one about the semantics of mental states—under the single heading of naturalizing content? I want an honest diagnosis supported by what each camp actually says about misrepresentation and synthetic cases, not a survey of theories. And I want to know whether anything—say, a formal distinction between etiological and systemic function, an actual experiment with synthetic chemotactic circuits, or a reconceptualization of error that does not presuppose selection—could actually move the debate, or whether the impasse signals that the original question is ill-posed.
