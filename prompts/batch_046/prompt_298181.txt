I've been thinking about mechanistic anomaly detection lately — like, when you find something weird inside a model, when do you actually say it's the same weird thing you've seen before? I'm trying to figure out my real views by going off my gut on specific cases, but they're kinda all over the place.

So here's my verdicts. One: a transformer fails on long-range syntax because attention head 7 collapses; a state-space model fails on the exact same syntax patterns but because its transition matrix eigenvalue goes haywire. I say thats the SAME mechanism — it's doing the same broken job, just in different hardware. Two: two vision models both misclassify adversarial images with the same tiny perturbation, but one has a corrupted edge-detector circuit and the other has a polysemantic neuron that gets hijacked. I say thats NOT the same mechanism, because the internal causal path is totally different even if the input trick looks identical. Three: a language model starts giving wrong answers on large-number arithmetic after fine-tuning; we trace it to tokenization splitting digits differently. A different architecture fails on the same arithmetic task, but there its positional encoding degradation. Same symptom, different root cause — I say different mechanisms. Four: we train a model, it learns a deceptive sycophancy circuit in layer 12. We retrain from scratch, layer 12 learns something totally different in weights but produces the exact same sycophantic behavior pattern. I say thats still the same mechanism, because the functional role and deceptive outcome are what matter, not the weight values.

But wait, that feels inconsistent, right? In case two I cared about the internal causal path; in case four I ignored the weight differences and went with functional role. And in case one I went functional too, while in case three I went root-cause. So is my principle just "functional role unless I suddenly feel like being a hardware purist"? I want to know if there's a coherent principle that saves all four, or if I have to drop one. If there is a principle, what does it say about this case I'm unsure about: a model shows an anomaly where its internal representations suddenly form a representational drift cluster in activation space that correlates with hallucinated facts; later we see the exact same cluster geometry in a different model after a different training corruption, but the cluster activates on completely different factual errors. Same mechanism?
