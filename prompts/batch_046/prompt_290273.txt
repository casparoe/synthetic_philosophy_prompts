I am currently taking a graduate seminar on value theory and the ethics of future generations, and after our session on Samuel Scheffler's conjecture about the afterlife—the idea that part of why human extinction horrifies us is not merely the deaths involved but the disappearance of the continuing human world that lends meaning to our long-term projects—two of my classmates, Mara and Leo, became entangled in a disagreement that I have been unable to resolve. Mara began by insisting that Scheffler has identified something genuinely distinct from standard intergenerational concern: she argued that when we build scientific institutions, write literature, or raise children, we implicitly depend on a background expectation that human life will continue beyond our own death, and that extinction destroys not just future welfare but the very framework that makes present sacrifice intelligible. Leo conceded that this psychological dependence is real but claimed that Mara had misread Scheffler as offering an objective axiological claim when he was really describing a contingent feature of human motivation; if so, Leo said, the argument cannot explain why someone with no multi-generational commitments—say, a hermit with no artistic or scientific projects—should regard extinction as bad for reasons other than the direct deaths. Mara replied that Scheffler's point is structural rather than personal: even intimate projects like marriage or friendship depend on a stable social world that extends past one's lifespan, so the afterlife is not an optional attachment but a general condition of meaningful action. At this point Leo introduced a twist that neither of them had anticipated: he asked whether a scenario in which human biological life ends but our cultural and scientific traditions are seamlessly continued by synthetic successors—digital minds that genuinely inherit our values and extend our inquiries—would still count as a loss of Scheffler's afterlife. Mara said no, because Scheffler's conjecture is specifically about human continuation, not about the preservation of information or function; Leo countered that if Mara is right, then the horror of extinction reduces to a kind of biological species-loyalty that Scheffler never adequately defends, and if she is wrong—if functional continuation is enough—then our intuition that human extinction is specially catastrophic should weaken in cases where benevolent artificial successors take over, which it does not seem to do. They then circled back to the original issue: Leo claimed that Mara's defense makes the badness of extinction depend on an undefended anthropocentric premise, while Mara insisted that Leo's functionalist alternative collapses the distinction between extinction and transformation, thereby failing to account for the particular grief we feel at the prospect of humanity's absolute end. Could you work through their exchange step by step, identifying where each is correct, where each goes wrong, and what both are failing to notice—particularly regarding whether Scheffler's argument requires biological continuity or can be reformulated without it?
