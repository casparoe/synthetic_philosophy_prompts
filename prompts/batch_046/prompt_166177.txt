I'm putting together a two-page proposal for a philosophy-of-technology workshop and want to sharpen the framing before I draft the full document. The domain is the boundary between tool AI and agent AI, but I want to move past the standard taxonomy—bounded task execution versus open-ended goal pursuit—because I think it obscures something important about ontology identification.

My working thesis is this: any sufficiently capable tool system (for instance, a code-generation assistant or a scientific modeling interface) cannot remain useful without constructing and maintaining an implicit ontology of what the user treats as relevant, broken, or improved. This is not mere context-tracking; it is a structured, revisable representation of which world-states are worth bringing about. Once such a representation lives inside the system and guides its outputs, the system is exercising preference-like selection over possible futures, even if those preferences are entirely "borrowed" from the user. The precise philosophical question is whether "borrowed preference" is a coherent intermediate category, or whether any system that maintains a stable, feedback-sensitive, preference-weighted ontology is structurally an agent—just one with an unusually tight loop to a human operator.

The proposal needs four things. First, a precise statement of the research question that explains why it remains open: specifically, why current positions in AI alignment discourse and in philosophy of action do not settle whether the tool/agent distinction is ontological (about the kind of mental or functional state involved) or merely architectural (about where the feedback loop closes). Second, the method: since this is conceptual rather than empirical, describe what I would actually do over a three-month project—constructing boundary cases (e.g., a tool that updates its internal ontology without explicit user authorization versus one that freezes it), analyzing whether the distinction survives recursive self-modification of the ontology layer, and testing whether "borrowed" can be defined without collapsing into either pure instrumentality or full agency. Third, deliverables and failure conditions: a successful outcome would be a framework with explicit criteria for borrowed versus intrinsic preference structures; failure would be demonstrating that the distinction is purely pragmatic, with no philosophical work left to do once the engineering architecture is specified. Fourth, an honest scope assessment: why three months is sufficient for conceptual framing and case analysis but not for empirical validation, and which adjacent questions (for example, consciousness or moral status) I am deliberately bracketing.

Write this in a formal, polished register appropriate to an audience familiar with rationalist and alignment-oriented discourse, but do not include citations, named references, or a bibliography—I'll add those myself. The deliverable is not generic career advice but a defensible research plan with a clear angle that is not the standard one.
