I am a high school student writing an independent paper on the philosophical idea that complex competence can emerge without any corresponding comprehension. I am focusing on what has been called Darwin's and Turing's strange inversions of reasoning: Darwin explains the origin of apparently designed biological structures through a process that lacks all foresight, while Turing proposes that a machine could exhibit intelligent behavior without possessing any understanding.

Below is a paraphrase of a passage from Charles Darwin's On the Origin of Species (1859), Chapter 6, in which he addresses the formation of the eye:

Darwin asks how an organ of such extreme perfection and complexity as the eye could possibly have been formed by natural selection. He notes that it seems absurd to suppose that the eye, with its precise mechanisms for adjusting focus, admitting different amounts of light, and correcting optical imperfections, could have arisen by random chance alone. But he argues that if we admit the existence of numerous gradations from a simple light-sensitive spot to the fully formed eye, and if each intermediate stage conferred some advantage on its possessor, then natural selection could preserve each slight improvement across countless generations. The entire process occurs without any conscious design, without the organism understanding optics, and without nature intending the outcome. The result is an organ that performs its function with remarkable competence, produced entirely by mechanisms that comprehend nothing.

I want to understand exactly how this counts as an inversion of reasoning comparable to Turing's. Specifically:

1. Where precisely is the comprehension gap located in Darwin's account? Is it in the individual organism that varies, in the environment that selects, or in natural selection itself? And how does this differ from Turing's gap, which seems to be inside the single machine?

2. Darwin's explanation removes intention from the causal history, yet the eye clearly serves a purpose for the organism. Does this mean that the competence of the eye is only a retrospective attribution—we say it is competent because it survived—rather than an intrinsic property? And if so, does Turing's machine face a parallel problem: is its intelligent behavior only retrospectively attributed by observers, or is there a real difference?

3. Turing's inversion is often read as a challenge to the idea that comprehension is necessary for intelligent performance. Is Darwin's inversion making an analogous challenge about biological adaptation—that adaptation is possible without any adaptive intention—or is the parallel misleading because evolutionary time introduces a distributive element that Turing's machine lacks?

Please help me work through these questions conceptually; I want to understand the logical structure of the comparison rather than receive a summary of standard interpretations.
