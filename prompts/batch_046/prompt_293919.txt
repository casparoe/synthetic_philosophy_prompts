I recently inherited my late father’s workshop, a small space where he repaired antique clocks by hand using tools and methods passed down from his own father. The work has never been profitable; I keep it running partly out of obligation and partly because sitting at his bench and working in his slow, solitary manner feels like a form of continuing our relationship that has no real substitute. Last month, a museum consortium proposed to modernize the site entirely: they would replace the hand-tools with digital precision equipment, standardize the repair protocols, and open the workshop to the public as an educational center serving thousands of visitors each year. Their director—who knows me personally—argued that if I genuinely care about preserving horological knowledge and benefiting future learners, I ought to accept without hesitation. He said that my reluctance is just sentimental attachment, and that allowing an emotional bond to one particular place and one particular set of practices to block a project with far greater social value is a failure of practical reasoning. I responded that this workshop matters to me not because it scores highly on some general calculus of welfare, but precisely because it is my father’s, and that working there in his manner is an expression of a specific loyalty that cannot be redirected to any equivalent outcome. He then suggested that I am allowing exactly one thought—the thought that this is ours and not anyone else’s—to crowd out the only consideration that should properly guide the decision, as if I am treating my father like an irreplaceable resource rather than recognizing that any comparable good could serve the same purpose. I do not feel that I am being selfish, yet I cannot articulate why his framing seems to miss the point so fundamentally, as though he is asking me to relate to my father through an intermediate principle rather than directly. Is there a defensible philosophical distinction between honoring a particular bond in this unmediated way and being merely irrationally partial? And is there something structurally wrong with the kind of reasoning he is demanding I apply—specifically, with requiring that every reason for action pass through a universal assessment of value before it can be legitimate?
