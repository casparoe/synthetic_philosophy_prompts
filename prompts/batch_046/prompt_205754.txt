I am a retired experimental psychologist who, since leaving the laboratory, has taken up philosophy of mind with the same attention to concrete phenomena that guided my earlier work. I keep returning to the distinction—famously drawn in this literature—between Orwellian revisions, in which experience is delivered correctly and then rewritten in memory, and Stalinesque revisions, in which the brain edits the narrative before it ever reaches awareness, with the color phi phenomenon serving as the canonical example of the latter. My own judgments across several cases do not yet cohere. In the standard color phi display, where a red dot and a green dot appear in rapid succession at different positions and are experienced as one dot that travels and changes hue midway, I judge this Stalinesque: consciousness receives a constructed sequence, not a corrected record. In Libet-style measurements of the readiness potential, where neural preparation for movement precedes the reported moment of conscious decision, I judge this Orwellian: the subject genuinely decides, but misplaces the event in retrospective memory. The cutaneous rabbit illusion—taps delivered at separated points on the forearm felt as a continuous hopping stimulus—strikes me as Stalinesque, structurally parallel to the visual case. Yet in the flash-lag effect, where a moving object and a flashed object at the same location are perceived with the flash trailing behind, I am uncertain whether the spatial displacement is baked into the perceptual display or reconstructed afterward in the judgment I am asked to make.

What principle, if any, distinguishes the Stalinesque from the Orwellian across these cases? Does the boundary turn on whether the error concerns qualitative content versus temporal ordering, on whether it survives explicit warning and introspective scrutiny, or on something else entirely? I also want the principle tested against a case I have not settled: imagine a listener presented with a triad in which the middle tone is physically absent but perceptually restored, so that the chord sounds complete; upon replay with the tone actually present, the listener insists nothing was added the first time. Is this restoration Stalinesque, like the color phi, or does the denial upon replay mark it as Orwellian? I ask that the response state the principle with precision, identify which of my four verdicts it must overturn if coherence demands it, and specify what evidence or argument—perhaps a demonstration that Libet subjects can correct their temporal reports under feedback, or that cutaneous-rabbit subjects can introspectively access the discrete taps—would lead me to abandon the distinction altogether, and where the reasoning is most likely to fail.
