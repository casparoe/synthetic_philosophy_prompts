I'm drafting a scene for my novel in which a philosopher delivers the keynote at the dedication of a university archive named for Kurt Gödel. The audience is split between modal logicians steeped in his modal ontological argument and the benefactor's family, for whom Gödel means only incompleteness theorems. The speaker must make the argument do real structural work: she needs to explain that if a concept contains all positive properties then its object exists necessarily, while using that very distinction to show why this archive—funded, contingent, brick-and-mortar—is not that object. She must navigate the ambiguity of "positive" between its modal-logical sense and its everyday moral or aesthetic sense, so that the family does not hear destiny and the logicians do not hear category error. She should acknowledge, without citation or bibliography, that Gödel's original notes required reconstruction by others, because the experts will notice its absence and the family will need it translated into plain language. The speech should be formal, roughly five to seven minutes, and must comfort or move the non-philosophers without saying anything the philosophers in the room would consider false.
