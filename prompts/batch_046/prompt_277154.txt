I have no formal training in typography, philosophy, or cognitive science, but I have been thinking on my own about a question that keeps returning to me: what makes every 'a' an 'a'? Whether it appears in a children's primer, a warning sign, or an elaborate calligraphic script, we recognize it instantly, even when its weight, curvature, and ornament are wildly transformed. To make sense of this, I have developed a personal idea I call the "refusal boundary" of a letterform: an 'a' can vary almost without limit, yet there seems to be a point of no return—if its upper enclosed counter-space is closed off entirely, or if it abandons its characteristic enclosed bowl, it stops being an 'a' and becomes something else (perhaps an 'o' or a 'q'). So I suspect that the essence of a letter lies not in any positive feature it must possess, but in the specific transformations it can refuse and still remain itself.
