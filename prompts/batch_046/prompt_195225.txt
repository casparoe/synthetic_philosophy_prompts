Two colleagues, Maya and Leo, are arguing about the derivation usually called Fitch's paradox of knowability. Maya opens by laying out the core argument: if every true proposition is in principle knowable, then for any unknown truth q one can form the conjunction q and not-known q; if anyone could know that conjunction, they would know q, which contradicts the second conjunct, so this conjunction is in principle unknowable, violating the knowability thesis itself. From this she concludes that there must in fact be no unknown truths—omnisience follows—so the knowability principle is false, not mysterious.

Leo agrees with the formal steps but insists Maya misplaces the blame. The derivation, he says, depends essentially on closure for knowledge under conjunction: if an agent knows A and knows B, the agent knows A-and-B. Only with that closure can one move from knowing the conjunction to knowing q, generating the contradiction. Leo suggests that if we model knowledge with resource-sensitive closure—where an agent might know two propositions separately without being able to integrate them into a single known conjunction because of cognitive or architectural limits—the contradiction never arises, and the knowability principle survives.

Maya replies that Leo confuses practical cognitive limits with logical possibility. The knowability thesis invokes what is possible in principle, not what any actual brain can process; even for an idealized agent with unlimited time, knowing q while also knowing that q is unknown remains contradictory. Moving the problem into a "resource-sensitive" logic, she argues, merely renames the contradiction rather than dissolving it.

Leo then introduces a further distinction he thinks Maya ignores: between individual knowledge and distributed collective knowledge. He asks her to imagine a research collective in which one subgroup knows q and a separate subgroup holds the verified record that no individual member has certified q. No single mind knows both conjuncts, so closure fails at the collective level without any individual being resource-limited. He takes this to show that Fitch's argument tacitly assumes an individualist model of the knowing subject, and that once we allow genuinely distributed epistemic architectures, there can be unknown truths that remain collectively discoverable without anyone being able to form the fatal conjunction.

Maya counters that Leo has not escaped the paradox but relocated it. The knowability principle is standardly read as saying that for any true p there exists some possible agent—perhaps an idealized or collective one—who knows p. If we allow distributed agents, then we should also allow a "super-observer" who aggregates both subgroups; such an observer could know q and also know that q lacked individual certification, thereby knowing the conjunction and reviving the contradiction. So Leo's collective architecture either collapses back into an individual-like knowing subject or it arbitrarily restricts the domain of possible agents to avoid the result.

Leo responds that the super-observer is not the same agent with the same properties; merging distributed architectures creates a new cognitive system with different verification conditions, not a single mind that transparently inherits both pieces of knowledge. He adds that there may be physically grounded boundaries—such as the impossibility of one brain instantiating two incompatible verification protocols—to closure that are independent of the paradox and that block the derivation without abandoning the intuition that truth is ultimately discoverable. He concedes, however, that he owes an independent account of why those boundaries are principled rather than ad hoc.

Maya closes by pressing exactly that point: without an independent motivation for treating collective architecture as a hard modal boundary, Leo's move looks like a tailored restriction designed solely to save the knowability principle. She also asks whether his position commits him to denying logical omniscience altogether, and whether that denial can be made precise without undermining ordinary knowledge claims.
