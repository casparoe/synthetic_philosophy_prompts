I serve on the advisory council of the Meridian International Writers' Residency, and I am writing to ask you to draft the written opinion I will circulate before next Thursday's meeting. We must decide whether to evaluate an application for our annual translation fellowship: the submitted work is a novel originally composed in an obscure dialect, rendered entirely by statistical neural translation with no human translator involved. The applicant contends that the output preserves the emotional architecture and narrative rhythm of the source, and that insisting on a human intermediary amounts to arbitrary gatekeeping against technological progress. Several members, including our chair, are inclined to accept the submission as legitimate literature if it reads well, while others hold that admitting it would betray our founding commitment to cross-cultural understanding rather than algorithmic approximation. I need a formal opinion recommending a concrete course of action—whether to evaluate the work, reject it outright, or establish a provisional category—together with a clear time frame for implementation, and it must treat the applicant's position with genuine seriousness rather than dismissing it as mere machinery. As a novelist, my instinct is that translation is not merely the transfer of information but the transmission of a consciousness encountering language; yet I want the reasoning to be rigorous rather than sentimental. The opinion should be composed in continuous prose, without headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, as one writes a formal letter or email, and it should avoid requesting citations, bibliographies, or reading lists, since I will supply any references separately. Please engage the philosophical stakes directly: does statistical translation, which operates through pattern-matching across vast corpora, achieve genuine semantic understanding or only produce a convincing surface? To avoid the usual stalemate, introduce a sharper conceptual distinction—perhaps between functional equivalence, which concerns whether the text produces the appropriate readerly effects, and intentional provenance, which concerns whether the meaning-bearing features were selected by a mind that understood them—and use it to structure the recommendation. Finally, include an honest account of the strongest consideration on the losing side, so that the council can see that its dissent has been heard.
