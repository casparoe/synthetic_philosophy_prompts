Here is roughly where I have landed on health, disease and dysfunction, and I should like a consistency audit before presenting it in a philosophy-of-medicine seminar. I hold: (1) a broadly naturalist account of disease—disease is internal mechanistic dysfunction that lowers survival or reproduction relative to species-typical levels, and mental conditions count as diseases only when there is identifiable biological dysregulation, not merely suffering or social maladaptation; (2) that dysfunction is inherently normative, since malfunction is failure relative to a standard of proper functioning fixed by evolutionary design or system-level goals, not by statistical rarity alone; (3) that social values and norms of harm should play no part in the concept of disease itself, though they may legitimately guide treatment priorities—so I reject hybrid analyses that require both dysfunction and socially evaluated harm; (4) that in public-health policy we ought to classify conditions such as obesity and addiction as diseases primarily because of the suffering and social disadvantage they cause, regardless of whether they involve clear failure of internal mechanisms relative to evolutionary design; and (5) that health is simply the absence of disease, with no independent positive normative component—there is no "ideal functioning" beyond the mechanistic and statistical baseline. Which pairs are genuinely in tension, which only look so, and what is the least costly revision in each case? Please rank the conflicts by severity, indicate for each what must give, and say which single change would resolve the most.
