In my graduate seminar on ancient political philosophy, the debate over Aristotle's doctrine of natural slavery has reached an impasse that strikes me as structurally interesting rather than merely stubborn. For decades, commentators have divided sharply over the argument in Politics I.4–7: one camp reads Aristotle as advancing a genuinely normative thesis—that certain human beings lack the deliberative part of the soul and therefore flourish only under the rule of a master—while another camp insists that the passage is either an unfortunate empirical generalization about barbarian societies or a rhetorical concession to Greek convention that sits uneasily with his broader hylomorphism and with the universal rational soul affirmed elsewhere in De Anima and the Nicomachean Ethics. What makes the stall puzzling is that both sides now command exhaustive textual evidence—references to the slave's possession of logos without deliberative capacity, to the slave functioning as a living instrument, to the naturalness of rule—yet neither side treats any new citation as decisive. My working hypothesis is that the disagreement is not primarily about Aristotle's text at all, but about a hidden normative premise: whether teleological function can ground political subordination without requiring the subordinate's rational consent. That is, the normative camp treats functional deficiency as sufficient for legitimate domination, while the apologetic camp assumes that any legitimate authority over a rational being requires at least the potential for autonomous deliberation—a premise drawn more from modern moral philosophy than from Aristotle's own framework. Is this diagnosis correct? Is the deadlock verbal—turning on whether natural means by innate constitution or according to cosmic order—or substantive, involving incompatible standards about what counts as adequate textual evidence for attributing a view to an author? And given your diagnosis, is there a conceptual distinction—perhaps between instrumental and intrinsic teleology, or between political and economic rule—that could actually move the debate, or should we conclude that the question is ill-posed because it forces Aristotle's biological metaphysics into a conceptual framework he would not recognize?
