I am a senior at a public magnet school and serve on our student governance council, and for an independent study in political philosophy I have been working through Aristotle’s treatment of exchange and acquisition in the Politics. Our school operates a student cooperative store that uses an internal labor-credit system rather than cash: students earn “work-points” by staffing the shop or maintaining shared spaces, and these points can be exchanged for goods at a fixed rate, but the bylaws impose both a hard accumulation ceiling and a monthly decay rate on unused balances. The explicit rationale is that points are designed to circulate as a measure of contribution, not to be accumulated or hoarded, yet several council members now argue that the very existence of a fungible, quantifiable medium within the school treats educational resources as commodities and introduces an artificial scarcity that conflicts with our mission.

I would like you to reconstruct what philosophical theory of money and exchange this institutional design actually presupposes, rather than simply labeling it Aristotelian or not. Does the combination of labor-based issuance, a fixed exchange-rate, and an anti-hoarding decay mechanism implicitly commit the policy to a distinction between natural acquisition (oikonomikē) and unnatural chrematistikē, or does the presence of any quantifiable exchange-medium necessarily presuppose the view Aristotle criticizes—that money can become an end in itself, even if we try to constrain it by rule? Please identify the specific commitments about value, function, and proper limit that make this policy coherent; say whether those commitments form a genuinely Aristotelian view or only borrow its vocabulary; indicate which rival accounts—such as a purely conventionalist or demand-based theory of value—the design quietly rules out; and explain what concrete features of the cooperative (the ceiling, the decay, the labor-link, or the fixed rate) would have to be abandoned or redesigned if we replaced the underlying theory with a modern contractualist view that treats exchange as a matter of mutual consent rather than natural teleological function.
