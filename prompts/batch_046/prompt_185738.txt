I’ve been chewing on this idea after a seminar on ancient biography—what if Plutarch’s Parallel Lives aren’t really about giving you heroes to copy, but about using the paired Greek/Roman lives as a kind of moral calibration tool? Like, you see how the same drive (say, ambition or stubbornness) gets expressed in Alcibiades versus Coriolanus, and the gap between the two is what lets you adjust your own sense of proportion. Hagiography messes this up precisely because it kills the comparison: you get one glowing figure with no shadow-counter-part, so their’s no dissonance to calibrate against. So my working view is that biography teaches morality through comparative dissonance rather than imitation, and that hagiography is actually anti-pedagogical in this specific frame.

What would have to be true for that to be wrong? I want defeaters that actually target the calibration claim, not just generic “people learn by copying heroes” pushback. For instance: psychological evidence that readers extract norms from single narratives just fine without pairing; historical cases where hagiographic texts clearly produced moral refinement; or the possibility that I’ve defined comparison so tightly that any counterexample gets dismissed. Rank them from fatal to awkward, say which ones a defender should concede versus dig in on, and flag the weak spot in my own position that I shouldn’t pretend is stronger than it is. I’m particularly worried about whether narrative moral learning can happen at all without some comparative baseline—even if it’s implicit—because if single-figure admiration works independently, the whole calibration story collapses.
