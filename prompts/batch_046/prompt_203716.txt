I am expecting my first child, and our genetic counselor has presented options that blur a line I assumed was firm: correcting a pathogenic variant versus selecting for traits such as height or cognitive profile that fall well within current human ranges. Before I can think clearly about consent and moral obligation in this context, I need a rigorous specification of what actually distinguishes therapy from enhancement—not a slogan, but a theory with genuine criteria.

Write the desiderata that any adequate account of the therapy/enhancement distinction must satisfy. For each requirement, state the motivating case or conceptual pressure behind it, and flag whether it is non-negotiable or a preference of a particular camp. The list must cover: (i) conditions that are statistically atypical but not harmful to the bearer, and whether identity-constitutive traits can be therapeutically modified without contradiction; (ii) the difference between restoring an individual’s own prior functioning and optimizing relative to a population statistical norm; (iii) intergenerational baseline drift, where therapeutic interventions applied to parents redefine what counts as normal functioning for the child; (iv) whether the boundary must remain stable across different social contexts or is inherently relational; and (v) consistency with biological results on phenotypic plasticity and gene-environment interaction.

Then identify whether any subset of these requirements appears jointly unsatisfiable—particularly whether a theory can both honor identity-constitutive traits and maintain a context-independent, stable boundary. Briefly indicate how the normal-functioning model, a welfare-maximizing account, and a morphological-freedom view fare against the list, but keep the weight on the criteria and their motivating cases rather than on scoring the contenders.
