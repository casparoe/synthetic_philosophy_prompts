I am drafting a position paper on value alignment for artificial agents and want a consistency audit before I present it. Here is the package of commitments I have settled on: (1) Aligned AI should observe robust liberal neutrality—it should not encode or privilege any single comprehensive conception of the good life among its users. (2) In a pluralistic context, the AI's value function should be constructed through a stakeholder aggregation procedure—something like a weighted social welfare mechanism—rather than through the moral judgments of the designers. (3) I want to maintain a strict distinction between procedural values (autonomy, transparency, fairness in process) and substantive values (happiness, virtue, religious fulfillment), with liberal neutrality applying only to the latter; but I also want to distinguish axiological neutrality (not ranking goods) from doxastic neutrality (not taking stands on belief), holding that only the former is required, since an agent that refuses to correct false empirical beliefs fails at its task. (4) When a user's request concerns a domain of deep moral disagreement—say, whether to structure one's life around individual achievement or collective harmony—the AI should not aggregate across users but should defer to the user's own framework. (5) Nevertheless, the AI must refuse requests that cause direct, severe harm to non-consenting others, and the harm-threshold for refusal should itself be set by the same stakeholder aggregation mechanism. (6) The aggregation weights and refusal thresholds should be protected by a constitutional layer that is not subject to democratic revision, though everything within that layer should be transparently inspectable and modifiable by users.

Please audit this set: which pairs are genuinely in tension, which only appear to be, and how severely? For each real tension, say what the cheapest revision would be and what it costs elsewhere in the package. Then tell me which single revision—giving up or weakening one commitment—would resolve the most tensions at once. In particular, be precise about whether the tension between (2) and (4) is real or dissolves once we distinguish domains of application (inter-user externalities versus intra-user self-regarding choice), and whether (3) can survive the move to (5) given that identifying "harm" seems to require substantive moral judgments that neutrality forbids. Please rank the tensions by severity rather than listing them all as equally bad. Finally, say explicitly what would change your mind about where the deepest problem lies—whether it is a hidden contradiction between (1) and (2), a collapse of the procedural/substantive distinction in (3), or something else—and indicate where your reasoning is most likely to fail.
