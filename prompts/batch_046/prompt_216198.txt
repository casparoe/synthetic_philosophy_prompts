I am trying to understand whether the socialist calculation debate actually predicts anything about real-world planning experiments, and I keep running into the same abstract summaries of Mises, Hayek, and Lange without seeing the theory disciplined by an actual institutional design. I would like you to analyze Project Cybersyn—the cybernetic economic management system built for Salvador Allende’s government in Chile between 1971 and 1973—through the lens of that debate.

Here is the episode with the details that matter: Stafford Beer designed a telex network linking state-owned factories to a central operations room in Santiago, transmitting production data daily; algorithms flagged deviations and triggered corrective directives downward; Beer embedded this in a recursive organizational model with autonomous factory-level nodes and escalating algedonic alerts—pain-signals that rose through the hierarchy when local managers could not resolve a bottleneck. The system was never fully scaled, and in October 1972 a nationwide truckers’ strike, combined with U.S. economic pressure and internal supply sabotage, paralyzed distribution; the project collapsed with the coup in September 1973. Among specialists it remains contested whether Cybersyn ever operated at meaningful scale and whether its failure was primarily computational, organizational, or political.

Using the socialist calculation debate—Mises’s argument that centralized planning lacks rational economic calculation because it cannot aggregate dispersed knowledge into a common unit; Hayek’s further claim that price signals coordinate tacit, local knowledge that no central authority can possess; and Lange’s market-socialist reply that competitive trial-and-error pricing can substitute for full market mechanisms—map the theory onto Cybersyn precisely: identify who counts as the planner, what if anything functions as the price signal, and whether Beer’s rapid feedback loops address the aggregation problem or merely accelerate central direction without solving it. Say explicitly where the theory explains Cybersyn’s design choices and where it is silent—especially on whether real-time data transmission solves Hayek’s knowledge problem or only masks it behind faster bureaucracy. Then run the analysis in reverse: if Cybersyn’s collapse was driven mainly by the truckers’ strike, foreign embargo, and the 1973 coup rather than by planners’ inability to compute inputs and outputs, does that undermine the debate’s relevance, or does it confirm Hayek’s broader claim that planning is politically unstable regardless of computational capacity? In particular, address whether Beer’s cybernetic architecture creates a hybrid coordination mechanism that neither Lange’s iterative pricing nor Hayek’s spontaneous order properly anticipated.

In your response, separate what is settled among economic historians and philosophers of economics—for instance, that Cybersyn was partially operational with several hundred factories linked, or that Lange’s model requires market-like price adjustments—from what is genuinely contested, such as whether cybernetic feedback counts as a non-market substitute for prices, or whether the Chilean collapse was an appropriate empirical test of planning at all. Do not present a contested interpretation—whether that Beer’s system fully solved the calculation problem, or that the coup alone explains the failure without bearing on planning feasibility—as though it were the consensus view.
