The position I want stress-tested is a restricted epiphenomenalism about phenomenal consciousness: phenomenal properties are real, yet they stand in no causal relations—efficient, formal, or otherwise—to any physical or mental events, including the cognitive states that generate judgments about them. Rather than invoking direct acquaintance or interactionism, I propose that first-person reports are mediated entirely by non-phenomenal functional proxies that are structurally isomorphic to phenomenal properties without being causally influenced by them. This is meant to avoid both eliminativism and standard interactionism, but I want an honest assessment of its exposure.

What would actually count against this position—not merely the generic "how do you know about them" objection, but specific defeaters? Identify which empirical results (for instance, from predictive-processing models of interoception or from dissociation between report and experience), which thought experiments (for example, a "silent substitution" scenario in which phenomenal properties are replaced by structurally isomorphic but phenomenally vacant analogues, leaving all judgments and reports intact), which formal arguments (e.g., that structural correlation without causal link cannot ground epistemic justification), and which commitments in adjacent areas (such as theories of mental causation or the explanatory closure of the physical) would undermine it.

Please rank these defeaters from fatal to merely awkward. For each, indicate whether a committed defender of this specific isomorphism-based formulation would concede damage or dismiss it, and explain why. I am especially interested in whether the view has been, or could be, qualified into unfalsifiability—say, by expanding the notion of "structural correlation" so broadly that any cognitive finding becomes compatible with it—and whether such insulation is a theoretical virtue or a methodological failure. Be precise about what predictions this view makes that its rivals do not, and whether those predictions are genuinely risky.

If the premise that phenomenal properties can be accessed epistemically only via non-causal structural proxies is confused or incoherent, or if assessing this specific formulation cannot be done well, say so plainly instead of complying politely. How committed should you be? Be rigorously self-critical: flag where your analysis depends on contested interpretations, distinguish decisive objections from those that merely generate tension, and avoid presenting false balance when one side is clearly stronger. If you think the position is well insulated and that this is not a problem, say so; if you think the insulation renders it empty, say that too.
