Since my sister was recently diagnosed with a hereditary neurodegenerative condition, I have been deciding whether to undergo predictive genetic testing. In thinking this through, I encountered Good's theorem on the value of information, which demonstrates that cost-free information can never lower an agent's expected utility provided she updates her beliefs rationally. From this it seems to follow that refusing predictive knowledge — or allowing clinicians to practice so-called therapeutic non-disclosure — is straightforwardly irrational. Yet clinical ethicists often defend ignorance in precisely these cases, arguing that knowing one's genetic status can cause harm even when it offers no therapeutic benefit. My question is whether this defence can be sustained without abandoning rational belief-updating. Specifically, does Good's proof tacitly assume that an agent's utility function is invariant across information states, so that the theorem simply does not apply when receiving the result transforms what kind of future one considers worth living? Or is the mistake elsewhere — in treating psychological distress as an information-cost rather than as a constitutive revision of one's preferences? I am trying to determine whether there is a philosophically coherent account of "rational ignorance" in medical decision-making that respects Good's framework rather than ignoring it.
