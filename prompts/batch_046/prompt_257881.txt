I hold that in genuinely armed-neutral states universal conscription is not merely an instrumental defense arrangement but a constitutive democratic institution: the citizen-militia prevents the armed forces from becoming a separate caste and binds the state's monopoly on violence directly to the democratic body, which means that armed neutrality is conceptually coherent only when paired with conscription rather than with a professional standing army. I treat Switzerland's militia-based system as the pure case, and I view recent shifts in Sweden and Finland—toward alliance membership and adjustments to conscription models—not merely as strategic adaptations but as conceptual dissolutions of the link between democratic citizenship and neutrality. What would genuinely count against this position? I want the arguments, historical counterexamples, and conceptual distinctions that should move me, ordered by how decisive they would be: whether democratic control can be structurally guaranteed without universal service, whether geographic or technological conditions can make mass conscription obsolete while preserving neutrality, and whether alliance membership necessarily severs the democratic-military link I describe. I also want an honest assessment of whether defenders of this view have qualified it into unfalsifiability—for instance, by treating any functioning professionalized neutrality as secretly not genuine neutrality—and whether that insulation is a defect for a view of this kind.
