I've been working through Dennett's distinction between original and derived intentionality in my philosophy of mind seminar, and I'm inclined to think that his two-bitser thought experiment shows Searle's Chinese Room argument misses the point: if two sub-systems, each with only derived intentionality, can jointly produce behavior that we have no choice but to interpret as genuinely understanding, then the absence of intrinsic semantics in any single component is not the fatal flaw Searle takes it to be. What I want to know is what would genuinely count against this position—not just the usual reply that all derived intentionality ultimately traces back to an original intentional source, but which specific intuitions about phenomenal consciousness, which results in distributed cognition or computational architecture, and which commitments about the unity of the subject it risks violating. Would a defender concede that the lack of a single, unified center of understanding is a serious cost, or dismiss it as a category mistake inherited from Cartesian theater? And among the possible defeaters—say, the claim that genuine understanding requires a unified phenomenal subject, or that any two-bitser decomposition always collapses back into a single interpreter—which should be ranked as fatal and which merely awkward?
