In the debate over phenomenal versus access consciousness, global workspace theory is standardly interpreted as accounting for the latter: information becomes globally available for reasoning, reporting, and control. The familiar open problems include whether workspace broadcasting can ever be sufficient for phenomenal experience and whether phenomenal content can overflow broadcast capacity. I want to focus on a less discussed framing: rather than asking how integration produces phenomenal coherence, ask whether phenomenal coherence might be what remains when workspace integration fragments or suppresses experience—making phenomenal consciousness not the output of global broadcasting but its absence or failure mode. If that framing is coherent, then the central research question shifts from sufficiency to dissociation: under what conditions does integration fail to suppress phenomenal coherence, and what empirical signatures would distinguish a state of pure access (no phenomenal residue) from a state where phenomenal coherence survives integration failure? Given this, which open questions or lines of work in this area matter most—especially for determining whether phenomenal and access consciousness come apart in principle? Prioritize them: which are upstream, such that resolving them would settle other debates; which are tractable with current empirical or conceptual tools; and which should be deprioritized because their answer would not change the core picture? Defend the ordering with explicit criteria.
