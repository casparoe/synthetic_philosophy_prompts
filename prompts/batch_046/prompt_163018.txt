Here is an argument a friend walked me into, and I cannot find the exit. (1) Popper's demarcation criterion holds that a claim is scientific only if it is empirically falsifiable—there must be some possible observation that, were it made, would rationally compel us to abandon the claim. (2) The criterion itself, call it R, is not an empirical prediction about nature but a philosophical rule about classification; no observation about the world could show R false, since R does not predict anything about the world. (3) So R is not empirically falsifiable. (4) By (1), R therefore falls outside science; it does not belong to the domain it defines. (5) If R lies outside science, then adopting or rejecting it is not an internal scientific decision but an external methodological choice. (6) But an external methodological choice carries no special rational authority over the practice it regulates; scientists may revise or discard it without violating any norm internal to science. (7) Therefore scientists are free to reject R and continue working with theories that accommodate every observation—frameworks that adjust hidden parameters after the fact—while still counting themselves as scientific. (8) So R either condemns itself and collapses, or exempts itself and becomes arbitrary; in neither case does it actually demarcate science from pseudoscience. I would like to keep (1) and (4) if possible, but I feel something slips between (4) and (6)—perhaps being outside science does not mean being powerless over it? I cannot name the mistake precisely. Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost?
