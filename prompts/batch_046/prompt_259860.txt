You chair the International Commission on Stratigraphy's Subcommission on Quaternary Stratigraphy ad hoc Working Group on Anthropocene Formalization. By the end of next quarter you must circulate a recommendation memo to the full Subcommission: should the Anthropocene be ratified as a formal chronostratigraphic epoch with a Global Boundary Stratotype Section and Point anchored at Crawford Lake, Ontario—using the plutonium-239/240 fallout peak around 1952 as the primary marker—should it be rejected and instead treated as a diachronous event or interval nested within the Holocene, or should formalization be deferred pending further criteria?

The case before you is not merely taxonomic. It raises a philosophical problem about what makes a geological period real: stratigraphic units are traditionally defined by globally synchronous, physically preserved signals that exist independently of the classifiers who name them, yet the Anthropocene's defining feature is the intentional, regionally asynchronous transformation of Earth systems by the very species now proposing the boundary. Some members of your working group raise what they call "stratigraphic presentism"—the epistemic circularity of using present-day observers to classify their own present as a distinct epoch—and they note that the so-called Great Acceleration lacks the single, globally simultaneous lithological signature that marks boundaries such as the Cretaceous-Paleogene iridium layer. Others reply that plutonium fallout, microplastic horizons, and concrete-derived technofossils form a durable, detectable stratigraphic package that future geologists could identify without reference to human intent, and that refusing epoch status risks reducing a genuinely novel planetary force to a merely cultural narrative.

Draft the working group's decision memo, recommending one of the three courses—ratify with the Crawford Lake GSSP, reject and subsume within the Holocene, or defer with specified conditions—and provide reasoning the Subcommission could act on. The memo must treat the strongest argument on the losing side with intellectual honesty: if you recommend ratification, address whether the boundary's observer-dependence and regional asynchronicity undermine its objectivity as a stratigraphic unit; if you recommend rejection or deferral, address whether denying epoch status amounts to an anthropocentric refusal to recognize a stratigraphically real transformation. Include one concrete procedural safeguard—such as a mandatory decadal review clause, a dual-marker verification requirement, or an explicit normative disclaimer—that responds directly to the philosophical tension rather than sidestepping it. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; the recipient needs only the conceptual reasoning and the actionable bottom line.
