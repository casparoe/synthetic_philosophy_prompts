I have just concluded a rather protracted argument with a close friend over dinner about the limits of rational acceptance, and I should very much like to know whether the position I defended is genuinely coherent or whether I was merely trading on incompatible intuitions. Roughly, my commitments are these: (1) I take the lottery paradox to show that rational belief cannot be closed under logical conjunction when the set is sufficiently large, yet I do not regard this as undermining the knowledge norm for flat-out assertion; (2) in the preface case, I maintain that the author rationally asserts each individual chapter-claim while also rationally asserting that at least one error is present, because the preface statement expresses a meta-epistemic policy of humility rather than a first-order belief that competes with the conjunction; (3) I want to reject any sharp numerical threshold—say, at 999 or 10,000 tickets—where acceptance suddenly becomes irrational, since such a boundary looks entirely ad hoc; (4) at the same time, I think that if one genuinely accepts that every ticket in a fair 100,000-ticket draw will lose, one ought to be willing to accept a simultaneous bet against all of them, which appears to generate a Dutch book. Does this package hang together? Specifically, does my refusal of a sharp cutoff in (3) undermine the distinction I draw in (2) between first-order and meta-epistemic attitudes, and does the Dutch-book concern in (4) force me to abandon the non-closure principle in (1) in favour of a more radical scepticism? I should be grateful for an audit ordered by severity: which pairs of commitments are genuinely in tension, which are only apparently so, and what the least damaging revision would be should I need to surrender one. Please treat this as an examination of my particular combination rather than a general survey of the literature.
