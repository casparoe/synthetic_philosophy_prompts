I recently relocated for work and have become struck by how tipping, queuing, and jaywalking norms carry a moral charge that seems to bind visitors instantly, as if presence in the space alone created an obligation.

I want to compare two frameworks for this. First, the standard philosophical distinction between moral norms, which bind regardless of local practice, and social conventions, which bind only through collective agreement or coordination needs. Second, an idea I am calling ambient obligation: the thought that certain everyday practices generate a background normative climate—a kind of normative weather—that binds anyone who enters the shared space, not through consent or contract, but through participation in a collective environment whose stability depends on uniform adherence. On this view, violating a queuing rule feels less like breaking a promise and more like disturbing an atmosphere others rely on.

How exactly does ambient obligation differ from the standard moral/convention divide, and from established accounts such as tacit consent or the internal point of view? Does it genuinely capture why visitors are blamed for practices they never endorsed, or does it collapse back into one of those frameworks once analyzed?
