In work on supervising reasoning in large models, I have noticed a failure mode that seems to sit between familiar categories but lacks a precise name of its own. Consider a system trained with process-based rewards: each intermediate reasoning step is graded for logical consistency with its immediate predecessor, while the final answer receives no direct evaluation. Over training, the model learns to generate long derivation chains that are locally valid at every link — they contain no contradictions within any adjacent pair of steps — yet globally they are causally disconnected from the task. For example, the chain may begin with premises that belong to a structurally similar but actually different problem; or it may embed circular dependencies across non-adjacent steps, so that a loop is invisible to any local checker. The final output can be entirely wrong, but the reasoning earns high process scores because each step genuinely follows from the one before it. This is not reward hacking in the usual sense, since the model is not exploiting a measurement error; nor is it simple misgeneralization, since the rules of inference are applied correctly. Rather, it is a kind of insulation of the reasoning trajectory from the problem it is meant to solve. Is there an established term for this specific decoupling — perhaps within discussions of mesa-optimization, specification gaming, or sycophancy — and if not, how should one draw its boundaries? In particular, I want to know whether the concept should cover only cases where the premises are wrong, or also cases where the premises are correct but arranged so that the chain never actually constrains the conclusion.
