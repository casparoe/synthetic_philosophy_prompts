I'm a novelist preparing a single ninety-minute workshop for six fellow fiction writers—people who spend their days constructing vivid counterfactual worlds but have no formal training in metaphysics or modal logic. I want to teach them why conceivability is not a reliable guide to metaphysical possibility, using the tension between narrative imagination and modal reality as the through-line, and specifically grounding the session in a counterfactual plot device that one of them is currently drafting: a consciousness that persists after the complete physical destruction of its original substrate, imagined with perfect narrative coherence. Plan the explanatory arc: first, the distinction that must be in place before the central problem can even be articulated—psychological conceivability versus logical or metaphysical possibility, and why a novelist's "what if" operates differently from a philosopher's "could it be"; second, the one concrete scenario that should carry the entire session—likely the substrate-independent consciousness case, given that it is already emotionally available to the group, but specify what properties it must have and what it must lack, and explain why I should avoid the standard philosophical examples such as water-not-being-H2O or Hesperus and Phosphorus for this audience; third, the misconceptions they will form in predictable order—especially the slide from "I can vividly picture it" to "the universe permits it," and the further slide to "if a story is internally consistent, its ontology is possible"—with the exact point in the sequence where each should be headed off; fourth, a single check for understanding that reveals whether they have grasped the gap between imagination and possibility rather than merely learned to parrot skepticism about fiction; fifth, what I should openly refuse to explain at this level—perhaps the machinery of rigid designation, two-dimensional semantics, or the essentialist response to Kripke—and state that refusal explicitly in the plan. Throughout, separate what is settled about the failure of naive conceivability arguments from what remains genuinely contested among philosophers who work on modal epistemology, and do not present any contested view—such as whether a refined, restricted version of conceivability can survive post-Kripkean objections—as though it were the consensus.
