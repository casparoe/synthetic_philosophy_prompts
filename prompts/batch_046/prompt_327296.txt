I'm preparing a sequence of interview questions for a philosophy podcast. Next week's guest is an unnamed philosopher who defends a grammatical, non-cognitivist reading of Wittgenstein on religious belief. On her view, religious pictures—such as the image of the Last Judgment—are neither empirical hypotheses open to evidential confirmation or falsification nor mere expressions of feeling or commitment. Rather, they function as regulative frameworks that organize reasoning, moral orientation, and practical commitment within a specific form of life. She insists this is not fideism: the framework can be criticized internally, through incoherence or failure to sustain the practices it regulates, but it cannot be overturned by external scientific or historical evidence. She distinguishes sharply between belief-in, understood as a relation of trust or commitment, and belief-that, understood as propositional assent, arguing that religious belief is primarily the former though it may involve the latter derivatively.

She anticipates three standard objections: that her view makes religious belief immune to rational criticism; that it collapses into relativism where any framework is equally valid; and that it cannot account for the phenomenology of belief, which often feels like assent to specific historical or metaphysical claims.

Please draft eight to ten interview questions, each paired with a brief follow-up keyed to her most likely reply. I want at least one question pressing the boundary between internal and external criticism—specifically, whether historical evidence against the resurrection could ever destabilize a Christian form of life from within, or whether it would necessarily be interpreted through the framework itself. I want at least one question on the belief-in versus belief-that distinction and whether it can handle doctrinal disputes—such as disagreement over the real presence in the Eucharist—without reducing them to differences in practice. And I want at least one question asking her to price the cost of her view: if religious pictures are genuinely non-representational, what distinguishes them from powerful but false metaphors, and must she concede that abandoning the picture is not a cognitive error but only a change of form of life?
