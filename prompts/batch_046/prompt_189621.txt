I hold that gradual mind uploading—where neural substrates are replaced incrementally by synthetic analogues—preserves personal identity only under what I call a monotonic integration constraint: there must be a continuous, non-branching causal pathway linking mental states across every intermediate stage, such that at no point do two equally legitimate continuers exist. This distinguishes the view from both psychological-continuity accounts, which allow replication, and bodily-continuity accounts, which require biological substrate persistence; it also implies that the standard teletransporter scenario destroys identity not because of psychological discontinuity, but because replication introduces a causal branch. I want an honest map of this position's exposure. What observations, conceptual arguments, or engineered scenarios would genuinely defeat it—rather than merely complicate it? Rank them by decisiveness: for instance, empirical evidence that neural replacement can occur with reversible interruptions that still preserve self-concern; formal arguments that causation in distributed cognitive systems is not the kind of thing that can be monotonic; or thought experiments in which a single pathway splits and then reconverges, testing whether branching is genuinely fatal or only temporarily awkward. Include what a defender of monotonic integration would concede as damaging, and assess whether the view risks being insulated by ad hoc qualifications—say, redefining branching post hoc—or whether it makes risky predictions that distinguish it from its rivals. Finally, does the position collapse into a redescription of familiar theories once its conditions are fully articulated, and is that collapse a problem for a view of this kind?
