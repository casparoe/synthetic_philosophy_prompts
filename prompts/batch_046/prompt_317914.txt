In our philosophy seminar, two classmates—Maya and Leo—disagreed about whether a single "I" can be distributed across two brains, drawing on Douglas Hofstadter's view that the self is not a private substance but a pattern that can persist partly in other minds. Maya argued that if my closest friend carries a complex, causally integrated version of my memories and values, then part of my "I" genuinely resides in her brain; she added that a temporary neural-link experiment—where two volunteers share working memory for thirty minutes—demonstrates one distributed consciousness across two skulls, with neither participant being the "original." Leo countered that this confuses representation with identity: her brain hosts a model of me, just as a mirror hosts an image, and during the link there are still two first-person perspectives that merely overlap rather than fuse into a single "I." Maya replied that the mirror analogy fails because patterns can be functionally co-owned—if the shared loop modifies both participants' future reasoning in a unified way, asking "which one is me" is like asking which half of a pair of scissors is the blade. Leo responded that without a single locus of experience, the word "I" loses its practical job, and while it is meaningful to value others as carriers of our patterns, calling them literal parts of us collapses a distinction we need for responsibility and care. I could not referee them. Please adjudicate point by point: on which specific claims is each correct, what essential distinction are they both neglecting, and how should the temporary shared-memory case be analyzed without reducing it to either pure metaphor or literal fusion?
