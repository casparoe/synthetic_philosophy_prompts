There's this weird asymmetry in a lot of US zoning boards: if you propose a four-unit building or even just an ADU in a garage, you get a public hearing where the people already on the block can complain about "neigborhood character" and traffic, and sometimes kill the project. But if you want to build a massive parking garage or tear down a small house to build a huge single-family place on the same lot, that usually gets approved with barely any fuss. And in the big developments that do go through, any community benefits deal gets hammered out with the local homeowners association — never with the people who will actually live in the units, since they aren't there yet to speak.

What theory of urban membership does that presuppose? Is it that owning property nearby gives you a kind of democratic veto over new residents? Or does it rest on a harm principle where only existant neighbors count as potential victims? Reconstruct the implicit view of who has standing in the city — is it compatible with treating housing as a regional public good, or does it turn every neighborhood into a private club with membership rules? And tell me what would actually have to change in the hearing process if we replaced that view with one where future residents have equal claim.
