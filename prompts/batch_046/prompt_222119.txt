I've been thinking on my own about Daniel Dennett's taxonomy of minds—the Darwinian creature running on hard-wired instincts, the Skinnerian one shaped by reinforcement, the Popperian one that can generate and test internal models before acting, and the Gregorian one that extends cognition through language and shared cultural tools. I keep wondering whether Mary Shelley's Frankenstein can be read as a single consciousness passing through all four stages in sequence, or whether the novel actually fractures that progression. The creature begins with pure reflex and survival, then learns cause and effect by painful trial and error in the wilderness, then clearly forms internal hypotheses about human behavior before he ever approaches the De Lacey cottage, and finally acquires language, history, and politics from books and hidden observation. Yet he never enters the living symbolic community that seems essential for true Gregorian cognition; he masters language in isolation, uses it to craft rational demands, but remains outside the cultural feedback loop that Dennett treats as definitive. Does the novel show that a Gregorian mind can be manufactured in solitude, or does the creature's eventual violence prove that language without a culture produces something monstrous—a Popperian mind armed with Gregorian tools but lacking Gregorian membership? I'd like a reading that takes both Shelley's narrative and the taxonomy seriously: which scenes mark each cognitive transition, where the framework illuminates a specific choice the creature makes, and where the novel resists the reading and forces us to revise the idea that these stages are cleanly separable. I'm especially interested in whether the creature's demand for a mate reveals a limit the taxonomy doesn't capture—a need for social recognition that isn't reducible to any of the four kinds—and whether that gap is a flaw in the framework or precisely the point Shelley is making about minds that can think like us but never belong with us.
