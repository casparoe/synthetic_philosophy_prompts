For a philosophy-of-mind seminar that assumes familiarity with the zombie argument but not with standard taxonomy labels, construct a decision tree that sorts respondents by their substantive commitments rather than by the names they adopt. The root is the conceivability claim: present the respondent with a concrete scenario—imagine a being atom-for-atom identical to you, behaving identically in every observable and functional respect, but lacking any phenomenal experience whatsoever—and ask whether they can coherently conceive this without contradiction, whether they can conceive it only as an epistemic possibility with hidden structure, or whether they treat it as impossible. From that root, build branches using the following diagnostic cases and distinctions: (1) whether phenomenal absence must be total and all-or-nothing, or whether it can be graded, using a fading-zombie case in which visual qualia diminish progressively while emotional phenomenology remains fully intact; (2) whether macro-consciousness could emerge from an aggregate of parts each individually lacking consciousness, using a micro-zombie aggregation scenario (for instance, a room-sized system of non-experiential subunits arranged like neural tissue but with no unified subject); (3) whether conceivability, if granted, reveals a metaphysical gap, an epistemic gap that leaves physical structure intact, or merely a limitation in our phenomenal concepts. The tree must terminate in named leaves that include: strong anti-physicalism (conceivability entails metaphysical possibility), type-F monism with a phenomenal-concept strategy, type-B physicalism that denies the root conceivability, property dualism restricted to an epistemic gap, eliminativism about phenomenal concepts, and a graded-absence view that accepts partial zombies but denies that total absence is conceivable. For each internal node, phrase the branching question as a scenario or claim the respondent can evaluate without recognizing which leaf it targets. Explicitly flag pairs of leaves the tree fails to separate—particularly whether the anti-physicalist and the property-dualist leaves diverge only at the aggregation node, and whether the graded-absence view is genuinely distinct from the epistemic-gap view or collapses into it. Include a brief diagnosis of any logically possible but unoccupied answer-combinations (for example, accepting total zombie conceivability while denying that biological neurons are micro-zombies), explaining for each whether the gap results from incoherence, unattractiveness, or mere neglect. Limit the tree to six decision nodes and avoid asking for bibliographies, named authors, or citation lists; the classification should work for someone reasoning entirely without external reference.
