I'm sitting on my city's public-arts grant committee and we're stuck on a funding decision, so I need your help drafting a memo for Thursday's vote. The proposal is for a stage biopic about Alan Turing's last years — that's not the issue — but the playwright invents a specific scene where Turing admits to a close friend that he deliberately falsified some early results out of vanity, something that never happened and has no basis in the record. The playwright calls this 'dramatic compression' and says it gives the tragedy a more human, Shakesperean arc. Meanwhile, a historical preservation group and some distant relatives wrote in objecting: they say inventing a moral failure for someone who can't defend himself is a kind of 'posthumous narrative harm,' and our charter requires funded work to show 'respect for historical subjects,' though nobody has ever defined that.

I want you to act like a professional philosopher of art and an experienced grant administrator, and think carefully before recommending anything. Start with some brainstorming: lay out the tension between artistic license and what we could call representational fidelity to the dead, and consider whether the dead can actually be harmed or whether the harm is really to the living — both the descendants and the public's shared historical understanding. Then draft the memo itself: recommend either approval, rejection, or approval with concrete conditions and a timeline (e.g., what must change by opening night). You need to engage the playwright's strongest argument honestly — maybe that all biography is already selective fiction, so drawing a line here is arbitrary — and also treat the objectors' worry seriously without just citing dead-people-can't-be-defamed legal rules. Please don't give me a bibliography or ask me to check citations; just reason it through. I definately need this to feel well-argued, not just legalistic. Write it like a formal document with a clear bottom line, but keep the reasoning accessible.
