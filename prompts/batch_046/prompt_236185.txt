Exam question: Distinguish indicative conditionals from counterfactual conditionals, and apply that distinction to evaluate this argument: "The claim that if universal basic income had been enacted in 2010, U.S. poverty rates in 2020 would be significantly lower should be dismissed, because UBI was not enacted in 2010, which makes the antecedent false; and false antecedents render indicative conditionals unassertible, so there is no factual basis for comparing worlds."

Student answer: Indicative conditionals, such as "If the meeting is moved to Tuesday, then the report is due Monday," are evaluated against the actual world and express evidential or causal support; if their antecedent is false, they may be discussed in probabilistic terms but typically lack direct assertoric force. Counterfactual conditionals, such as "If UBI had been enacted in 2010...," depend on similarity relations among possible worlds: we hold fixed history and natural laws up to a divergence point—what we might call trunk-level conditions—then vary the antecedent minimally and check the consequent. Therefore the argument errs by applying indicative assertibility norms, specifically that false antecedents undermine evaluation, to a counterfactual claim, which can be true despite an actually false antecedent.

That said, David Lewis analyzes both indicative and counterfactual conditionals through the same mechanism of selecting the closest possible world where the antecedent holds, using an identical similarity metric; the distinction is merely pragmatic, turning on whether we rigidly hold the past fixed. So the argument is not fully mistaken: indicative evaluation rules do bear on counterfactuals on Lewis's unified account. Furthermore, because counterfactual assessment requires fixing actual-world trunk-level conditions up to the divergence point, counterfactuals depend on indicative knowledge about reality. From this dependence it follows that if the antecedent is false in actuality—especially when indicative evidence shows it was structurally impossible—the counterfactual comparison is undermined, since the divergence becomes too remote to support reliable inference. Hence, while the argument misapplies assertibility norms, its core worry that actual-world falsehood weakens counterfactual comparison is justified.
