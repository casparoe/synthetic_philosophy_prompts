I am composing a novel in which a long-dead poet is revived through an unspecified scientific procedure and asked to judge the work of contemporary machines. The poet I have in mind is Clément Marot, the sixteenth-century translator of Ovid whose versions were praised not for literal fidelity but for capturing what later critics called the music of language within the strictures of French meter. My narrative requires a precise conceptual conflict, so I have invented a technology that does not yet exist in quite this form: neural translation engines that operate within a prosodic embedding space, enforcing rhyme, syllable count, and syntactic parallelism through adversarial penalties that punish semantic deviation only weakly but reward phonetic and structural fidelity strongly. These engines can take a Latin elegy and produce an octosyllabic French couplet with perfect feminine rhyme and correct caesura, yet the imagery may be entirely reconstructed—sometimes producing lines that sound unmistakably like Marot's courtly voice but express sentiments he never held.

Given Marot's actual practice—his willingness to reshape Ovid's narratives to suit the tonal expectations of the French court, his conviction that translation must be a rebirth rather than a mechanical reproduction, and his evident belief that the harmony between sound and meaning is the true measure of a poem—how would he likely assess these machine outputs? Would he regard the preservation of formal architecture as a triumph of the translator's art, even when the semantic content has been replaced by statistical inference? Or would he consider the substitution of imagery a fatal rupture, arguing that form without the poet's intended sense is merely an ornate vessel emptied of its wine?

Furthermore, if Marot were consulted by the engineers who built these systems, do you think he would advise them to strengthen the adversarial constraints on literal meaning—accepting clumsier meter in exchange for semantic proximity—or to relax them further, allowing the algorithm to pursue phonetic beauty even at the cost of becoming, in essence, an original poet rather than a translator? I ask not for historical documentation or a reading list, but for a reasoned speculation that respects both the technical logic of the hypothetical system and the aesthetic commitments visible in Marot's known translations.
