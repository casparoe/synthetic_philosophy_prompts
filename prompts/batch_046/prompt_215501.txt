I'm examining how institutional evaluation protocols handle statistical evidence, and I'm struck by a recurring design choice in regulatory science and public-policy assessment. In many agencies, an intervention—whether a pharmaceutical, an educational program, or a social-service model—is licensed or funded based chiefly on its pooled effect size across an aggregated study population. Subgroup analyses, even when they show a consistent reversal of the pooled direction within every identifiable stratum (the structure of Simpson's paradox), are typically classified as secondary, exploratory, or insufficient for overturning the aggregate result unless they survive severe multiplicity adjustments. The practice treats the pooled parameter as the authoritative causal fact, while disaggregated patterns are treated as potential artifacts.

What philosophical commitments about causation, statistical reality, and distributive justice does that institutional architecture presuppose? Must it be committed to the existence of a single "true" average treatment effect that is metaphysically prior to local causal structures, or does it instead presuppose that group-specific differences are epistemically unreliable? Could the pattern be sustained without any deep ontological commitment—perhaps grounded only in tractability, democratic demands for a unitary public metric, or a normative view that policy should be blind to group membership? Reconstruct the implicit theory of value and causation that best explains why the pooled result retains institutional authority despite uniform reversal in every subgroup. Then distinguish what this evaluation practice strictly requires from what it merely accommodates, and describe what a licensing regime that treated subgroup-sensitive causal reasoning as primary would have to alter—not only in its statistical procedures, but in its underlying conception of legitimate policy justification.
