I am a journalist who covers science and ideas for a general audience, and I have been invited to deliver the opening remarks at an interdisciplinary workshop this week titled "After the Triptych: Rethinking the Middle Ages, the Renaissance, and the Scientific Revolution." The audience is split between historians and philosophers of science who have long debated whether these three labels describe genuine discontinuities in practice or are largely retrospective constructions assembled by later scholars, and between university administrators and grant officers who need to understand why this historiographical question affects institutional life—departmental boundaries, funding categories, and the stories universities tell about their own origins. Please write a speech of roughly five to seven minutes, composed entirely as continuous prose with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, in which the problem of periodization does real structural work rather than serving as historical decoration.

Specifically, I want the remarks to be built around the claim that naming an era a "revolution" or a "renaissance" functions as a normative sorting device: it retroactively assigns heroic or backward status to intellectual practices based on whether they anticipate modern epistemic norms, thereby creating what we might call a moral economy of anachronism in which figures like Galileo or Bacon are read as revolutionaries despite not having experienced their own work as a rupture with a continuous medieval tradition. The speech should be able to acknowledge, for the specialists in the room, that the standard triptych was largely assembled by nineteenth- and early-twentieth-century narratives—without requiring me to cite specific texts or offer a bibliography—while still explaining to the non-specialists why treating these boundaries as invented rather than natural changes the ethical and practical stakes of how we organize archives, evaluate interdisciplinary research, and train students. I also want you to seed the remarks with one concrete institutional example: our own university library's classification system, which physically separates medieval manuscripts from early modern scientific texts in distant stacks, thereby silently performing the periodization it claims merely to describe. The speech should open by welcoming both halves of the audience, then move through the conceptual confusion that makes the triptych feel inevitable even when historians have shown it to be fragile, and close with a single clear implication for how this workshop should proceed—namely, that if the boundaries are invented, then collaboration across them requires justification of a different kind than collaboration within a supposedly natural order of knowledge.
