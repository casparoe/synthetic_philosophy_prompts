As a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy, I have been trying to understand Quine's argument about the indeterminacy of translation — specifically the "gavagai" thought experiment in which a field linguist could construct multiple translation manuals, all equally compatible with every observable utterance and behavior, yet assigning radically different meanings to the same native expressions. The debate that followed, involving figures like Davidson and others, has now run for decades without visible convergence: one side insists that behavioral evidence genuinely underdetermines semantic fact; the other insists that charity, shared logical structure, or normative constraints on interpretation block the slide into indeterminacy. The same moves keep recurring — appeals to analytical hypotheses, counter-appeals to the principle of charity, and worries about whether internal representation can vary without behavioral trace — yet nobody seems persuaded.

My own suspicion, informed by work on distributed systems, is that the impasse is partly a category mistake disguised as a semantic dispute. In software engineering, two services can be observationally equivalent — identical APIs, identical outputs for every input — while maintaining entirely different internal data models and state representations. We generally do not ask which internal schema is the true semantics of the interface; we treat semantics as fixed by the observable contract plus pragmatic constraints on maintainability and interoperability. By analogy, I wonder whether one philosophical camp is treating meaning like an internal representational schema that can vary without behavioral change, while the other treats it like the observable contract itself, constrained by norms of rational interpretation. If so, they may not be disagreeing about the same thing: one side may be asking a metaphysical question about what meaning consists in, the other an epistemic question about what justifies one translation manual over another, both under the heading of whether translation is indeterminate.

I am not asking for a survey of interpretations or a verdict on Quine versus Davidson. I want a structural diagnosis. Is the disagreement substantive — is there a real fact of the matter about whether meaning extends beyond behavioral equivalence? Is it verbal — do "meaning," "reference," and "translation" carry different success conditions for each camp? Or are the sides answering genuinely different questions — one in semantics, one in the metaphysics of mental representation — that have been collapsed into a single problem?

Please engage concretely with what each side actually says. Do Davidson-style critics deny that empirically equivalent manuals are possible, or do they deny that such equivalence entails semantic indeterminacy? Do Quineans claim that internal representations are irrelevant to meaning, or only that they are radically underdetermined by any possible evidence?

Finally, given your diagnosis, what would count as genuine progress? Would a formal result — for instance, a proof that any two observationally equivalent translation manuals are computably inter-translatable, or that no such inter-translation exists in principle — resolve the dispute, or merely relocate it? Or is the debate ill-posed because it conflates the epistemic challenge of learning a language with the metaphysical question of what fixes meaning? And if progress is unlikely, say so explicitly and explain why.

Do not provide a bibliography, reading list, or citations; I need an analysis that stands on its own. Avoid framing this as a comparison of views on their merits — I want the architecture of the disagreement mapped, the misconceptions that keep it running, and an honest assessment of whether a conceptual distinction, a formal result, or a reframing could actually end it.
