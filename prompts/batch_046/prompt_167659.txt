I was recently asked to make a consequential decision about whether to endorse a risky institutional reform, and I realized that my belief about its probable success rested on less evidence than Clifford's evidentialist standard permits, yet withholding judgment felt practically impossible in a way James's defense of the will to believe might recognize. Rather than another historical comparison, I want the underlying specification: what must a theory of doxastic ethics actually deliver to be adequate?

Write the desiderata. For each requirement, identify the motivating case or conceptual distinction, explain why it functions as a genuine constraint rather than a camp preference, and flag whether it is non-negotiable or negotiable. The list must cover: (1) how the theory handles James's framework of living, forced, and momentous options without collapsing the distinctions among them; (2) the scope and limits of Clifford's evidentialist prohibition—whether it targets belief proper, acceptance, or working assumption, and whether satisfying it renders practical reasoning impossible; (3) the voluntarism problem, including whether the theory requires direct voluntary control over belief and how it accommodates psychological doxastic involuntarism; (4) consistency with empirical findings about belief formation, particularly the inability to suspend belief at will; (5) a precise distinction between epistemic and prudential reasons for belief, including whether prudential considerations can ever legitimately close an evidential gap without collapsing into self-deception; and (6) the case of institutionally performative beliefs, where an agent's holding the belief is partially constitutive of the social conditions that determine its truth-value, raising the question of whether such beliefs can be epistemically evaluated independently of their causal effects.

Then identify any pair of desiderata that appears jointly unsatisfiable—especially tensions between requiring direct doxastic control and maintaining an evidentialist constraint, or between permitting belief in evidential gaps and avoiding institutional self-deception. State which single requirement, if enforced strictly, eliminates the largest number of popular positions.

Finally, briefly score James's will-to-believe defense, Clifford's evidentialist ethics, and a moderate view that permits belief in evidential gaps only under conditions of practical necessity and transparent non-deception, against your list—just enough to show where the criteria themselves do the exclusionary work. Keep the weight on the specification, not on the scorecard.
