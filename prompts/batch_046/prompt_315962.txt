As a retired condensed-matter physicist who, since leaving the laboratory, has spent several years auditing philosophy and cognitive-science seminars, I have become preoccupied with a thesis I first encountered in Douglas Hofstadter and Emmanuel Sander’s Surfaces and Essences: that analogical mapping is not an ornament to cognition but its substrate, and that what we call a “category” is merely an analogy that has been compressed—over long experience—into a single mental node with adjustable parameters, or “knobs,” that can be tuned without conscious reconstruction of the underlying mapping. I want to stage this idea bodily, not explain it.

For a two-hour workshop of eighteen professionals with no formal philosophy or psychology background, in a community-education setting, design a structured activity—preferably with rules, hidden information, and small-group roles—that forces participants to feel analogical reasoning as something they are constantly doing, even when they believe they are applying logic or recognizing essences. The rules should require groups to classify novel objects and solve coordination problems, but they should be arranged so that participants’ initial “essence-based” judgments are actually tracking unnoticed surface similarities. At a predetermined point, a familiar analogical frame should collapse—for instance, by introducing an object that shares surface features with one category but functions analogically with another—forcing the groups to rebuild their system explicitly rather than tuning a knob. Only in the debrief should the facilitator name what has occurred.
