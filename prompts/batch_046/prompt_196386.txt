In my course on eighteenth-century political thought we have been arguing for several weeks about Rousseau’s attack on theater in his letter to d’Alembert regarding the proposed playhouse in Geneva, and the discussion has reached a frustrating standstill. One side insists that the argument is fundamentally about civic virtue and institutional displacement: theater fosters luxury, softens martial character, and replaces the austere public festivals that bind citizens together. The other side reads the same text as a philosophical attack on representation itself, claiming that theatrical spectacle substitutes appearance for being and corrupts the spectator by making him prefer the image of virtue to virtue itself, regardless of Geneva’s particular civic needs. Both sides cite the same passages, both agree that Rousseau condemns the proposal, and yet neither has convinced anyone that the other is misreading the core claim. I have begun to suspect that the stall is not about Rousseau’s intentions at all but about a hidden ambiguity in what “theater” denotes: is it being criticized as an institution that occupies civic space and shapes collective habits, or as an epistemic mode that structures individual perception through mimetic identification?

Before I present my view to the group, I want a diagnosis rather than a verdict. Is this disagreement substantive, verbal, or a case of two different questions—one about political sociology, one about the metaphysics of presence—being pursued under a single heading? If it is verbal, what precise distinction would dissolve it; if it is substantive, is there a conceptual or textual finding that could settle whether Rousseau’s primary target is civic corruption or representational deception; and if the question is genuinely ill-posed, what does that imply about how we should read the text? Please base your answer on what each side actually maintains, not on a generic survey of scholarship, and tell me honestly what, if anything, would count as progress given your diagnosis.
