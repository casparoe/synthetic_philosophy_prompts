In a graduate seminar on aesthetics and race, one student invoked W. E. B. Du Bois's well-known claim—often rendered as all art is propaganda and ever must be—to dismiss a classmate's defense of abstract painting as politically neutral. She argued that if every artwork necessarily advances some racial or ideological position, then non-representational form offers no refuge from politics. A second student objected that Du Bois was issuing a normative prescription for Black artists in a specific historical situation, not advancing a universal descriptive thesis about every artwork everywhere. A third student added that propaganda itself might denote intentional persuasive messaging, or it might denote any representation that structurally reinforces group interests regardless of the artist's intent, and that these two readings yield very different verdicts about whether geometric abstraction counts as propaganda. I am trying to draft a reply and realize I do not know which interpretations of the slogan are actually available. Please lay out the precise claims that all art is propaganda could be expressing, arranged from the trivial to the substantive. For each reading, indicate whether it supports dismissing politically neutral art, and clearly separate the normative prescription from the descriptive universal, and intentional advocacy from structural ideological function. Then say which reading best aligns with Du Bois's own argument, and identify what all three discussants are missing about the relation between aesthetic form and political content.
