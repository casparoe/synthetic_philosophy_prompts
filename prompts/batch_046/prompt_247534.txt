I am preparing a seminar on philosophy of perception for sixteen upper-level students—mostly advanced undergraduates and graduate students—meeting in a standard classroom for seventy-five minutes. They have encountered the classic exchange about whether a person blind from birth who suddenly gains sight could immediately distinguish a cube from a sphere by vision alone, but they complain that the historical framing feels too remote from actual perceptual psychology. I want them to feel in their own reasoning and sensory experience the force of the idea that cross-modal recognition depends on empirically calibrated associative schemas rather than on innate geometric concepts or on direct sensory fusion, and I want this tension to arise from the rules and stakes of the exercise itself before I name it in discussion.

Please design a runnable classroom simulation—complete with assigned roles, a precise four-phase timeline, physical materials, interaction rules, and a non-monetary scoring mechanism—such that the conceptual problem emerges from what participants do, not from your explanation afterwards. The setting is the seminar described above; the group size is sixteen; the time available is seventy-five minutes; and what participants should come away seeing is that recognizing an object across modalities requires a learned representational mapping whose absence produces a specific cognitive failure that neither pure reason nor pure sensation can resolve. To move the exercise beyond the standard framing, inject a somewhat niche but not obscure twist: use pairs of three-dimensional printed forms—specifically, objects that share an identical continuous tactile profile but differ topologically or geometrically in vision, such as a smooth cylinder and a modified Möbius strip with a sealed continuous edge, both feeling like uniform loops to a blindfolded hand—so that the failure of immediate identification is structurally built into the materials rather than merely imagined.

Your design must include: (1) a rotation of roles—subjects, experimenters, and observers—with explicit constraints on what each may say or know; (2) a timeline broken into approximately twenty, fifteen, twenty, and twenty minutes, specifying when blindfolds or barriers are introduced, when shapes are handled or viewed, and when justification is required; (3) a rule-based interaction protocol in which subjects must defend identifications by appealing either to innate geometric intuition or to learned tactile-visual correlation, while experimenters award points for internal consistency of justification rather than for correct answers, creating a visible tension between epistemic authority and empirical calibration; (4) a debrief that maps specific moments—tactile learning, visual surprise, identification failure, attempted recalibration—onto elements of Molyneux’s problem, explicitly stating which feature of the original philosophical case (for instance, lifelong absence of visual experience in congenital blindness) the simulation cannot reproduce and how you will supply that missing element verbally; and (5) an honest prediction of what will predictably go wrong the first time—such as subjects abandoning role constraints to guess visually, or observers dominating the debrief—and concrete instructions for handling that failure without rescuing the conceptual point prematurely.
