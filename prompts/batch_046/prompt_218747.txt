I just had a long argument with a friend about whether 1984 is best read as a warning that negative liberty is crushed by totalitarian interference, or whether Orwell is actually dramatizing something closer to republican domination—control that persists even when no one is actively coercing the subject. I would like you to read the novel through Berlin’s distinction between positive and negative liberty and through Pettit’s republican conception of freedom as non-domination, but with a specific twist: focus on the rented room above Mr. Charrington’s shop and on Winston’s arrest and re-education. Does the room represent a space of negative liberty (mere absence of interference), positive liberty (self-mastery through writing and private thought), or neither, because the Party’s hidden presence makes it a case of domination without interference? When O’Brien explains that the Party seeks power not for any external end but for its own sake, is he articulating a perversion of positive liberty, or is he revealing that republican domination can operate precisely when the subject believes himself free? Take the philosophical taxonomy seriously—do not simply map characters onto concepts—and say where the novel resists it: for instance, does Julia’s practical, bodily conception of rebellion fit any of the three categories, or does she expose a gap in the framework? End by saying what Orwell’s depiction adds to the philosophical debate that argument alone does not capture, particularly about the phenomenology of unfreedom.
