I've been turning over the phrase "history is written by the victors," which I keep encountering in debates about school textbooks, museum labels, and documentary films—usually as a quick way to dismiss an official account by suggesting it serves the powerful. Recently I noticed a tension that the slogan doesn't seem to resolve: in some conflicts the victors generate vast state archives, while in others they deliberately destroy their own records and the defeated preserve private ones, yet in both cases the dominant narrative still tracks the victors' self-image rather than the surviving paper trail. That left me unsure whether the claim is about who controls the archives, who controls interpretation, or something about the nature of historical truth itself. Could you lay out the precise theses this phrase could express—distinguishing causal claims about record-keeping from epistemic claims about reliability, and normative claims about correction from metaphysical claims about whether past events depend on present power? For each reading, please indicate whether it is trivial, whether it is substantive and defensible, whether it is false, and whether it actually supports the skeptical move people make with it. I am also wondering whether there is a defensible version that extends beyond military or political victors to include the "victors" in conceptual or scientific disputes—those who establish a dominant framework—without collapsing into the obvious point that successful ideas tend to spread. If different readings yield different answers there, please say so, and tell me what kind of case would best distinguish the plausible versions from one another.
