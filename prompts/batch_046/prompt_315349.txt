Plan a single 75-minute session for architecture graduate students with no philosophy background, introducing the conflict between William James's defense of the will to believe and W.K. Clifford's ethics of belief. Do not explain the texts to me; design the pedagogical arc. First, identify the conceptual prerequisite that must be in place before either thinker is intelligible, and the dependency order of the remaining components. Second, propose one carrying example drawn from design practice or urban risk assessment—not religious faith, not Clifford's shipowner—and specify what features it must possess to illuminate both positions without collapsing into one, and why the standard cases fail for this audience. Third, map the misconceptions they will likely form (for instance, that James licenses mere wishful thinking, that Clifford demands Cartesian certainty, or that the debate is only about religion), indicating the exact point in the sequence where each should be intercepted. Fourth, provide the one open-ended diagnostic question to ask at the end that reveals whether they grasp the structural disagreement about when belief-formation is ethically evaluable, rather than whether they can name the authors. Finally, identify one standard element of the debate—such as James's taxonomy of live, dead, forced, and momentous options, or Clifford's arguments about testimonial belief—that you would deliberately exclude at this level, and justify why leaving it out protects the core insight.
