I want a decision-tree taxonomy—not a named-theory quiz—that sorts genuine commitments on the question of which computations matter morally and where the boundaries of sentience lie. The audience is conceptually literate readers who can reason about hypothetical systems but do not carry the philosophical literature in working memory, so every branch must split on a concrete case or operational claim, never on a label like “are you a functionalist?”

Build the tree from the logical space upward: first state the diagnostic questions and the answer combinations they allow, then locate the actual positions as leaves within that space, then flag any unoccupied combinations and briefly diagnose whether the gap is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. The tree should have exactly eight leaves.

Each internal node must present a scenario the respondent can judge without knowing theory-names. The cases must include at least these three: (i) a computation isomorphically identical to human pain-processing executed in silicon with no global integration or behavioral reportability; (ii) a computation running continuously in living neural tissue that is causally isolated from all behavior and from any internal monitoring architecture; and (iii) a novel scenario you should construct and name the “Stutter-Loop”—the same pain-avoidance algorithm executed in intermittent one-millisecond bursts across geographically distributed hardware, with each burst using different physical substrates, but the temporal sequence across bursts preserving functional continuity. For each case, the branch splits on whether the system merits direct moral consideration (not merely derivative or instrumental consideration).

The eight leaves must include these five: (A) substrate-independent pure functionalism—any computation with the right functional profile matters regardless of substrate or integration; (B) integration-dependent functionalism—only computations embedded in a unified causal or global-workspace architecture matter; (C) biological substrate essentialism—only computations running in living neural tissue have direct moral status; (D) phenomenal-computational coupling—only computations that are both functionally appropriate and necessarily accompanied by phenomenal experience matter; (E) anti-attributionist skepticism—no computation, however structured, bears direct moral status; attribution is always derivative of designers, users, or social practices. Add three further leaves only if the logical space genuinely requires them to preserve the tree’s binary structure; if the space collapses so that two of the required leaves are indistinguishable given your cases, flag that failure explicitly rather than forcing separation.

After the tree, identify one specific answer-pattern combination that the branching logic permits but that no coherent position occupies, and diagnose the vacancy. Also flag any pair of required leaves that your cases fail to separate, and say what further distinction—perhaps involving temporal continuity, causal density, or normative directionality—would be needed to pull them apart.

Do not request bibliographies, citations, or reading lists; the instrument should be answerable by reasoning about the cases alone. Be explicit about where you are inventing the Stutter-Loop from first principles versus describing an established debate, and note any contested readings of functionalism or phenomenal coupling that your tree tacitly assumes.

Please be self-critical: if the Stutter-Loop smuggles in a hidden premise about temporal continuity that unfairly advantages one branch, or if the distinction between integration-dependent functionalism and phenomenal-computational coupling collapses under the cases given, say so plainly. If the task as posed cannot be done well—for example, if the logical space is smaller than eight genuinely distinct positions—state that directly and propose the corrected tree size. I want pushback, not polite compliance with a confused framing.
