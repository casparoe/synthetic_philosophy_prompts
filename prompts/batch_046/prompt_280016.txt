Write a fourteen-line poem divided into an octave and a sestet, with a volta at line nine. The subject is Wittgenstein's notion of family resemblance—particularly the argument that the concept "game" has no single common essence and is instead held together by overlapping similarities, like the fibers in a rope where no one thread runs the full length. The poem's form should enact this: establish in the octave a rhyme pattern that seems regular (for instance, ABAB CDCD) but allow it to dissolve in the sestet into partial echoes—slant rhymes, assonance, or conceptual resonances—so that the reader experiences the absence of any essential structural feature. At the volta, the speaker should recognize that searching for the one feature all games share is like searching for the one rhyme that defines the poem; both searches fail, and that failure is the point. Render the idea precisely enough that someone familiar with Philosophical Investigations §§65–67 recognizes the rope image and the anti-essentialist move, without naming Wittgenstein or quoting him verbatim. After the poem, include a brief note identifying where the formal experiment takes liberties with Wittgenstein's actual view—especially whether treating "overlapping similarity" itself as a hidden essence risks misrepresenting his pragmatic, context-sensitive account—and say what observation or argument would most convince you that the form either succeeds or inadvertently collapses into the essentialism it depicts.
