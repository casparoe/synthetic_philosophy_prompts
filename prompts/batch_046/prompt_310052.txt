Compare Albert Camus's absurdist account of meaning—specifically the idea that human significance is sustained through perpetual revolt against the silence of an indifferent universe, without appeal to transcendent justification—with Susan Wolf's theory that a meaningful life consists in active engagement with projects that have objective value and that subjectively fulfill the agent. Your comparison should center on a single, narrowly defined problem: what each account implies about the persistence of meaning when an agent undergoes a radical revision in her beliefs about the objective value of the projects to which she is committed. Call this the problem of normative residue. Does Camus's non-teleological, self-affirming revolt preserve meaning independently of such belief-revisions, and if so, does this make his view more resilient—or merely more arbitrary—than Wolf's fitting-fulfillment model, which seems to tie meaning directly to the agent's correct apprehension of objective worth? Evaluate which framework better explains why an agent might rationally continue a commitment after discovering that its original value-grounds were illusory, and identify the precise point at which the two accounts diverge on whether meaning can survive the loss of its own justification.
