As a novelist accustomed to tracing how collective intentions unravel when they encounter structures larger than themselves, I wish to propose an analytical exercise in conceptual architecture. Take the Greek referendum of 5 July 2015 — the rejection, by approximately sixty-one per cent, of the bailout conditions proposed by the European institutions — and examine it not as a fiscal footnote, but as a dramatic case study in the theories of sovereignty, democratic deficit, and ever-closer union that underpin the European project.

The common structural facts are these: a government, elected on an anti-austerity mandate, submitted punitive creditor terms to a direct popular vote; the electorate refused them with emphatic clarity; within a matter of days, that same government accepted a third memorandum containing conditions yet more severe; and the democratic refusal was thereby rendered, in practical terms, inoperative. I ask you to analyse this sequence through the lens of EU democratic-deficit theory, but with a specific conceptual injection: distinguish between authorial sovereignty, understood as the democratic capacity to determine the terms of one's own constraint, and executive sovereignty, understood as the practical power to enforce those terms against external structural imperatives. Treat sovereignty not as a unitary possession that states either retain or cede, but as a bifurcated narrative authority distributed among the electorate, the national government, and the supranational institutions.

Your analysis should proceed in both directions. First, apply the theory: identify the relevant actors — the Greek electorate, the Syriza administration, the Eurogroup, the European Central Bank — and specify what the literature on democratic deficit and pooled sovereignty would predict each should do or suffer. Which elements of the crisis does the theory illuminate — for instance, the tension between national democratic expression and eurozone stability — and which does it misdescribe or leave conspicuously silent? Second, allow the episode to test the theory: does the referendum represent a democratic surplus rather than a deficit, a moment of perfect popular clarity that the supranational framework could not accommodate without self-contradiction? Does the rapid nullification of the Oxi vote confirm the inevitability of ever-closer union as a constraint on national autonomy, or does it reveal that the democratic-deficit framework was constructed to describe gradual erosion rather than abrupt democratic refusal?

Please be rigorous in your mapping — say explicitly where the theory offers no guidance — and avoid forcing the conceptual framework to account for details it was never designed to address. Write in British English. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations, and do not invoke obscure theoretical variants or highly specialised historical minutiae; rely only on the widely known structure of the crisis and on the conceptual distinction introduced above.
