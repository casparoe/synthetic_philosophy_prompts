I've been sketching out materials for a seminar on consciousness and causation, and a colleague noted that consciousness researchers almost never have to administer the institutional logic of the phenomenal-judgment paradox in practice. I'd like a satirical parody of an internal corporate policy manual—about 700 words, deadpan bureaucratic tone, numbered sections with sub-clauses—issued by the fictional "Institute for Non-Efficacious Phenomenology" (INP). The target is the tension in consciousness studies between treating first-person phenomenal reports as authoritative scientific data and accepting a strict epiphenomenalist framework in which phenomenal states possess no causal powers whatsoever. The manual should govern how researchers and subjects file, verify, audit, and appeal "phenomenal judgment reports" under doctrine that explicitly denies those reports are caused by the experiences they describe.

The humor must come from exaggerating real philosophical pressure points rather than inventing straw positions: every report has to be traced to its actual neural substrate; there must be an appeals board for "causal orphan" reports (those whose documented neural cause does not match the purported quale); and there should be a "Continuity and Refund Policy" governing cases in which neuroimaging conflicts with verbal testimony. The piece must stay rigorously internally consistent—phenomenal states must never be granted causal efficacy, not even in an accidental slip—so that a reader familiar with the Chalmers-style paradox of phenomenal judgment recognizes the satire as fair. Include at least one invented institutional detail, such as the 2019 "Munich Continuity Dispute," in which a subject's report of sharp pain was traced exclusively to a spinal reflex arc rather than to any phenomenal pain state, prompting the current policy that misattributed reports are eligible for partial neural-credit refunds. Write in the voice of an over-cautious HR and compliance office: precise, legalistic, slightly defensive, with footnotes that quietly undermine the very possibility that the reports being evaluated could be about the states they claim to be about. By the end, a reader should feel the genuine instability: if consciousness is truly epiphenomenal, then the entire edifice of first-person consciousness science rests on causally disconnected utterances, and institutional authority collapses into pure neuroanatomy.
