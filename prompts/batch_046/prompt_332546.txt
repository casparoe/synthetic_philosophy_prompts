Next month I am presenting a seminar paper defending a tri-partite account of social construction: race functions as an ascriptive kind constituted by phenotypic sorting practices, gender operates as a performative kind sustained through embodied interaction rituals that are neither reducible to biological sex nor to purely discursive assignment, and money operates as a fiduciary kind whose existence depends on collective institutional authorisation rather than on bodily practice or ascription. I argue that these three share a social ontology yet diverge in their conditions of persistence and dissolution—gender, I claim, is more resistant to individual exit than race, yet lacks the formal backing that stabilises monetary value—so that treating them as a single undifferentiated "social construction" misses structural differences that matter for ethics and policy.

The audience is a philosophy of social science reading group comprising analytic metaphysicians sceptical of non-natural kinds, feminist philosophers concerned that construction talk erases lived bodily experience, and a philosopher of economics familiar with institutional-fact theories. Please prepare me for the Q&A: list the objections I will actually face, in roughly the order they will arise and phrased in the vocabulary and commitments of each subgroup; for each, indicate whether it carries real force and what I should concede up front; provide a concise reply suitable for the time available; and note explicitly where that reply is weakest. Include the single objection I am hoping nobody asks—the one that presses the tension between my claim that gender is embodied and my claim that it is fully constructed—and suggest how to navigate it without collapsing into either biological essentialism or pure discursive idealism. The response should be written in British English.
