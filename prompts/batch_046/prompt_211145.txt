After a long argument with a friend about whether inheritance taxes actually secure equality of opportunity or merely penalize familial loyalty, I am drafting an upper-level political philosophy exam. I want one essay question, answerable in about 60 minutes, that students cannot answer by reciting Rawls's difference principle or Nozick's entitlement theory alone. The question should present a specific institutional design: a tax system that levies 60 percent on liquid financial inheritances above a high threshold, but grants a full exemption for capital placed in irrevocable human-capital trusts restricted to education, apprenticeship, and healthcare for descendants. Students must analyze what theory of equal opportunity this design implicitly presupposes—particularly whether it can consistently distinguish between family-circumstance advantages and individual-choice advantages, and whether the exemption reveals that the tax targets only positional goods rather than all dynastic advantages. Please provide the exam question itself, a model answer of approximately 550 words that makes at least three specific philosophical moves (for example, applying the brute-luck versus option-luck distinction inside the family unit, addressing the boundary problem between state and parental responsibility, and assessing whether the human-capital exemption undermines the tax's redistributive logic), and a marking scheme that assigns point values to each move, distinguishes an A answer from a B answer by naming which moves are present, and notes the typical student error of defending the exemption as intuitively just without analyzing the underlying theory of value.
