I've been following the argument about whether people on the left and right literally inhabit different moral worlds, sparked by research that clusters human moral intuitions into domains like care, fairness, loyalty, authority, and purity, and finds that many on the left weight mainly the first two while many on the right distribute concern across all five. In every discussion I encounter, the deadlock is identical. One side treats loyalty, authority, and purity as evolutionary residues—useful for group cohesion, perhaps, but not genuine moral reasons, more like preferences for in-group order or cleanliness that feel authoritative only because they arrive with a strong emotional charge. The other side says reducing morality to care and fairness is like reducing vision to brightness and contrast: you can do it, but you miss dimensions that are actually present, and the fact that some people lack them is a deficiency of moral perception rather than a purification from bias. Both sides cite the same survey patterns, the same cross-cultural correlations, the same neural activation maps. Nobody disputes the descriptive clustering. Yet after years of this exchange, neither camp has moved. So I want to ask: is this disagreement actually about psychology at all? Or is it about what separates a moral reason from a mere taste or bias, with the psychological data serving as a kind of Rorschach blot? Might there be a hidden empirical question—perhaps about whether judgments rooted in loyalty or authority survive reflective equilibrium in the same way care-based judgments do—that is secretly doing all the work? Or are the two sides answering entirely different questions—one asking what evolved, the other asking what is justified—under the single heading of who is right? I also keep returning to what I think of as the calibration problem: we seem to have no neutral instrument to tell whether possessing five moral foundations is like possessing five senses, yielding a fuller picture of reality, or like possessing five allergies, yielding more reactive noise to non-moral stimuli. Given your diagnosis—whether verbal, substantive, empirical-in-disguise, about standards of evidence, or about bedrock intuitions that neither side can argue for—what would actually count as progress? Would it take a conceptual distinction that cleanly separates evolutionary origin from normative authority? An experimental design that tests whether one cluster produces more stable judgments across varied contexts? Or is the stall a sign that the question is ill-posed, and that no resolution should be expected because the disagreement is ultimately about which intuitions we treat as data rather than distortion?
