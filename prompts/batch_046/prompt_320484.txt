I am a high school student working through anthropic reasoning for an independent study, and I have encountered what seems to be a hidden equivalence between the self-sampling assumption (SSA) and the self-indication assumption (SIA) that I cannot resolve. Consider a bounded scenario I constructed: either ten or ten thousand high schools maintain active philosophy clubs, and I observe that I belong to one. Under SSA, I treat my membership as a random draw from the reference class of all club members, which seems to leave my prior about the total number of clubs unchanged. Under SIA, the world with ten thousand clubs contains far more observers, so my existence should strongly favor that hypothesis—yet in my bounded classroom framing, both procedures appear to reduce to counting members within the reference class I already occupy. This leads me to think that Nick Bostrom's presumptuous philosopher objection—that SIA allows one to infer an enormous universe with near-certainty from one's own existence—might not be a special problem for SIA at all, but rather an unavoidable feature of any reference-class reasoning once the boundary is fixed. Specifically, I wonder whether the distinction between SSA and SIA collapses whenever we restrict the reference class to observers who could actually ask the question, since the observer-weighting of SIA then becomes indistinguishable from the random-sampling of SSA within that same boundary. Is my reasoning correct, or is there a formal feature of SIA—perhaps involving how it treats the prior over world-size independently of the reference-class boundary—that makes it uniquely vulnerable to the presumptuous philosopher charge in a way that SSA is not? I would appreciate clarification on whether my philosophy-club analogy exposes a genuine structural overlap or merely confuses two different treatments of prior probabilities.
