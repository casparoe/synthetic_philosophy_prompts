I have been working through some material on intertemporal choice theory for a graduate seminar, and during a conversation with a colleague afterward we reached an impasse about the centipede game that I have not been able to settle. She argued that the backward induction paradox is routinely mischaracterized in textbooks: rather than demonstrating that rational agents must defect at the very first node, it actually exposes a hidden metaphysical commitment in subgame-perfect reasoning—namely, the assumption that the decision-maker at node n and the decision-maker at node n+1 are strictly identical practical subjects across time. I countered that this seems like an unnecessary philosophical overlay, since standard decision theory already accommodates intertemporal choice through consistent discounting without requiring any deep unity of the self. Yet she pressed further: if rationality is defined with respect to a temporally extended plan, and if each successive node can be occupied by a successor self with altered present-biased preferences and no authoritative power to bind future nodes, then the backward induction chain collapses not because of an epistemic failure—such as imperfect common knowledge of rationality—but because there is no single normative authority governing the entire sequence.

Weigh the standard game-theoretic defense of immediate defection, which relies on common knowledge of rationality and the coherence of subgame-perfect equilibrium, against the objection that rationality across nodes presupposes a unity of practical identity that cannot be taken for granted without argument.
