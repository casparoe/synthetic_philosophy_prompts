If Dennett is right that the color phi illusion can be explained either Stalinesquely—where the brain constructs the intermediate motion and color change wholesale at the moment consciousness occurs—or Orwellianly—where we initially register the discrete flashes veridically and only afterward revise our memory to include the bridge—then both accounts seem to require that awareness of the first stimulus is held in abeyance for roughly the inter-flash interval, about two hundred milliseconds, until the second stimulus arrives and the brain can decide what narrative to present. On that reading, the only real disagreement is whether the fabricated bridge is inserted into working memory or into long-term storage. But if both views concede that there is a universal, fixed delay between physical event and conscious registration, why is the Stalinesque model usually treated as the more conservative, real-time option? Shouldn't the Orwellian account—since it at least allows a brief, unedited perceptual record before revision—be the one that preserves direct access to the present, and therefore be less threatening to the idea of an immediate specious present?
