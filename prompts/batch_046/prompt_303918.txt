After spending much of the evening in a long, unresolved argument with a friend about presentism in historiography, I have been reflecting on a sharper formulation of the same disagreement that I heard between two colleagues—let us call them Mara and Lin—during a seminar discussion last week. I report their exchange below as a bystander, without signaling which side I favor, and ask for an adjudication.

Mara opened by defending the inevitability of presentism in principle. She argued that the very selection of what counts as a historically significant fact is always guided by questions that arise in the present. For instance, asking when anthropogenic climate change began to reshape human settlement patterns is a framing that would have been conceptually unavailable in 1800; thus, the historian’s archive is always filtered through contemporary concerns. From this she concluded that historians can never fully step outside their own epistemic moment.

Lin responded by distinguishing two senses of presentism. One is an unavoidable hermeneutic condition: we indeed ask questions from our own standpoint. The other is a moral or evaluative imposition: judging past actors by standards they could not have known. Lin granted the first but denied the second is unavoidable. By using contextualist reconstruction—recovering past actors’ own categories, intentions, and institutional logics—a historian can suspend present moral judgment in the descriptive core of the work.

Mara then offered a specific counter-case. She pointed to historical assessments of nineteenth-century British colonial physicians in India that apply modern bioethical norms of informed consent. Even when such studies claim to be contextualizing—explaining, say, how colonial doctors understood patient autonomy through the lens of imperial authority—their evaluative vocabulary implicitly treats the absence of consent protocols as a deficiency. For Mara, this shows that contextualist description collapses into presentist evaluation as soon as normative language enters the account.

Lin replied that evaluation and description can be separated into distinct operations. A historian can first reconstruct colonial medical practice entirely within its own conceptual framework—using categories like miasma, humoral balance, or imperial hygiene—without importing modern bioethical norms. Only in a second, explicitly marked normative step might the historian compare those practices to contemporary standards of consent, and she can do so without attributing moral failure to the historical actors. The error, on Lin’s view, is not evaluation itself, but allowing the normative comparison to structure the descriptive narrative from the outset.

They asked me to referee, and I honestly could not settle it.
