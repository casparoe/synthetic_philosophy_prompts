I'm giving the closing remarks at the final session of a restorative-justice circle that has met monthly for the past year. The person who betrayed a position of deep trust within my family — specifically, my sister — is present, and our mediator has asked me to address whether maintaining moral accountability requires me to preserve my resentment toward him, or whether letting it go amounts to treating him as something other than a fully responsible agent. I want the speech to turn structurally on Strawson's distinction between participant reactive attitudes and the objective attitude, but I want it to do real work for the mixed audience: my parents and sister, who have no philosophical training, and the mediator, who does. In particular, I want to show that forgiveness is not identical to adopting the objective attitude — that is, to treating him as an object of management or clinical intervention rather than as a fellow participant — and that moral responsibility actually occupies the space between sustained resentment and that kind of detached objectification. Write a formal, four-minute address that makes this point without sounding like an academic lecture, that does not name philosophers, and that avoids presenting either resentment or detachment as the only mature response.
