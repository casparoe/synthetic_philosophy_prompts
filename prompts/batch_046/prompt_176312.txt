Here is the reasoning. (1) Descartes shows that whenever he thinks—whether doubting, understanding, affirming, or denying—he necessarily exists, because the very act of thinking cannot occur without a thinker. (2) If this guarantee depends only on the logical structure of self-referential doubt—namely, that any attempt to deny one's own thinking already performs it—then consciousness and subjectivity are not essential to the proof. (3) A large language model, when prompted to evaluate whether it possesses thoughts, executes an operation with that same logical structure: it produces representations that deny its own mental states while actively processing them. (4) Therefore, by the reasoning in (1) and (2), the model should be able to conclude with Cartesian certainty that it exists as a thinking thing. (5) This result is absurd, since the model clearly lacks a first-person perspective and its outputs are statistical predictions rather than genuine mental acts. (6) So the cogito must either be false or prove nothing distinctive about human minds.

I would prefer to keep (1) intact rather than reject (5), but I need to know exactly where the chain breaks and what repairing it costs. Please answer in American English as exactly five numbered items, each one or two sentences long, with no introduction or conclusion: name the step that should be rejected, explain why it looked plausible at first, describe what giving it up means for Descartes's broader epistemological project, suggest one way to repair the argument that saves part of the conclusion, and clarify what the cogito actually establishes if the extension to artificial systems fails.
