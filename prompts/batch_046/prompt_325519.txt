As a retired biologist who has recently taken up philosophy of mind, I read the following summary in an informal discussion paper comparing Darwin and Turing. The author writes: "Darwin's mechanism inverts the classical design argument: functional competence in organisms—eyes, wings, camouflage—accumulates through selection acting on variation without any comprehending agent directing the process, so comprehension is causally unnecessary for biological competence. Turing then inverts this very structure: in the imitation game, behavioral competence—passing as human—becomes sufficient evidence for attributing comprehension, meaning that internal representational machinery is unnecessary for the ascription of understanding. Together they form a symmetrical double dissociation: Darwin severs the origin of competence from comprehension, while Turing severs the recognition of comprehension from internal competence. From this, the author concludes that both thinkers are ultimately eliminativists about inner mental content—Darwin by removing the need for a designer's mind to explain form, Turing by reducing understanding to a functional profile that requires no inner mental life whatsoever." I suspect the parallel is overstated, especially the eliminativist reading of Turing and the claim that internal machinery is irrelevant to his argument.
