I'm a doctoral student in comparative economic history working on the conceptual architecture of the Great Divergence debate. I'm trying to decide how to allocate limited research time over the next two years: specifically, one conceptual chapter and one focused empirical study. The standard framing — 'why Britain and not China?' — has been criticized on multiple fronts, but I want to know which open problems are genuinely upstream and worth tackling first, rather than treating them as an undifferentiated list.

I see four live conceptual-methodological issues that seem to structure the rest of the debate. First, whether contrastive historical explanation requires an explicit counterfactual-support framework — and if so, whether that framework should be modal (possible-worlds semantics for institutional paths) or interventionist (probabilistic causation under perturbation) — or whether historiography can do without formalization altogether. Second, the unit-of-analysis problem: both Britain and Qing China were composite polities with substantial internal divergence (e.g., regional specialization in Jiangnan versus Lancashire, or fiscal-military capacity differences across provinces), which raises the question of whether the binary comparison is vitiated, or whether it should be replaced by subnational comparison, or whether coherence can be recovered through a different aggregation rule. Third, the recent quantitative turn — growth accounting, climate-economy regressions, and structural-break models — seems either to resolve the Needham question by making divergence precise, or to dissolve it by showing overdetermination; I need to know whether engaging with this literature solves the framing problem or merely relocates it. Fourth, there is a possible teleological bias: treating industrialization as the predetermined outcome to be explained, rather than treating the divergence itself as one contingent path among others; replacing it with a contingency-of-outcomes model might change which evidence counts as explanatory and which is treated as noise.

Of these, which two should take priority in my research plan, and in what sequence? I want the ranking defended with explicit criteria: tractability for a single researcher without large-scale quantitative infrastructure; upstreamness, meaning that resolving this issue would restructure how the others are approached; and payoff for the actual practice of comparative history, not just philosophy of science. I also want to know whether there are important lines I'm neglecting — for instance, connections between the framing problem and empirical work on intra-imperial trade networks or state-capacity measurement — and, for each priority you identify, what kind of intermediate result over the next eighteen months would cause you to demote it or elevate it. The deliverable should be a defended agenda, not a survey or a reading list.
