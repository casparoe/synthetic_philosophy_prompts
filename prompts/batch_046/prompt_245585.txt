In a recent discussion of Kierkegaard's Fear and Trembling, an interlocutor kept repeating the claim that the single individual is higher than the universal in order to defend the view that Abraham's readiness to sacrifice Isaac proves authentic faith must always override universal ethical norms. I am trying to see exactly what philosophical work that slogan is doing, since it seems to conflate several different theses. Could you lay out the precise readings it might carry—for instance, an ontological claim about the priority of particular existence over Hegelian universals, a normative claim that an individual's absolute relation to God supersedes moral duty, a descriptive claim about Abraham's inward psychological state, or a structural claim about the teleological suspension of the ethical itself? For each reading, indicate whether it is trivial, genuinely defensible within Kierkegaard's framework, or false, and specify which version my interlocutor actually needs to reach the conclusion that religious conviction licenses the suspension of ordinary morality. Finally, say whether any of the defensible readings avoid reducing Kierkegaard's position to religious fanaticism, and how they relate specifically to the concept of teleological suspension rather than to Abraham's narrative more broadly.
