so im on this committee for our small county library system and we're rewriting the collection policy and one member keeps pushing this idea she got from tolstoy's late essay on art — that art works like an infection, where the artist literally transmits their emotional state to the audience and thats basically the only thing that makes it art at all. she wants every fiction purchase to come with a feeling-impact statement from the librarian, saying exactly what emotion the book is designed to spread, and then we shelve fiction by emotional target instead of genre or author. i think its a weird bureaucratic twist on his theory but im not sure what philosophical commitments we're actually signing up for.

what i need is for someone to reconstruct the implicit view of art baked into this practice. does requiring those statements and that shelving system mean we're committed to tolstoy's full position — that emotional transmission is not just important but the defining feature of art, and that beauty or technical craft are basically irrelevant? or could the same practice make sense under a different theory, like art as expression or art as social practice? there's also this tension: our non-fiction section stays organized by subject and we don't ask for feeling-statements for history or science books, so are we implicitly treating fiction and non-fiction as totally different kinds of things? please lay out what has to be true about art and about knowledge for this policy to be coherent, flag where it's philosophically underdetermined so several theories could license it, and say what would have to change in our library if we replaced the underlying view. also include the hard cases the procedure can't settle — like a technically brilliant novel thats emotionally cold, or a book that spreads an emotion the librarian finds harmful — and be honest about where an editor just has to make a call.
