I am a control-systems engineer fluent in open-loop and closed-loop architectures, state-transition tables, setpoint tracking, disturbance rejection, feedforward compensation, and adaptive controllers that revise goals rather than merely correct errors, but I have little formal training in philosophy. Please restate the philosophical discussion of sphexishness—the rigid behavioral loop of the sphex wasp (paralyzing prey, dragging it to a burrow, entering to inspect, dragging it inside, and laying an egg, with repetition of the inspection step if the prey is displaced during the check)—entirely within that engineering vocabulary. Map the wasp's sequence to a specific controller architecture; map human action that exhibits contextual flexibility to the corresponding adaptive or supervisory architecture; and translate the claim that such rigidity undermines free will or agency into terms of loop structure, setpoint mutability, or supervisory control. Then provide a residue report: identify which philosophical claims about mechanical rigidity, flexibility, and freedom map cleanly onto control-theoretic distinctions; which survive only through distortion or analogy (for instance, treating 'agency' as the presence of a meta-controller); and which have no counterpart in the framework at all. Conclude by assessing whether the missing residue reveals a genuine limitation of control theory—something it is designed to exclude—or whether it suggests that the original philosophical distinction between rigid and free action was less technically determinate than it first appeared.
