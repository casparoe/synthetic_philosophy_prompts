I cover science and ideas for a general-interest publication, and I am currently drafting a feature on whether macroeconomic history can ever deliver causal knowledge about national prosperity. While reporting, I sat in on a rigorous exchange between Dr. Aisha Okonkwo, a political economist, and Dr. James Whitmore, a comparative historian. I am asking you to referee their disagreement for my readers.

Dr. Okonkwo began by contending that comparative historical analysis can never genuinely isolate institutional effects from geographic and cultural confounders, because at the national scale the stable unit treatment value assumption fails: global trade networks, colonial knowledge diffusion, and epidemiological linkages render cases interdependent rather than experimentally independent. She pointed to the divergence of North and South Korea and to Japan versus Qing China as standard institutional narratives that claim explanatory victory, yet she argued that in neither case were geography and culture ever truly held constant.

Dr. Whitmore responded that natural experiments—arbitrary colonial borders in West Africa, or the 1945 division of Korea—do provide meaningful causal leverage. Even absent perfect isolation, he said, institutional variation within shared geographic and cultural contexts reveals mediation: institutions may not be pure treatments, but they are the mechanisms through which deeper conditions are mobilized into economic outcomes.

Dr. Okonkwo then introduced a deeper objection. She argued that Whitmore's mediation defense presupposes causal modularity—the assumption that institutional and cultural channels operate like separable circuits in a complex adaptive system. She proposed instead that macro-history is characterized by structural overdetermination: multiple sufficient causes co-evolve and are observationally equivalent at the aggregate level, so there may be no determinate fact about which factor is doing the work. Without counterfactual independence, she concluded, mediation claims are metaphysically ambitious rather than empirically grounded.

Dr. Whitmore granted the co-evolution point but maintained that the difficulty is epistemic, not ontological. He argued that careful process tracing—documenting temporal sequences in which formal rules change, behavioral norms adjust, and economic indicators shift—can support mediation even without perfect counterfactual isolation. The real barrier, he suggested, is not structural overdetermination but the scarcity of granular temporal data in the historical record.

Please adjudicate this exchange with precision. For each round, indicate who makes the stronger point and why. Then render an overall verdict: is Dr. Okonkwo's charge of structural overdetermination decisive against institutional mediation, or does Dr. Whitmore's epistemic framing preserve a meaningful role for institutional explanation? Finally, identify what both interlocutors seem to overlook—whether regarding contingent leadership, the problem of equifinality in qualitative comparison, or some other gap in their reasoning.
