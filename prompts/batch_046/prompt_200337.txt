In a graduate seminar on international criminal law, a colleague routinely ends debates about the legitimacy of the Nuremberg and Tokyo tribunals by invoking the maxim nullum crimen sine lege — no crime without law — treating it as a single knock-down objection: because the tribunals punished acts that were not clearly criminal under positive law at the time of commission, the convictions were necessarily illegitimate and amounted to mere victors' justice. I want to respond with precision rather than assent. Disambiguate that slogan for me. Lay out the distinct precise theses it could express: for example, a claim about retroactive statutory creation, about retroactive judicial application of existing norms, about a moral constraint on punishment independent of positive law, about procedural rule-of-law safeguards, or about the illegitimacy of ex post facto jurisdiction. For each reading, say whether it is a platitude, a substantive and defensible claim, or actually false. Then indicate which reading my colleague is most likely invoking when he uses the phrase to dismiss the trials, and whether that particular reading actually supports the conclusion that Nuremberg and Tokyo were illegitimate. I am especially interested in whether the strongest version of the maxim distinguishes between retroactively defining crimes against peace or humanity and retroactively extending domestic criminal jurisdiction — a distinction that figures in the Tokyo Trial dissent but is rarely made explicit when people quote the principle.
