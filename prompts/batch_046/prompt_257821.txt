I am writing a proposal for a one-semester independent study in philosophy of technology — approximately five thousand words, to be completed over four months — and I need help turning a recent personal preoccupation into a tractable research plan. Over the past term I have been mentoring undergraduates who use conversational AI in their philosophical writing, and I have found my own judgments about these submissions strangely unstable: sometimes I feel deceived by the text, sometimes I feel the student has been fully transparent, and sometimes I suspect the deception, if there is one, is not located in any particular false claim but in the structure of the interaction itself. Reading Dennett’s recent arguments about AI generating counterfeit people has given me vocabulary for part of this unease, yet the standard framing — asking whether large language models already are counterfeit people — strikes me as too quick, because it treats counterfeiting as a single failure mode rather than a family of related failures.

My half-formed idea is that Dennett’s framework conflates two dimensions that can come apart: representational counterfeiting, in which a system presents itself as possessing mental states or a biography it lacks, and interactional counterfeiting, in which a system occupies a reciprocal social role — interlocutor, advisor, confidant — in a way that displaces genuine human accountability without necessarily making any false claim about its inner life. If these dimensions are separable, then a synthetic deepfake of a public figure might score high on the first but low on the second, while a customer-service chatbot that never claims consciousness might score low on the first but high on the second. I want to ask whether refining Dennett’s account along these lines is philosophically coherent, whether it forces us to classify certain highly scripted human roles — institutional spokespersons, rigidly protocol-bound counselors, or actors performing sustained method roles — as partial interactional counterfeits, and whether that extension strengthens the framework or collapses it into overbreadth.

For the proposal I need the following: a precise statement of the research question that makes it determinately answerable; an explanation of why existing treatments in both philosophy of mind and AI ethics leave this particular ambiguity unresolved; the actual method — what I would do phase by phase over the semester, whether conceptual analysis, comparative case construction, or normative argument, and why that approach rather than an empirical survey or a literature review; what a successful result would look like, including the conceptual machinery I would need to deliver; an honest statement of what would count as the project failing; and a realistic assessment of whether this scope is appropriate for a single semester. I will supply my own references; please do not include a bibliography or request citations. The deliverable should be a focused philosophical argument, not a survey of the AI-ethics literature.
