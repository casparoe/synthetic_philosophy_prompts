I'm on the appeals panel for our university's centre for international research ethics and we've got a messy case that touches on pragmatics but needs a concrete decison. A Dutch PI and a Japanese postdoc in the same lab have filed opposing complaints: the Dutch researcher insists that in group critiques everyone must state exactly what they take to be false and correct it bluntly, arguing that anything less is a white lie that violates the norm that you should only assert what you know; the Japanese researcher says this violates the indirect-correction norms of her trainning, where pointing out an error directly causes face-loss and undermines collaborative trust, and she claims the Dutch PI is actually asserting things he hasn't fully verified since he never re-ran her stats. The board has to decide whether to mandate a Universal Direct Assertion Protocol for all collaborations, or allow culturally adaptive feedback norms where indirect formulations are permitted even when they technically involve saying something not strictly believed.

There's also a philosophical knot I want the memo to untangle without turning into a literature review. Some board members think the Dutch view maps onto the knowledge norm of assertion, others onto a simpler truth norm, and one member brought up the niche idea that Japanese indirectness isn't really assertion at all but a kind of social-contract speech act that shouldn't be judged by assertoric standards. I want the memo to treat all three seriously but not drift into bibliographies or citations, since half our board don't have library access.

Draft the board's decision memo — approve the protocol, reject it, or approve with conditions — with reasons the researchers could actually respond to. Be honest about the strongest consideration on the losing side. Start with the bottom-line recommendation in bold, then reasoning. Keep it to one page, max 550 words. Don't ask us to cite papers we can't check.
