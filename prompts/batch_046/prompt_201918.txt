So me and some friends have been arguing about the dirty hands problem in politics—like when a leader has to lie or do something morally rotten to stop a bigger disaster. We read some Walzer and then got into this weird side-track about modern bureaucracy. Half the group says the problem only really bites if there's one clear person whose hands are dirty, but nowadays decisions are spread across committees and advisors, so the moral stain gets diluted like a drop of ink in a bucket—everyone gets a tiny smudge but nobody actually has dirty hands anymore. The other half thinks thats exactly wrong, and distributing the decision just multiplies the number of people who have to live with having done wrong, even if each share is smaller, kind of like splitting a bill at a resturant but the bill is guilt.

We've been stuck on this for like three months now and it's getting frustrating because nobody moves. Is this disagreement actually about whether moral responsibility can be divided up like property? Or is one side focused on the subjective feeling of being stained and the other on objective accountability structures? Maybe we're answering totally different questions—one about political ethics and one about organizational psychology—but we're using the same phrase "dirty hands" to cover both and thats why we keep talking past each other. I want a real diagnosis of what kind of stalemate this is, not just another summary of the standard view. And if you think it's substantive, tell me what distinction or conceptual move could actually unstick us, or be honest and say it's just bedrock intuition about collective responsibility and we should move on.
