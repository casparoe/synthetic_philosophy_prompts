A housing cooperative maintains encrypted access logs for shared spaces, but now proposes destroying the decryption keys while retaining the files—creating permanent physical presence coupled with permanent epistemic absence. One board member argues that if no one can ever recover the differential record between permitted and unpermitted presence, then the very idea of "unauthorized access" loses its grounding: identifying a breach requires contrasting actual presence against an authorized baseline, and without recoverable traces that contrast collapses. Another member counters that the encrypted files still exist as potential evidence, so the conditions for identifying a breach remain intact even if access is permanently blocked—like a sealed legal record that exists though unreadable. Assess whether permanently blocking access to a record is conceptually equivalent to destroying it in this context, determine whether the first member correctly identifies a necessary condition for the concept of privacy violation or wrongly treats an evidential gap as a conceptual one, and advise whether destroying the keys should be regarded as self-defeating in principle or only as an irreversible practical risk.
