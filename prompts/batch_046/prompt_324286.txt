I'm preparing a six-month research proposal for a philosophy-of-mind fellowship, and I want the central question to turn on pain asymbolia—not as a clinical curiosity, but as a pressure point for theories that identify the badness of pain with its phenomenal character or with its representational content about bodily damage. Patients with asymbolia report pain, locate it, and rate its intensity, yet they do not find it aversive and do not initiate avoidance behavior; they sometimes describe the sensation as "not hurting" despite acknowledging that it is pain. The existing literature tends to split between two-factor theorists, who take this as proof that sensory-discriminative and affective-motivational components are separable, and unitary representationalists, who argue that the patients still have the badness but have lost the behavioral link. Both sides treat the dissociation as either confirming or not confirming a standard architecture, but neither has asked whether asymbolia reveals a third possibility: a failure not of sensation, not of evaluation, but of what I will call valence-locking—the mechanism by which a nociceptive state acquires its intrinsic motivational charge without passing through an explicit evaluative judgment. My proposal asks precisely this: does the possibility of valence-lock failure show that the badness of pain is neither part of its phenomenal content nor a separate cognitive appraisal, but rather a non-representational binding condition that can be disrupted while both content and appraisal remain intact?

The project would proceed in three stages. First, I would map the logical space of architectures: unitary phenomenal badness, dual-track with binding, dual-track without binding, and the valence-lock model I am introducing. Second, I would use the phenomenological reports and behavioral profiles of documented asymbolia cases—not to settle empirical disputes, but to test whether each architecture can accommodate the specific pattern of preserved discrimination, absent avoidance, and intact second-order reflection without ad hoc adjustments. Third, I would draw out the metaphysical and normative consequences: if valence-locking is structurally distinct from both representation and judgment, then arguments that ground moral obligations about pain in its representational content or in its subjective unpleasantness both miss the feature that makes pain matter. A useful deliverable would be a conceptual framework that specifies exactly what would count as evidence that the lock mechanism is part of the experience rather than downstream of it, and that identifies the costs of accepting or rejecting that claim. The project could fail if the distinction collapses into the familiar affective-sensory divide; I would guard against this by requiring that the valence-lock model generate predictions about other dissociation syndromes that the standard two-factor view does not generate. I am not asking for a bibliography or citation list—I will supply references myself—so the proposal should explain why this framing is tractable for a single researcher in six months, what a negative result looks like, and why the angle is not just a relabeling of positions already in circulation.
