Analyse the dissolution of the Soviet Union between 1989 and 1991, and the subsequent “shock therapy” transition in Russia during the early 1990s, through the lens of Karl Popper’s critique of historicism and his distinction between piecemeal social engineering and utopian social engineering. First, treat the collapse itself as a test case for whether history can be governed by discoverable laws: was the rapid cascade predictable in principle by any historicist framework—whether Marxist-Leninist or liberal-teleological—and does Popper’s argument that social systems are open, and that predictions about them can become self-fulfilling or self-defeating, explain why both Soviet and Western analysts largely failed to foresee it? Then turn to the reform period: in what sense did the rapid privatisation and price-liberalisation programmes pursued by the Yeltsin government, advised by Western economists, constitute an attempt to redesign society according to an abstract blueprint without adequate trial-and-error? Assess whether Popper’s preference for piecemeal, reversible interventions offers a convincing explanation of why shock therapy produced such severe welfare losses and institutional breakdown, or whether the scale and speed of transition were forced by conditions that made gradualism impossible. Finally, say what the episode reveals about the limits of Popper’s framework: can his rejection of historical laws be reconciled with the clear structural pressures—fiscal crisis, imperial overextension, technological stagnation—that made some form of systemic crisis likely, and does his theory have anything to say about why the specific form of collapse—relatively bloodless in Russia proper, violent in parts of the periphery—took the shape it did? Be disciplined about the mapping: identify exactly who the relevant actors were, what options they faced, and where the theory is silent rather than forcing it to speak.
