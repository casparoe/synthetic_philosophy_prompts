I was arguing with a friend over coffee about the frame problem — not the standard AI version you read in textbooks, but a more stubborn variant I think people miss. My view is that the frame problem only arises if you buy a particular representationalist picture: that to act rationally, an agent has to explicitly encode which features of the world stay constant across actions. I reject that premiss. Instead I think cognition works through selective updating — only disruptions to an internal predictive model get propagated, and everything else is left alone by default, not represented as unchanged. So in my view the frame problem isn't a deep technical obstacle; it's a pseudo-problem generated by a false dichotomy between total explicit representation and total ignorance. Modern predictive-processing architecures, even the simpler ones, show what this middle ground looks like in practice. But I want to know: what would actually count against this? I'm not asking for a list of philosophers who disagree — I want the evidence, formal results, or concrete failure cases that should move me. For instance, do predictive models secretly smuggle frame axioms back in at a lower level? Are there empirical robotics cases where selective updating fails in exactly the frame-problem way? Has my position been qualified into unfalsifiability — and if it has, is that a fatal flaw for a view of this kind? Be honest and take a definite line; don't just lay out options and leave me to choose.
