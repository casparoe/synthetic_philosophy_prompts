As a software engineer without formal training in philosophy, I have been trying to understand whether self-deception is even conceptually possible—the classic paradox that if I deceive myself, I must simultaneously know and not know the same fact, which seems logically incoherent. I keep returning to Kazuo Ishiguro's The Remains of the Day, in which Stevens, the butler, spends the novel reconstructing his years of service to Lord Darlington and his unacknowledged attachment to Miss Kenton in ways that look like sustained self-deception but never quite collapse into conscious lying. I would like a philosophical reading of the novel through this specific problem: does Stevens genuinely deceive himself about Lord Darlington's political entanglements and about his own emotional life, or is he merely performing ignorance for social and professional reasons? To keep the analysis precise, please test two competing resolutions of the self-deception paradox against the text: one that treats the self as divided into simultaneous, non-communicating belief-states—something analogous, in my field, to access-controlled modules that hold information without exposing it to the executive process—and another that treats self-deception as a single agent's strategic inattention, where the knowledge is present but never activated rather than genuinely hidden from a part of the mind. Apply both models to specific, well-known moments—Stevens's speeches about professional dignity, his refusal to acknowledge Lord Darlington's Nazi sympathies, his interactions with Miss Kenton, and his final retrospective narration on the pier. For each scene, say where the model illuminates Stevens's choice and where the novel resists being reduced to that model. Does Ishiguro ultimately dramatize a self-deception that requires a genuinely divided mind, or does he show that Stevens always possessed the relevant knowledge and simply managed not to act on it? I am not asking for a bibliography or a survey of scholars; I want to think this through directly. Finally, I am less interested in whether the novel merely illustrates a philosophical thesis than in what it adds to the debate that abstract argument alone misses—perhaps something about the temporal structure of retrospection, the masking function of social role, or the difference between deceiving oneself about facts and about feelings. Where should an honest reader admit that the philosophical framework distorts the novel, and what does the novel reveal about self-deception that pure conceptual analysis tends to overlook?
