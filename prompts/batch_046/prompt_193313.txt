As I develop the philosophical backbone of my current novel—which follows a protagonist moving between a Confucian-influenced community and a Western liberal one—I have settled on a set of commitments about personhood and community that I want to check for coherence before I write further. I hold that (1) the Confucian relational model, in which the self is constituted by roles and reciprocal obligations rather than prior to them, is ontologically correct and the Western atomistic model is largely a fiction; (2) that genuine moral criticism requires a standpoint not fully constituted by the roles one happens to occupy, since otherwise dissent collapses into mere social deviation rather than normative evaluation; (3) that these two models represent genuinely incompatible ontologies of the person, not merely different cultural descriptions of a common human substrate; (4) that individuals raised between such frameworks do not synthesize them into a hybrid identity but instead experience what I call an "ontological friction," inhabiting each framework sequentially without merging them; and (5) that evolutionary or universalist accounts of human nature should be rejected, because they would dissolve these differences into superficial variation. Do these hang together? Specifically, is the tension between (1) and (2) fatal, or can relational constitution be preserved alongside a critical distance? Does (4) genuinely conflict with (3), or does the appearance of contradiction dissolve once we allow one consciousness to instantiate incompatible ontologies in different contexts? And does (2) secretly depend on the universalist assumption I reject in (5), making the package unstable at its core? I am not asking for a survey of East-West philosophy, but for an audit: rank the tensions by severity, identify the least costly revision, and distinguish the real conflicts from those that only look like conflicts.
