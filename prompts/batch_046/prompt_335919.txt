The historiographical dispute over the French Revolution has settled into a long stalemate between structuralist accounts—which treat the Terror as the necessary outcome of class struggle, ideological radicalization, and the internal logic of revolutionary sovereignty—and contingency accounts, which treat it as the product of wartime emergency, factional rivalry, and individual decision under extreme pressure. In my graduate seminar, participants on both sides accept the same chronology—the Law of 22 Prairial, the Thermidorean Reaction, the economic crisis of 1793—and work from the same archives, yet neither side has moved an inch on the core question of whether revolutions structurally “devour their children” or whether the Terror was an avoidable accident. I am inclined to diagnose the impasse as partly methodological: the structuralists seem to be offering a causal explanation that makes the Terror predictable from deeper class dynamics, while the contingency theorists seem to be insisting on a normative condition—whether the Revolution betrayed its own principles—disguised as a causal claim about inevitability. Is that diagnosis correct? Or is there a hidden empirical disagreement about the actual autonomy of the Committee of Public Safety relative to sans-culotte pressure that both camps are masking with philosophical talk about structural necessity? More specifically, is this disagreement substantive (about the nature of historical explanation), verbal (centered on the word “inevitable”), or a case of two groups answering different questions—one about causal structure, one about moral accountability—under a single heading? Given your diagnosis, what would count as genuine progress: a new archival finding about decision-making in 1794, a philosophical distinction between structural causation and narrative explanation, or a reconceptualization of the question away from inevitability altogether? I want a diagnosis supported by what each side actually claims, not a survey of the literature, together with an honest assessment of whether anything—evidence, conceptual distinction, or reframing—could actually move the debate.
