I am currently studying Sartre’s ontology of freedom in a final-year existentialism seminar, and I have become stuck on an analogy our tutor introduced to explain the relation between radical freedom and bad faith. She compared the for-itself to the sole author of an unfinished serial novel: there is no outline imposed from outside, yet the author cannot deny that previous instalments have already been printed, which stands for facticity. Bad faith, on this picture, is like the author pretending that the publisher’s marketing category — say, romance — has rigidly determined every narrative choice, thereby disowning the authorship that remains operative even within generic constraints. I can see how this captures Sartre’s idea that freedom is unavoidable, but I am unsure how far the mapping holds once we press on his claim that freedom is always situated rather than abstract. Specifically, does the analogy implicitly import a dualism between the transcendent author and the immanent character that Sartre’s rejection of the ego-substance is meant to dissolve? And if we accept that the printed instalments constrain without determining, can the analogy still license the conclusion that every choice is radically undetermined, or does it collapse into a compatibilist picture of freedom within constraints? I would like an explicit audit of the correspondences, an account of which disanalogies matter for Sartre’s argument against self-deception, and a judgement on whether a better analogy — perhaps drawn from mathematics or law — might avoid importing the author-character split.
