Spinoza’s political writings defend democracy as the form of government closest to the state of nature and therefore the most absolute expression of collective sovereignty, yet he restricts full civic participation to free adult males, excluding women, servants, and foreigners on the grounds that dependence undermines the independent power required for citizenship. This creates a tension that is often noted but rarely treated as a live problem for democratic theory rather than a historical curiosity. I want a defended agenda, not a survey: among the open lines of work that take this tension seriously—whether the exclusion is structurally required by Spinoza’s metaphysics of power or merely a contingent limitation of his era; whether the concept of the power of the multitude can be reconstructed without exclusionary conditions; and whether comparing his direct-democracy model to modern representative institutions reveals a neglected problem about scale and democratic agency—which matter most, and for what end? Rank them according to explicit criteria such as importance for contemporary democratic legitimacy, tractability within Spinoza’s own framework, and whether resolving them would change how we think about the boundaries of the demos.
