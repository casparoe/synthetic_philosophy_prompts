I am a postgraduate student enrolled in a course on nineteenth-century philosophies of history, and next week I am presenting a twenty-minute paper to our departmental graduate seminar. The audience will be split between intellectual historians who specialise in German historicism and philosophers of science who are deeply wary of reducing methodological disputes to psychological genesis. My thesis is that Nietzsche’s tripartite distinction between monumental, antiquarian, and critical history—drawn from his 1874 meditation on historiography and life—ought to be read not as a taxonomy of scholarly methods that historians freely adopt, but as a diagnostic of involuntary psychological compensations that arise when a culture’s present vitality is too weak to generate its own future. Specifically, I argue that critical history is routinely misinterpreted as an emancipatory practice; on my reading it functions more like an autoimmune response in which life, failing to draw nourishment from the past, turns destructively upon its own history—not to liberate the present, but to confirm the present’s exhaustion.

Please prepare me for the question period as if I were the speaker. I need a prep sheet, not a full defence: order the objections by how likely they are and how damaging they would be, phrase each in the vocabulary that faction would actually use, and for each indicate what has real force, what I should concede up front—perhaps that Nietzsche himself occasionally writes as though historians deliberately select a mode—and what reply must land if the proposal is to survive. The historians will likely resist the psychologisation of archival practice and the collapse of normative method into symptom; the philosophers of science may charge me with conflating the context of discovery with the context of justification, or with smuggling in a functionalist account of knowledge that I have not defended. Crucially, include the objection I am hoping nobody asks: whether my reading collapses the distinction between critical history and mere cultural nihilism, and whether I can defend that distinction without appealing to a normative theory of historical objectivity that Nietzsche explicitly rejects. Keep the tone practical, formal, and pitched to someone who must reply in the time available.
