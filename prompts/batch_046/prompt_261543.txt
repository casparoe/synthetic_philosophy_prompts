I'm moderating a long-form interview for a rationalist-leaning philosophy podcast. The guest is an unnamed philosopher of science defending a specific, somewhat unorthodox thesis: that Darwin's evolutionary mechanism and Turing's universal computation instantiate the same "strange inversion"—namely, that functional competence (producing adaptive biological form, or executing correct computation) systematically precedes and operates independently of comprehension (intentional design, or explicit knowledge of rules). She argues this is not mere metaphor but a reversal of the explanatory order: the substrate-level process generates the outcome first, and any reasoning about that outcome is always retrospective reconstruction. She distinguishes her view both from standard evolutionary emergence accounts and from the familiar "design without a designer" framing.

Draft ten interview questions. They should not ask her to restate or summarize the thesis. Instead, include at least one question pressing on whether this inversion genuinely undermines the appearance/reality distinction in evolutionary biology, or whether it merely redescribes it; one that connects her claim to the broader literature on competence without comprehension in linguistics and cognition, but forces her to specify whether the Darwinian and Turing cases are structurally analogous or only superficially parallel; one that anticipates the standard objection that a Turing machine is deliberately engineered and therefore involves no inversion of reasoning at all—include a direct follow-up that asks whether she means a causal inversion, a logical inversion, or an epistemic inversion, and why that distinction matters for the coherence of her argument; one that asks what empirical finding—say, in evolutionary developmental biology or in mechanistic interpretability of large neural networks—would count as genuinely disconfirming her view rather than merely requiring a more complex model; and a closing question asking whether, if this inversion is taken seriously, it should change how we evaluate scientific explanations generally—specifically, whether we ought to privilege retrospective, outcome-driven explanations over forward-reasoning ones. Keep the tone rigorous but conversational, appropriate for an audience familiar with basic philosophy of science and with rationalist forum conventions.
