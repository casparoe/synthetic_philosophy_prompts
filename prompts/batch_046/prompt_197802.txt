I am observing a dispute between two philosophers, Priya and Leo, concerning Davidson's principle of charity in radical interpretation. They accept the framework—charity, triangulation, and the rejection of scheme-content dualism—but disagree over a particular case.

The speaker repeatedly asserts, 'The crop will fail,' yet takes every practical measure to ensure a successful harvest; the assertion is never withdrawn.

Priya argues that charity requires attributing the belief that the crop will succeed, treating the verbal utterance as a ritualised expression of anxiety rather than a literal belief. She maintains that Davidson's constraint is to maximise rationality and agreement, so multiplying logical contradictions is worse than attributing a non-literal speech act.

Leo replies that this saves rationality only by ignoring triangulation: the speaker's actions are causally coupled with the world in ways aligned with success, not failure, so attributing a belief in failure severs the shared causal environment Davidson requires. He proposes instead that the speaker employs a modal framework in which 'will fail' expresses a normative or precautionary stance, not a predictive belief—thereby preserving both rational action and literal meaning.

Priya counters that Leo has smuggled in Quinean indeterminacy: altering the speaker's logical or modal structure to accommodate behaviour violates Davidson's insistence that there are no genuinely incommensurable conceptual schemes. If such adjustments are permitted, she says, interpretation collapses into arbitrary reconstruction.

Leo responds that he is not positing an incommensurable scheme, but noting that Davidson's triangulation permits shared causal interaction without requiring identical logical constants. He further asks whether charity ought to apply not only to first-order beliefs but also to the speaker's second-order interpretation of the interpreter's intentions—an aspect Davidson's account of social interaction hints at but rarely develops.

Can you adjudicate their exchange point by point, saying who is right at each step and not only who wins overall? In particular, assess whether Leo's appeal to second-order interpretation is compatible with Davidson's rejection of scheme-content dualism. Please state how confident you are in your claims, and identify the specific point about which you are least sure.
