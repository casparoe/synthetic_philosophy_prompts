Write a pantoum of four quatrains following the classic repetition scheme: in each successive quatrain, lines two and four of the previous stanza become lines one and three, with the opening stanza's lines eventually returning in altered positions in the final quatrain to resolve the form. Use the compulsive return of lines to enact the principle structurally: let the refrains begin as assertions of diachronic coherence, then shift meaning with each recurrence so that by the third quatrain the same words express the anxiety of anticipating a trusted expert's contrary judgment. The turn should come in the second stanza, where the speaker realizes that expecting to be corrected is not identical to expecting to be right, and that reflection might stabilize present error rather than cure it. The poem should be precise enough that a reader familiar with the diachronic Dutch-book defense and its standard counterexample—anticipated evidence that undermines one's current view—can map each repeated line onto a specific stage of the debate, yet it should read as verse first and a philosophical exercise second. About sixteen lines, no fixed meter required, no citations or source lists, and no explanatory prose after the poem—just the pantoum itself.
