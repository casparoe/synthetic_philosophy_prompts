A colleague summarized the Bayesian response to the problem of old evidence—the challenge that data already well-known before a theory is proposed seem unable to confirm it—approximately as follows. She said that Bayesians solve the difficulty by denying that rational agents ever assign credence 1 to observed facts; instead, they maintain a small residual uncertainty, or epistemic slack, about every empirical claim, so that learning that a new theory entails an old datum can still raise the theory's posterior probability through Bayes's theorem. She added that on this view confirmation tracks changes in logical relationships between propositions rather than changes in belief about the world, and that critics are mistaken to think Bayesians must invoke counterfactual reasoning about what an agent would have believed had the data not been known. She concluded that, once this slack is built in, old evidence confirms a theory precisely to the extent that the theory renders the evidence less surprising, but that this collapses the distinction between genuinely predictive successes and post-hoc accommodations, since both involve the same logical update.

I am unsure how accurate this is. Which parts correctly represent the Bayesian strategy—particularly the role of learning an entailment versus learning the evidence itself, and the place of counterfactual or counter-actual reasoning in standard accounts? Which parts conflate distinct proposals, such as Jeffrey conditioning with other forms of belief revision? Is the idea of maintaining epistemic slack a genuine feature of the Bayesian literature or a misreading? And does her final conclusion about predictive success versus accommodation actually follow from the premises she assigns to Bayesians, or does the framework retain resources for distinguishing them?
