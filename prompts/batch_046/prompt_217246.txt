I have no formal background in history or archival theory, but I've been working through an idea on my own about the different textures of absence in historical records. Most discussions of archives and power focus on whose voices are missing—for example, illiterate laborers versus well-documented officials. I'm trying to push this further by distinguishing not just who is silent, but why the silence exists, because I think the cause of an absence shapes what we can legitimately infer from it. In particular, I'm drafting an essay that proposes three categories of archival silence: omission by social exclusion (the subject never had access to record-making); erasure by deliberate destruction (the record existed but was targeted); and decay by format mismatch (the record was created in a medium that preservation systems were not designed to maintain, such as early magnetic storage or informal digital communications). I'm tentatively calling this framework "differential archival decay," though that phrase is my own invention. The essay argues that conflating these silences leads to a kind of interpretive error—treating a destroyed document as if it had never existed, or treating technological loss as if it reflected social marginalization. Before I finalize the draft, I would like comments on several points. First, is the tripartite distinction actually stable, or do the categories collapse into each other in practice? Second, am I overstating the practical difference for someone trying to read a historical source—does knowing the cause of absence actually change the interpretive method, or is it mainly a descriptive refinement? Third, is there an obvious historical or contemporary case that undermines the framework, perhaps one where a single absence results from all three causes simultaneously? Finally, I would welcome suggestions for sharpening the stakes: I want the argument to feel like more than an exercise in classification, but I am unsure how to connect it to broader questions about whose stories survive without falling into generic claims about power and representation.
