You serve on the Ethics and Public Space Committee of Villebrune, a mid-sized city with a diverse population and a longstanding commitment to republican neutrality. The committee must decide whether to approve a proposed “Contemplative Commons” inside the new central library: a bookable modular room with movable screens, floor cushions arranged in no fixed orientation, and a policy permitting users to bring temporary, non-installed religious or philosophical items during reserved slots, while prohibiting any permanent symbol or fixture. The secularist bloc on the city council argues that using public funds to maintain a space predictably used for religious practice violates laïcité, creating indirect state endorsement and risking the confessionalization of civic space. The interfaith coalition counters that refusing the space forces religious citizens into invisibility, reproducing the Anglo-American multiculturalist error of treating neutrality as mere absence rather than equitable presence. A staff philosopher has submitted an internal analysis distinguishing procedural neutrality—the rules apply identically to all users—from substantive neutrality—the design must not structurally favor any comprehensive doctrine—arguing that the room’s “secular grammar” (individual, orientation-independent, temporary use) implicitly disadvantages communal prayer and ritually directed practice, so formal openness may mask substantive bias.

Draft a formal policy memo to the committee recommending either approval with enforceable conditions, denial with explanation, or a redesigned alternative. Your memo should:
1. Clarify the conceptual distinction between state neutrality and state secularism (laïcité) as it applies to plural societies, and state where this proposal falls.
2. Assess whether the “secular grammar” objection is decisive, and if not, what specific design or policy adjustments could mitigate it without abandoning modular neutrality.
3. Present the strongest argument for the position you reject—if you recommend approval, treat seriously the laïcist concern about indirect endorsement and the slippery slope toward recognizing group-specific spaces; if you recommend denial, treat seriously the multiculturalist concern about equal dignity, visibility, and the privatization of religious identity.
4. Conclude with a concrete, actionable recommendation that the committee could adopt, including explicit conditions, monitoring mechanisms, or structural modifications.

Write for committee members who are not specialists in political philosophy but need to grasp the stakes. Do not include bibliographies, citations, references to specific scholarly texts, or requests for further reading. You may invent institutional details, but the reasoning should stand on its own.
