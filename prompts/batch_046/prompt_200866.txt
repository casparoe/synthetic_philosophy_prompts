As a novelist who has spent years considering how fictional characters can represent human types without actually being anyone, I find myself stalled in a structurally similar debate about artificial minds. In mechanistic interpretability, one camp—the realists—holds that neural networks genuinely represent concepts: directions in activation space correspond to semantic features, causal interventions on those directions alter downstream behavior in predictable ways, and probing classifiers reveal structured, extractable knowledge. The opposing camp—the eliminativists, or instrumentalists—replies that weights and activations are optimized statistical correlations and nothing more; calling them “representations” illegitimately imports intentionality and normative evaluation that these systems lack, while phenomena like polysemy and superposition demonstrate that there is no stable, discrete content being carried. For years the exchange has cycled through the same repertoire: realists cite linear representations and causal tracing; anti-realists cite distributed encoding and the absence of a “consumer” for any putative content. Nobody converts anyone, and the literature feels frozen.
