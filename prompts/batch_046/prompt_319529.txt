I am drafting my thesis for an independent philosophy seminar at my school, and I would like a consistency audit before I present. My argument concerns Montaigne's essay Of Cannibals, and I have adopted the following five commitments. (1) I read the essay as advancing a principle of reciprocal scrutiny: a culture's moral condemnation of another practice is epistemically legitimate only if the condemning culture applies the same critical standard to its own institutions with equal rigor. Because European Christendom condemns cannibalism while practicing religious torture and warfare, its judgment is disqualified—not because cannibalism is innocent in any absolute sense, but because the judge fails its own test. (2) Nevertheless, I deny that Montaigne is a full cultural relativist. I hold that he draws a substantive distinction between ritual cannibalism, which he portrays as lacking gratuitous cruelty, and European violence, which he treats as genuinely vicious; this allows cross-cultural evaluation without hypocrisy. (3) I think this distinction depends on a minimal, culture-transcendent normative claim: unnecessary physical suffering is a defect in any social practice, regardless of cultural framing, and this gives Montaigne the leverage to condemn European practices without excusing cannibal ones. (4) At the same time, I accept Montaigne's broader Pyrrhonian skepticism—evident elsewhere in the Essais—about reason, custom, and natural law. I take this to imply that reason itself is culturally conditioned, so the universalist suffering-claim in (3) cannot be rationally grounded in any neutral standpoint. (5) Finally, I believe Montaigne's admiration for cannibal nobility—their courage in battle, fidelity to captives, and refusal to lie—commits him to a virtue-ethical framework in which traits like courage and loyalty are objectively admirable across cultures, independent of local custom.

Please go through these commitments and tell me which pairs are in genuine tension and which only appear to conflict. For each genuine tension, rank them by severity, state which single commitment is the least costly to revise or abandon, and explain what that revision costs in terms of my overall interpretation. I am especially unsure whether (4) undermines both (3) and (5) simultaneously, or whether there is a way to insulate one from the other; and I want to know whether (1) and (3) are actually in conflict, or whether the tension dissolves once the procedural nature of reciprocal scrutiny is properly understood. Do not provide a bibliography or reading list; I need an internal audit of the position set, not external references.
