I'm on the district's curriculum arbitration panel for our new global-studies pilot, and we're meeting thursday to decide whether to approve a 11th-grade textbook package that's causing a huge fuss. The publisher's proposal is kind of unusual: for the American founding unit, they don't integrate 1619 and 1776 into one narrative — they run them as parallel tracks (one centered on slavery and dispossession, one on republican liberty and constitutional development), then stick a "synthetic" chapter at the end that tries to hold both in tension without resolving them. They do the exact same thing for the Japan-Korea unit: parallel tracks on colonial modernization vs. exploitation and the comfort women system, then a synthetic chapter on the 1965 treaty and memory politics. Teachers say it's pedagogically chaos and parents are yelling about "both sidesism." I need a decision memo for the board — approve, reject, or approve with conditions — with reasoning that actually engages the strongest worry from the other side. Don't just restate that history is complicated; recommend a concrete course, maybe with a trial timeline or conditions on teacher training. And please don't include any reading lists or citations — our board members won't read them and half of them don't have library access anyway. I was arguing with my colleague about whether this "dual-track" format is actually philosophically coherent or just avoids taking a stand, and I want the memo to address that too, maybe by saying whether epistemic humility requires narrative fragmentation or whether coherence is itself a pedagogical value we shouldn't abandon.
