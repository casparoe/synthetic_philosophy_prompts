I'm trying to determine my own view on how higher-order evidence interacts with first-order belief, particularly in cases that border on epistemic akrasia. I want to know whether there's a single principle behind my judgments, or whether they genuinely conflict.

Here are my verdicts on concrete cases, going by gut feeling.

Case 1: I derive a theorem through a long, complex proof. I then learn that mathematicians at my approximate skill level make errors in proofs of this length about 40 percent of the time. I carefully check my work twice and find no mistake. Verdict: I should still believe the theorem; the general base rate doesn't defeat my specific, accessible check.

Case 2: After reading several sources, I form a firm political judgment J. I then learn that people with my education, geography, and upbringing reach J in roughly 85 percent of cases, largely regardless of which sources they actually read. I can identify no particular error in my reasoning. Verdict: I should reduce my confidence in J, perhaps substantially, because the etiology suggests my reasoning wasn't genuinely driving the conclusion.

Case 3: As a clinician, I observe symptoms that strongly point to disease D. I then learn that my personal false-positive rate for diagnosing D is 30 percent. Verdict: I should remain somewhat confident that this patient has D, but calibrate downward—treating it as more likely than not, but not highly probable—because the clinical signs are present but my track record is poor.

Case 4: I take a standardized reasoning assessment and score in the bottom decile. Immediately afterward, I solve a specific logic puzzle, check my answer, and feel highly confident I'm right. Verdict: I should maintain my belief that the answer is correct; my general poor performance doesn't automatically defeat this particular successful exercise.

Case 5: In good lighting, I visually perceive an object O and form the belief that O is present. A trusted friend then tells me that I have unknowingly ingested a drug known to cause vivid false perceptions, though I feel entirely normal and notice no other hallucinations. Verdict: I should suspend or at least severely weaken my belief in O; the specific undermining evidence about this very experience outweighs my intact first-order phenomenology.

Case 6: Through sustained reflection, I come to believe that my ethical reasoning is systematically distorted by self-interest in ways I cannot introspectively detect. I then reach a specific ethical conclusion C through what feels like careful, unbiased reasoning, and I can locate no particular flaw in the steps. Part of me thinks I should reduce confidence in C given my general belief about corruption in this domain; part of me thinks that without identifying the specific distortion, reducing confidence is just self-undermining, and I should hold C until I can point to the error. Verdict: I'm genuinely torn.

Is there one principle—perhaps distinguishing general statistical weakness from specific causal undermining, or calibrating confidence rather than all-or-nothing defeat—that accommodates Cases 1 through 5 and gives a definite answer for Case 6? If not, which of my verdicts should I drop to preserve simplicity? And whatever principle survives, what does it predict for a case I haven't listed—say, a scientist who revises her model after learning that her initial parameter choice was influenced by an unconscious preference for round numbers, even though the revised parameters still fit the data?

Please take a committed position: state the principle precisely, defend it with reference to the cases that motivate each clause, say explicitly which of my verdicts it cannot save, and don't merely lay out rival accounts for me to choose between.
