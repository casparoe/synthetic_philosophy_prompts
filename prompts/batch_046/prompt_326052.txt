I'm currently taking an upper-level seminar on philosophy of science and philosophy of mind, and we've been working through the idea that certain reasoning strategies invert the usual explanatory order—Darwin's selection mechanism producing functional design without a designer's comprehension, and Turing's computation producing intelligent behavior without internal understanding. I've developed a set of commitments about these inversions and their application to contemporary artificial cognition, and before I present them in our reading group, I'd like to check whether they actually hang together or whether I'm smuggling in an unstated premise.

Here is where I stand. First, I accept Darwin's inversion as genuine: evolutionary adaptation is a case of sustained functional competence—survival and reproduction—produced by mechanisms (variation, selection, inheritance) that possess no comprehension of the problems they solve. Second, I take Turing's inversion seriously: a system can exhibit behavioral competence indistinguishable from intelligent understanding without possessing any internal grasp of meaning, and we should not require transparent internal mechanisms before attributing functional intelligence. Third, applying both inversions together, I hold that large language models exhibit precisely this structure—sophisticated linguistic and reasoning competence generated by statistical optimization and architectural design, with no reason to attribute comprehension to the system itself. Fourth, however, I want to maintain that human cognition is genuinely different: human understanding is not merely more complex pattern-matching but involves a form of embodied, world-directed comprehension that LLMs lack, and this difference justifies treating human and artificial linguistic outputs differently in epistemic and moral contexts. Fifth, I think this human comprehension itself emerged through evolutionary processes that never involved comprehension guiding them—selection built predictive and social capacities that eventually yielded conscious understanding, but the process was competence-without-comprehension all the way through. Sixth, I also hold that in scientific modeling, unrealistic idealizations are defensible only when they isolate a mechanism that actually operates in the target system; evolutionary psychology explanations often fail this test because they postulate adaptive mechanisms without showing they actually produced the trait, which makes them closer to just-so stories than to Darwin's genuinely mechanistic inversion.

Please audit these six commitments for tension. In particular, I'm worried that (5) undermines (4): if human comprehension emerged from a process that never involved comprehension, and if that process is structurally continuous with the statistical optimization that produces LLM outputs, then what grounds my insistence on a categorical rather than merely gradual distinction between human and artificial cognition? At the same time, I wonder whether (2) and (6) pull in opposite directions—if Turing warns us not to demand internal transparency, and Darwin shows that competence can arise without comprehension, then shouldn't I be more skeptical of my own confidence that I can distinguish LLM competence from human comprehension from the outside? I also want to know whether (6) introduces a hidden tension with (1): am I being inconsistent in praising Darwin's mechanistic inversion while criticizing evolutionary psychology for lacking mechanism? Rank the tensions by severity, identify any apparent tensions that dissolve on inspection, and tell me which single revision—giving up one of these six—would clean up the package most effectively, and what that revision would cost me.
