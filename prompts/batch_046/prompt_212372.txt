I spent most of last evening arguing with a close friend about moral luck after we both read a case study involving two software engineers who wrote functionally identical autonomous-vehicle algorithms; one version was deployed and caused a fatality, the other was shelved by a random server outage. My friend kept falling back on the line, "you can't hold someone responsible for what wasn't up to them," and used it to insist that the deployed engineer deserves no more blame than her twin. I think there's a sleight of hand happening between different kinds of "up to them," but I can't articulate it. Could you lay out the distinct, precise theses that slogan might express?

For each reading, please specify: what modality of control is required—causal, epistemic, volitional, diachronic, or agent-causal; whether the principle governs assessments of actions, character, outcomes, or all three; and whether it functions as a conceptual claim about the nature of responsibility, a normative claim about deserved blame, or an empirical claim about our actual practices. Then evaluate each: is it trivial, defensible but substantive, or false? Crucially, say whether that reading actually gets my friend his conclusion—that the deployed engineer should not be blamed more—without also committing him to the implausible view that constitutive luck (the kind of person one is) is always excused, or that resultant luck can never affect deserved blame. I am especially curious about a version that distinguishes synchronic from diachronic control and allows that an agent can be responsible for exercising a capacity even when the outcome was not within her direct causal reach. Please present the answer as a menu of readings rather than selecting one, and indicate which, if any, survive the objection that they collapse moral evaluation into pure quality-of-will assessment.

Finally, conclude with a single line reading exactly: Verdict: [a brief statement of whether any non-trivial reading supports my friend's argument without entailing universal exculpation].
