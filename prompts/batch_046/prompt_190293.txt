I work as a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy, and I have been trying to work out a coherent stance on whether the brain is essentially a computer. Before I discuss these views with others, I want a consistency audit of the package I have arrived at. Here are my commitments:

(1) I hold a weak, model-relative computationalism: cognitive processes can be usefully described in algorithmic terms, but this is an engineer's modeling choice rather than an ontological claim that the brain literally runs code, executes instructions, or instantiates discrete syntax.

(2) I think mental states are largely substrate-independent: if one reproduced the relevant causal-functional architecture in silicon or another medium, one would reproduce the corresponding mental states.

(3) I reject the idea that neurons manipulate discrete symbolic representations; brain computation is analog, continuous, parallel, and lacks anything resembling source code, variables, or formal syntax.

(4) I take Searle's Chinese Room argument seriously as showing that pure symbol manipulation is insufficient for intentionality or understanding, yet I do not think it undermines computationalism about the brain specifically, because I think the brain does not perform symbol manipulation in the first place.

(5) I think phenomenal consciousness—the subjective quality of experience—is real and not an illusion, but I also think it depends specifically on the brain's biological, analog dynamics. I do not think a digital simulation that merely approximates those dynamics at a functional level would possess real phenomenal consciousness, even if it matched all behavioral and cognitive outputs.

(6) Because brains evolved rather than being engineered, I think there is no single "correct" functional abstraction of the brain; useful computational descriptions depend on the predictive goals of the modeler, and different levels of description are appropriate for different explanatory questions.

Please audit this set for joint consistency. I am not asking for a general lecture on each view; I want to know which commitments genuinely pull against each other, which pairs only appear to conflict but actually cohere upon inspection, and—ordered by severity—what the least costly revision to each real tension would be and what I would sacrifice by making it. Specifically, address whether there is a hidden tension between my rejection of discrete neural syntax (3) and my weak computationalism (1); whether my substrate-independence claim (2) survives my view that consciousness is tied to biological analog dynamics (5); whether there is a conflict between substrate independence (2) and the denial of a correct functional architecture (6); and how my reading of the Chinese Room (4) interacts with the rest of the package. Rank the actual tensions by severity, identify the apparent tension that dissolves on closer examination, and for each genuine tension specify which single commitment is the cheapest to revise and what that revision costs. Do not ask me which view I prefer to abandon; simply state what has to give in each case.
