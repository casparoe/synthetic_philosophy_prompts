I've come to think that the old worry about sphexishness—the rigid, almost programmed way certain wasps execute an unvarying burrow-checking sequence—might actually point toward what free will is, not away from it. My view is that human freedom isn't some mysterious uncaused cause, but a specific kind of behavioral elasticity: the capacity to interrupt an ongoing routine when circumstances have shifted in a way the routine never anticipated, without needing an external jolt to break the loop. On this account, freedom is graded flexibility rather than metaphysical exemption from causation. If an agent can revise its own action-plan mid-execution using internal representations of counterfactual outcomes, then it has escaped sphexishness, regardless of whether those revisions are themselves fully determined.

But I want an honest map of what would count against this, not just the usual compatibilist versus libertarian exchange. Specifically, what findings about the neural basis of habit interruption, what formal results about deterministic flexibility, or what observed human behaviors would show that this elasticity is illusory or collapses into a more complex rigidity? I'm particularly interested in whether a system could display perfect behavioral unpredictability—never repeating a pattern—yet still lack anything worthy of being called freedom, and whether that possibility undermines my claim that flexibility is sufficient. I also want to know whether defenders of this view should concede defeat if every human "interruption" turned out to be triggered by hidden sensory cues we haven't detected, or whether that is only an awkward qualification. Please rank the defeaters from fatal to merely uncomfortable, say which ones a committed defender would grant as genuinely damaging, and assess whether this position risks being qualified into unfalsifiability—if every counterexample can be absorbed as "just a more complex routine"—and whether that insulation is a virtue or a vice for a theory of this kind.
