In philosophy of biology, the dispute between organizational and evolutionary definitions of life has persisted for decades without resolution, even though both sides accept the same borderline cases: viruses, synthetic protocells, and organisms in extreme cryptobiosis such as tardigrades. The organizational view holds that life requires autopoietic closure—a self-producing boundary that maintains metabolic identity—so viruses are not alive because they depend on host machinery, and synthetic cells qualify only when they achieve genuine organizational closure. The evolutionary view holds that life is fundamentally a population-level process of heritable variation with differential fitness, so viruses can be alive if they participate in evolutionary dynamics, and closure is merely one contingent mechanism among others. Both sides invoke self-replicating digital programs and tardigrade cryptobiosis as evidence, yet interpret them in opposite directions. Is this a substantive disagreement about which properties are essential, a verbal disagreement about whether "life" names a natural kind or a cluster concept with family resemblance, or are the two camps answering different questions—one about the structure of an individual system, the other about the dynamics of a lineage—under a single heading?
