I'm trying to determine whether honouring sunk costs is always irrational, by testing my intuitions against concrete cases. Paying for an inedible restaurant meal and forcing oneself to finish it: clearly irrational. Continuing a failing research programme solely because one has already published three papers in it: also irrational, yet somehow less blameworthy than the meal case. Staying in a long marriage largely because of shared history and years invested: not obviously irrational, and perhaps even admirable. Sending additional troops into a conflict because soldiers have already died: widely condemned as fallacious, yet politically almost unavoidable. Keeping a promise to complete a collaborative artwork with a friend who has withdrawn, motivated by the months already invested: I genuinely hesitate here. Is there a principle that distinguishes permissible from impermissible honouring of sunk costs—perhaps turning on whether the investment is private or collective, or whether abandoning the course would violate a commitment constitutive of the project itself? State the principle precisely, apply it to these verdicts and to the case I keep revisiting—refusing to switch to a more effective medical treatment for a chronic condition because one has already endured years of side effects from the current one, even though the switch offers a better expected outcome. Identify which of my verdicts your principle must overturn, and say exactly what argument or discovery would lead you to change your mind, together with the weakest point in your reasoning.
