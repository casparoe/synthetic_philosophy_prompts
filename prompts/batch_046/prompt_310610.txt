I am currently writing an essay for my philosophy of language module, and I have become genuinely stuck on a disagreement that seems to recur every time Quine's critique of the analytic-synthetic distinction is discussed—both in recent literature and in our seminar room. One group maintains that Quine's confirmation holism dissolves any sharp boundary: since every statement, including those of logic and mathematics, can in principle be revised in light of recalcitrant experience, there is no class of truths genuinely immune from empirical correction, and talk of truth in virtue of meaning alone is therefore a confusion. The opposing group insists that holism governs the justification of beliefs, not their semantic status; they argue that we can still distinguish statements that hold by linguistic convention—such as the claim that all bachelors are unmarried—from contingent reports about the world, even if both are embedded within a holistic web of belief. For several weeks, our reading group has circled precisely this exchange: the holists cite the revisability of geometric postulates and logical principles, while the defenders cite the apparent absurdity of treating synonymy as an empirical discovery. Neither side appears to address the other's core concern, and I am no longer sure whether they are disagreeing about Quine's argument, about the nature of meaning, or about something else entirely.

My own hypothesis is that the stall arises because both camps are operating with incompatible models of what analyticity could be. The first camp seems to attack a definitional conception—analytical truths as those reducible to synonymy or explicit definition—while the second camp appears to defend a structural conception: analyticity as the functional role a statement plays within an entire conceptual scheme, quite independent of whether individual sentences can be isolated and defined. If this is correct, then they may not be disputing a single phenomenon at all, but rather defending different conceptual structures under one name. Yet I also wonder whether there is a deeper, substantive disagreement concealed beneath this verbal divergence—specifically, about whether semantic content is ever recoverable atomistically, or whether it is necessarily distributed across inferential networks. Given this diagnosis, what kind of disagreement is this actually—verbal, substantive, or a case of an ill-posed question? And if progress is possible, what form should it take: a formal framework that distinguishes structural from empirical roles within theories, a clarification that renders the original distinction obsolete, or an argument that the very concept of analyticity fragments irrecoverably once we abandon definitional reduction?
