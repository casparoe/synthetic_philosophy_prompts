Draft the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between a pairwise contractualist and a scaled contractualist on whether Scanlon's contractualist framework can accommodate the aggregation problem without abandoning its core commitment to justifiability to each affected individual.

The pairwise contractualist maintains that moral wrongness is determined by whether an act is permitted by a principle that no affected individual could reasonably reject, applied strictly in pairwise fashion; they hold that aggregating small harms across many people introduces an external quantitative metric that violates the separateness of persons, and that Scanlon's logic offers no non-arbitrary way to scale rejection-reasons without collapsing into aggregate welfare comparison.

The scaled contractualist maintains that the reasonableness test applies to patterns of justification that must hold for every affected party, and that when a principle permits a small harm to each of many individuals, each of those individuals retains independent standing to reject it; they argue that accommodating scale requires not summing welfare but recognizing that the replicability of a justification across affected pairs itself generates a structural reason for rejection, which is still internal to contractualist logic.

Produce a document containing the following, in formal and precise prose, without bibliographies, citations, reading lists, or verbatim quotations from published texts:

1. A numbered section of agreed claims, including: the core contractualist formulation that wrongness consists in acting on principles that could not be reasonably rejected by those affected; the formal structure of the aggregation problem, illustrated by cases in which saving one person from a severe harm requires inflicting minor harms on several others; the contractualist explanatory obligation, namely that any account of wrongness in such cases must not reduce to aggregate welfare maximization; and the shared commitment that reasons for rejection are individually possessed and not substitutable across persons.

2. A disagreements table with two columns, each phrased in wording its own side would accept. The table should cover at least: (a) whether the reasonableness of rejecting a principle is invariant when the same principle is applied to additional, similarly situated individuals; (b) whether a principle that permits minor harm to each of five people in order to prevent death for one can be reasonably rejected by each of the five without appealing to summed harm; and (c) whether introducing a representative-sample or proportional-replication condition violates the separateness of persons or merely extends pairwise logic consistently.

3. The cruxes—sub-questions such that resolving them would move at least one side. These should include: whether the separateness of persons prohibits any quantitative comparison across individuals or only prohibits substituting aggregate welfare for individual reasons; whether a principle licensing widespread minor harm can be rejected by each affected party on grounds that do not tacitly aggregate; and whether there exists a non-utilitarian structural feature of scale that remains internal to contractualist justification.

4. For each crux, specify a concrete conceptual case or argument both sides accept as bearing on it, with each side's prediction recorded in advance. For the first crux, use the constructed archival rescue scenario: a single archivist can save either one irreplaceable manuscript—representing the life work of one author—from certain destruction, or five irreplaceable manuscripts—representing the life works of five authors—using the same resources, but the method that saves the five necessarily destroys the one. Both sides agree on the scenario's description and on the contractualist framework's applicability. Record what each side predicts about whether saving the five is permissible, about whether the reasoning must invoke the number five in any form, and about whether each of the five authors could reasonably reject the principle that permits their own manuscript's destruction.

5. A brief concluding assessment identifying whether any portion of the dispute is verbal—particularly whether disagreement about "aggregation" concerns welfare-summation versus pattern-replication across pairs—and, if so, specifying the verbal component precisely and explaining why the remaining disagreement is substantive rather than terminological.

Write so that the prompt does not depend on access to specific editions, page numbers, or external sources. Paraphrase theoretical positions without quotation marks. Ensure the two sides' formulations are neither vacuous nor slanted.
