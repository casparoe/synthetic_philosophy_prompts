I am preparing material for a course in environmental historiography and have been debating with a colleague whether the Anthropocene should be taught in history departments as a named epoch comparable to the Renaissance or the Cold War, or whether it ought to remain strictly within the domain of stratigraphic science. The controversy turns on a feature that is rarely foregrounded in general discussion: the proposed boundary of the Anthropocene is being determined not by a conventional historical event—no treaty, no revolution, no dynastic collapse—but by the global appearance of plutonium isotopes, plastic polymers, and nitrogen signatures in the geological record, with specialist bodies favoring a mid-twentieth-century boundary linked to what is often called the Great Acceleration, while other scholars have pressed for earlier dates tied to colonial ecological transformation or the widespread adoption of fossil-fuel industrialization. If we accept that a historical period can be defined by geochemical synchrony rather than by shared social experience, what are the most important implications for how historians assign causal responsibility, construct narrative coherence, and distinguish local agency from global structural forces? In particular, I want to understand whether adopting this stratigraphic framing forces us to treat the massive carbon emissions and land-use transformations that occurred before the proposed boundary as somehow lying outside the Anthropocene proper, and whether that temporal mismatch undermines the common moral claim that the epoch names a unified human project for which collective responsibility can be assigned. Please do not provide bibliographies or reading lists; I am reasoning without access to external sources and need the argument to stand on conceptual analysis alone. You may also consider whether treating the Anthropocene as a historical period rather than as a geological epoch silently imports an assumption of human universality that obscures the radically unequal distribution of both the benefits and the harms that produced those stratigraphic signals.
