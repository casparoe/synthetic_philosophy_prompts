I have recently been confronting practical questions about post-mortem digital preservation—specifically, whether a high-fidelity neural reconstruction should be regarded as my literal continuation—so I want to audit the philosophical commitments I have been relying on. Here is where I stand: (1) I accept a broadly Lewisian framework in which individuals do not exist in more than one possible world and cross-world talk is always mediated by counterpart relations governed by qualitative similarity. (2) Within the actual world, I take psychological continuity combined with an appropriate causal connection to be sufficient for personal identity over time, and I reject bodily continuity as necessary. (3) When I imagine a preservation protocol that reconstructs my functional architecture in a digital substrate after biological death, I am strongly inclined to say that the reconstructed entity is me—not a counterpart—provided its cognitive and affective profile falls within a narrow fidelity band, say within five percent deviation from my current baseline. (4) If two reconstructions were produced with different fidelity levels—one within that band and one outside it—I want to say that the high-fidelity instance is literally me, the low-fidelity instance is merely a counterpart, and there is no intermediate or partial identity relation between them. (5) I also maintain that within any single world, personal identity is an all-or-nothing matter: either an entity at time t2 is the same person as me at t1, or it is not, with no gradations.

Do these commitments form a coherent package? In particular, does my willingness in (3) and (4) to assert literal identity across a death-and-reconstruction boundary conflict with my acceptance in (1) of counterpart theory, which should seem to treat the reconstructed entity as existing in a counterfactual scenario and therefore as a counterpart rather than me? Does the fidelity-threshold in (4) introduce a graded relation that violates the all-or-nothing claim in (5)? And does my reliance on psychological continuity in (2) commit me to treating even low-fidelity reconstructions as partial continuers, undermining my sharp distinction in (4)? I am not asking for a lecture on the literature; I want a disciplined audit that orders the tensions by severity, distinguishes real contradictions from merely apparent ones, and tells me which commitment is the cheapest to revise if I want to preserve my intuitive judgment about the preservation case.
