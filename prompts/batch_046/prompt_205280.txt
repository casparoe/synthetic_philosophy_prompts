I was arguing with a friend about whether revolutions basically have to devour their own kids, specifically in the French case, and I realized there's this pattern I keep seeing but can't name. People explain the Terror or the purges by saying that once you commit to total popular sovereignty or absolute equality, there's this internal logic that forces you to keep finding hidden enemies among your own allies—like the more perfectly you apply the founding idea, the more people you have to exclude. They make it sound structurally inevitable, almost like a machine running on the ideology itself. But then when those same writers talk about the Revolution actually producing durable legal equality or dismantling feudal privileges, they switch gears completely and talk about contigent alliances, specific leaders making lucky choices, or historical accidents that could easily have gone differently. So the destructive parts are explained by the revolution's core logic, and the constructive parts are explained away as flukes.

I've seen this in at least three different interpretations—some Marxist-leaning ones that stress class struggle, some that focus on ideology, and some that want to say everything was just contigent. In each case, though, the asymmetry is there: failure belongs to the system, success belongs to chance. Is there an actual concept for this kind of explanitory double-bookeeping? Or maybe a term for the idea that a revolutionary project becomes self-destructive precisely because it succeeds too well at implementing its own principles? I don't need names of books or anything, just want to know if this carving is something philosophers or historians have already mapped out, and if so where its edges are—like, does it apply only when there's an explicit purity test, or can it happen with economic revolutions too?
