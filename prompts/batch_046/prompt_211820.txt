I'm drafting a philosophical profile for a seminar on the legacy of logical empiricism, and I want to know whether my commitments about analyticity, convention, and Quine's critique form a coherent package or whether I'm smuggling in assumptions that undermine one another. Here is the set: (1) I accept Quine's circularity argument in "Two Dogmas of Empiricism"—attempts to define analyticity via synonymy, explicit definition, or semantic rule all presuppose an equivalent notion—so the classical analytic-synthetic boundary is unsustainable. (2) Despite this, I want to retain Carnap's distinction between internal questions, settled within a linguistic framework by convention or logic, and external questions, which concern the choice of framework itself and are settled pragmatically. (3) I endorse Quine's meaning holism: individual beliefs do not face experience alone, but only as part of a larger corporate body of theory, so that in principle no statement is absolutely immune to revision. (4) At the same time, I reject the conventionalist thesis that analytic truths are true purely by linguistic convention; convention may fix the rules of a framework, but it does not by itself explain the necessity or explanatory stability of logical and mathematical truths. (5) I take Kripke's necessary a posteriori discoveries to show that necessity and analyticity come apart, which reinforces Quine's skepticism about a semantic analytic category; yet I still want to distinguish a class of "framework-central" statements—basic logic, arithmetic, and definitional identities—that can be revised only at extreme theoretical cost. (6) Finally, I maintain that there is a real practical difference between altering a logical framework and revising an empirical hypothesis, though I deny that this difference tracks any deep metaphysical divide between meaning and fact.

Please audit this combination. For each pair of commitments, indicate whether they are genuinely in tension, only apparently in tension, or fully compatible. Rank any real conflicts by severity, specify exactly which commitments generate the pull, and say what the cheapest revision would be—along with what that revision costs me in terms of the other commitments I hold. If there is one single change that resolves the largest number of tensions with minimal damage, identify it. I am especially concerned about whether I am using Carnap's internal-external distinction without the analytic-synthetic machinery that was supposed to ground it, and whether my denial of conventionalism leaves me without resources to explain why framework-central statements occupy their privileged position. Do not lecture me on Quine or Carnap in general; treat my specific combination as the object of analysis.
