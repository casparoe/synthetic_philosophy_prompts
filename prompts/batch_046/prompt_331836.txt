I am a communications ethics officer at a national public-health agency. Each week I must review two to four proposed behaviour-change campaigns—on vaccination uptake, alcohol reduction, organ-donation registration, and similar—before they go to design teams. I have thirty minutes per proposal, I must co-sign with a senior colleague, and I have no in-house psychologist or philosopher on call. I need a reusable decision procedure, expressed as an ordered flowchart in words, that turns the philosophical distinction between legitimate persuasion and manipulation into a practical checklist my team can run without resolving metaphysical disputes from scratch every time.

The theoretical backbone should treat legitimate persuasion as communication that respects the recipient’s reason-tracking capacity: the recipient can identify the reasons offered, relate them to the desired belief or action, and retain the ability to reject them after reflection. Manipulation, by contrast, should be understood not merely as deception about content, but as structural concealment or subversion of the recipient’s evaluative mechanisms—causing belief or action through pathways that the recipient cannot correct for even when aware of them. To make this operational, inject two specific conceptual tools into the procedure. First, distinguish structural concealment (hiding the persuasive architecture—sequencing, emotional priming, framing choices—while leaving content technically accurate) from mere content omission. Second, apply a counterfactual endorsement test: if the recipient knew the full communicative design, including why particular statistics, images, or orderings were selected, would they still regard the message as giving them adequate reason? Also include the concept of reason-tracking failure: a campaign fails at this stage if it relies on mechanisms—such as subliminal patterning or affective flooding timed to suppress reflection—that bypass rational evaluation rather than engaging it.

The deliverable should be the procedure itself: the questions to ask in strict order; what counts as a sufficient answer to each; the exact points where the procedure stops with a clear reject or approve; the points where it must hand the decision to a person because the issue is one of values rather than procedural fact; and explicit markers of where judgement cannot be eliminated. The procedure must handle hard cases: campaigns that are factually impeccable but frame risk selectively; campaigns that disclose their goal but embed emotional triggers in a sequence designed to reduce reflective capacity; and campaigns that use accurate social-norm data but exploit comparative cognition in ways that disappear under the counterfactual test. Apply the procedure to three proposals: (a) a social-norm feedback campaign showing drinkers they consume more alcohol than the regional median, with the explicit aim of reducing intake; (b) a vaccination-information leaflet that presents relative risk reduction without the corresponding absolute baseline, arranged so that the framing choice itself is not disclosed; and (c) a grief-based narrative campaign encouraging organ-donation registration, using verified bereaved-family testimonies followed immediately by a registration prompt, where the emotional sequencing is engineered but every factual claim is true. Include at least one case where the procedure yields an answer you—the officer—will not like, and explain why you must accept it or override it formally. Finally, state clearly what the procedure cannot do: for instance, predict actual psychological effects without empirical audience testing; resolve deep disagreements about whether autonomy should always outweigh welfare gains; or replace institutional sign-off when values conflict.
