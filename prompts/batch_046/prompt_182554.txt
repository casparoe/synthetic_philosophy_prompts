I have been reading, without any formal training, David Hume’s 1748 essay on liberty and necessity, and I want to make sure I understand a particular distinction he draws. As I read him, he separates liberty of spontaneity—acting according to one’s own will without being physically forced or restrained—from liberty of indifference, which would require that the will itself not be determined by prior causes, so that one could genuinely have chosen otherwise. Hume seems to say that only the first kind matters for moral responsibility: if I act because I want to, and no one is holding my arm, then I am free in the only sense that makes praise or blame appropriate, even if my wants were themselves caused by my character and circumstances. The second kind, he treats as an illusion that is neither present in human action nor required for judgment.

But I have constructed a case that troubles this for me, and I am unsure whether it troubles Hume’s argument or only my grasp of it. Imagine a person, call him A, who decides on his own to return a lost wallet. His decision flows entirely from his own desires and character; there is no actual interference. However, unknown to A, a hidden mechanism—say, a precisely calibrated neural stimulator—stands ready to compel the same action had he shown even the slightest hesitation. Because he never hesitates, the mechanism never activates. A could not have done otherwise in the counterfactual sense: had he been about to fail, he would have been forced to succeed. Modern discussions, I gather, treat scenarios like this as Frankfurt cases, using them to ask whether moral responsibility requires the ability to do otherwise. My question is not about the modern literature but about the older passage. Does Hume’s distinction between spontaneity and indifference actually address the presence of such an unexercised counterfactual controller? If liberty of spontaneity only requires the absence of actual constraint, then A clearly possesses it, and Hume might say he is fully responsible. Yet one might think that the mere presence of the backup mechanism undermines something—perhaps not spontaneity itself, but the kind of authorship we care about. Does Hume’s framework implicitly rule out these hidden counterfactual conditions, or does it leave them untouched? And if it leaves them untouched, is that a gap that his distinction was meant to cover but misses, or is it exactly the point—that responsibility depends only on what actually happens, not on what might have happened in some unrealized branch? I would appreciate help seeing precisely where Hume’s argument stands on this, using only the conceptual tools he provides, without asking me to read further texts.
