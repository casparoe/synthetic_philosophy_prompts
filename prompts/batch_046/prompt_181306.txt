I am examining English translations of Clément Marot's poem to the sick lady—commonly referred to as Ma mignonne—produced under strict formal constraint, and I have encountered a recurring pattern for which I lack a precise term. In renderings that preserve the original rhyme scheme and syllable count, the rhyme-eligible vocabulary available in the corresponding English metrical positions tends to be disproportionately concrete, monosyllabic, and physically direct. The result is not merely lexical substitution but a predictable migration of pragmatic stance: the speaker's courtly, distanced tenderness shifts toward an intimate, almost bodily immediacy, not because the translator has deliberately chosen intimate diction, but because the formal slots themselves pull the register in that direction. The propositional content and the formal architecture both survive; what drifts is the illocutionary posture—the way the speaker stands toward the addressee—driven by the target language's phonological-lexical economy rather than by interpretive choice. Does this configuration have an established name in translation theory or pragmatics? How should it be distinguished from ordinary register shift, compensatory adjustment, and simple semantic loss? If no term exists, please define the phenomenon with explicit boundary conditions: it must arise from formal constraint rather than semantic decision, be predictable from the distribution of rhyme-eligible vocabulary in the target language, and remain invisible to analyses that check only propositional fidelity or formal equivalence.
