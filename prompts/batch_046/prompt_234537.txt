I have been attempting to articulate my own stance on which varieties of free will are genuinely worth wanting, and I should like an audit of whether these commitments hang together. Here is my profile:

- I am a compatibilist about moral responsibility: an agent is responsible provided her actions flow from a coherent, reflectively endorsed hierarchy of desires.
- I accept the Frankfurt-style counter-examples as decisive against the Principle of Alternative Possibilities; moral responsibility requires no ability to do otherwise.
- I reject libertarian free will as conceptually incoherent, since it requires an uncaused cause that nevertheless exerts causal power.
- At the same time, I believe the variety worth wanting is "deliberative elasticity": the capacity for an agent's evaluative weights to be genuinely reshuffled by reasons in a manner not fixed by the prior causal history of her mental states.
- I hold that for this elasticity to be genuine, an agent must be capable of suspending and revising her highest-order volitions, and that this revision cannot itself be determined by her pre-existing character or environment — otherwise it collapses into hierarchical determinism.
- I deny that phenomenal consciousness is necessary for free will; a suitably structured artificial system possessing reflective endorsement could, in principle, exhibit deliberative elasticity.
- Lastly, I maintain that sourcehood is more fundamental than leeway, but that authentic sourcehood requires precisely this open-ended, non-deterministically fixed capacity for self-revision.

Please examine these commitments and identify which pairs stand in genuine tension, ordering them by severity. For each, specify how severe the conflict is and what the cheapest revision would be. Also point out any pairs that appear contradictory but can be reconciled on closer inspection. Finally, advise me which single alteration to my position would resolve the greatest amount of tension with the smallest philosophical cost.
