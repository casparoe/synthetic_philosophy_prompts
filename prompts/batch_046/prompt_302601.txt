A colleague has led me through an argument that begins with Black's spheres and ends by denying that anything can persist unchanged through time. The chain runs as follows:

1. The strongest form of the Principle of Identity of Indiscernibles holds: if x and y share every property—intrinsic and relational—then x is numerically identical to y.
2. In Black's universe, sphere A and sphere B are qualitatively identical in every intrinsic respect. They stand only in symmetric spatial relations: each is one mile from the other, and with no other bodies present there is no frame to distinguish left from right.
3. Spatial position is not an intrinsic property but a relation to other entities. Because there are no other entities in that universe, the spheres do not differ even relationally.
4. Hence, by (1), they are not two spheres but one sphere with two disconnected spatial manifestations.
5. Apply the same structure diachronically. Consider an object O at time t1 and O at time t2 in a universe where O alone exists and nothing else varies. O-at-t1 and O-at-t2 share all intrinsic properties and stand only in symmetric temporal relations (each is separated by one hour, with no other events to anchor temporal direction).
6. Temporal location, like spatial location, is not intrinsic; it is constituted by relations to other events. With no other events, there is no qualitative difference between O-at-t1 and O-at-t2.
7. Therefore O-at-t1 and O-at-t2 are identical. Persistence through time without qualitative change collapses into a single timeless entity.
8. But this conclusion is intolerable: it implies that an unchanging rock in an otherwise empty universe does not genuinely endure, and it erodes the distinction between stasis and change altogether.

My colleague claims that anyone who accepts Black's counterexample to the Principle must therefore reject endurance. I suspect a hidden slide—perhaps an equivocation between spatial and temporal relations, or an unstated ontological commitment about time—but I cannot name the faulty step. Which premise should be rejected, and what does abandoning it cost? Specifically, if I block the analogy between space and time to preserve endurance, am I forced to concede that Black's spheres are genuinely two after all, or can the two judgments be kept apart?
