im trying to name a pattern i keep seeing in debates about whether buddhist ethics is consequentialist. heres the setup: buddhist texts clearly care about reducing suffering and the karmic fruits of actions for everyone involved, which sounds outcome-oriented. but they also say the right mental state, cetana or intention, is what makes an action good, and that you shouldnt be weighing results like a calculator when you act. now the twist: defenders of a consequentialist reading say the precepts are just heuristcs that track good outcomes in normal cases. critics reply that its not consequentialism because if you actually calculated outcomes to decide, your mental state would be greedy or attached, which changes the karmic result — so the evaluation of the action depends on not using outcome-evaluation in deliberation. its like the criterion for rightness and the criterion for correct reasoning are coupled in a loop. ive heard this called indirect consequentialism but that usually just means follow rules that usually maximize utility. this feels tighter: the mental-state constaint is constitutive, not just a speed bump. does this loop have an established name in moral philosophy? and is there a closer analog outside buddhist studies — maybe in virtue ethics or something about reflexive evaluation?
