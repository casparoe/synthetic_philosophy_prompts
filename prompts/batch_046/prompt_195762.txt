I've been working through my own position on the badness of pain, particularly in light of pain asymbolia, and I'd like a consistency check on a cluster of commitments that I think are individually defensible but may not all sit together cleanly. I take it that the unpleasantness of pain is not a primitive feature of its phenomenal quality but derives from its representational content—pain represents tissue damage or threat—together with the subject's practical interest in avoiding that damage; consequently, the bare sensory character of heat, pressure, or sharpness is axiologically neutral on its own. Pain asymbolia seems to support this dissociation: patients with parietal damage can localize and describe nociceptive stimuli without the usual aversive framing, often reporting the sensation as curious or detached rather than threatening, which suggests that the sensory and affective-evaluative pathways are genuinely separable. Nevertheless, I also want to say that an asymbolic state is still pro tanto harmful for the subject, not because they suffer, but because the decoupling of sensation from protective motivation means injuries can accumulate unnoticed, so there is an objective functional harm that persists independently of phenomenology. I further hold that phenomenal consciousness carries no intrinsic value or disvalue absent some evaluative uptake or functional role, so if a patient genuinely lacks both suffering and preference-frustration, there is no hidden phenomenal badness we are obligated to eliminate. At the same time, my clinical intuition is that we should defer to the patient's own report: if an asymbolic individual describes the sensation as neutral, does not seek relief, and prefers not to be sedated, then imposing analgesia treats a harm that exists only in an external functional assessment, not in the subject's actual welfare. Finally, I think evolutionary explanations of pain's aversiveness are explanatory but not normative—they account for why the mechanism is usually coupled to avoidance, not why any particular instance is bad—so I reject attempts to derive the wrongness of this headache from its adaptive history.

Please rank the tensions by severity rather than listing them all as equally bad, and then identify the single change that would clean up the most conflict at the lowest philosophical cost.
