I've recently become preoccupied with whether one can ever genuinely audit one's own reasoning for errors when the audit itself must be conducted by the very faculties under review. After a concussion last month, I attempted to assess my cognitive recovery by working through logic puzzles and then grading my own performance; each apparently correct self-assessment seemed to supply fresh, independent confirmation that my judgment was sound, despite the fact that the evaluator and the evaluated were identical. The experience is not merely confirmation bias: the circularity is structural, yet each iteration is felt as cumulative corroboration rather than repetition. I am asking whether this specific configuration—structural circularity in first-person epistemic auditing that is phenomenologically experienced as progressive verification—has an established name, and how exactly it relates to the bootstrapping problem and to concerns about easy knowledge in reliabilist epistemology. If no distinct concept exists, please define the phenomenon precisely, distinguishing it from ordinary circular reasoning and from standard bootstrapping cases. Finally, please say what consideration or argument would change your mind—such as evidence that the iterations are genuinely independent, or that the escalation is purely psychological rather than epistemic—and point to where your reasoning is most vulnerable to error.
