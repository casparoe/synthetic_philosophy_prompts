Read Akira Kurosawa's Rashomon through the lens of source criticism about the distant past: the tension between a Thucydidean ideal—adjudicating contradictory testimony by weighing proximity, consistency, and the witness's interest in deception—and a Herodotean practice of preserving variant accounts (logoi) without suppression, on the view that contradiction itself reveals the social conditions under which knowledge is produced. The film offers four incompatible accounts of a death in the woods, framed by a ruined gate and a commoner who steals a dagger and abandons an infant. Does Rashomon treat these contradictions as proof that source criticism must collapse into epistemic nihilism about the past, or as archival data that a Herodotean method should preserve? Focus especially on the woodcutter's late version: does it function as a Thucydidean synthesis that resolves conflict by appealing to a disinterested observer, or does the framing story undermine even that synthesis by showing every narrator—including the "neutral" witness—to be morally compromised? Which scenes best dramatize the problem of interested testimony, and where does the film resist being reduced to a philosophy-of-history case study rather than a drama about human vanity? Conclude by saying what Rashomon adds to the debate about knowing the distant past that a purely methodological comparison of Herodotean and Thucydidean practice cannot supply on its own.
