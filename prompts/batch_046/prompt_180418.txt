I am preparing materials for a graduate metaphysics seminar and want to move students past the familiar Lewis-ersatz-actualist trichotomy. I need a taxonomy—structured as a decision tree—of positions on the ontology of possible worlds, built from three diagnostic commitments rather than from philosopher-brand recognition. The commitments are: (A) whether the actual world enjoys metaphysical privilege over other worlds or only an indexical privilege; (B) whether de re modal claims about particular artifacts (e.g., that this bronze statue could have been cast from different metal) are grounded in counterpart relations across worlds or in the object's own persistence conditions; and (C) whether the space of worlds is generated by a combinatorial principle (free recombination of actual parts and properties) or constrained by non-combinatorial principles such as essential origins or kind-essentialism.

Please construct the tree so that each internal node is one of these commitments phrased as a yes/no or either/or question that a student can answer without knowing which interpretation it leads to. Each leaf should carry either a named position—for example, Lewisian genuine modal realism, Plantingan actualism, or a counterpart-free concrete-worlds view for artifacts—or the label unnamed, together with one sentence describing its most awkward ontological commitment. The tree must have at least eight leaves, must place the three named positions above in genuinely distinct branches, and must include at least one unnamed combination—specifically, a view that accepts concrete worlds, denies counterpart theory for artifacts, and treats the actual world as only indexically privileged. For any pair of leaves that the tree cannot cleanly separate without adding a further distinction, flag them explicitly rather than obscuring the overlap.

Organize the response as follows:
1. The diagnostic framework (approximately 150 words): present the three branch questions in sequence, explaining for each why it splits the logical space in a way that prevents mere label-matching.
2. The decision tree (approximately 350 words): display the branching structure, labeling each node with its question and each leaf with its position name (or unnamed) and awkward commitment sentence.
3. Empty cells and inseparability (approximately 200 words): discuss the unnamed combination mentioned above and any other unoccupied branches, diagnosing whether each is incoherent, unmotivated, or merely unexplored; then list the leaf pairs the tree fails to separate.
4. Pedagogical note (approximately 100 words): suggest how an instructor could use this tree in class to check whether students grasp the logical geography or are only reciting names.
