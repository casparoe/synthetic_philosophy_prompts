I'm trying to work out what I actually think about whether certain mental conditions are luminous, judging case by case. When I'm fully awake and alert, I know I'm awake, and I also know that I know it. When I'm sound asleep, I don't know I'm asleep, and that's as it should be. The middle ground is where I get tangled. If I'm noticeably drowsy—say, I can still answer simple questions but my reaction time is clearly slowed—I think I do know I'm drowsy, though I'm less certain about the second-order claim. But if I'm only barely drowsy—reaction time just ten milliseconds slower than my baseline—I think I am drowsy without knowing it; and if I happen to form the correct judgment that I'm drowsy, I don't think that judgment counts as knowledge, let alone as knowing that I know. At the same time, I feel there should be some cutoff point in the drowsiness gradient where knowledge suddenly appears or vanishes, which strikes me as implausible given how continuous the underlying state is. Yet I also reject the idea that there is a precise numerical threshold—say, exactly 0.73 on some objective drowsiness scale—where the epistemic status flips. Is there a principle, or a small family of principles, that can capture all of these verdicts together? Specifically, can any account preserve both my first-order judgments and my commitment to something like the KK principle for clear cases, or does accommodating the barely-drowsy case force me to drop either the sharp-cutoff intuition or the second-order knowledge claim? And what should the resulting view predict about a case I haven't settled: being in a deep flow state where I'm highly focused, performing well, but have no explicit self-monitoring of my own mental condition?
