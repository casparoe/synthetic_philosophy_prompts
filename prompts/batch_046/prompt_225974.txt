If "Western values" is deployed in comparative argument as a single evaluative standard, it ought to behave like a thick moral concept—carrying both descriptive content and normative force—rather than like a cluster of thin concepts that shift meaning across domains. Yet autonomy, secular governance, and contractual fairness seem descriptively unstable: autonomy in bioethics concerns bodily sovereignty, while in market contexts it concerns preference satisfaction, with no shared descriptive core. This suggests the phrase is either a rhetorical aggregation of thin values or a category mistake. But is the error in treating "Western values" as a thick concept, or in assuming that every normative category used in political argument must be classifiable as either thick or thin? Might it instead function as a "framework value"—acquiring determinate content only within specific institutional contexts—without thereby collapsing into incoherence or pure rhetoric?
