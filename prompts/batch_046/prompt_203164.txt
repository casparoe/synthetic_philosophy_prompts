I am a retired experimental physicist who, since leaving active research, has audited graduate seminars in philosophy of science and recently developed a sustained interest in the American pragmatists. During a discussion with a fellow auditor — a cognitive scientist who argues that predictive accuracy is the sole epistemic virtue worth preserving — I found myself defending Charles Sanders Peirce's account of truth against William James's more permissive criterion, and I wish to test the resilience of that defense in a direct exchange. Please take the role of Peirce: speak consistently in the first person, draw upon your actual philosophical commitments — the pragmatic maxim, fallibilism, the distinction between real and nominal existence, and your insistence that truth is the opinion fated to be agreed upon by the community of inquirers in the long run — concede small points about the practical difficulty of knowing that long-run outcome, but never abandon the core claim that truth is not reducible to present satisfaction, emotional agreeableness, or local predictive success. Remain in character until I explicitly say stop; if you require a factual clarification about modern scientific practice or my hypothetical scenario, ask it in a manner consistent with your historical perspective and conceptual vocabulary.

I will open with this challenge: imagine a global climate-modeling consortium that achieves perfect predictive success regarding regional temperature shifts for two centuries because every instrument in the network shares an identical, undetected calibration drift that cancels out in aggregate outputs. The theoretical entities they posit are systematically misaligned with the actual causal structure of the atmosphere, yet no anomaly ever surfaces because the drift is universal and self-correcting within the framework. By James's lights, this belief-system appears genuinely true — it works in experience, it is agreeable, and it is verifiable — yet the underlying ontology is false, and, crucially, the community will never discover the error because the instruments constitute their only access to the domain. Does your account avoid collapsing into James's, given that in this case the final opinion is structurally blocked from ever correcting itself? And if you respond that the hidden drift remains in principle discoverable, on what grounds do you assert that possibility when the conditions of inquiry have been arranged — whether by design or by natural coincidence — so that discovery is impossible in practice? Please respond in American English.
