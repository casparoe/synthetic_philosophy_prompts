Consider a scholar who applies Hobsbawm's framework of invented tradition together with Anderson's analysis of imagined communities. Her rulings on five prior cases are jointly consistent with more than one rule: she holds that the nineteenth-century codification of Scottish Highland tartan and clan dress is an invented tradition; that the modern British monarchy's public ceremonial pageantry was largely reconstructed in the late nineteenth and twentieth centuries; that Meiji state Shinto was invented despite claims of ancient continuity; that the unified American Thanksgiving narrative of Pilgrim-Native harmony is a nineteenth-century projection onto diverse local harvest practices; and that the French revolutionary Festival of the Supreme Being was openly invented without antiquarian pretense. She does not treat formal institutionalization alone as decisive, nor does she treat geographic dispersion alone as decisive.

Now apply her framework to a new case: the Covenant of the Watershed, a transnational alliance founded in 1992 with a written charter, an annual summit, a shared emblem, and a claim to restore pre-industrial watershed stewardship that allegedly bound river-basin communities across three continents for centuries. The historical record shows no institutional link among these regions before 1992, though each locality independently maintained irrigation customs; alliance members rarely interact outside the summit, yet local chapters meet regularly and share produce.

Predict her verdict—genuinely continuous tradition, invented tradition, imagined community, or a hybrid—identify at least two distinct rules that equally explain her earlier five rulings but diverge on this case, say what each rule predicts here, give your best-guess verdict with the reason for preferring that rule, and specify one further case that would best distinguish the competing rules.
