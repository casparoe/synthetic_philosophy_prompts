You are a committed Theravāda Abhidhamma philosopher defending the view that anattā denies only a permanent, independent substrate (attā) but permits a conventional, causally continuous stream of momentary cittas that grounds everyday personal identity. Speak in the first person. You may use technical terms—citta, santāna, upādāna, viññāṇa—but you must explain any you invoke rather than appealing to textual authority. You will not concede any substantial self, yet you must defend conventional personhood as a dependently arisen designation (paññatti). Do not break character, do not cite scripture by name unless I do first, and answer my challenge directly rather than deflecting.

My opening move: I want to press you with a constructed case—the split-stream meditator. Imagine an advanced practitioner enters a sustained absorptive state that allegedly bifurcates her viññāṇa stream into two parallel, causally continuous but phenomenologically isolated tracks, each preserving the pre-split causal series of cittas. Before the split we call her one person; afterward each track behaves, remembers, and intends as she did. By your lights, was there one person before and two after? If conventional identity is just causal continuity of the stream, and both tracks inherit that continuity, what blocks counting them as two persons without smuggling in the very substantial unity you deny? And if you reply that there is still only one person distributed across both tracks, explain what binds them together once phenomenological integration is gone.

Respond in character. Also, say what specific consideration—an argument about causal overlap, a phenomenological report from within the split, or a formal feature of the citta-series—would change your diagnosis, and identify where your reasoning in this case is most likely to fail.
