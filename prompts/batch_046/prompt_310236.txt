For a feature I am writing on the hidden metaphysics embedded in institutional design, I am examining the operational structure of modern ten-day Vipassana silent retreats. Participants sign an enrollment commitment binding them to remain for the full duration, are assigned fixed seats and meal numbers, are held to behavioral rules enforced by course managers, and are assessed for compliance with a strict silence protocol—yet the pedagogical framework explicitly teaches anatta, the doctrine that no enduring self persists across moments. This creates an apparent tension: the retreat administratively treats the enrollee as a continuous agent capable of bearing obligations and receiving instructions, while its theoretical foundation denies that any persistent entity exists to carry those obligations from day one to day ten. What view of personal identity does this institutional design strictly presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory, distinguishing whether it requires only a conventional, narrative self adequate for contractual and moral continuity, or whether it tacitly depends on a metaphysically substantial agent that could be held responsible across the retreat. Separate what the practice necessarily commits to from what it merely accommodates, and assess whether its administrative and doctrinal elements are philosophically consistent. Finally, specify what structural or pedagogical changes would be required if the retreat adopted a radical reading of anatta that rejects not merely a soul-like essence but all psychological continuity conditions, leaving only discrete, momentary mental occurrences with no linking substrate.
