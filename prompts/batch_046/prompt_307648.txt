I've been trying to wrap my head around this philosopher David Lewis and his idea that conventions are basically solutions to coordination problems that stick around because everyone knows everyone knows to stick with them — common knowledge, I think he calls it. And then there's this other layer about norms evolving on their own without anyone planning them.

I keep thinking about Sweden's big traffic switch in 1967, Dagen H, when the whole country flipped from driving on the left to driving on the right overnight. It wasn't some bottom-up thing that evolved; the goverment just passed a law and threw up signs everywhere, and they even had to change bus doors and put police on every corner. So I want to analyze that episode through Lewis's lens: was left-hand driving ever a true Lewisian convention, and did the law destroy it or just replace it with a new one? The part that really confuses me is that people in remote rural areas kept driving on the left for years after, almost like the old norm was still existant in pockets even though the state said otherwise. Can Lewis's model explain why the convention stuck in Stockholm but faded slowly in the countryside?

I want you to map out the actual players (drivers, cops, bus companies), what the coordination payoff looked like before and after, whether this case proves conventions need organic evolution or can be legislated into existance, and specifically where Lewis's framework goes silent or breaks down when the state forces the switch rather than people coordinating themselves. Don't explain Lewis from scratch — I want the Swedish case to do the work of testing the theory, especially that weird urban vs rural split.
