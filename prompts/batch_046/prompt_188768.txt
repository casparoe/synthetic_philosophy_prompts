Im trying to map out my ethics profesor's view on whether genuine moral dillemmas are even possible. Her verdicts so far: in the Sophie-style case where a parent must choose which child gets the only antidote and the other dies, she said yes, thats a real dilemma. In the broken-promise case, where you miss picking up a friend to stop a stranger from being seriously hurt, she said no, thats just a hard choice with a clear right answer. Then there was the double-commitment case: you promised to be best man at two weddings happening simultaneously in different cities, both grooms depend on you, and theres no physical way to do both; she said yes, real dilemma. Last, she looked at a case where a clinician thinks she must either breach confidentiality or violate a reporting law, but it turns out the data was already public so the conflict was illusory; she said no, not a genuine dilemma.

Next case is a hospital triage committee facing two patients who arrive at the exact same instant, identical prognosis, both need the one open ICU bed. The hospital runs a formal lottery protocol for ties. They do it fairly, one gets the bed, the other dies. Would she call that a genuine moral dilemma for the committee?

Im less interested in the bare answer than in the rule shes running. One interpretation is the exhaustion rule: a real dilemma only when two equally weighty duties clash and theres no legitimate procedural way to break the tie. Another is the residue rule: a real dilemma whenever every available path leaves you having failed a serious moral demand that doesnt just vanish, even after a fair process. Maybe theres a third.

Predict her verdict, say which of those rules she is implicitly running, and tell me which further case would best tell them apart. Also, which of her earlier cases is the closest analog, and does that analog push one way?
