Focus on conceptual precision, fidelity to Quine’s actual commitments regarding ontology, ideology, and the web of belief, and the quality of the comparison drawn with the hypothetical framework of dispositional moral indexing (DMI). Question: In his essay On What There Is and in related writings, Quine distinguishes ontology from ideology, holding that existence claims are relative to the quantificational apparatus of our best scientific theories. Some philosophers have extended this framework to ethics by arguing that moral values belong not to ontology but to ideology, functioning within our conceptual scheme as coordinating devices for normative expectations rather than as descriptions of intrinsic features of reality. Assess whether this extension is consistent with Quine’s broader naturalism. In particular, discuss how his rejection of the analytic-synthetic distinction bears on the status of moral claims, and evaluate whether Quine’s position can be usefully compared to dispositional moral indexing (DMI), a hypothetical framework that treats moral predicates as relational markers tracking patterns of intersubjective agreement rather than describing intrinsic properties or expressing non-cognitive attitudes.

Student answer:
Quine’s ontology-ideology distinction lets us separate what a theory says exists from the conceptual machinery it employs to say it. Moral values are not part of the furniture of the world because our best scientific theories do not quantify over them; they sit in ideology. Since Quine rejects the analytic-synthetic distinction, there is no sharp fact-value boundary, and moral statements belong to the web of belief alongside empirical claims, revisable in light of experience. This does not make him a moral anti-realist, however, because he allows that if an indispensable scientific theory required quantification over moral properties, they would enter the ontology; so his view is best read as a cautious moral naturalism.

The framework of dispositional moral indexing is essentially the view Quine advances in Word and Object, where normative predicates index stable dispositions within a linguistic community. Like Quine, DMI rejects intrinsic moral properties and non-cognitive expressionism, treating moral language as a relational device embedded in our conceptual scheme. The only real difference is that DMI makes the intersubjective dimension explicit, whereas Quine leaves the social coordination implicit in the holistic web of belief. Overall, Quine’s naturalism is consistent with this extension, provided we keep in mind that for him all ideology remains ultimately answerable to the constraints of scientific practice.
