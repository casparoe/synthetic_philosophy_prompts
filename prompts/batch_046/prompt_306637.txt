As a retired evolutionary biologist who has recently turned to the philosophy of social science, I now serve on the International Research Ethics Oversight Panel. We must reach a decision on Protocol 2024-7, a proposed cross-cultural public-health study in a region where local investigators distribute personalized material gifts and provide individualized social favors to households in exchange for informed consent, sustained participation, and follow-up care. Our Western compliance officers classify these practices as clientelism and corruption, insisting on an absolute prohibition aligned with universal anti-bribery norms. Our local partners contend that these exchanges are not discretionary bribes but structurally necessary reciprocity—without them, the local social infrastructure that delivers care collapses, and participants are effectively abandoned. Rather than applying a standard Western compliance checklist, I want the Panel to evaluate the practices through an analytical framework drawn from ecological interaction theory: specifically, whether the exchanges in question are functionally analogous to obligate mutualism (interdependent cooperation that produces net benefit for all parties and cannot be unilaterally withdrawn without systemic harm) or to parasitic extraction (one party captures surplus at net cost to the other, with withdrawal improving the host’s condition). Draft a formal institutional memo addressed to the Panel recommending whether to approve, reject, or conditionally approve Protocol 2024-7. The memo should include an executive summary, a reasoned analysis applying the mutualism-versus-extraction framework honestly to the specific practices described, a clear actionable bottom line with conditions if applicable, and an explicit, fair treatment of the strongest argument for the Western prohibition—namely, that universal procedural norms are necessary to protect vulnerable populations from rationalized coercion, and that functional analogies risk masking power asymmetries. Conclude with an assessment of whether Western anti-corruption standards should be treated as genuinely universal in this domain or as culturally particular norms that require contextual adaptation. Write in polished, formal institutional prose suitable for direct circulation to panel members.
