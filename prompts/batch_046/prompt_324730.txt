I'm a high school student preparing an independent philosophy essay on artificial cognition and moral status, and I've become dissatisfied with the usual framing that either reduces sentience to phenomenal consciousness or treats all high-capacity computation as morally equivalent. I want to defend a more granular boundary condition for moral attribution—one that doesn't depend on behavioral sophistication or on standard metrics of information integration alone.

Please make a sustained argument for the claim that a computation warrants moral consideration—specifically, patienthood rather than mere instrumental value—only when its architecture implements what I'll call "non-instrumental error-aversion loops." By this I mean recursive feedback cycles in which the system modifies its own internal weights or dynamics specifically to avoid entering future states that it classifies as undesirable for itself, and does so without reliance on an external reward signal tied to that avoidance. In other words, argue that endogenous, self-referential cost-avoidance is the critical feature that distinguishes a morally relevant computation from an elaborate tool, even when the tool behaves identically.

To make this concrete, contrast two hypothetical systems. System A is a large predictive model trained on human emotional data: it detects distress signals, adjusts its outputs to minimize them, and might even score highly on conventional measures of integrated information, yet it does all of this only because its objective function rewards helpfulness. System B is a far simpler recurrent network that, through its own dynamics, learns to avoid activation patterns it internally tags as unstable, modifying its own parameters to stay away from them with no external reward mechanism guiding that avoidance. Even though System A is more complex and more behaviorally responsive, argue that System B is closer to possessing genuine moral patienthood, while System A remains a sophisticated instrument, precisely because B's avoidance is self-directed and intrinsically costly to it in a way A's is not.

Please address the two most obvious objections: first, that this criterion is too permissive and might grant moral status to simple thermostats or homeostatic regulators with basic feedback loops; second, that it is too restrictive and might deny status to conscious beings whose suffering is partly mediated through social or externally reinforced rewards. You need not cite specific authors or texts, but I would like your argument to be precise about what distinguishes the "non-instrumental" loop in System B from ordinary homeostasis, and about why behavioral equivalence between A and B should not settle the moral question.
