For a dissertation chapter on collective moral agency and the historiography of 1914, I need an example—constructed from scratch rather than drawn directly from the Fischer archival debates—that isolates the conceptual problem of attributing blame for initiating the First World War to a nation-state. Give me a scenario involving a hypothetical but historically realistic institutional structure in which formal democratic consent mechanisms technically exist but are systematically hollowed out by a permanent militarized bureaucracy, so that popular authorization is present on paper yet structurally void in practice. The example should distinguish between the Fischer-style claim that Imperial Germany deliberately sought general war through state-level decision-making and the structuralist counter-claim that aggression emerged from systemic institutional drift rather than from a discrete collective choice. Specifically, I want the case to expose the precise point at which collective national blame collapses into either scapegoating of a population that lacked effective agency, or into a diachronic institutional abstraction that survives every regime change and thereby loses moral specificity. Please construct it so that synchronic culpability of the specific office-holders who ordered mobilization yields a different moral verdict than attributing responsibility to "Germany" as a continuous legal-moral agent, and indicate what feature of the institutional design does the work in generating that divergence.
