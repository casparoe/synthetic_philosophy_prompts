As a retired molecular biologist who has recently begun attending a philosophy seminar on value theory, I want to close our final session—devoted to whether a work's aesthetic merit can survive its creator's moral corruption—with a short poem. I ask for a Petrarchan sonnet of exactly fourteen lines, octave rhyming ABBA ABBA and sestet CDECDE, with a sharp volta at line 9. The philosophical idea of separating the work from the artist should be enacted structurally, not merely asserted. The octave should describe the work's internal formal properties—composition, chromatic relations, structural balance—using only aesthetic vocabulary and never alluding to the maker. At line 9, the poem should pivot abruptly to the creator's biography, narrating a specific and serious moral failing in plain, non-aesthetic language. The rhyme words that bridge the volta—particularly the B-rhymes linking lines 2, 3, 6, 7 and the C- or D-rhymes entering in the sestet—must be terms that possess both an artistic and an ethical sense (for instance, 'frame,' 'pure,' or 'composition'), so that the sounds are shared across the boundary while the meanings remain conceptually insulated from one another. Outside those rhyme positions, no word from the octave's semantic field should recur in the sestet, and vice versa. The final couplet should leave the two judgments—the work's merit and the creator's fault—standing in unresolved adjacency, mirroring where our discussion actually ended. The result should work as poetry first and as philosophy only second, readable without prior knowledge of any particular artist or theory.
