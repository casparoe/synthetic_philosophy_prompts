Question: Explain the “tranquilist” thesis that only the absence of suffering and mental craving (tanha) contributes to well-being, with pleasure, achievement, and satisfied desire adding nothing positive. Contrast this with hedonism and desire-fulfillment theory. Then evaluate whether tranquilism can accommodate the intuition that a life of active, successful striving is better than a life of passive, craving-free contentment, provided both lack suffering.

Student answer:
Tranquilism holds that individual well-being consists solely in the absence of suffering and mental craving (tanha), with no positive contribution from pleasure, achievement, or satisfied desire. The view is often traced to Schopenhauer’s metaphysics of the will, where he treats all apparent happiness as merely the temporary cessation of striving; in this sense it functions as the individual-level counterpart to negative utilitarianism, directing evaluation solely toward the reduction of badness rather than the promotion of good.

Contrasted with hedonism—which treats pleasure and pain as the sole intrinsic values—and with desire-fulfillment theory—which identifies well-being with getting what one wants, regardless of affect—tranquilism denies that either satisfaction or pleasure can improve a life once craving and pain are absent. Even a life of profound achievement adds nothing if the achiever retains no residual craving.

This framework accommodates the intuition that active striving is less good than passive contentment, because striving necessarily involves craving, which is intrinsically bad. In fact, since conscious awareness always carries some minimal background craving, tranquilism implies that no conscious being can ever reach a state of genuinely positive well-being—only degrees of less-badness. From this it follows that tranquilism must reject the claim that suffering is bad, for if absence alone is valued, then suffering itself cannot be negatively valued; rather, suffering is merely the presence of what should be absent. Consequently, the experience-machine scenario offers no special problem: if eliminating craving in a simulated environment achieves the same absence, there is no independent loss of real-world achievement.
