I'm in an upper-level philosophy-of-language seminar, twelve students, ninety minutes. Design a structured, rules-based activity that lets us feel the constitutive force of Davidson's radical interpretation—specifically the tension between the principle of charity and the interpreter's own grip on logic and basic quantification—without naming Davidson or using the term until the debrief. To keep it from feeling like a standard translation exercise, frame the unknown speakers as consistently denying visible quantitative relations (e.g., asserting there are not more red blocks than blue ones when there plainly are) and asserting identity contradictions, while otherwise navigating their environment with apparent skill.

Split us into three interpretation committees of four. Give each committee an identical transcript of speaker utterances paired with observable environmental conditions. Their task: construct a radical interpretation manual that assigns both meanings and beliefs, maximizing charity. Force a hard choice for at least one contradiction: either preserve standard logical and quantitative vocabulary and attribute deep irrationality or perceptual failure, or reinterpret those vocabulary items so the utterance comes out true, and then defend the ripple effects across the rest of the manual.

Rules and timeline: twenty minutes of silent committee analysis; then each committee presents its manual for ten minutes, followed by five minutes of real-time defense against a "review board" formed by the other committees, who challenge consistency on three other transcript lines that seem to use the same terms standardly. After all three present, run a fifteen-minute debrief.

What we should come away seeing: that interpretation fixes belief and meaning together, that saving charity by revising logical vocabulary buys coherence in one place at the cost of interpretive complexity elsewhere, and that charity functions not as kindness but as a condition of recognizing an interpretable subject at all.

In the debrief, map each stage onto Davidson's framework—charity, the interdependence of belief and meaning, and the indeterminacy of interpretation. Be frank about what this simulation cannot reproduce: the genuine absence of any shared conceptual background, and tell the facilitator exactly how to supply that missing piece verbally without undermining the exercise's discovery structure. Also note the predictable failure mode—committees defaulting to attributing mental defect rather than revising vocabulary—and how to redirect them in real time. No lecture up front, no readings assigned, and no citations.
