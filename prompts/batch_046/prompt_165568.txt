Consider this line of reasoning, pressed in discussion of Jaspers’s fourfold taxonomy of guilt, about whether collective responsibility can survive across generations without collapsing into indiscriminate ascription.

(1) Jaspers insists that criminal and moral guilt are strictly individual: they require personal consciousness, choice, and an internal relation to one’s own conscience; only political guilt may be borne collectively, by citizens for the deeds of their state.

(2) But a state is not a momentary aggregate of contemporaries; it is a diachronic institution with continuous legal and political identity, so citizenship is membership in a persisting collective agent rather than in a present-tense crowd.

(3) If that membership alone is enough to make present citizens politically guilty for crimes committed before they were born or despite their dissent—crimes they neither authorized nor could have prevented—then the ground of guilt is structural position within the enduring agent, not personal involvement.

(4) Jaspers also describes metaphysical guilt as arising from human solidarity: we share in the failure to prevent injustice anywhere, and this solidarity attaches to our position within the human community rather than to particular acts we performed.

(5) So structural membership in a collective—whether the state or humanity—appears to be the common ground of both political and metaphysical guilt; and if it can carry guilt diachronically within the state, consistency demands it carry guilt synchronically across all human injustice.

(6) Once guilt is assigned collectively by structural membership, there is no principled barrier to extending it to moral guilt (solidarity restricted to one’s own political community) and then to criminal guilt, since criminal law already assigns liability on the basis of organizational roles—command responsibility, corporate personhood, and the like.

(7) Hence Jaspers is apparently committed to saying that every present citizen is collectively criminally, morally, and metaphysically guilty for every ancestral state atrocity, and indeed that every human being bears collective guilt for every human injustice.

(8) This cannot be right: Jaspers introduced his distinctions precisely to block such indiscriminate ascription, preserving the individuality of moral and criminal accountability.

Which step should I reject, and what does abandoning it cost? I am especially reluctant to give up (3) or (4), since both seem to capture something real about how collective life outlasts individual lives; yet if they stand, the collapse in (7) looks unavoidable.
