For a philosophy of mind seminar focused on neuropsychological cases, lay out the desiderata that an adequate theory of phenomenal unity after total corpus callosotomy must meet. Your list should cover: (a) the classic hemispheric dissociations in visual, tactile, and linguistic processing observed in split-brain subjects, where information presented to one hemisphere is behaviorally unavailable to the other; (b) the persistence of a single first-person perspective and unitary autobiographical narrative in ordinary conversation, despite those dissociations; (c) the resolution of bimanual conflict in tasks where one hand initiates an action that the other corrects without verbal report; (d) the role of intact subcortical and anterior-commissural pathways in allowing limited cross-hemispheric integration—particularly in emotional and attentional coordination—without restoring direct neocortical callosal transmission, and whether such pathways undermine claims of hemispheric independence; and (e) the absence of subjective reports of experiential doubling or divided agency, even when behavioral measures suggest parallel processing streams.

For each desideratum, give the motivating clinical or experimental consideration, distinguish non-negotiable constraints (e.g., consistency with the neuroanatomical severance of callosal fibers, fidelity to first-person reports) from negotiable camp preferences (e.g., whether unity requires a single center of narrative authority, whether functional integration is sufficient for phenomenal unity, or whether metaphysical identity of the subject must be preserved), and note where different researchers genuinely disagree. Then identify whether any subset of these requirements is jointly unsatisfiable—for instance, whether maintaining strict hemispheric independence together with a genuinely unitary phenomenal field forces one to deny either the neuroanatomical facts or the authority of subjective report. Keep the emphasis on the criteria and their interrelations rather than on scoring named theories. Separate what is well-established in the clinical and experimental literature from what remains contested among specialists who study these cases, and do not present a disputed interpretation as the consensus.
