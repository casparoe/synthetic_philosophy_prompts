In my seminar on moral psychology we keep returning to a claim that gets repeated as if it were one precise thesis: that an action possesses moral worth only when performed from duty, and that any contribution from inclination, sympathy, or personal satisfaction strips the act of that worth. Our instructor applied this to a pair of cases—one firefighter who risks her life because she recognizes rescue as a categorical obligation despite finding it terrifying, and another who does the same work with equal reliability but because she genuinely thrives on the adrenaline and the survivors' gratitude—to conclude that only the first firefighter's actions have moral worth. I am skeptical that this conclusion follows from any defensible version of the claim, but I cannot tell whether the problem lies in the slogan itself, in my professor's interpretation of it, or only in the way she maps psychological descriptions onto normative verdicts. So I want the slogan precisely disambiguated before I bring the objection back to class.

Could you lay out, as a menu rather than a single answer, the distinct precise theses that "moral worth requires acting from duty rather than inclination" could be expressing? For each reading I want to know: whether it requires that inclination be entirely absent, merely subordinate, or present but not the reason for which the agent represents her action; whether "duty" here means a de dicto recognition ("this is required of me") or a de re alignment with objective moral reasons; whether inclination functions as a canceling condition, a diminishing one, or merely an unreliable indicator; and whether the claim concerns the agent's actual psychological state, the counterfactual stability of her motivation, or the normative basis she would cite upon reflection. Then, for each reading, say whether it actually supports the verdict that the adrenaline-driven firefighter lacks moral worth while the terrified-but-obligated one possesses it, and mark which readings are trivial, which are substantive and defensible, and which are false or strawmen. I am especially interested in whether there is a non-trivial interpretation on which both firefighters have equal moral worth, or on which neither does, and what that interpretation commits one to about the nature of moral motivation and the relationship between first-order desires and second-order normative endorsement.
