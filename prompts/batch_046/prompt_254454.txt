Take the role of a philosopher of religion with expertise in classical Daoist texts. Before auditing, briefly list the five commitments I describe below so the structure is explicit. Separate what is well-established in Zhuangzi scholarship from what remains genuinely contested, and do not present any disputed interpretive reading as the consensus.

I am working out my interpretation of Zhuangzi on death for a seminar and want a consistency check. Here are my commitments:

1. Death is a transformation of qi and form, not annihilation; the self disperses into the larger natural process without any enduring soul or personal survival.

2. The episode in which Zhuangzi drums and sings upon his wife's death shows that grief is an artificial, culturally imposed reaction; the genuinely natural response is equanimity or even celebration, because no real loss has occurred.

3. His question about whether loving life and hating death is like a child who has wandered off and does not know the way home expresses deep epistemic humility: we should suspend judgment about whether death is better, worse, or neutral compared with life.

4. Emotional attachment to particular people remains meaningful and should not be eliminated — it is part of the natural flow of feeling — but it should not include the belief that death harms the beloved.

5. The correct practical attitude is complete wu wei: no preference between living and dying, since both are equally natural phases.

Go through these and tell me which pairs are in genuine tension, how severe each tension is, and what the cheapest revision would be for each. Also identify at least one pair that looks conflicting but dissolves on inspection, explaining why. Rank the real tensions by severity rather than treating them as equally bad. Then say which single revision to my set would clean up the most problems at once, and what it would cost me — especially whether it forces me to abandon anything I clearly want to keep.
