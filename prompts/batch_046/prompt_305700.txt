I'm in an undergraduate philosophy-of-history seminar and need to design a 60-minute explanatory session for about twelve first-year students who have read brief excerpts from Herodotus and Thucydides. They arrive thinking that knowing the distant past is basically a matter of "finding unbiased eyewitness accounts," and they have never faced the structural problem that, beyond living memory, testimonial chains break and material traces must be interpreted through frameworks that can depend on the very narratives they are meant to verify. I want them to grasp not generic source criticism, but specifically the dependency between testimonial transmission and material corroboration, including what I will call "negative source criticism"—the evaluation of structurally compelled silences in an archive—and the circular risk that scientific dating of material evidence is sometimes calibrated against the textual traditions it is supposed to independently check.

Plan the session's explanatory arc: state which idea must be in place before the question of distant-past knowledge even makes sense to them, then the order in which the remaining components have to be introduced and why (what concept depends on what), the one carrying example or case that should do the work and what features it must have and must lack, the misconceptions they will predictably bring or form in the order they will arise and the precise point in the sequence where each should be headed off, how the sequence should absorb each objection without derailing, the check at the end that would show they have understood the structural problem rather than just feeling uneasy about history, and what should be deliberately left out at this level with an explicit note that it is being omitted. The example should involve an event known mainly through later chronicles and an ambiguous archaeological layer, where the dating of the layer relies partly on chronological assumptions drawn from those same chronicles.
