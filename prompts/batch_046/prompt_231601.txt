Here is an argument I constructed for a seminar on negative causation, and I now find it suspicious because it seems to license causal claims everywhere. (1) On an interventionist account, X causes Y only if there is a well-defined intervention on X—holding other model variables fixed—that changes whether Y occurs. (2) A pure omission, such as my failure to water your plant, is the absence of an event; there is no variable representing the omission itself that can be intervened upon, only a variable representing whether the watering occurs. (3) So pure omissions are never causes. (4) Yet consider a structured omission in a distributed system with assigned roles: agent A is designated to monitor temperature, and agent B is designated to act only upon receiving A's failure signal. If A fails to monitor and B, seeing no signal, takes no action, the resulting system failure is specifically attributed to A's omission, not to B's inaction. (5) In this case, the interventionist can model causation by treating the variable as A's fulfillment of the assigned monitoring role rather than as the bare event of watering; intervening on role-fulfillment or role-assignment changes the outcome. (6) Thus structured omissions are genuine causes, because the institutional role supplies the intervention variable that the bare absence lacked. (7) Now observe that for any absence whatsoever, one can retroactively construct a role-structure: define a hypothetical agent whose designated function was to prevent state S, and then treat that agent's failure to act as a structured omission relative to S. (8) Consequently, if structured omissions are admissible, then virtually every absence is a cause of virtually every state that obtains in its absence—for instance, my failure to learn Japanese becomes a cause of my not speaking Japanese, but also, via an arbitrarily assigned preventive role, a cause of a distant earthquake's occurrence since I did not prevent it. (9) This proliferation is intolerable. (10) Therefore either (3) holds and pure omissions remain non-causal, which forces us to reject (5)-(6) and deny that distributed-role cases involve real omission-causation, or we must accept that causation by omission is radically unrestricted.

I want to preserve (1) and (4): I am committed to the interventionist framework, and I think the distributed-system case genuinely reveals that omissions can be causally salient when embedded in a normative structure. Which step should I reject, and what is the cost of rejecting it? Is there a hidden equivocation between intervening on a role-variable and intervening on an absence, or is the argument valid and the real price is that we must sharply restrict when role-structures can be legitimately invoked?

Please push back plainly if you think the notion of structured omission I have introduced conflates a pragmatic rule for assigning blame with a metaphysical causal relation, or if the task is ill-posed because interventionism was never meant to apply to omissions in the first place. I am not asking for a bibliography or citation list—only the diagnosis of the flaw and an honest accounting of what giving up each premise costs.
