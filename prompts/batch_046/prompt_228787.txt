In debates about rational AI design, people often compare acquiring free information to being handed a flashlight before entering a dark room: illumination can only improve navigation, and if the beam reveals something unwanted you can always close your eyes. The analogy is used to defend the claim that a rational agent should never refuse costless data and that information-seeking ought to be a hard-wired default. Audit the correspondence: what in the decision-theoretic framework plays the role of the flashlight, the dark room, the walker, the option to ignore, and the absence of any cost for carrying the light? Then identify the disanalogies that actually matter—particularly cases involving precommitment strategies, identity-dependent preferences, or strategic interaction where receiving knowledge changes the game being played—and determine whether the conclusion that free information is always beneficial survives once those breaks are explicit. If the flashlight picture misleads, suggest a more accurate analogy for the conditions under which information genuinely carries non-negative expected value.
