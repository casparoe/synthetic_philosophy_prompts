I am a high school senior presenting my independent research thesis at an inter-school humanities symposium. My central claim is that the standard dichotomy in explaining Holocaust perpetrators—Browning’s account of situationally pressured ordinary men versus Goldhagen’s account of ideologically motivated willing executioners—is conceptually inadequate because both overlook what I term bureaucratic tempo: the rhythmic segmentation of violent labor into administratively discrete, time-bound tasks that fragments moral cognition without requiring either deep antisemitic conviction or passive social conformity. The audience comprises four AP European History teachers who have taught both texts for over a decade, two school psychologists steeped in Milgram and Zimbardo, and a visiting organizational sociologist who works on Weberian rationalization. They will not be hostile, but each faction will defend its disciplinary home ground. I need you to prepare me for the objections in the order they are likely to arise, phrased as each group would phrase them: which have genuine force, what I should concede in my opening remarks, where I must hold firm, the weakest link in my own argument that I should defend rather than hide, and—crucially—the objection I am hoping no one raises, particularly any appeal to the quantitative leave records of Reserve Police Battalion 101 or to Goldhagen’s statistical sampling of private antisemitic correspondence, since I lack archival access and cannot verify primary figures. Structure this as a practical prep sheet for a twenty-minute presentation followed by fifteen minutes of questions, with concise reply strategies suited to that time frame.
