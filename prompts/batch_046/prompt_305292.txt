Draft the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between two opposing camps in the philosophy of moral revolution: Camp One holds that norms such as chattel slavery and dueling collapse primarily through shifts in material interests and honor-status (a Williams-style thesis), with rational argument playing at best a post-hoc rationalizing role; Camp Two holds that such collapses require conceptual innovation—specifically, a reconceptualization of the practice as incompatible with the practitioner’s own dignity or honor (an Appiah-style thesis)—without which interest shifts alone produce only suppression, not genuine normative abandonment. Your deliverable is the document both camps could sign, but it must be built around a novel analytical distinction you are required to introduce and operationalize: normative decay (the erosion of enforcement mechanisms and reputation costs, leaving the practice technically available but socially expensive) versus normative inversion (the active reclassification of the practice as degrading to those who engage in it). Structure the statement precisely as follows: (1) a numbered section of agreed claims about slavery and dueling—covering material incentives, legal prohibition, peer-status dynamics, and the public circulation of arguments—phrased in wording both camps accept; (2) a disagreements table stating each camp’s position in language its own members would endorse, focused specifically on whether decay can explain full normative abandonment without inversion, and whether inversion can occur in the absence of prior decay; (3) the cruxes, identifying at least two sub-questions whose resolution would move at least one camp, with one crux centered on whether a practice can be genuinely abandoned while still being conceptually available to its former practitioners; (4) for each crux, a concrete comparative case or counterfactual that both sides agree is relevant—such as a society where dueling was legally banned but honor codes persisted versus one where peer sanction dissolved without explicit legal intervention—together with each camp’s explicit prediction about what that case demonstrates regarding decay and inversion; and (5) a closing analytical note stating which element of the decay/inversion distinction does the primary argumentative work, what happens to the disagreement if that element is removed or redefined, and how the camp that comes out worse under the framing would attack the distinction itself rather than the historical interpretation. Write in the formal voice of a doctoral student in philosophy of history or moral philosophy preparing this for an interdisciplinary workshop. The prompt should be self-contained, require no bibliographic lookup, avoid verbatim quotations from real texts, and should not invoke obscure historical episodes that depend on specialized archival knowledge; yet it should be sufficiently conceptually demanding that a model cannot answer by reproducing a standard summary of Williams or Appiah.
