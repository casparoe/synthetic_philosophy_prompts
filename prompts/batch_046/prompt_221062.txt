I'm working through Leibniz's moral and metaphysical writings for a graduate seminar on rationalist ethics, and a classmate keeps describing his account of divine justice and the selection of worlds as if it were a market-clearing mechanism: each possible world is like a sealed bid containing some ratio of metaphysical good to permitted evil, God functions as an auctioneer who selects the bid with the highest net perfection, and the actual world is simply the winning contract executed without further negotiation. The analogy is meant to make Leibniz's rationalist ethics feel intuitive—moral value gets quantified, conflicts among possibles get resolved by optimization, and "the best" acquires an unambiguous meaning independent of any particular observer's preferences. But I'm unsure how far it actually extends, especially since Leibniz insists that justice is founded on the love of God and the harmony of reasons rather than on any calculus of pleasure and pain.

Could you lay out an explicit correspondence table—what plays the role of the bidder, the currency, the auction rules, the externalities, the reserve price, and the failure-to-deliver conditions—then identify precisely where the mapping breaks: for instance, whether monads have preferences or agency in any sense that makes "bidding" meaningful, whether evil is genuinely fungible with good like currency in a balance sheet, whether God's selection involves comparative evaluation in the way an auctioneer ranks prices, and whether the "contract" metaphor smuggles in a voluntarist or consequentialist framework that Leibniz's doctrine of sufficient reason actually excludes? Please separate the disanalogies that merely decorate the picture from the ones that genuinely undermine the conclusion that Leibniz's morality is best understood as an optimization procedure, and say whether a better analogy—perhaps architectural harmony, legal jurisprudence, or something else—would capture the relation between perfection, compossibility, and divine choice without importing the wrong conceptual machinery.
