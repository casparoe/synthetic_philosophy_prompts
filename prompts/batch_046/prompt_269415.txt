okay so I'm the managing editor of this interdisciplinery journal called Threshold, and we're constantly getting letters from readers saying we should correct or retract something because of one weird study or logical flaw they spotted. Most of the time it's noise, but sometimes it's real. I was reading about Wittgenstein's hinge epistemology — the idea in On Certainty that some beliefs aren't really supported by evidence but act like hinges that hold the whole system in place, like the earth being old or that my hand is still there when I look at it. I want a decision proceedure my editorial board can actually run when a challenge comes in: step-by-step questions to ask in order, what counts as a good enough answer at each step, and where we just stop and say this is a hinge belief that one paper can't overturn. It needs to keep what the principle actually says, not just water it down into common sense. The board has lawyers and scientists on it who don't do philosophy, so the steps have to work for them too. It needs hard cases — like a letter claiming a published math paper assumes ZFC consistency but actually ZFC might be inconsistent, or someone saying a philosophy article relies on the general reliability of testimony which they claim is a hinge that shouldn't be assumed without proof. The procedure should flag exactly where judgment takes over and we can't automate it, and I want it applied to two cases: one where a single statistical study challenges a well-cited finding in social psychology, and one where a reader says our logic column made a mistake about modus ponens being valid (they think it's actually questionable). Be honest about where it fails or gives answers we'll hate.
