Write a villanelle—nineteen lines, five tercets closing in a quatrain, with two refrains that rhyme A and alternate through the poem—about guilt and shame as competing moral emotions, but do not treat them as the familiar private-versus-public pair. Make the first refrain a line that figures guilt as a reparable contract (something like “I owe, and the debt can still be closed”), and make the second a line that figures shame as a visibility that survives the act (something like “The eyes remain open; nothing is erased”). Let the villanelle’s unavoidable repetitions do the philosophical work: each return of a refrain should appear in a slightly different register—first interpersonal, then institutional, then cultural—so that the form itself asks whether a society can choose which emotion dominates by redesigning who witnesses failure and whether the social record can be expunged. Incorporate the less-standard view that guilt and shame are not universal discrete categories but points on a continuum of accountability-to-others, differently weighted by a culture’s architecture of visibility—who can see, who remembers, and whether forgiveness can be made collective rather than personal. The closing quatrain should bring both refrains together without resolving the choice, leaving the reader with the impression that selecting one emotion over the other is not like picking an instrument but like altering the optics through which persons are recognized at all. The poem should be accurate enough that a moral psychologist recognizes the conceptual moves, and structured tightly enough that the rhyme and repetition are inseparable from the argument.
