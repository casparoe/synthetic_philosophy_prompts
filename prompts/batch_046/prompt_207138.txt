I'm prepping for a seminar next Tuesday where I'm defending what I call the indexical collapse reading of Glaucon's ring of Gyges challenge. The core claim is that total invisibility doesn't just remove the risk of punishment — it dissolves the intersubjective feedback loops that stabilise practical reasoning, so the rational agent Glaucon imagines continuing to act justly while unseen is already conceptually incoherent. I take this partly from social ontology but apply it to moral psychology, and I'm expecting serious pushback because it sounds like I'm letting sociology invade ethics.

The audience is our philosophy of ethics reading group: about twelve people, mostly analytic ethicists who are sympathetic to rational-choice contractarianism in a Gauthier-style vein, plus a classicist who wrote her thesis on Plato's Republic and gets irritated when modern speakers treat Glaucon as a strawman for conventionalism. They care about whether I'm smuggling in a non-rationalist premise without arguing for it, and whether I'm misreading the physis-nomos distinction in the text itself. I need a prep sheet, not a polished defence: list the objecitons in roughly the order they'll come, phrased the way each camp would actually phrase them, ordered by how damaging they are, with a short reply for each, a note on where that reply is weakest, and clear guidance on what I should concede up front versus what I should hold firm. Include the question I'm secretly hoping nobody asks — probably whether this makes morality depend on actual observers, which pushes me toward a moral relativism I definitely don't want. Keep the whole thing under 800 words, and lead with the bottom line up front: the single strategic concession I must deliver in the first thirty seconds or the rest of my position collapses. No bibliographies or reading lists, please — I won't have time to check any sources before Tuesday.
