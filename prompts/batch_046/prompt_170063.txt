I'm preparing a conceptual fiction exercise for a small seminar on social choice and autonomy, and I need a short story that can serve as a living case study for Sen's liberal paradox without reducing the characters to mere logical symbols. Set the narrative inside the Consensus Spire, a post-scarcity habitat where every resident holds, under the Charter of Minimal Autonomy, the right to veto exactly one sensory stream entering their private quarters—an institutional translation of minimal liberal rights into environmental design. The protagonist should be an archivist who discovers that her neighbor's veto over a shared ambient soundscape—the low, resonant hum of the Spire's climate engines—interacts with her own veto over the visual projection of archival maps in a way that forces the collective governance algorithm into a state both residents rank below the current status quo. Because the Spire's protocol forbids any Pareto-inferior collective outcome yet refuses to override either veto, the algorithm stalls, proposing compromises that honor neither preference fully.

Make the logical structure tangible through concrete sensory and emotional detail: describe what the climate-engine hum actually sounds like to the neighbor, why controlling it matters to him beyond mere preference, and what the archivist's projected maps contain that makes their suppression feel like an erasure rather than an inconvenience. Then inject an additional twist: the archivist realizes that if she and her neighbor were to merge their two individual veto rights into a single joint entitlement—treating the pair as one liberal agent rather than two—the paradox dissolves immediately, since a unified agent can reorder preferences without contradiction. However, this merger violates an older, unwritten norm of the Spire that autonomy must never be pooled or transferred, a norm she had previously treated as axiomatic. Let the story build toward her proposal of the merger, and allow the ending to remain genuinely ambiguous: she might offer it and be refused, she might withhold it out of principle, or the algorithm might reject it on procedural grounds, but the narrative should leave open whether dissolving the paradox is worth the erosion of indivisible individual rights. Avoid any explicit lecture on social choice theory; let the conflict emerge from the architecture of the Spire and the characters' attachments to what they can and cannot control.
