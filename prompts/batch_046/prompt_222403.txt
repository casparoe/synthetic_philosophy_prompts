Write the opening remarks (about six minutes) for a workshop titled “Democratic Inquiry in Practice,” attended by municipal policymakers and graduate students in philosophy and political theory. The workshop asks whether local land-use decisions can be structured as Deweyan experiments—hypotheses about collective life tested by their social consequences rather than justified by prior authority—without reducing citizens to experimental subjects. Your speech must let Dewey’s experimentalism do real structural work: for the policymakers, explain why framing a zoning reform as an “experiment” is not an abdication of responsibility or a license for arbitrary trial, but a specific, accountable form of democratic authority; for the philosophers, distinguish Dewey’s fallibilism from mere proceduralism or populist preference-aggregation, and directly address the tension that social experiments are not replicable—one cannot run the same city twice—so that democratic error-correction operates through public feedback rather than through peer replication. Do not resolve that tension into a platitude; let it shape what you can and cannot promise the room. Avoid citations, reading lists, or bibliographic references. The audience is mixed: some will recognize Dewey’s idea of “the public” as formed by indirect consequences of action, others will not; write so both can follow, but do not flatten the philosophical point for accessibility.
