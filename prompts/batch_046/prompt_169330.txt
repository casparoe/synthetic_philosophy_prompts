I am defending what I call the fragmentation thesis in agricultural ethics: targeted insecticides are permissible not because insects lack sentience, but because nociceptive signaling in organisms with distributed ganglionic architecture—lacking a central integrating mechanism—fails to constitute a unified subject of suffering, so aggregate mortality does not scale as a singular harm comparable to harming one vertebrate. I am presenting this at an interdisciplinary workshop on environmental ethics attended mainly by biocentric egalitarians and conservation philosophers who treat ecosystem integrity as non-instrumental, plus a cognitive scientist skeptical of using neural architecture as a moral boundary. Prepare my Q&A defense sheet. List the objections I will actually face from this audience—not generic animal-rights objections—ordered by likelihood and damage, each phrased in their vocabulary (functional welfare accounts, precautionary reasoning against architectural essentialism, the charge of disguised anthropocentrism). For each: state the real force, what I should concede immediately, the concise reply I should give, and the precise point where that reply is weakest. Identify the single reply that must land for the thesis to survive the session. Finally, include the objection I am hoping nobody raises—likely about the epistemic risk that unknown or variable neural architectures could license mass harm without adequate verification—and draft a reply for it that avoids both hand-waving and an unprincipled retreat to pure utilitarian aggregation.
