I have no formal background in history or philosophy, but I have been thinking on my own about how archives are never neutral—about whose lives leave paper trails and whose vanish without a trace. I want an exam question that puts students in the weeds, so they cannot answer it well simply by repeating familiar slogans about power and knowledge.

Consider this concrete situation: a city digitizes every police report filed between 1950 and 2000, but the scanning protocol deletes any file in which no arrest was made and no injury was listed, on the grounds that these are administrative noise. At the same time, a local collective conducts an oral-history project, yet interviews only people who lived in a single housing block that was demolished in 1985. A historian then writes about police-community relations across those decades using both collections.

Please draft a single 90-minute exam question, suitable for an upper-level seminar rather than an introductory course, carrying 20 marks. The question should require students to work directly with the scenario above and should be impossible to answer well by summarizing well-known theories about archives. In particular, require them to distinguish between what I will call a structural silence—the kind of event the police form was never designed to capture in the first place—and a curatorial silence—the kind of record that existed on paper but was removed by the digitization rule. Then ask them to evaluate a proposed remedy: a negative archive, meaning a deliberate institutional practice of documenting what was not recorded, who was not interviewed, and which files were discarded, together with metadata about the exclusion criteria themselves. The students must say whether this remedy actually escapes the power dynamics of archiving, or whether it merely converts absence into another kind of governable presence.

Provide a model answer and a marking scheme. The scheme should list exactly five points that a top answer must hit, with each point assigned a specific mark value that sums to 20. For each point, include a one-line note on the particular shallow move or misconception that typically costs candidates those marks. The difficulty should come from identifying the crux—separating students who understand the issue from those who have only memorized the labels. Do not ask for bibliographies, citations, or reading lists; the answer should be fully answerable by a student who has no access to outside sources.
