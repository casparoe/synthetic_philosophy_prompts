Imagine you are chairing a three-year conceptual research program—philosophers of biology and theoretical evolutionary scientists, with no empirical labs—tasked with clarifying how evolutionary explanations are evaluated in light of the spandrels critique and its descendants. The goal is not another literature review but a defended agenda: which open conceptual problems, if solved, would most change how researchers distinguish adaptive explanations from constraint-based or byproduct explanations, and how should limited researcher-time be divided among them?

Specifically, rank the following four problem-types—do not treat them as equally important, and do not add more—according to what resolving each unlocks for the others:

1. The metaphysical and modeling status of spandrels once evolutionary developmental biology and niche construction are taken seriously: is the architectural metaphor still load-bearing, or has it collapsed into a generic claim about developmental correlation?

2. The integration of exaptation into the evaluation framework: can a trait be correctly classified as an exaptation only relative to a specific ancestral function, and does that relativization undermine the adaptationist/spandrel dichotomy?

3. The boundary between spandrel-like byproducts of selection on one trait and genuinely non-adaptive features that selection never touched, particularly in cultural evolution where selection pressures are diffuse and intentional design is present.

4. The downstream implications for evolutionary psychology and behavioral ecology: which conceptual results from the first three actually change the evidential standards for claiming that a human cognitive trait is an adaptation versus a spandrel or exaptation?

For each, explain why it is tractable for philosophers and theorists rather than requiring new field data, what argument or result over the next two years would move it up or down your ranking, and which other questions depend on its resolution. Defend your criteria—whether importance, tractability, neglectedness, or dependency on upstream answers—and be explicit about trade-offs: for instance, if solving (1) renders (2) moot, say so; if (4) is important but intractable without (3), say that too. The deliverable is a prioritized, defensible research agenda, not a survey, not a bibliography, and not a list of recommended readings.
