Here is a sketch for a short essay I am drafting. Its central claim is that the standard framing of the symbol-grounding problem usually presupposes what I will call "direct-coupling grounding": the idea that a symbol is genuinely grounded only if it stands in an unmediated causal-sensory relation to its referent. I want to argue that this presupposition obscures a far more common form of grounding in actual cognition—what I will call "relay grounding," in which a symbol's connection to the world is mediated by a chain of other grounded agents, artifacts, and representational practices. From this I derive the notion of "grounding debt": the accumulated epistemic liability that each intermediate link in the relay introduces. Applied to large language models, the argument is that every symbol an LLM processes carries the full grounding debt of the human writers who produced the training text, yet the model itself holds no independent sensory collateral—no check against corruption or drift—anywhere in that chain.

I am especially interested in whether this framing survives three specific objections. First, that relay grounding ultimately collapses back into direct grounding at the endpoint of the chain, so an LLM that learns about physical objects only through text is no different in principle from a human scientist who learns about quarks only through papers and instruments designed by others. Second, that "grounding debt" is merely a redescription of familiar problems about testimony and epistemic dependence, and therefore adds nothing specifically to the philosophy of symbol grounding. Third, that if grounding is genuinely distributed across a relay, then there is no principled reason to deny that the LLM itself participates in grounding, since it generates new linguistic outputs that other embodied agents subsequently read, interpret, and act upon—making the model a functioning node in the relay rather than an ungrounded outsider.
