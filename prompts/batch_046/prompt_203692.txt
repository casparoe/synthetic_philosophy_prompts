I hold that Jaspers' distinction among criminal, political, moral, and metaphysical guilt, when pressed across generations, yields a coherent but narrowly restricted form of collective national guilt — one that survives only if we reconceive the nation not as an aggregate of present citizens but as a persisting institutional agent bound by unrepaired constitutional obligations. On this view, post-regime generations do not inherit guilt through blood or culture; they inherit it only insofar as they continue to inhabit and benefit from the same legal-personal structure that authored the wrong, without having dissolved or fundamentally reconstituted it. This makes national guilt real but exclusively political in Jaspers' sense, contingent on institutional continuity rather than collective sentiment, and nullified by genuine constitutional rupture. What would count against this position? I want the arguments, conceptual distinctions, and historical or hypothetical cases that should move me, ordered by how decisive they are: observations that show institutional continuity is either too easy to manufacture or impossible to sever; analogies from other domains of collective responsibility that break the model; formal or conceptual results showing that treating states as persisting agents commits us to unacceptable conclusions about democratic change; and an honest assessment of whether this view has been qualified — for instance, by allowing defenders to redefine continuity post hoc — into something that risks nothing and predicts nothing. Include what defenders of pure individualist morality would concede as genuinely damaging, not merely awkward, and say whether the view's insulation, if present, is a virtue or a vice for a theory of collective guilt.
