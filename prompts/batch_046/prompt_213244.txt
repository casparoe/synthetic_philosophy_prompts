I've been working through the Jain idea of anekāntavāda — that reality is irreducibly many-sided and every view grasps only part of it — from general essays rather than formal study, and I've been testing my understanding against the informal judgments of a friend who applies the same intuition to everyday disagreements. She says that when a physician calls a treatment successful because the tumor shrank, while the patient calls it a failure because her capacity for meaningful work was destroyed, both are partly true: they are viewing one outcome through different legitimate aspects, biological efficacy versus lived flourishing. She extends this to metaphysical disputes, holding that describing ultimate reality as personal in one tradition and impersonal in another is not a flat contradiction but a reflection of different standpoints on something that exceeds either description. But she draws a hard boundary elsewhere: if two mathematicians disagree over whether a proof contains a hidden contradiction, only one can be right, since mathematics offers a single shared criterion of validity. She applies the same boundary to empirical shape: she rejects any partial-truth status for a flat-earther opposing a geographer. From these verdicts I can already see two competing rules: one that restricts many-sidedness to value-laden, experiential, or metaphysical domains, and another that permits it wherever there are genuinely different frames or methods of access, provided no single shared criterion can settle the dispute.

Now I want to predict her verdict on a case she has not considered, and I think her precedents may not fully determine it. The case involves a synthetic organism engineered in a laboratory: it lacks evolutionary ancestry, yet it metabolizes, reproduces, and emits behavioral signals functionally identical to distress responses in evolved animals. Three researchers disagree. The first claims it genuinely suffers, because functional equivalence to animal pain is sufficient. The second claims it only simulates suffering, because consciousness requires a biological substrate absent here. The third claims the question is malformed, since suffering applies only to naturally evolved life forms. Given her track record — the physician-patient case, the religious disagreement, the mathematical proof, and the flat-earth case — which analog is closest, and what should I expect her to say? If her precedents genuinely underdetermine the answer, what principle would she be adopting with each choice — the domain-restriction rule versus the shared-criterion rule? And what further, still-uncovered case would best tell those two rules apart?
