I run a graduate epistemology seminar with eight students, one 90-minute session. Design a structured in-class simulation—not a lecture or discussion guide—that makes participants feel the force of epistemic akrasia in a specific, non-canonical form: each student holds a first-order belief supported by direct private evidence, but also possesses higher-order evidence that the reasoning process behind that belief is currently unreliable, and they must choose whether to act on the first-order belief or to suspend it. To avoid a familiar rehash of standard drug-case or Newcomb-style examples, inject the framing of “calibration contagion”: every participant receives a private “signal card” that is either genuine or corrupted by a hidden “bias token” assigned randomly; they must evaluate a collective scenario—distributing limited resources among competing claims—using both their private signal and public meta-evidence about corruption rates in the deck. The rules should require an actual collective decision (vote or allocation) with small real stakes, and should be arranged so that using first-order reasoning conflicts with deferring to meta-evidence before anyone names the conflict.

Include: (1) the setup and roles (e.g., analyst, auditor, decider, with rotation); (2) a round-by-round timeline that forces the conflict to emerge organically; (3) a payoff scheme using points that convert into a shared tangible outcome (e.g., a contribution to or deduction from a class fund for refreshments) so stakes are real but modest; (4) a debrief that maps specific moments in the activity onto first-order versus higher-order reasoning, onto the phenomenology of believing against one’s own best judgment, and onto the question of whether suspension is rational or self-undermining; (5) an explicit, honest note about which feature of epistemic akrasia—most likely the felt irrationality or alienation of overriding one’s own reasoning—the mechanics can approximate but not fully reproduce, and what the facilitator must therefore supply verbally; and (6) a prediction of what will predictably go wrong the first time you run it (e.g., participants conflate “corrupted source” with “false content,” or auditors dominate the group) together with a concrete facilitation fix.

The idea should appear in what happens under the rules, not in an opening explanation: participants should experience the tension between trusting their card and distrusting their process before the facilitator labels it epistemic akrasia. Be frank about the limits of the simulation, avoid asking for bibliographies or citations, and do not depend on any obscure named theory beyond the concept itself.
