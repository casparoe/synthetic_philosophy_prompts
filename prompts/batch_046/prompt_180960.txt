You are a philosopher of technology and an experienced seminar facilitator with expertise in metaphysics, virtual ethics, and workshop design. Before proposing anything, spend a few minutes brainstorming three distinct mechanics by which a group of participants could experience a conflict between the modal fragility of virtual objects and the persistence of harm across virtual environments. Then commit definitively to one mechanic and defend that choice in writing: explain precisely why the other two fail to generate the specific phenomenological tension required, and take a clear position on whether your design ultimately demonstrates that virtual harms are genuinely real or that they are merely projected onto physical-world relations.

Design a 75-minute workshop for 12 philosophy graduate students on the question: are virtual objects and virtual harms real? To avoid well-worn examples, center the activity on a framing you will introduce within the session—call it “cross-world identity debt.” In this framing, a participant’s actions in one simulated environment generate obligations that survive the complete deletion of that environment and carry over into a second, structurally unrelated environment through an abstract identity ledger. The ledger has no physical substrate, can be forked into duplicate records, and binds the participant even though the original world and its objects no longer exist. The rules, roles, and incentives must be arranged so that students encounter the pull of obligation across worlds before the concept is named; only afterward should they discover that the object of the alleged harm has vanished and that their own identity record may have been duplicated.

Provide the following: (1) specific roles and rules, including a payoff scheme using only non-monetary stakes such as control over discussion time or sequencing of presentation; (2) a minute-by-minute timeline for the 75-minute session; (3) a debrief that maps specific moments in the activity onto the metaphysical distinction between harm-to-object and harm-to-relational-identity, and onto the problem of forking without physical continuity; (4) an honest assessment of what the activity predictably fails to reproduce about virtual harm—particularly regarding phenomenal experience—and what you as facilitator must supply verbally; and (5) a brief reflective note stating where your design takes liberties with theories of personal identity and whether those liberties serve or distort the conceptual point.

The deliverable should be a committed, polished proposal: do not present your design as one neutral option among equals. Build the rules to support your stated interpretation, justify that architectural choice, and be explicit about the limits of what a classroom simulation can show.
