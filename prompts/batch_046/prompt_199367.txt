I am drafting my contribution for a philosophy-of-science seminar on scientific representation and idealization, and I want to check whether the following commitments form a jointly tenable package before I present them. Here is the profile I have settled on:

(1) I endorse the view—often associated with Friedman—that unrealistic assumptions in scientific models are methodologically virtuous rather than defective, because they isolate dominant causal tendencies and yield bold, falsifiable predictions.

(2) I maintain that scientific explanation is mechanist: a model explains a phenomenon only if the core causal entities and relations it posits are approximately true of the target system.

(3) I hold that all idealizations are dispensable in principle: with sufficient computational resources, any model built on false assumptions could in principle be replaced by a descriptively accurate counterpart preserving the same predictive power.

(4) I believe that when relaxing an unrealistic assumption destroys predictive accuracy—when the falsehood is load-bearing—this demonstrates that the world genuinely lacks the feature the assumption denies, rather than showing the model is merely a convenient fiction.

(5) I take an instrumentalist stance toward model ontology: ontological commitment should be minimized and driven solely by predictive utility, not by representational fidelity.

Please audit this set for consistency. Specifically: identify which pairs are in genuine tension, rank those tensions by severity, and for each specify the least costly revision; distinguish these from pairs that only appear to conflict but can be reconciled on closer inspection. Finally, say whether giving up any single commitment among (1)–(5) would dissolve the greatest number of conflicts, and if so, which one and at what philosophical cost.
