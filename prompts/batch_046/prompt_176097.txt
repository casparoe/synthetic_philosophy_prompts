I am a high school student preparing a philosophy essay on the simulation argument and its bearing on long-term ethical commitment. The usual debate asks whether being simulated undermines the meaning of our lives, but I want to explore a narrower, less-discussed question: whether the possibility of simulation should dampen what some ethicists call fanaticism about the far future—that willingness to accept large present costs for outcomes thousands or millions of years hence. If our cosmos might be a simulation with a finite runtime, or one that an external observer could terminate, then actions taken for the sake of the distant future may never be realized in any ontologically stable way. Yet simulations might also be nested, with each level possessing its own distant future.

Write a short story that dramatizes this specific tension. The protagonist should be someone who attempts to formalize a “simulational discount rate” for moral concern—weighting the value of future welfare by the estimated probability of being at each nesting level—only to discover that if base-reality agents apply the same reasoning, the argument collapses into a universal temporal parochialism that applies regardless of nesting. Ground the philosophical conflict in a concrete decision, such as allocating scarce resources between immediate climate resilience and a centuries-long terraforming or preservation project. Let the plot turn on the logical structure of nested ontological uncertainty and its implications for longtermist ethics, rather than on virtual-reality spectacle or action sequences. The story should feel philosophically precise: show how the character’s attempt to calculate moral weight either succeeds, fails, or transforms into an unexpected stance about what we owe to futures we cannot verify.
