Second, when someone spends her discretionary income on music that deeply moves her rather than on malaria nets, without claiming that music is objectively more valuable, I treat this as a genuine preference about the legitimate use of her own resources.

Third, when an interview panel favors applicants whose conversational rhythms mirror their own, defending the choice as good chemistry, I regard that as a bias: interpersonal comfort is being illegitimately weighted in what should be a merit-based decision.

Fourth, when a culture assigns higher funeral honors to deaths occurring in winter, and members defend this as traditional taste, I think the verdict turns on correlation; if winter tracks an agricultural calendar with no social hierarchy, it is preference, but if winter correlates with caste status, it is bias.

The tension arises in the fifth and sixth cases. Fifth, a triage physician admits she feels greater urgency to save patients who share her obscure hobby of collecting antique barometers; she insists this is a harmless personal affinity that never enters her clinical reasoning. I want to call it a bias. Sixth, however, I think a research scientist may legitimately prefer collaborators with a shared analytical style—say, a pronounced taste for formal modeling—even though that style correlates with educational background, provided the style is genuinely relevant to the project's aims. The two cases seem structurally parallel—both involve using an apparently irrelevant or only-correlated trait to guide a high-stakes allocation—yet I want opposite verdicts.
