I'm taking a graduate seminar on classical reception and moral education, and my classmates and I have been arguing about whether the modern leadership industry has essentially reinvented Plutarch's Parallel Lives as a self-improvement technology—only now with more hagiography and less irony. For a writing exercise, please compose a satirical, deadpan parody of an executive-education program brochure—approximately 650 words, written in the polished, aspirational bureaucratic voice of an elite university's continuing-studies catalog. The brochure should advertise a fictional "Certificate in Parallel Lives Leadership" that teaches moral self-cultivation through comparative biography, pairing ancient figures such as Pericles or Cato the Younger with modern "exemplars" like an unnamed tech founder and a venture capitalist in the manner of Plutarch. Include course descriptions, a sidebar on "hagiographic correction," notes on tuition and alumni outcomes, and at least one testimonial. The satire should target the contemporary practice of treating biography—especially of successful people—as a direct, prescriptive instrument for character formation: exaggerate the real tendencies to smooth away moral complexity, to treat worldly success as evidence of virtue, to force false parallels based on fame rather than shared moral structure, and to present descriptive life-narratives as normative "character calibration" exercises. Keep the irony sustained and deadpan throughout; never wink, never announce that the premise is absurd, and let the absurdity emerge entirely from the faithful extension of the target logic.

After the brochure, append a brief author's note—no more than 150 words—explaining where the parody takes liberties with Plutarch's actual comparative method (for instance, that his pairings were often structured around shared virtue or vice rather than market capitalization or cultural influence) and identifying what specific argument or empirical evidence about biography's pedagogical efficacy, or about the gap between descriptive narrative and prescriptive moral learning, would lead you to regard the satire as unfair rather than illuminating. In other words, state clearly what would change your mind about the fairness of the parody.
