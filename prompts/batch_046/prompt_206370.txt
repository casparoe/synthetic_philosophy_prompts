I've been trying to formalize what Fitch's paradox of knowability actually commits us to, beyond the textbook gloss that it threatens omniscience. Here is the framing I'm using: take any true proposition p that is currently unknown. By the knowability principle, (p ∧ ¬Kp) should be knowable, but knowing it requires knowing p, which contradicts ¬Kp. So far so standard. But I want to push this further and treat the paradox not as a global reductio of the knowability thesis, but as identifying a specific modal profile—call it being Fitch-shadowed—that applies to particular unknown truths: a proposition is Fitch-shadowed just when it is true, unknown, and such that its conjunction with its own unknown status is logically inaccessible to knowledge.

The part where I think I'm going wrong is in trying to use this profile for actual credence management. If I'm reasoning about an open problem in, say, set theory—a specific claim C whose truth value I don't know—should I treat C as potentially Fitch-shadowed and therefore assign it a special kind of epistemic status? Specifically, should my prior over C be constrained differently from other unknown propositions, on the grounds that if C is true and unknown, then there's a structurally unobservable fact (namely, that C is true and unknown) that no amount of evidence could ever bring into my evidence set? And more generally, does this mean Bayesian updating needs a special handling rule for propositions that might occupy this shadow region—perhaps a prohibition on conditioning on the absence of knowledge itself?

I realize the paradox is usually read as showing that the universal knowability principle collapses into the claim that there are no unknown truths at all. But I'm wondering whether there's a coherent intermediate interpretation—maybe in a non-idealized epistemic logic—where the paradox doesn't entail global omniscience, but instead carves out a local boundary class of truths that are genuinely true and genuinely unknown, yet paired with an unobservable meta-state. Is there a principled reason, within standard epistemic logic or rationalist belief norms, to reject this intermediate reading? Or is my error deeper: am I misapplying a logical result about the closure properties of the knowledge operator to first-person credence calibration in a way that confuses modal logic with decision theory?
