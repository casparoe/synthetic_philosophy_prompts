Design a 75-minute workshop activity for a graduate seminar of approximately 14 students in philosophy of mind and logic, structured so that participants encounter the pressure of a Gödelian anti-mechanist argument in the Lucas-Penrose tradition through their own collective reasoning rather than through exposition. Instead of presenting Gödel numbering via arithmetic, have the group co-author a finite, explicit rule-system—call it Protocol K—that governs how they may derive conclusions about statements printed on cards. Protocol K should be mechanically applicable, apparently complete for its vocabulary, and strictly adhered to during a cooperative derivation phase. Once the group operates Protocol K fluently, introduce a single card bearing a self-referential statement constructed entirely within Protocol K’s vocabulary, asserting its own non-derivability under Protocol K. The group must attempt to derive the statement using only Protocol K; they will systematically fail. Then open a brief meta-phase in which participants may discuss, outside Protocol K’s rules, whether the statement is true. They should converge on recognizing it as true precisely in virtue of its underivability within the system, and then face the pivot question: if their collective reasoning just exceeded the boundaries of Protocol K, yet Protocol K was designed to exhaust the reasoning capacities available to them, what follows for the thesis that such reasoning is mechanizable?

The deliverable should specify: (1) the complete rules of Protocol K and the physical materials required—card templates, derivation logs, and role assignments—so that no external text is needed; (2) a minute-by-minute facilitation timeline indicating exactly when participants should discover the derivation failure, when the meta-phase begins, and when the pivot question is posed; (3) a debrief that maps the derivation deadlock onto Gödel’s first incompleteness theorem, the meta-phase recognition onto the Lucas-Penrose claim that human reasoners can grasp truths inaccessible to formal systems, and the collective structure of the exercise onto the problem of whether a distributed mechanism could replicate the meta-step; (4) an honest forecast of predictable failures on the first run—most likely premature meta-reasoning, confusion about self-reference, or treating the card as paradoxical rather than true—and precise corrective moves for the facilitator; and (5) a frank admission of which feature of the Lucas-Penrose argument this activity cannot reproduce—specifically, the argument’s reliance on the claim that individual human mathematicians possess a single, consistent, non-mechanizable faculty of direct insight rather than a fallible, socially distributed reasoning process—and explicit guidance on how the facilitator must supply that missing element in the closing discussion without undermining the experiential force of the exercise. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; the activity must be fully runnable for participants with no prior assigned texts.
