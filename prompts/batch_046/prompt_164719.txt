Assume the role of a senior philosopher of action and metaphysics with expertise in moral psychology and the free will debate. I am preparing a graduate seminar on agency, and before we map the standard terrain of compatibilism versus incompatibilism I want to establish the evaluation criteria rigorously rather than rehearsing familiar positions. Please proceed carefully and deliberately: begin with a brief brainstorming outline of what you take to be the core conceptual puzzles, then move to the structured task below.

Write the full list of desiderata that any adequate theory of free will must satisfy if it is to account not only for ordinary intentional action but also for cases of strategic self-binding—situations in which an agent deliberately restricts her own future options (for instance, through Ulysses contracts, pre-commitment devices, or self-imposed institutional rules) in order to safeguard a long-range plan. For each desideratum, provide the motivating case or conceptual consideration that makes it necessary; distinguish which ones you regard as non-negotiable and which are open to theoretical revision; and explicitly flag any pair you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied. In particular, your list should address whether the theory must preserve a robust ability to do otherwise at the exact moment of action, whether it must render rational the voluntary limitation of that very ability, and whether it must differentiate the freedom of the initial binding act from the freedom (or absence thereof) of the subsequent bound behavior. Once the list is complete, score compatibilism and incompatibilism very briefly against these criteria—drawing only on their standard conceptual commitments, not on specific textual citations, bibliographic references, or named works.
