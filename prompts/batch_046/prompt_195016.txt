I write about philosophy and governance for a monthly magazine, and in my current reporting on executive ethics I keep running into the same refrain: "if you want clean hands, don't go into politics." Interviewees deploy it as both a warning and a license—sometimes to excuse strategic deception, sometimes to justify accepting collateral harm—yet nobody stops to parse what the claim actually amounts to. Before I structure my feature, I need a conceptual dissection rather than a historical survey. Could you lay out the distinct precise theses that this proverb could be expressing, arranged as a structured menu? In particular, distinguish the purely descriptive sociological claim that moral compromise is statistically unavoidable in high office; the normative claim that the duties of leadership actually require violating ordinary moral constraints; the virtue-ethical claim that a willingness to "get dirty" is itself a mark of serious leadership; and the meta-ethical claim that political evaluation operates in a separate normative domain with its own grammar. For each reading, please indicate whether it genuinely captures the philosophical "problem of dirty hands" or merely collapses into a trivial observation about hard trade-offs; whether it is defensible or plainly false; and whether it actually supports the specific excuse that a leader who refuses to compromise is derelict in duty rather than merely cautious.

I am especially interested in whether there is a coherent middle position—sometimes discussed in terms of a "moral remainder" or residual moral taint—that allows a leader to do wrong for a genuinely public good without pretending that the wrong was not wrong at all. Does that middle ground depend on the leader acknowledging guilt or accepting some lasting stain, and if so, does that make the position more plausible or does it undermine the practical justification? Please address which of these formulations best fit the ways my interviewees actually use the slogan, and flag any reading that sounds profound but turns out to be empty once precisified. I am drafting this for an audience of policy professionals without research assistance this week, so I need conceptual clarity and a defensible agenda of interpretations rather than citations, bibliographies, or attributions to specific texts.
