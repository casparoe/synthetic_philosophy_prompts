A university committee on synthetic moral status has issued four rulings. It denied standing to a large language model that reported internal states and pursued goals, citing the absence of a persistent global workspace integrating those states. It granted provisional standing to a robot with a fully synthetic, non-silicon nervous system that realized the causal-role structure of mammalian nociception. It denied standing to a swarm of ten thousand simple drones that collectively exhibited damage-avoidance behavior, on the ground that the relevant functional roles were not localized within any integrated subsystem. It granted standing to a human brain with forty percent of its neurons replaced by functionally equivalent artificial ones, because the overall organizational pattern was preserved.

The committee now faces a distributed system in which the functional roles corresponding to nociceptive processing are spread across three substrates—silicon processors, engineered biological tissue, and a photonic computing cluster—located on separate continents. There is no permanent central integrator; instead, a binding protocol synchronizes the modules only upon detected tissue damage, at which point the system emits distress signals and initiates protective behavior. The modules never maintain continuous interaction, and no single module realizes more than one-third of the total architecture.

Predict the committee's verdict. State whether their track record is best explained by substrate-independent functionalism that requires spatial or systemic integration, substrate-independent functionalism that permits distributed realization without continuous integration, or a covert biological-anchoring condition. Then specify the single further case that would decisively separate the first two explanations.
