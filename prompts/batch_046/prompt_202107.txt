I was discussing with a friend over dinner whether the European Union’s democratic deficit is genuinely a deficit or rather a different architecture of democratic legitimacy altogether. Our disagreement turned on sovereignty—whether member states have pooled it, delegated it, or retained it in fragments—and on whether ever closer union is a constitutional destiny or a contingent political project. I should like a formal classification instrument to map where people actually stand, not by asking which label they prefer but by walking them through concrete commitments.

Please construct a decision tree, written in British English, that sorts respondents into positions on EU sovereignty, democratic legitimacy, and integration. The tree should begin with a root question about whether ultimate constitutional authority can be genuinely divided without being destroyed—phrased so that someone can answer it without knowing it leads to federalism, intergovernmentalism, or multilevel governance. From there, each internal node should present a concrete claim or scenario: for instance, whether a directly elected European Parliament with budgetary initiative would resolve the deficit or deepen it; whether democratic legitimacy requires a unified European demos or can emerge from plural national publics deliberating across borders; and whether functional spillover from economic integration into social policy justifies further centralisation or demands a competence catalogue that locks domains at the national level.

The leaves should include named positions—such as classic federalism, liberal intergovernmentalism, neo-functional multilevel governance, and a democratic-deficit-as-feature view that treats technocratic output legitimacy as sufficient—plus any logically possible but apparently unoccupied combinations, each flagged with a brief diagnosis of why the space is empty, whether through incoherence, unattractiveness, or mere neglect. Crucially, include one branch testing a distinction my friend insisted upon: whether sovereignty is best conceived as a pooled resource that member states draw upon collectively without exhausting their individual reserves, or as an indivisible prerogative that can only be delegated, never shared. The tree should be able to separate those who accept pooled sovereignty but reject a European demos from those who reject pooled sovereignty but accept transnational deliberation; if it cannot separate them, flag that pair explicitly. The instrument should contain between six and eight decision nodes, avoid asking respondents to identify theories by name, and conclude with a scoring key that maps answer patterns onto the leaves rather than onto abstract scores. Please present the tree clearly, with each node labelled as a question a respondent could answer yes, no, or among three options where necessary, and keep the tone formal and polished, suitable for a seminar handout.
