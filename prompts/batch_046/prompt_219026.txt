I've been thinking about whether generational labels—Boomers, Millennials, Gen Z—track anything real in the ontology of social groups, or whether they're just convenience categories we reify. A colleague who writes about demographic ontology has taken clear positions on several cohorts: she holds that Baby Boomers (born roughly 1946–1964) constitute a real kind because they share a specific post-war economic and institutional trajectory that shaped durable preferences and risk profiles; that Millennials (born roughly 1981–1996) do not form a real kind, since the category mostly captures age-related behaviors—social media adoption, urbanization, delayed homeownership—that any cohort would exhibit at that life stage, with no distinct formative shock binding them together; that Gen Z (born roughly 1997–2012) is a real kind, but specifically because universal adolescent exposure to algorithmic, smartphone-mediated social environments created a shared cognitive architecture that differs both from older Millennials and from the cohort that follows; and that Xennials (born roughly 1977–1983) are not a real kind, being a marketing boundary with no sharp discontinuity in experience or behavior relative to adjacent birth years.

She also distinguishes, in passing, between a cohort being a real kind and being a useful fiction: a useful fiction can support prediction or policy, but lacks the modal robustness—counterfactual stability across nearby worlds—of a real kind.

Now consider a new case I want to test her framework on: the Lockdown Cohort, defined as those born between March 2020 and December 2022, whose first 1,000 days of development occurred under conditions of sustained social distancing, near-universal caregiver mask-wearing, absence of non-household peer interaction, and heavy mediation of all social contact through screens. Developmental studies suggest a measurable, cohort-level delay in joint-attention acquisition that largely normalizes by age five, but with a persistent, subtle shift toward mediated interaction preferences. The birth window is narrow—about thirty-three months—and institutional recognition exists mainly in clinical literature, not in popular culture or self-identity. Adjacent cohorts born in 2019 and 2023 experienced partial versions of these conditions, with no sharp cutoff.

Given her earlier verdicts, should she classify the Lockdown Cohort as a real kind? I'm less interested in the bare yes or no than in the rule she seems to be running, the alternative rules that fit her four earlier judgments just as well, and which hypothetical cohort case would best separate those rules. In particular, I'd like to know whether her framework turns on (i) the presence of a critical-period environmental shock that is both intense and cohort-specific, (ii) the existence of sharp statistical discontinuities at the birth-year boundaries, or (iii) something like modal robustness across counterfactual histories—where the cohort's distinctive traits would persist even if the shock had been slightly differently distributed. And if she adopts one rule over the others for this case, what exactly is she committing herself to about the ontology of generations?
