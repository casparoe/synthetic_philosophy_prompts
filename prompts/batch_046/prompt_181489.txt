I keep hearing people invoke "the wisdom of crowds" when committees vote separately on interlocking questions — for instance, whether a proposed policy is legal, whether it will actually work, and whether it ought to be adopted — and then combine those separate majorities into a collective decision. In one case I noticed, the crowd said yes to legality and yes to efficacy, yet the same members voted no on adoption, because different majorities formed on each premise. The chair still defended using the separate votes, saying we should trust the crowd on each piece. I want to understand what precise claims that slogan could be making: statistical averaging of errors, independence conditions, diversity of viewpoints, or rules about how judgments should be aggregated? For each reading, say whether it is trivial or substantive, whether it applies when the judgments are logically connected, and whether it justifies keeping the premise-votes even when they contradict the conclusion-vote. Lay the readings out as a menu with verdicts. Please say how confident you are in the analysis, and single out the specific point you are least sure about.
