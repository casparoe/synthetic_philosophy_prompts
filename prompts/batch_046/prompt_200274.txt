I'm currently taking a graduate seminar on consciousness and have recently begun using a consumer-grade EEG headband that displays real-time "anxiety signatures" via a small LED. Last week I noticed something unsettling: I often find myself reporting anxiety only after the light flashes, and during one session the device alerted while I felt no corresponding distress—likely an artifactual spike. This made me wonder whether my ordinary judgments about mental states work analogously, and I have sketched out an argument that now strikes me as suspicious because it proves far more than I want it to.

(1) Epiphenomenalism holds that conscious mental states are caused by brain events but never cause physical events themselves, including utterances or beliefs. (2) In my headband case, the judgment "I am anxious" is triggered by the neural signal N, not by the anxiety itself; indeed, N can occur and trigger the judgment even when anxiety is absent. (3) By direct analogy, all judgments about my mental states are triggered solely by their neural correlates, not by the mental states, since those states have no causal efficacy. (4) But if a judgment is triggered solely by a neural correlate, then in any scenario where that correlate occurs without the mental state—whether through artificial induction, a misfiring, or a phenomenally empty twin— I will still make the judgment. (5) Therefore my judgments about my mental life are not sensitive to whether those mental states actually exist. (6) If a judgment is not sensitive to the presence of the state it reports, it cannot amount to knowledge of that state. (7) So if epiphenomenalism is true, I lack knowledge of all my conscious states. (8) But without knowledge of my own mental states, I cannot form the reasoned judgments that constitute philosophical inquiry—including the very reasoning that led me to consider epiphenomenalism in the first place. (9) Hence epiphenomenalism is self-defeating: accepting it requires the very self-knowledge it renders impossible.

The conclusion feels absurdly strong, since it does not merely challenge epiphenomenalism but seems to show that no one could rationally hold any view about consciousness at all. I would prefer to preserve (1) and (8), because the causal-inertness claim and the self-defeat worry both seem genuinely pressing. Where exactly does the chain break? Which premise should be rejected, why did it look plausible in the headband analogy, and what do the standard responses—the correlationist, the direct-acquaintance defender, and the causal-efficacy opponent—each have to pay if they reject that particular step?
