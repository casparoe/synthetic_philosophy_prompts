As a software engineer without formal philosophical training, I sit on my firm's internal technology-impact review board, and I am trying to understand whether our past deployment rulings form a coherent decision framework—particularly regarding problems of consequentialist cluelessness, where near-term outcomes are measurable but long-range effects are not.

Our record on four prior proposals is consistent in form but puzzling in substance. Case one: we approved an update to our recommendation engine that improved engagement metrics; we acknowledged that effects on user autonomy across multi-decade horizons were structurally unquantifiable, yet treated this as acceptable background uncertainty rather than a veto condition. Case two: we rejected an expansion of behavioral-data collection intended to train future foundation models; near-term product value was high, but we found the thirty-year trajectory of privacy norms and institutional trust to be not merely unknown but unframeable within any forecasting framework, and we blocked the proposal. Case three: we approved—subject to continuous audit—the open release of model weights; we recognized substantial uncertainty about misuse pathways five to ten years ahead, but reasoned that distributed oversight and community monitoring supplied a correctable architecture. Case four: we rejected automation of mental-health triage; near-term efficiency gains were well documented, yet we concluded that the long-term dynamics of therapeutic relationships and diagnostic drift could not be rendered into any predictive model, and that this irreducible blindness made deployment impermissible.

We now face a fifth proposal: an autonomous agent framework that negotiates and executes routine contracts on behalf of individual users. Near-term transaction-cost reductions are substantial and measurable. Over longer horizons, however, widespread adoption could restructure norms around contractual agency and legal attribution in ways that current sociology and jurisprudence provide no tools to anticipate—though the system includes a universal kill-switch, mandatory human-override protocols, and a complete audit trail permitting rollback at any point.

If we wish to be consistent with ourselves, what should our verdict be? Identify the closest analog among the four precedents and explain precisely where the analogy succeeds and where it breaks. If our record genuinely underdetermines the outcome, then articulate the competing implicit principles—such as near-term quantifiable benefit dominance, structural-opacity veto, or reversibility-through-architecture—and state what each principle predicts for this new case. Give your best-guess verdict with explicit reasoning that selects among these candidates. Finally, specify one clearly different hypothetical case that would cleanly separate the principle you believe we are actually running from its nearest competitor.
