As a retired physicist who has only recently begun studying philosophy in a structured way, I am organizing a ninety-minute workshop for an interdisciplinary reading group of about ten people—mostly working scientists with some philosophical curiosity—on Friedrich Nietzsche’s second Untimely Meditation, the essay on history for life. I want the session to center on his distinction among monumental, antiquarian, and critical history, together with the specific danger each poses to living culture. Rather than delivering a lecture, I want a runnable in-class simulation or structured deliberation that makes the participants feel the tension themselves: the rules, roles, and resource constraints should push them into acting out each mode and then confronting its corresponding disadvantage before anyone names the taxonomy aloud. Please design the activity with the following specifications.

First, the scenario: the group must collectively decide the fate of a detailed but fictional archive from 1920s experimental science—containing discredited laboratory notebooks, personal correspondence, and one physically imposing but obsolete apparatus—under conditions of limited institutional funding and a ticking clock.

Second, assign each participant a role tied to one of Nietzsche’s three modes, but also introduce a hidden constraint card that activates only after thirty minutes, revealing the particular disadvantage of that mode (for instance, the monumentalist’s drive to emulate the past begins to suppress present innovation; the antiquarian’s preservationism blocks necessary destruction; the criticalist’s negation leaves nothing to build upon).

Third, inject a niche element drawn from scientific practice rather than Nietzsche’s own text: include a fourth role, the predictive archivist, who treats the entire historical record solely as training data for forecasting future failures, arguing that the past has value only as an input to predictive models. The group should discover through negotiation whether this scientific mode serves life in Nietzsche’s sense or represents a new, unrecognized form of historical suffocation.

Fourth, provide clear rules, a timeline for the ninety minutes, a non-monetary payoff or scoring mechanism—perhaps collective reputation points or veto powers that affect the final institutional decision—and materials that can be printed on a single sheet.

Fifth, include a debrief that maps specific moments of the simulation back onto Nietzsche’s concepts, predicts what will go wrong the first time the activity is run and how to correct it, and frankly states which feature of Nietzsche’s account—specifically the physiological or vitalist grounding of life as an organic, feeling-driven force—this classroom exercise cannot reproduce and must instead be supplied by me as facilitator.

Finally, please write the response in American English, do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations, and do not quote Nietzsche’s text verbatim unless you have fetched and verified the passage; otherwise paraphrase without quotation marks.
