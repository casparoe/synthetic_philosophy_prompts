I'm preparing the interview guide for an upcoming episode of my podcast on philosophy of language and social ontology. Next week's guest is an unnamed philosopher—let's call her Dr. Mara Chen for the conversation—who has recently defended an extension of Frege's puzzle about identity statements into the domain of collective entities and institutional reference. On her view, the classic problem of why a = a is trivial while a = b can be genuinely informative is not adequately resolved by the standard sense-reference distinction alone, because both Fregean and direct-reference accounts assume that the objects referred to are fully determinate individuals with clear persistence conditions. She argues that institutions—universities, corporations, states—are genuinely referred to in identity statements like the university founded in 1850 is the same corporation that now employs four thousand people, but that these statements carry cognitive significance precisely because the institution lacks the kind of individual persistence conditions that make identity strictly binary. She proposes that we treat such cases as involving graded modes of presentation that track functional continuity rather than strict numerical identity, claiming this avoids both the anti-realism of some social-constructivist accounts and the reification problems of standard essentialist ones.

I want a sequence of eight to ten interview questions that press her on the actual mechanics of this position rather than letting her restate it. I need at least one question addressing how her graded modes of presentation differ empirically from a standard Fregean treatment that simply assigns different senses to institutional descriptions; at least one on what her view implies for Leibniz's law if identity is not strictly binary; and at least one on whether her framework can accommodate institutional dissolution without collapsing into the claim that every organizational change produces a new entity. For each of these three, please include a follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give—for instance, if she appeals to functional continuity, ask what specific discontinuity would count as identity failure rather than mere transformation; if she invokes cognitive architecture to explain the cognitive significance, ask how she distinguishes her view from mental-file theories of reference; and if she distinguishes her position from social constructivism, ask which metaphysical commitments she retains that a thoroughgoing constructivist must reject. The remaining questions should probe where the literature actually presses: whether her view makes all institutional change a matter of degree, how she handles cases where two descriptions seem to refer to overlapping but not identical institutional structures, and what she would say about legal judgments that treat corporate identity as all-or-nothing for liability purposes.
