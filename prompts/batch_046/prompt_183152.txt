I spent the better part of yesterday evening arguing with an old friend from my undergraduate years about whether historical explanations can be genuinely overdetermined, and by the end I was less convinced that I understand the intuition behind it than I was at the start. We kept returning to the same imagined case—picture a cooperative shipping firm that collapses in the winter of 1911, brought down simultaneously by an internal embezzlement scandal that rendered it insolvent and by a sudden tidal storm that destroyed its physical warehouses, either event alone being sufficient to force dissolution—yet we could not agree whether saying that both caused the collapse is just an innocent pluralism or whether it hides a real structural tension about explanatory redundancy and the economy of reasons. I am now running a philosophy-of-history workshop for eight graduate students, none of them specialists in metaphysics, meeting for seventy-five minutes next Thursday, and I want them to feel that specific tension in their bones rather than merely discuss it in the abstract. Please design a runnable in-class activity, essentially a structured deliberation or simulation with assigned roles, explicit rules, and small intellectual stakes, that forces the group to construct a joint explanatory narrative of this fictional but richly detailed dissolution. Give them concrete roles—perhaps an economic historian charged with tracking financial sufficiency, an institutional historian charged with tracking organizational breakdown, and a causal auditor whose only job is to flag when the group assigns full explanatory weight to two independent chains—along with rules that require them to distribute a fixed pool of explanatory points or to defend a single primary narrative in a simulated journal submission, so that they must act before they learn that both causal chains are individually sufficient. Include a precise timeline that fits the seventy-five-minute window, a brief description of the scenario’s documents or evidence they will handle, and a debrief, all written in continuous prose, that maps each moment of frustration—especially the predictable point where one participant tries to weaken one cause into a mere background condition—onto the conceptual distinction between contributing causation and genuinely redundant sufficient causation. Be frank about what will go wrong the first time I run it, likely that the group will split along disciplinary instincts or attempt to resolve the contradiction by redefining what sufficiency means, and tell me honestly which feature of philosophical overdetermination this exercise cannot reproduce and I will have to supply verbally myself. Write the entire response as continuous prose without headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, as if you were writing a letter to a colleague who will run the session, and do not include any request for bibliographies, citations, or reading lists, since the participants will come in cold.
