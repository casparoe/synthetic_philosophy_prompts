As a high school student reading about economic history, I have become stuck on a debate that has persisted for decades without any visible movement, despite involving some of the smartest people in economics and history. The question is why some nations became rich while others remained poor. One camp—often called the institutionalists—argues that inclusive political and economic institutions are the deep cause, citing comparative cases such as the divergence of North and South Korea and the so-called reversal of fortune in regions that experienced different colonial regimes. Another camp emphasizes geographic endowments: climate, disease ecology, navigability, and resource accessibility, treating institutional differences as largely downstream of these constraints. A third camp appeals to cultural norms—trust, religious inheritance, work-related values—suggesting that institutions and geography only operate where certain cultural preconditions hold. The same canonical comparisons are recycled, the same natural experiments are invoked with opposite interpretations, and nobody seems to change their position.

My working hypothesis is that the deadlock is not mainly about missing empirical facts, but about an unacknowledged divergence in what counts as a legitimate causal claim when experimental manipulation is impossible at the national scale. Specifically, I suspect the institutionalists implicitly demand explanations that are counterfactually stable across multiple equilibrium paths—meaning the mechanism would hold even if history had branched differently—while the geographers and culturalists are satisfied with predictive correlation across the observed historical path, even if the system permits path-dependent branching that undermines cross-path stability. If so, the sides may be answering different questions—one about structural causation, one about historical prediction—under the single heading “why are some nations rich?”

Are there bedrock intuitions about human agency versus environmental determinism that neither side can argue for? Could the impasse mean the question is ill-posed—perhaps because “wealth” aggregates too many incommensurable outcomes, or because “nation” is not the right unit of analysis?
