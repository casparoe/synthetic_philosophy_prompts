I run a ninety-minute philosophy-of-mind workshop for twelve graduate students and want an activity that makes them feel, before anyone names it, the strange pull of the idea that an “I” might survive distributed across brains if its pattern of memories and values is preserved elsewhere—something in the neighborhood of Hofstadter’s loop-migration, but staged so the tension arises entirely from rules and roles rather than from lecture. Design a structured deliberation using four triads, each given a constructed fictional “memory core” of first-person cards describing specific emotional habits, bodily routines, and value-commitments tied to a single narrative. Then introduce the “asymmetric archive” rule: in each triad, one member’s cards are physically removed and distributed to members of other triads, so the original holder can recover them only through second-person reports from strangers who now hold the cards. The holder must decide, defending the choice solely in first-person testimony and without appealing to bodily continuity, whether these recovered memories still belong to the same “I,” have been incorporated into another’s loop, or now constitute a distributed self shared between brains. Give them real small stakes—token votes that determine whether the group collectively grants the holder exclusive rights over the cards, shared co-ownership, or no rights at all—and require each voter to justify the choice as if speaking from inside the affected perspective. Provide a timeline, the card materials, the token scheme, and a debrief that maps each decision-point onto the problem of pattern-based persistence versus biological boundary, including an honest statement of which feature of the view the activity cannot reproduce, especially the continuous subjective flow that makes distribution feel intuitively wrong, so I know exactly what I must supply in discussion. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it. Respond in continuous prose only, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, as in a single letter.
