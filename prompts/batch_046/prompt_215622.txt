For a reading-group profile I'm drafting on value theory, I want a consistency audit of the following commitments before I present them:

(1) Moore's organic-unity principle is correct: intrinsic value belongs to wholes and never fully decomposes into the value of isolated parts.
(2) The only intrinsic goods are friendship and aesthetic experience, but friendship is genuinely present only when each party appreciates real good-making qualities in the other; mere affection or companionship is insufficient.
(3) Social wholes are mereologically essential: a friendship is essentially this relation between these particular people, so qualitative duplication with different individuals would not preserve the original value.
(4) Goodness is simple and non-natural, so psychological or evolutionary explanations of friendship can never justify its value, only explain its occurrence.
(5) If the mental states that constitute a friendship are present but rest on widespread false belief about the friends' virtues, the organic whole remains intact and retains its value, since value supervenes on the mental states and the relation rather than on representational accuracy.

Please trace the tensions: which pairs genuinely conflict, which conflicts are only apparent, and what is the least costly revision? Rank them by severity. In particular, check whether (3) introduces an asymmetry between friendship and aesthetic experience—since in the aesthetic case the "parts" seem to be mental states that could in principle be duplicated in another subject without loss—thereby undermining (1)'s generality. Also say whether abandoning (5) or (3) does less damage to the overall Moorean framework.
