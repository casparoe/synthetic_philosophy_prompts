I'd like a move-by-move adjudication of a disagreement I observed between two members of a rationalist discussion forum — call them Jordan and Riley — about the ethics of removing statues and renaming streets that commemorate historical figures whose primary legacies involve grave moral harms, such as colonial administration or slaveholding economies, but who also made significant domain-specific contributions. The argument turned on whether public commemoration is best understood as active endorsement or as neutral historical indexing, and on whether there is a defensible principle for deciding which commemorations should survive.

Jordan began by arguing that monuments and named streets are not neutral archives but active allocations of scarce honorific space. Because societies do not commemorate every historical actor, the figures who remain represent a curated endorsement. Keeping a statue or street name in place is therefore a present-tense decision to continue honoring that figure, not a passive preservation of history. Jordan added that attempts at "legacy partition" — isolating a figure's achievements in science, politics, or art from the moral crimes they committed or enabled — usually fail in practice, because the very reason the figure is commemorated is often inseparable from the system of harm they administered.

Riley replied that Jordan was conflating remembrance with endorsement and falling into "presentist moral auditing." Statues and street names serve an indexical function: they mark causal importance in history, not moral approval. Riley argued that if every commemorated figure required full moral certification by current norms, societies would be forced into constant revision that destroys stable shared reference points. Riley also introduced an asymmetry: omitting a figure from future commemoration is a passive omission, but actively tearing down a statue or renaming a street is an erasure that imposes real cognitive and bureaucratic costs — what Riley called "institutional path dependence" — without a clear corresponding gain.

Jordan countered that Riley's asymmetry mistakes institutional inertia for neutrality. The "shared reference point" is already a political artifact; preserving it privileges those who benefit from the status quo and imposes continuing symbolic harm on those who see the figure as a perpetrator. Jordan further argued that Riley's fear of endless revision is overstated if we apply a consistent criterion: figures whose primary historical significance derives from actions now widely regarded as grave moral violations should not occupy honorific public space, regardless of secondary achievements. Jordan termed this the "honor budget" constraint — since public commemorative space is scarce, it should be spent only on figures we would willingly honor today.

Riley conceded that curation is inevitable but insisted that Jordan's criterion still requires a principled line between grave violations and ordinary human failings, and that without such a line the renaming process is vulnerable to "epistemic capture" by whichever faction currently controls the decision-making body. Riley also noted that legacy partition, while imperfect, is sometimes appropriate: we can teach about a figure's harmful actions in museums or textbooks without placing them in places of honor, but we should not pretend that those harmful actions erase all other historical significance.

Please adjudicate this exchange point by point. For each of Jordan's and Riley's moves, rate the argument from clearly mistaken to clearly correct, explain where each side lands its best point, and identify what both are missing — for instance, whether a coherent middle position exists between active endorsement and pure indexing, or whether the scarcity of public space actually supports Jordan's honor-budget framing more strongly than Riley admits. Write your response in British English (for example, use honour, behaviour, centre, analyse).
