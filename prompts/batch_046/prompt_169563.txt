I am trying to systematize my judgments about when, if ever, it is philosophically defensible to speak of a "national character" or to generalize about the dispositions of a people. My intuitions on particular cases are stable, but I cannot see the principle that unifies them, and some pairs seem hard to reconcile.

Here are my verdicts. First, describing mid-twentieth-century Swiss political culture as exhibiting a contingent institutional preference for direct democracy and fiscal conservatism strikes me as perfectly legitimate, provided the claim is anchored to specific historical structures rather than to an ethnic essence. Second, the sociological observation that contemporary Japanese organizational life tends toward high-context communication and consensus-oriented decision-making also seems permissible, but only when framed as a culturally transmitted pattern rather than as an innate racial disposition shared by all individuals of Japanese ancestry. Third, the casual stereotype that the French are romantic feels relatively harmless, yet the claim that the French are romantically inclined in ways that systematically impair strategic reasoning crosses into objectionable essentialism—though I am unsure whether the difference lies in content, in implied causal explanation, or merely in tone. Fourth, a historian's claim that post-colonial Nigerian administrative elites developed a distinctive patron-client style of governance shaped by specific colonial legacies seems acceptable, whereas a travel writer's assertion that Nigerians are naturally hospitable makes me uneasy, despite both being broad characterizations of a population. Fifth, I judge it clearly wrong to assert that the Roma possess a genetically inherited nomadic instinct, but I hesitate to reject the claim that Roma communities have historically maintained endogamous social structures as an adaptive response to sustained marginalization. Both generalize about a people, yet my reactions diverge sharply.

Is there a coherent principle—distinguishing, perhaps, historically contingent institutional patterns, statistically distributed cultural tendencies, and biologically anchored essential traits—that accommodates Cases 1, 2 (in its restricted form), 4, and the second half of Case 5, while excluding the others? Or does consistency require me to abandon one of these verdicts? In particular, how should I resolve Case 3, where the boundary between benign stereotype and harmful essentialism feels unstable? Please state your proposed principle precisely, indicate which of my verdicts it forces me to drop, and say what it predicts for a case I have not considered: attributing a specific risk-averse entrepreneurial temperament to members of a diaspora community formed in response to centuries of legal exclusion from property ownership.
