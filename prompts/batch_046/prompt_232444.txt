I hold the position that the intrinsic badness of pain is not a feature of its phenomenal sensory quality — the so-called 'raw feel' of nociception — but is constituted entirely by its affective-motivational profile: the aversive valence, the motivational urge to escape, and the emotional distress that typically accompany bodily damage signals. Pain asymbolia, in which patients report intact sensory discrimination of harmful stimuli yet exhibit no avoidance behaviour, no emotional perturbation, and no preference for analgesia, demonstrates that when this affective component is dissociated, the residual experience is not genuinely harmful and may even be phenomenally neutral or merely curious. To sharpen this, I distinguish between 'nociceptive phenomenal content' and 'aversive valence tagging', holding that only the latter carries normative weight; the sensory component is, in itself, normatively inert.

What would have to be true for this position to be false? I want a map of its genuine exposure: which empirical findings, conceptual arguments, phenomenological reports, or neurological developments would count decisively against it, and which would merely be awkward? Specifically, I am interested in whether a case of 'reverse dissociation' — intense aversive distress coupled with a complete absence of reported sensory quality — would support my view or undermine it by showing that aversion can float free of sensation, and whether intermediate or 'graded' dissociation cases (where affect is diminished but not absent) favour my binary distinction or instead suggest that badness tracks a continuous integration of sensation and affect. I also want to know what this view predicts that its rivals — for instance, the view that badness is an irreducible phenomenal property of the sensory experience itself — do not predict, and whether my position has been qualified into unfalsifiability: have I insulated it by redefining 'genuine pain' so that any apparently bad sensory experience is automatically classified as involving hidden affect? If so, is that insulation a theoretical vice for a view of this kind? Finally, please distinguish the evidence that defenders of this position would themselves concede as damaging from the criticisms that only opponents raise, and assess whether the view risks anything substantive or has become well protected — and whether that protection is intellectually costly.
