I'm a retired astrophysicist who got into philosophy after retirement, and now I help run a tuesday evening discussion circle for about 10 other retired scientists and engineers who are all new to this stuff. We've been arguing about utilitarianism and I keep feeling like we miss the force of the seperateness of persons objection because we talk about it like it's just a math error — adding up people wrong. So can you design a 90-minute classroom activity for us, almost like a role-play game with rules and paper tokens, that makes us actually feel the boundary between lives breaking down?

Here's the niche framing I have in mind: instead of separate individuals, the rules temporarily cast us as nodes in a single "aggregate welfare network" where we vote collectively on distributing colored cards — black for suffering-points, gold for joy-points — but each of us privately knows whether we personally hold a black card. The payoff scheme should be arranged so that there's one move where the network's total sum is clearly maximized by giving one person three black cards while everyone else gets one gold card each. Then, halfway through, the rules should suddenly allow us to "secede" and claim individual boundaries, but only if a unanimous vote happens. I need the actual rules, the token economics, a timeline, assigned roles like a "network controller" and "nodes," a debrief that maps the secession moment onto Rawls's worry about treating persons as mere containers, and an honest note about what this game can't reproduce — probably the deep metaphysical claim that persons are fundamentally distinct substances rather than just socially separated agents. Also tell me what's going to go wrong the first time, like someone refusing the black cards on moral grounds and breaking the simulation. Keep it practical — no bibliographies or reading lists, since I'm writing this for people who don't have university library access.
