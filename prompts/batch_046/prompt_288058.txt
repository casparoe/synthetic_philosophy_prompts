For the final examination of an upper-level seminar in normative ethics, design a single essay question on consequentialist cluelessness that can be answered in 75 minutes and is worth 25 marks. The question must not be satisfiable by summarizing the standard epistemic objection—that agents lack predictive access to the distant outcomes of particular actions. Instead, present the following recursive framing: any decision procedure an agent adopts to cope with first-order outcome cluelessness—whether satisficing, deference to moral rules, or delegation to institutional heuristics—initiates its own causal chains extending into an unpredictable future, thereby generating a second-order procedural cluelessness. Ask students to determine whether this recursion undermines the original cluelessness objection to consequentialism, collapses into a broader skepticism about practical reason altogether, or can be accommodated within a two-level consequentialist framework that distinguishes act-evaluation from rule-selection. Require them to (i) clearly separate first-order outcome cluelessness from second-order procedural cluelessness, (ii) explain why the recursive move does or does not generalize into skepticism about all decision-making, and (iii) assess whether a consequentialist can consistently endorse a meta-level decision rule without violating the principle that the moral status of an action depends on its actual consequences rather than on the agent's beliefs or adopted procedures. Provide a model answer of approximately 700 words and a marking scheme built around five must-hit analytical moves, assigning specific marks to each (total 25), together with a one-line note on the characteristic failure mode for each point.
