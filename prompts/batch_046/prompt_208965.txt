A colleague sketched an argument that seems to dissolve phenomenal consciousness into global workspace broadcasting, yet the conclusion strikes me as self-defeating. The steps run as follows.

(1) Global Workspace Theory supplies a complete mechanistic account of access consciousness: a representation is access-conscious exactly when it is globally broadcast and available to reasoning, verbal report, and action control.

(2) Phenomenal consciousness is standardly conceived as whatever conscious quality remains once access is fully accounted for — the subjective qualitative character that might persist even in the absence of broadcasting.

(3) If phenomenal consciousness is genuinely distinct from access consciousness, then there must be possible cases in which phenomenal properties vary while the global broadcasting profile remains fixed.

(4) In any such case, the phenomenal variation makes no difference to reasoning, report, behavior, or any other downstream functional or physical state.

(5) So phenomenal consciousness, if separated from access, is causally and epistemically inert: it produces no observable effect and supports no inference.

(6) Direct introspection, however, is itself an access mechanism — to introspect a state is precisely to render information about it globally available within the workspace.

(7) Therefore, if phenomenal consciousness is never broadcast, it cannot be introspected at all; and if it is broadcast, it collapses into access consciousness.

(8) It follows that phenomenal consciousness is either identical to access consciousness, or is a feature that could never be detected or known, even in one's own present experience.

(9) But then our ordinary conviction that experience has a subjective qualitative dimension either reduces to the claim that representations are globally broadcast — which explains nothing additional — or refers to something that plays no role in generating that conviction.

(10) Hence phenomenal consciousness either does not exist or is explanatorily empty.

My colleague takes this to show that the hard problem was ill-formed from the start. I worry instead that it undermines first-person authority about our own minds. Which step should be rejected, and what does that rejection cost? I would prefer to preserve (1) and (3) if possible, but I want to know the price of whatever move is actually required.
