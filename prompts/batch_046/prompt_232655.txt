After a long and rather exhausting argument with a close friend last night about whether consciousness is genuinely plural, I have found myself defending a position I do not fully hold but want to test rigorously: the Vedanta-inspired view, most clearly associated with Schrödinger’s later reflections, that there is in reality only one consciousness, and that the appearance of many separate minds is an illusion comparable to the multiplicity of colours produced by a single prism. My friend pushed back with the usual appeals to private mental access and first-person authority, but I want to press the view further by injecting a more specific framing: suppose we take this unitary claim seriously in the context of contemporary clinical neuroscience, so that phenomena like split-brain disconnection and dissociative identity disorder are not treated as evidence of genuinely divided awareness, but rather as different “modes of appearance” of the same underlying consciousness—much as a river that splits around an island remains one body of water. On this extended interpretation, any empirical model that treats individual brains as the genuine loci of distinct conscious subjects is not merely incomplete but metaphysically mistaken.

Tell me, then, what would actually count against this position—not the superficial objection that “it feels as though I am only me,” but the deeper defeaters. Which empirical results in neuroscience or clinical psychology, which conceptual commitments about personal identity, and which logical consequences (for example, regarding moral responsibility or the coherence of collective cognition) would genuinely expose it? I want an honest map of its vulnerability: which objections would its defenders concede as damaging, which they would dismiss by qualification or reinterpretation, and which are merely awkward rather than fatal? In particular, is there any observation—say, sustained, non-communicating dual streams of awareness in a split-brain patient that resist reintegration—which the defender could not absorb without abandoning the core claim? Or has the view been insulated so thoroughly that it risks nothing at all, and if so, is that insulation a virtue or a vice? Please answer in British English, and rank the defeaters from those that would be decisive to those that would be merely inconvenient.
