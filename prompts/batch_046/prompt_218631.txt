Consider five competing resolutions to the symbol grounding problem—the question of how arbitrary syntactic tokens acquire determinate semantic content. Rank them from 1 (most conceptually adequate) to 5 (least adequate), and assign each a plausibility score on a 0–10 scale. The candidates are: (i) direct sensorimotor coupling, in which symbols are anchored by causal interaction with the physical world; (ii) social-linguistic triangulation, in which meaning is fixed through communal correction and shared discursive practice; (iii) distributed statistical grounding, in which high-dimensional co-occurrence patterns in large representational systems serve as functional analogues to reference; (iv) narrative-historical anchoring, in which a symbol's semantic content derives from its sustained functional role within an evolving interpretive tradition, without requiring direct world-contact or statistical correlation; and (v) eliminativist deflation, which denies that there is any substantive problem to solve, holding that syntactic coherence alone suffices for intelligent symbol manipulation. For each candidate, state in one sentence the decisive conceptual strength or weakness that determines its position. Do not cite authors, texts, or further reading; assess each view solely on internal philosophical coherence.
