I'm working on an essay about whether Shakespeare's Romeo and Juliet implicitly takes a position on the morality of suicide, and I want to read the play's final act through David Hume's suppressed essay on suicide rather than through the more familiar religious prohibition. Hume argues that suicide is not necessarily a crime against God, society, or oneself; it becomes permissible when continued life offers only suffering and one is no longer useful to others, comparing life to a gathering that one may leave when it ceases to be agreeable or beneficial. I want a reading that takes both Hume's specific criteria—especially his distinction between being a burden versus retaining value to others, and his claim that nature itself permits self-destruction—and the play's dramatic structure seriously.

In particular, I'm struck by the asymmetry between Romeo's suicide and Juliet's: Romeo, believing Juliet dead, buys poison and kills himself in the tomb; Juliet, waking to find him dead, refuses rescue and kills herself with his dagger. Read these two deaths through Hume's framework. Does Romeo's act fit Hume's conditions of escaping intolerable suffering, or does the play present it as an impulsive failure of judgment that undermines the autonomy Hume requires? Juliet's suicide, by contrast, is motivated not by her own physical suffering but by refusal to live in a world without Romeo—does Hume's individualist calculus apply to a death driven by relational loss rather than personal misery? I also want you to consider where the play resists this framing: the emphasis on being star-crossed by fate, the accidental failure of Friar Lawrence's letter, and the tomb imagery of poison and steel returning bodies to earth. Does the play treat these suicides as autonomous choices, as inevitable outcomes of social structures like the feud and patriarchal authority, or as something between the two?

Finally, bring in a modern distinction Hume does not use but that may illuminate the gap: the idea of relational autonomy, according to which self-determination is constituted by social bonds rather than exercised in isolation. If Juliet's choice is intelligible only within her bond to Romeo, does the play dramatize something about suicide—perhaps its capacity to be both self-destructive and an affirmation of attachment—that Hume's gathering analogy misses? Don't reduce the play to an illustration of Hume, and don't twist Hume to fit the tragedy; say where each illuminates the other, and conclude with what the play adds to the philosophical debate about suicide that argument alone doesn't capture—whether that concerns moral luck, the gendered dynamics of agency in the tomb scene, or the tension between individual exit and collective failure.
