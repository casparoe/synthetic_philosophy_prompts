The debate between hierarchical (Tarskian) and dialetheist (Priest-style) responses to the Liar paradox has persisted for decades without genuine convergence: both sides accept the same derivation from apparently valid reasoning, both concede that unrestricted classical logic must be modified or restricted, and both have constructed technically adequate formal frameworks. Yet neither camp has persuaded the other, and the literature now cycles through refinements rather than resolutions. My working hypothesis is that the stall is not a hidden formal disagreement—say, over the admissibility of self-referential terms in a well-founded syntax—but a divergence in what counts as an adequate solution: hierarchicalists treat the paradox as a boundary problem for formal language requiring stratification, while dialetheists treat it as a revelation that a single, unstratified concept of truth can tolerate genuine contradictions, with the disagreement ultimately turning on whether ordinary-language assertions like “That statement is false” must be preserved as literally true or explained away as pragmatic misfire. Is this correct, or is the impasse better diagnosed as two groups answering different questions—one about the structure of a formal semantic theory, the other about the normative dynamics of assertion, commitment, and rational retraction—under the single heading “the Liar paradox”? Diagnose the disagreement using what each side actually commits to, particularly regarding the strengthened Liar and the principle of transparent disquotation, and specify whether any development—an inference-pattern that one framework cannot capture, a formal result about expressive power, or a conceptual distinction between assertoric stability and semantic ascent—could actually move the dispute, or whether the persistence of the stall indicates that the question is ill-posed and should be dissolved into separate problems about language and rational agency.
