I've been thinking about the debates over taking down statues and renaming streets, and I keep noticing that people on both sides reach for the same comparison: they treat a city's public monuments and named streets as if they were a museum's permanent collection. The defenders of keeping things say removal is like a museum burning its own archives or throwing away art it no longer likes; the critics say it's like a museum finally deaccessioning stolen pieces or pulling forgeries from the walls. I want to know how far this museum analogy actually carries the argument. Can you lay out the correspondence table — what plays the role of the curator, the acquisition board, the gallery walls, the voluntary visitor, the deaccession protocol, and the public funding? Then show me where it breaks: for example, street names are navigational infrastructure that residents must use to find their way home, not optional exhibits inside a building people choose to enter, and statues sit in shared democratic space rather than in an institution with membership rules. There's also something that rarely comes up in these arguments: street names don't just honor people, they function as indexical coordinates — they literally direct movement — so renaming disrupts practical orientation in a way that rehanging a painting does not. Does that navigational feature survive inside the museum frame, or does it expose a hidden assumption that public space should be curated like an elite cultural institution? Once the mapping and the breaks are on the table, tell me whether the conclusion — that cities ought to have formal, museum-style deaccession procedures for monuments and names — survives, or whether the analogy was doing all the moral work. And please say what would change your mind: which feature of public commemoration, or which argument about democratic space versus institutional curation, would lead you to reject the comparison or to think renaming is morally different from museum deaccession? Also note where your reasoning is most likely to fail.
