I am constructing a diagnostic handout for an advanced philosophy-of-language seminar focused on Donald Davidson's theory of radical interpretation, and I need a branching taxonomy—structured as a decision tree or scored quiz—that sorts substantive philosophical commitments rather than testing familiarity with the literature. The instrument should begin with concrete interpretive scenarios and move outward to named positions, so that a respondent can be mapped onto a view without ever being asked which philosopher they agree with. I want the tree to cover the logical space of how the principle of charity operates under conditions of conceptual alienness, not merely list standard interpretations.

Please build the tree so that each internal node poses a specific claim or case: for example, whether charity applies primarily to individual beliefs or only to sentences embedded in a holistic web; whether a speaker who systematically asserts contradictions in one domain but behaves adaptively elsewhere should be interpreted as holding contradictory beliefs, as being misinterpreted with respect to logical constants, or as failing to be an interpreter's target altogether; whether logical constants must be shared a priori between speaker and interpreter or can themselves be fixed by the interpretation; and whether radical interpretation describes an actual psychological process or is purely a normative reconstruction of meaning-ascription. The leaves must include: strict Davidsonian orthodoxy (charity as constitutive, massive error impossible); a Quinean indeterminacy view (charity satisfied but belief-contents underdetermined); a weak-charity revisionist (localized massive falsehood permitted if correctable); a conceptual-relativist position (genuinely incommensurable schemes allowed by relaxing logical-sharing); and a deflationary or instrumentalist stance (belief-attribution as predictive tool with no ontological commitment). For any answer-combination that yields no named view, include an unnamed leaf with a brief diagnosis—whether the position is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Flag explicitly any pair of named leaves that your tree cannot separate, and explain on which branch they collapse. Phrase every node as a judgment a graduate student could make without knowing the label of the destination, avoid all requests for bibliographies or citations, and do not ask respondents to identify specific texts or authors.
