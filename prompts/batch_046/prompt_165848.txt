I am serving as the head teaching assistant for an upper-level seminar in political philosophy centered on competing conceptions of liberty, and I am grading the take-home midterm for a cohort of advanced undergraduates. The exam presented a hypothetical municipal “predictive mobility ordinance”: an algorithmic risk-assessment system flags certain residents as “high-risk” and restricts their entry into designated public districts, but offers a voluntary behavioral-coaching module that flagged individuals may complete to restore full access. Students were asked to analyze the ordinance through Isaiah Berlin’s negative and positive liberty and through Philip Pettit’s republican liberty understood as non-domination, explaining for each conception whether the ordinance respects or violates freedom and why.

Please evaluate the following student answer, which I am treating as representative of a solid but not exceptional response. The student writes: “The ordinance clearly violates negative liberty, since it erects an external obstacle—physical exclusion from public space—for anyone the algorithm flags. The coaching module, by contrast, promotes positive liberty in Berlin’s sense: it supplies the rational discipline and self-mastery that allow people to direct their own lives rather than being driven by impulse. Republican liberty, as Pettit develops it, is essentially positive liberty relocated to the political domain—freedom from being subjected to another person’s will. In this case republican liberty is actually satisfied, not violated, because the algorithm applies uniform, transparent rules to everyone. Since the criteria are public and no official singles anyone out based on personal whim, there is no arbitrary power here; domination requires capricious interference, and this system is entirely rule-governed. The only genuine liberty concern is therefore negative liberty, which could be remedied by letting people opt out of the restriction through the coaching program.”

Grade this answer as if you were issuing the authoritative evaluation—assign a specific score out of 20 points and provide a point-by-point justification. Identify precisely where the student correctly applies the three frameworks and where the reasoning slips: in particular, check whether republican liberty is conflated with positive liberty, whether “arbitrary” power is misunderstood as mere whim or non-uniformity rather than as power that is not tracked by the interests of those subject to it, and whether the opt-out mechanism is treated too quickly as resolving the negative-liberty problem. Your assessment should read like concrete grading commentary tied to individual claims, not a broad summary, and you should not signal any prior opinion you hold about the answer’s overall quality.
