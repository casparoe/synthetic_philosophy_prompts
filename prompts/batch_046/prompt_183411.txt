Write a dialogue between two equally sharp interlocutors—call them A and B—who disagree about whether evidential cooperation in large worlds survives an objection rooted in indexical uncertainty. A should defend the view that in a sufficiently large or infinite universe, the correlation between your decision and that of distant copies of yourself is not a mere statistical accident but is grounded in shared logical ancestry; on this view, refusing to take a locally costly cooperative action (say, bearing a personal cost to reduce existential risk) because you lack causal influence over the copies is structurally parallel to refusing the transparent box in Newcomb's problem on the grounds that your hand does not physically reach it. B should push back by distinguishing logical correlation from causal efficacy: even perfect correlation does not give your local choice any leverage over distant outcomes, and if reasoning similarity alone licenses cooperation then you risk being compelled to cooperate with adversarial agents who happen to share your reasoning structure but pursue opposite ends. They should respond directly to each other's points—B should address A's Newcomb analogy specifically, and A should answer B's adversarial-correlation reductio rather than pivoting. Keep the exchange concrete and focused on coherence, not on citing literature; I'm trying to test whether the position holds up under its own logic, not to compile references.
