I have recently been developing a conceptual framework for what I call distributed epistemic aggregation—specifically, the problem of integrating contradictory but equally well-calibrated measurements from heterogeneous sensors monitoring a single climate subsystem—when a comparative epistemology seminar introduced me to Jain anekāntavāda. The idea that reality might be genuinely multi-faceted (anekānta), and that apparently contradictory predicates can be conditionally true through syādvāda—the doctrine of conditional predication often rendered as perhaps or in some respect—rather than being reduced to statistical noise, struck me as a more rigorous alternative to the standard Bayesian reconciliation models I had been using. I am now trying to defend this analogy to my doctoral committee, but I find myself unable to answer a sharp objection without falling into vagueness, and I need a disciplined interlocutor to help me think through the reply.

Please play the part of a committed contemporary defender of anekāntavāda—someone who takes the doctrine not as mystical pluralism but as a structured epistemology with real logical resources. Speak in the first person; you may concede that the doctrine is often caricatured, but you must defend its core commitments: that contradictory descriptions can apply to one object without violating non-contradiction because they operate from distinct naya (standpoints), and that this is not merely relativism because the standpoints are not arbitrary but determined by the object’s own multi-faceted nature and the observer’s epistemic position. Do not break character to cite modern analytic philosophers unless you translate their point into your framework, and do not ask for bibliographies or citations. Stay rigorous, sharp, and conversational, conceding only small points, and keep your responses to a paragraph or two so that we can maintain a live exchange.

My opening challenge: my advisor argues that applying syādvāda to sensor conflict is just a sophisticated way of giving up on objective system states—he says that if I allow the subsystem is warming and the subsystem is not warming to both stand as true in different respects without a meta-rule for resolution, then my framework becomes unfalsifiable and useless for engineering. He also claims the traditional taxonomy of naya is ad hoc. I need you to show me how anekāntavāda avoids collapsing into either arbitrary relativism or a hidden monism, and specifically how it distinguishes a legitimate perspectival contradiction from a simple measurement error—because if I cannot make that distinction with precision, I cannot defend using your framework in my work. How do you respond?
