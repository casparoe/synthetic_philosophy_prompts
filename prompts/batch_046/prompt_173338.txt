I am giving the opening remarks for a one-day interdisciplinary workshop at a science museum's education center, co-hosted by philosophers of cognitive science and curators of an interactive artificial-intelligence exhibit. The room is split: half the audience are specialists who have worked through Dennett's distinction among the physical, design, and intentional stances and his account of real patterns; the other half are museum educators and machine-learning practitioners who think about AI through user interaction, predictive accuracy, and exhibit design rather than through philosophy of mind. The workshop asks whether contemporary large language models and predictive systems ever genuinely warrant the intentional stance, or whether they remain confined to the design or physical levels.

The philosophical framework must do structural work in the address, not serve as ornament. Because the framework distinguishes real patterns that license intentional explanation from those that only license design-level prediction, it should determine exactly what I can and cannot say about the museum's own interactive exhibits. I must avoid unqualified assertions that the models "have beliefs" or "understand language," since those claims invoke the intentional stance without the predictive robustness and counterfactual stability that real patterns at that level require; at the same time, I must avoid reducing the systems to mere physical computation, since that erases the genuinely predictive regularities that the design stance captures and that the exhibits actually demonstrate. To keep the question from being a retread of standard AI ethics debates, I want to inject a somewhat niche framing: compare the workshop's central problem to Dennett's under-discussed example of a chess-playing computer that exhibits intentional-level patterns without intentionality, but update it specifically for generative systems that produce novel linguistic outputs rather than selecting from pre-programmed move trees, asking whether output novelty changes which stance is warranted.

Please write a ten-minute opening address. Structure your response in four titled sections in this order: (1) The Welcome and Frame (about two minutes of speaking time) — establish the museum context and the split audience without talking down to either group; (2) The Conceptual Scaffold (about three minutes) — present the three stances and the criterion for real patterns in terms accessible to educators and precise enough for philosophers, using the updated chess-computer-to-generative-system analogy; (3) The Structural Constraint (about three minutes) — articulate precisely which metaphors and research questions are licensed or forbidden by the framework, and show how this shapes the workshop's agenda; (4) The Transition (about two minutes) — close with an invitation that respects the boundary between design-level and intentional-level explanation without dismissing either audience's concerns. Throughout, let the stances determine what can be said rather than using them as decorative references.
