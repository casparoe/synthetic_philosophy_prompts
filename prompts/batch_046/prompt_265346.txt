I'm a doctoral student in philosophy of mind, and in our departmental working group two colleagues—Riley and Sam—have reached an impasse over whether dreams constitute experiences, specifically in light of Malcolm's conceptual argument and Dennett's cassette theory of dream recall. I am reporting their exchange as a bystander; I do not want a reading list, citations, or bibliographic guidance, but a point-by-point adjudication of the arguments exactly as they made them, including what both parties are neglecting. Here is the back-and-forth.

Riley opened by challenging Malcolm's claim that it is conceptually incoherent to report having been in pain during a dream. She granted that pain-ascription requires behavioral criteria, but argued Malcolm conflates observer-accessible behavior with the first-person conditions for applying the concept. She noted that sleeping subjects do exhibit micro-behaviors—facial grimacing, vocalizations, limb movements—that satisfy those criteria in a subdued register. More specifically, she introduced protocols involving forced micro-arousal during REM: subjects awakened abruptly can report specific phenomenal qualities—localized pressure, thermal shifts—that differ systematically from their morning retrospective narratives. For Riley, this indicates a genuine phenomenal state present during sleep that is subsequently reconstructed, not fabricated ex nihilo as Dennett's cassette theory might suggest.

Sam replied that Riley misreads Malcolm. Malcolm's argument, he said, concerns not whether an observer can detect pain-behavior, but whether the sleeping subject possesses the conceptual framework to apply pain to themselves when the behavioral criteria grounding the concept are suspended. Even if micro-behaviors occur, the sleeper cannot integrate them into a self-attributive judgment. Sam then extended Dennett's idea beyond morning recall to what he called hypnagogic fragments—brief, non-narrative sensory images at sleep onset. He argued these are not experiences in the philosophically relevant sense because they lack temporal integration and self-attribution. The prefrontal deactivation characteristic of REM, he added, means no integrative mechanism is available even in principle; hence there is no subject for whom a state could be present, and morning reports are confabulatory reconstructions triggered by arousal itself.

Riley countered that Sam smuggles in a substantive theory of experience—requiring temporal integration and self-attribution—under the guise of Malcolm's conceptual point. She asked why brief subliminal perceptual flashes in waking life count as experiences if REM micro-states do not. If a subject can, upon attention, integrate a subliminal flash into the stream of consciousness, she argued, then abrupt arousal reports in REM should count analogously: there is something present to be reported immediately, not reconstructed later. The burden, she insisted, is on Sam to justify sleep-specific suspension without begging the question against phenomenal presence.

Sam responded that the subliminal-flash analogy fails precisely because of the deactivation point. In waking life, integrative mechanisms remain online; attention can retrospectively bind the flash into consciousness. During REM those mechanisms are offline, so there is no in-principle availability for integration. The micro-arousal reports, he concluded, are false memories generated by the awakening protocol—analogous to how leading questions generate false visual reports—rather than veridical disclosures of prior phenomenal states.

Please adjudicate step by step: on the interpretation of Malcolm's behavioral-criterion argument; on whether micro-arousal reports genuinely undermine Dennett's cassette theory; on the legitimacy of the subliminal-flash analogy; and on whether prefrontal deactivation establishes the absence of a subject for experience. For each point, indicate who is closer to being right and identify a specific consideration both are neglecting. Finally, say whether their disagreement is primarily conceptual, empirical, or metaphysical, and whether either position can accommodate the other party's strongest point without contradiction.
