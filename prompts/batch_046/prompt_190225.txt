I hold a reinterpretation of coherent extrapolated volition that breaks with the standard individualist framing: rather than treating CEV as the endpoint of a single agent’s own preferences after idealization and coherence-building, I understand it as an intersubjective bargaining equilibrium—specifically, as the outcome that would emerge from applying a Nash-style weighted bargaining procedure across the idealized preference sets of all affected agents, where each agent’s contribution is weighted by the intensity and legitimacy of her affected interests. On this view, an individual’s “extrapolated volition” is not sovereign but is instead her fair share in a collective outcome, and there is no special authority granted to the temporal slices of one agent over others. What would count decisively against this collective-bargaining interpretation? I want the arguments and evidence that should move me, sorted by how decisive they would be: conceptual objections (does the notion of volition collapse if it is no longer tied to a single will?), normative objections (does this erase the moral significance of autonomy or self-authorship?), practical objections (is the bargaining procedure determinable or computable in principle, given that idealized preferences may be incommensurable?), and comparative objections (does this reinterpretation make CEV indistinguishable from contractualist or Rawlsian frameworks, and if so, does it lose whatever distinctive normative work CEV was meant to do?). Please include what defenders of the standard individualist CEV would themselves concede as weaknesses of the collective version, not only criticisms from external opponents, and assess whether the view risks being qualified into unfalsifiability—for instance, by redefining “affected interests” so broadly that any outcome can be accommodated. If the position is well insulated, say so and whether that insulation is a virtue or a defect for a view of this kind.
