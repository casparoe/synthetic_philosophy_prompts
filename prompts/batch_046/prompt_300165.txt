I am a doctoral student in philosophy of social science, and I have been tracking a long-running dispute about whether demographic cohorts—Baby Boomers, Generation X, Millennials, Generation Z—constitute real social kinds or are instead institutional fictions with no genuine ontological weight. The debate has persisted for decades without resolution. Proponents of the “real kind” view, often drawing on demographic sociology, argue that shared historical location produces durable, projectible dispositions—attitudes toward risk, authority, or technology—that survive controls for age and period, and that these patterns justify treating cohorts as explanatory entities. Critics, emphasizing the age-period-cohort identification problem and substantial within-cohort variance, counter that birth-year boundaries are arbitrarily fixed, that statistical models cannot uniquely identify cohort effects, and that “generation” functions primarily as a marketing or policy heuristic rather than as a category that carves social reality at its joints.

What makes the stall puzzling is that both sides appear to agree on the relevant evidence—large-scale survey data showing attitudinal clustering, the underidentification of APC models, the institutional reality of generational labeling in census and commercial contexts—and yet neither camp has revised its core position. My working hypothesis is that the disagreement is not primarily metaphysical but methodological and verbal: one side is asking whether cohort membership explains residual variance in social attitudes after controlling for age and period, while the other is asking whether the category has non-arbitrary membership conditions and causal homogeneity. If so, they are answering different questions under the single heading “Are generations real kinds?”

I want an honest diagnosis rather than a survey of positions. Is this impasse a substantive disagreement about social ontology—perhaps about whether social kinds require causal mechanisms, predictive projectibility, or institutional recognition to be “real”? Is it a verbal dispute about the word “kind,” with one side using a metaphysically loaded criterion and the other a pragmatic one? Or is there a hidden formal disagreement—say, about what counts as an adequate explanation in cohort analysis—that neither side has made explicit? To push the diagnosis further, I have been considering a distinction that may or may not help: between “interval generations,” defined by fixed birth-year ranges, and “indexical generations,” defined by shared orientation to a specific formative event regardless of birth year. Would applying this distinction dissolve the dispute, reveal that the sides are talking past each other, or merely rename the same boundary problem?

Please separate what is settled—such as the statistical underidentification of pure cohort effects in standard APC frameworks, or the fact that generational labels are institutionally operative in policy and marketing—from what remains genuinely contested among scholars who work on this. Do not present a contested view about social ontology or demographic method as the consensus. And given your diagnosis, tell me what would actually count as progress: a new formal result in cohort identification, a conceptual distinction that clarifies membership conditions, a change in the question being asked, or an argument that the ontological question is ill-posed and should be abandoned?
