I recently read a summary in a popular, unnamed introduction to British empiricism—written for a general audience, not a scholarly monograph—that characterized Berkeley's master argument and its surrounding metaphysics roughly as follows. The summary states that Berkeley begins by observing that we never perceive anything except our own ideas, from which he infers that the notion of "matter" existing outside every mind is literally unthinkable: if you try to imagine a tree in a park with no observer present, you are still framing that tree as an object of your own imagination, which proves it exists in a mind. From this, the summary concludes, Berkeley derives that matter is not merely unknown but impossible, and that physical objects are therefore best understood as private hallucinations that happen to be shared among human observers. To prevent the world from vanishing whenever people close their eyes, the summary continues, Berkeley introduces God as a kind of cosmic security camera—an eternal backup perceiver who keeps watching everything—so that trees remain in existence between human observations. Finally, the summary dismisses the argument as circular: it claims Berkeley assumes from the outset that only minds and ideas can exist (esse est percipi), then uses that assumption to prove matter cannot exist, which makes the master argument a petitio principii disguised as an insight about the limits of imagination.

Is God's role correctly described as the eternal ground of archetypal ideas, or is the "security camera" framing a misleading reduction of his theological metaphysics? Does the charge of circularity stick if we carefully separate the master argument's premises about conceivability from its conclusion about the impossibility of unperceived existence? And does the summary's final claim—that Berkeley has shown matter to be impossible—actually follow from the accurate parts of the account, or does it depend on the misrepresentations?
