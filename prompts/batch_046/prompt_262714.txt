I am writing the final examination for an upper-level philosophy of science seminar that has spent several weeks on explanatory reduction, emergence, and Daniel Dennett’s critique of greedy reductionism through the cranes-and-skyhooks framework. The students are familiar with the basic distinction—cranes build complexity incrementally from the ground up, while skyhooks invoke unexplained top-down powers—but I want a question that cannot be answered by reciting that taxonomy. Instead, it should test whether they can apply the framework to a contested intermediate case in evolutionary biology and recognize where the conceptual pressure points actually lie.

Please draft one essay question suitable for a 90-minute exam, carrying 30 marks, aimed at students who have encountered Dennett’s arguments but have not specialized in philosophy of biology. The question should present developmental niche construction—the idea that organisms actively modify their selective environments across generations, thereby feeding back into evolutionary dynamics—not as a topic to be summarized, but as a test case. Specifically, students should be required to determine whether niche construction can be reconstructed as a legitimate ontological crane (incrementally building evolutionary complexity without invoking downward causation), whether it functions merely as an epistemic scaffold (a useful explanatory strategy that carries no ontological commitment), or whether it secretly operates as a skyhook by presupposing that environmental structures possess irreducible normative or causal powers over genetic change. The prompt must also compel them to distinguish this evaluation from a mere instance of greedy reductionism—the error of insisting that all biological explanation must eventually bottom out in molecular physics—so that they see why avoiding reduction is not automatically the same as avoiding a skyhook.

Along with the question, provide a model answer of roughly 800 words that demonstrates the specific conceptual moves a top answer must contain: a precise characterization of greedy reductionism as an epistemic mistake rather than an ontological claim; a clear separation of ontological cranes from explanatory scaffolds; an analysis of niche construction that addresses the feedback-loop objection without collapsing into group-selectionist downward causation; and a concluding judgment on whether the framework survives scrutiny. Then include a marking scheme that allocates the 30 marks across five must-hit moves, assigns specific point values to each, and notes—in one line per criterion—the characteristic error that costs marks (for instance, conflating anti-reductionism with anti-physicalism, or treating any feedback loop as a skyhook). Do not ask for bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; the evaluation should depend entirely on conceptual precision and argumentative structure.
