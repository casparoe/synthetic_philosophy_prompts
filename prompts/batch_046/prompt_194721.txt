I am drafting a literary novel centered on a retired mathematical logician who, in her later years, becomes obsessed with the boundary between formal proof and human insight; in preparing her interior monologues I have found myself drawn into a debate that seems impervious to resolution, and I want to understand its anatomy rather than declare a winner. The dispute concerns whether Gödel's first incompleteness theorem places any genuine constraint on mechanistic or algorithmic accounts of human mathematical cognition. One side—echoing a line of argument associated with Lucas and later developed by Penrose—maintains that because any consistent formal system adequate for arithmetic must contain true but unprovable statements, and because human mathematicians appear capable of recognizing precisely such truths, the human mind cannot be exhaustively modeled as a single fixed algorithmic framework. The opposing camp, composed largely of working logicians and philosophers of mathematics, responds that this reasoning collapses into a category mistake: the theorem applies only to precisely defined, consistent axiomatic structures with a fixed Gödel numbering, not to fluid, biologically instantiated, adaptively revising cognitive processes, and that no valid bridge has ever been constructed from formal incompleteness to cognitive transcendence. For years the exchange has cycled through seminars and published exchanges without movement; both sides accept the mathematical content of the theorem, both accept that humans solve open problems, and yet the positions remain frozen.

What kind of disagreement is this, actually? Is there a concealed empirical question about whether neural architectures implement closed formal systems? Is it partly verbal—hinging on whether the term system should be restricted to static axiom sets with explicit syntax, or whether an open-ended, self-modifying neural network qualifies? Or does the stall reveal a clash of bedrock intuitions about whether logical self-reference bears any substantive analogy to cognitive self-awareness, such that neither camp can argue for its starting point without begging the question against the other? I have begun to suspect that the impasse is driven by tacitly incompatible notions of formalization itself: one camp treating consistency and closure as static properties essential to Gödelian applicability, the other treating human reasoning as inherently open-ended in ways that might genuinely evade a fixed Gödel numbering. I offer this only as a tentative hypothesis.
