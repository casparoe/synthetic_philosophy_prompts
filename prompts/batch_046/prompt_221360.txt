I've been working through an argument about illusionism that I first sketched in response to a forum thread, and the more I formalize it the more suspicious it becomes, because the conclusion seems to swallow the premises that make reasoning possible. Here is the structure. (1) Illusionism about phenomenal consciousness denies that there are phenomenal properties; instead, there are representational states that systematically misrepresent themselves as having qualitative character. (2) If a cognitive mechanism can systematically misrepresent its own outputs as possessing some property P, then first-person reports mediated by that mechanism are unreliable specifically with respect to P. (3) The mechanisms underlying first-person reports of experience are not informationally encapsulated; they recruit the same predictive-processing architectures and high-level self-modeling resources that generate reports about beliefs, intentions, and reasoning steps. (4) Therefore, unless we can draw a principled boundary, consistency demands that we treat these shared mechanisms as unreliable not only about phenomenal properties but also about the propositional-attitude properties they report. (5) Yet no such boundary is available: both phenomenal reports and belief reports are reconstructive, depend on introspective access to representational contents rather than on direct transparency, and can be experimentally dissociated from the underlying states they purport to describe. (6) Taken consistently, illusionism thus collapses into a global unreliability thesis about first-person mental content, which in turn undermines the very belief-formation and practical-reasoning processes that illusionists themselves rely on. (7) Because self-undermining skepticism is unacceptable, illusionism must be rejected. I feel the slip is somewhere between (3) and (5), perhaps an equivocation on what reliability requires, or an unjustified homogeneity assumption about introspective mechanisms, but I can't pin it down precisely. Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? In particular, can an illusionist defend an asymmetry between phenomenal and doxastic reporting without smuggling in the kind of privileged access they typically deny? I'd prefer to preserve (1) and (7) if the logic allows, but I'm prepared to see that (3) or (5) carries the hidden weight.
