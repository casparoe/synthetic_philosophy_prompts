I am drafting an essay on the metaphysical implications of Erwin Schrödinger’s later philosophical writings, specifically his engagement with Advaita Vedānta, and I would welcome your critical comments on both its accuracy and its conceptual architecture. My central claim is that Schrödinger’s well-known endorsement of the “unity of consciousness” is usually read either as a mystical coda to quantum mechanics or as a straightforward metaphysical monism, but that a more defensible reconstruction treats it as an epistemic topology: the thesis that quantum-mechanical observer-dependence requires a single, non-localized subject of experience, while allowing that subject to sustain internally differentiated phenomenal states without collapsing into solipsism. On this reading, the Vedāntic identification of Ātman and Brahman functions not as an ontological reduction of multiplicity to illusion, but as a structural constraint on any interpretation of superposition—namely, that if measurement outcomes are genuinely relational to an observing consciousness, and if consciousness is numerically one, then the apparent plurality of observers must be accounted for as modal differentiation within that unity rather than as genuinely distinct mental substances.

I develop this by distinguishing, in Schrödinger’s texts, between numerical identity of consciousness (which I take him to affirm consistently) and qualitative identity of mental content (which he clearly denies, given his acceptance of distinct individual experiences). I then argue that this distinction lets him avoid both the hidden-variable commitments he rejected and the naïve idealism that treats wavefunction collapse as literally caused by discrete personal minds. Instead, collapse—or rather, the appearance of definite outcomes—emerges as the dissolution of phenomenal boundaries within a unitary field of awareness, structurally analogous to the Vedāntic notion of māyā as superimposition upon an undivided ground. I am aware that this risks conflating Schrödinger’s scientific agnosticism about interpretation with a positive metaphysical system, so I try to restrict the argument to what his epistemological premises commit him to, rather than attributing to him a fully worked-out Vedāntic ontology.

Could you comment on the following? First, is my distinction between numerical and qualitative identity of consciousness textually supportable in Schrödinger’s essays, or am I importing a distinction from contemporary philosophy of mind that distorts his actual concerns? Second, does my reconstruction of the “epistemic topology” avoid implicitly attributing to him a hidden-variable or non-local hidden-mind ontology that conflicts with his explicit rejection of Bohm-style supplements? Third, does the analogy to māyā hold up without reducing Schrödinger’s view to a merely poetic overlay on quantum formalism—specifically, can one maintain that observer-dependence genuinely requires unitary consciousness without sliding into the claim that physical multiplicity is literally illusory? I am particularly interested in whether the argument survives correction of its metaphysical overreach, and whether there are internal tensions in Schrödinger’s own writings—perhaps between his biological naturalism in earlier work and his later monistic turn—that undermine the coherence of the position I am attributing to him. Any suggestions on sharpening the conceptual machinery, or on points where the essay overstates what can be extracted from the primary texts, would be most helpful.
