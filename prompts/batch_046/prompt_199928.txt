I have been working through how multi-level polities constrain central overreach, and I keep encountering what appears to be a single mechanism operating under different institutional names. In the European Union, Article 5 TEU pairs subsidiarity with proportionality, and the Court of Justice reviews whether Union legislation is genuinely necessary because member states cannot sufficiently achieve the objectives at the national or regional level. By contrast, in the United States, the Tenth Amendment reserves powers to the states, yet federal courts rarely invalidate statutes on the ground that states could regulate the matter more efficiently; review centers on whether the activity falls within an enumerated power, particularly under the Commerce Clause. Germany’s Basic Law and the Swiss Federal Constitution both treat subsidiarity as an organizational principle, but neither applies a judicial efficiency-test comparable to the EU’s. Given all this, my question is whether American federalism is structurally defective for lacking a proportionality-style review that asks whether federal intervention is actually necessary, or whether there is a deeper reason—perhaps rooted in the difference between conferred powers in an international organization and reserved sovereignty within a single state—why the EU’s two-stage subsidiarity-plus-proportionality framework could not be imported into US preemption doctrine without violating the Supremacy Clause. More specifically, if the point of subsidiarity is genuinely to assign authority to the lowest competent level, why does US constitutional law treat the question as one of categorical enumeration rather than comparative institutional capacity?
