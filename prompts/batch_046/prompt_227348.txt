I am a retired experimental physicist who, since leaving the laboratory, has begun attending a philosophy workshop on collective action and moral responsibility. We have been examining the inefficacy objection—why individual choices in climate mitigation, dietary consumption, or democratic voting appear causally impotent, and whether that undermines obligation—but I find the discussions repetitive, always returning to the same bucket-of-water analogies. I should like a classroom activity that makes the objection’s cognitive pull visceral, yet avoids those well-worn tropes by embedding a more precise conceptual structure.

Please design a structured, 90-minute simulation for 14 to 18 participants in a seminar room, with no preparatory reading required. The setting is a single-session workshop in which participants experience token-level causal inefficacy within a system of hidden, non-linear thresholds. Frame the harm-generation mechanism as a step-level collective bad: individual contributions are quantitatively continuous, but the moral and causal status of the collective outcome shifts discontinuously once an invisible boundary is crossed, and participants operate under epistemic opacity about others’ concurrent choices. Include: (1) a ruleset with real, small stakes—points that determine an actual minor collective reward or penalty, not hypothetical currency—plus role allocation; (2) a round-by-round timeline featuring secret binary choices over five rounds, a hidden aggregate threshold revealed only after round three, and one forced deliberation point where participants must vote on whether to enter a binding subgroup covenant before knowing the final outcome; (3) a debrief protocol that maps specific moments onto conceptual distinctions—token versus type efficacy, overdetermination, and expressive versus instrumental rationality—without lecturing, and that predicts what will characteristically go wrong when the activity is run (for instance, participants conflating the covenant’s coordination effect with a change in physical impact) together with how the facilitator should correct it; and (4) an honest assessment of which feature of real-world collective harm—such as temporal dispersion across generations, irreversibility, or the absence of any central coordinator—this exercise necessarily fails to reproduce, and how the facilitator must supply that missing element verbally. To keep the design fresh, inject the niche idea that efficacy here should be analysed not as counterfactual dependence on a single act, but as membership in a structurally overdetermined set whose boundary conditions are themselves opaque to the members.
