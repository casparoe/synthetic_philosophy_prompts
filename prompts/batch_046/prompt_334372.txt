Modern rich countries have basically solved the economic problem Keynes talked about—productivity per worker is way up, we could in principle work far less—but instead of cutting hours, we keep raising retirement ages, tying health insurance to full-time jobs, and measuring national success by GDP growth and labor-force participation. At the same time, we run public education campaigns saying follow your passion and treat unemployment as a psychological crisis that needs therapy and retraining, not just income support. What view of the good life does that combo presuppose? Does it assume work is constitutively good for human flourishing, or just instrumentally necessary? And is it consistant with Keynes's idea that once scarcity is beaten, teh real problem is how to live wisely and agreeably—meaning work should shrink to maybe 15 hours and the rest should go to art, friendship, whatever? Or does the practice actually depend on a darker anthropology, like that people without paid jobs fall into vice unless structured by markets? Reconstruct the implicit theory, say whether it's one coherent view or just path-dependent mess, and tell me what a retirement/pension system built on Keynes's framing of abundance and leisure would actually have to look like—would it credit unpaid care, artistic practice, or just reduce the workweek across the board?
