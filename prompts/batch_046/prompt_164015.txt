Consider Kripke's skeptical argument about rule-following in Wittgenstein on Rules and Private Language. A philosopher claims that the skeptical solution avoids the paradox by replacing truth-conditional semantics with assertibility conditions based on communal agreement, but that this makes the normative force of rules merely descriptive of what the community happens to do, leaving no room for individual error. Another philosopher responds that this misreads Kripke because the skeptical solution retains normative force precisely through the asymmetry between first-person avowals and third-person attributions. Evaluate this exchange. In your answer: (a) explain the skeptical paradox and why it threatens both mental-state and dispositional accounts; (b) clarify what the skeptical solution actually substitutes for meaning-facts; (c) assess whether the first philosopher's charge of descriptive reduction is fair, paying attention to the distinction between justification conditions and truth conditions; and (d) discuss whether the second philosopher's appeal to asymmetry resolves the normative problem or merely relocates it.

Here is the student answer:

"(a) The skeptical paradox arises because any finite mental state or behavioral disposition is compatible with indefinitely many rules — for example, adding 2 versus adding 2 after reaching 1000 (the quus function). So no fact about me now determines that I meant addition rather than quaddition. This undermines both the view that meaning is an inner mental state and the view that meaning is just my disposition to respond, since both leave the future applications undetermined.

(b) The skeptical solution accepts that there are no meaning-facts. Instead of asking whether my response is true to some independent rule, we ask whether I am warranted in asserting it. Warrant comes from communal agreement: I am correct if my answer matches what others in my linguistic community do. So assertibility conditions replace truth conditions.

(c) The first philosopher is essentially right. By making correctness depend on what the community happens to do, Kripke collapses meaning into sociology. This is basically Dummett's verificationist anti-realism applied to rules: there is no transcendent fact, only communal practice. The charge of descriptive reduction is fair because if the community all started quadding, that would become correct. So the normative 'ought' is just a statistical 'is'.

(d) The second philosopher is correct that asymmetry saves normativity. In first-person cases, I just express my inclination without justification; in third-person cases, the community corrects me. This asymmetry means there is a real distinction between correct and incorrect use, because the community's judgment serves as an objective standard. Therefore, the skeptical solution does retain normative force, and the first philosopher's worry is resolved."
