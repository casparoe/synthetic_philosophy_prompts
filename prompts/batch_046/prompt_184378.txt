After a long argument with a friend about whether evolutionary biologists explain too many traits as adaptations, I need a practical protocol. I work for a regional museum's taxonomy unit and must classify morphological traits in our database as either adaptation or spandrel for exhibit design; I have no molecular budget and thirty minutes per specimen. Write a reusable decision procedure my curators can follow: the ordered questions, what answers are sufficient at each step, the stopping rules for labeling a trait a spandrel versus an adaptation, and the explicit points where judgment must replace the procedure because the distinction collapses into values or unresolved biology. Ground the steps in the Gould-Lewontin spandrels critique—particularly the requirement for direct selection evidence, the separation of current utility from historical origin, and the risk of developmental coupling—without requiring genetic data. Include a brief checklist of quick checks. Then apply the procedure to two traits: the elongated neck vertebrae of a long-necked browsing mammal, where intuition strongly favors adaptation but the procedure should expose hidden vulnerabilities, and the iridescent throat patch of a male songbird that also functions as a warning signal in toxic relatives, where the procedure should reach a conclusion that clashes with the obvious adaptive story. Be explicit about misfires, especially when a trait is both a spandrel and an exaptation or when selection and architectural constraint are inseparable. Conclude with a single line beginning Verdict: identifying the one unavoidable locus of human judgment.
