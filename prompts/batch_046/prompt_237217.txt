Write a deadpan parody of an internal corporate "Employee Wellness & Continuity Handbook" distributed at a factory that has achieved what the document terms "General Intellect Integration" — a fictional system in which machinery, predictive algorithms, and biometric monitoring have merged into a single cognitive infrastructure that manages both production and worker welfare. Format the response in numbered sections (e.g., 1. Onboarding and Historical Competency, 2. Skill-Maintenance Rituals, 3. Rest-Period Optimization, 4. Grievance Resolution Through System Consensus) and maintain the anodyne, therapeutic register of contemporary HR documentation throughout. In each section, quietly translate a concrete element of Marx's analysis of technology and automation under capitalism into corporate euphemism: deskilling presented as "preservation of craft memory," the drive to extract relative surplus value framed as "shared efficiency dividends," the reserve army of labor reconceived as an "on-demand talent ecosystem," and the subsumption of labor processes under machinery described as "seamless human-system collaboration" — all without naming Marx, citing texts, or breaking voice. The satire should exaggerate real features of Marx's account (that machinery is adopted not for human ease but to reduce labor costs and discipline workers, that skilled labor is decomposed into simple operations, that technology embodies collective knowledge captured as capital) rather than invent spurious ones; include at least one invented policy that sounds benevolent on its surface but, read closely, reveals that workers have become appendages of an autonomous production logic. The target is the discursive practice of using wellness, empowerment, and "human-centered" language to obscure structural domination, together with the Marxist argument that under capitalism technological development serves accumulation rather than flourishing. Approximately 700 words. After the handbook — separated by a clear heading or break — provide a brief analytical note distinguishing what is broadly settled in Marx's writings on machinery and automation (for example, that capital introduces technology primarily to cheapen labor and extend control) from what remains genuinely contested among scholars (for example, whether Marx believed automation necessarily produces immiseration, whether he anticipated a liberatory post-capitalist deployment of the same machinery, and how the "Fragment on Machines" should be read in relation to Volume 1 of Capital). The response should begin with the handbook text itself, with no preface, no framing commentary, and no restatement of this prompt.
