I have drafted the following notes for an essay on coherent extrapolated volition and the architecture of idealized preferences. The central claim is that rigorous idealization does not merely refine an agent’s preferences but dissolves the boundary between preference and agent-identity, producing what I call the mirror problem: the extrapolated volition reflects a universal rational structure rather than the particular agent’s will. I argue that if we resist smuggling particularistic attachments back into the model, the result is not a single coherent volition but an equilibrium point in a multi-agent preference space, which undermines the normative authority that CEV is supposed to carry for the original agent. I further propose that treating idealized preferences as bargaining outcomes rather than as discovered internal states resolves certain coherence issues but sacrifices the motivational transparency that makes extrapolation attractive. The essay distinguishes between preference architecture—the functional organization of what an agent wants under full information—and agent architecture—the structural conditions that make those wants belong to that agent. Please comment on the internal consistency of this distinction, on whether the mirror problem genuinely follows from the premises of idealized preference theory or depends on a hidden confusion between epistemic improvement and ontological transformation, and on whether my move to a multi-agent equilibrium analysis is a legitimate extension of the framework or an illegitimate change of subject. I am particularly interested in whether the argument illegitimately assumes that coherence across time requires identity across possible worlds, and in whether there is a defensible intermediate position that preserves both the universality of rational extrapolation and the particularity of the agent without collapsing into practical dualism.
