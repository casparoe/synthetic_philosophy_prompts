For a column I'm writing on philosophy in fiction, read William Golding’s Lord of the Flies through the is-ought gap and Moore’s naturalistic fallacy, using a framing that treats the conch and Piggy’s spectacles not merely as symbols of civilization but as fragile institutional mechanisms that try to derive prescriptive rules from the boys’ shared descriptive situation—who may speak, who may see clearly. Where does the novel slide from describing their descent into violence to implying that strong authority is therefore necessary? Does it commit the fallacy when Ralph’s democratic assemblies collapse into Simon’s murder, or does it resist that slide—perhaps in Simon’s encounter with the beast or in Ralph’s final weeping—by showing that recognizing human cruelty does not automatically license any particular political remedy? Focus especially on the assembly rules, the killing of Simon, and the naval officer’s arrival: does that ending close the gap by validating adult authority through the boys’ failure, or does it leave the gap exposed, with the officer’s irony undercutting any normative lesson? Be faithful in both directions: do not reduce Golding to a conservative parable, and do not distort the philosophical problem to fit the plot; point to specific scenes an honest critic must admit resist your reading. Finally, say what would change your mind—whether a different interpretation of the officer’s irony or of Simon’s vision—and identify where your reasoning is most vulnerable, particularly if you over-interpret the conch as a normative device rather than a narrative one.
