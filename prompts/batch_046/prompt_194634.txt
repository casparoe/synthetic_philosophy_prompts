I'm preparing a feature for a science-and-ideas publication on how classical moral philosophy is being taught in interdisciplinary graduate seminars that bridge ethics, institutional design, and cognitive science. During interviews with faculty, I was shown an exam from a seminar titled "Agent-Relative Reasons and Integrity," which asks students to apply Bernard Williams's integrity objection—including his observation that certain moral justifications involve "one thought too many"—to both the classic interpersonal case and a system-level analog in emergency allocation protocols. The exam prompt reads: "Explain Bernard Williams's integrity objection to impartialist moral theories, focusing on his claim that some justifications contain 'one thought too many.' Illustrate with the drowning-wife scenario, then evaluate whether the same structural problem arises if an institutional triage algorithm encodes relational proximity as a weighted factor in its utility function."

One student responded as follows: "Williams's integrity objection is that utilitarianism and Kantian ethics both force agents to step back from their own commitments and justify actions by principles that apply to everyone. In the classic case, a man saves his wife from drowning rather than a stranger. If he saves her because he calculated that his wife's survival maximizes overall happiness—including his own emotional well-being—then he has introduced an unnecessary justification. As Williams puts it, this is 'one thought too many'; he should have saved her simply because she is his wife, with no appeal to utility. This shows that moral life requires space for projects and attachments that are not derived from impartial rules. For this reason, Williams is best understood as a virtue ethicist who rejects consequentialist reasoning altogether in personal relationships. Turning to the institutional analog, if a hospital triage algorithm assigns extra weight to patients who are close relatives of staff, this seems to reproduce the husband's reasoning at a systemic level. Because the algorithm applies the weight uniformly, no individual agent introduces an extra thought—the proximity factor is built into the protocol. Therefore, the algorithm avoids Williams's objection. The system acts partially without any particular person violating impartiality, which suggests that institutional design can solve the tension between integrity and fairness that individuals face." I am grading this response as an external examiner and should not signal my own assessment. Please provide a precise numerical grade out of 10, followed by specific, itemized comments that justify the score point by point. In particular, address whether the answer correctly distinguishes agent-relative reasons from mere partiality, whether it accurately identifies Williams's target (impartialist ethics rather than all forms of reasoning), whether it mischaracterizes his broader philosophical commitments, and whether its analysis of the institutional analog avoids conflating individual integrity with procedural design. Deliver your evaluation as a point-by-point assessment rather than a general impression.
