In philosophy of science circles, people keep invoking Lakatos’s slogan that a research programme is progressive only if it predicts genuinely novel facts, usually to dismiss a theory as degenerating because it merely accommodates data after the fact. Before I use that line in an argument, I want it precisely disambiguated. What are the distinct theses the slogan could express? At minimum, distinguish: whether “novel” means not yet observed, not used in constructing the theory, or not derivable within rival programmes; whether “predict” requires strict temporal priority or allows risky post-hoc derivation; whether progressiveness is binary or graded; and whether the criterion applies to the hard core, the protective belt, or only to specific auxiliary hypotheses. Lay these readings out as a menu from the trivial to the radical, flag which ones actually license branding a theory degenerating for ad hoc adjustments, and note which readings collapse into one another. Then address this specific tension: if we apply the strict temporal-priority reading to Lakatos’s own canonical examples—such as the Bohr atom model’s adjustment of orbital rules to match known spectral lines—does the criterion survive, or does it prove self-defeatingly strong? I want the precisifications and their verdicts, not a survey of interpretations.
