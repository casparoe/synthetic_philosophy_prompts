I have spent the past year as the primary caregiver for my father, who was diagnosed with a progressive form of semantic dementia. Watching specific capacities erode—his conviction that he had already eaten lunch, his desire to visit a particular friend, his belief that he was still employed—did not look like the removal of discrete propositional attitudes from a mental box. Instead, the dissolution seemed granular and context-dependent: he retained fragments of old commitments in some settings but not in others, without any clear boundary between “holding the belief” and “lacking it.” This experience made the philosophical debate between eliminative materialists and defenders of folk psychology suddenly feel less abstract and more urgent, and I want to understand why that debate has stalled for so long.

The eliminativist argues that beliefs and desires are theoretical posits of an obsolete folk-psychological framework that neuroscience will eventually replace, much as “phlogiston” was replaced. The defender replies that we cannot consistently deny the existence of beliefs without employing belief-like attitudes in the denial itself, and that folk psychology remains our most successful predictive framework for human behavior. The eliminativist responds by distinguishing ontology from pragmatic utility: predictive success may track nothing more than robust behavioral dispositions, not discrete mental entities. The defender then insists that predictive success tracks real entities and that eliminativism collapses into performative contradiction unless it abandons rational argument altogether.

Yet neither side, in my reading, accommodates the kind of fragmentation I observed clinically. The eliminativist expects a wholesale replacement of the folk framework, not a patchwork erosion; the defender expects beliefs to persist or disappear as coherent units, not to splinter across contexts. So I wonder whether the stalemate is actually a sign that both sides answer different questions—one about the ontology of mental representation, the other about the normative structure of explanation—under the shared label “beliefs and desires.” Or perhaps the disagreement is verbal, turning on whether “real” requires discrete neural correlates or only stable predictive roles.

Can you diagnose what kind of disagreement this really is? Specifically: are the eliminativist and the folk-psychological realist disputing the same fact about the world, or are they operating with different standards for what counts as a mental state? Does the clinical phenomenon of fragmented dispositional erosion—where a subject behaves as if holding a belief in one interaction and lacking it in another, without clear criteria for either—expose a hidden empirical gap, or does it merely show that both theories are underdetermined by neuroscience? And given your diagnosis, is there any conceptual distinction, empirical finding, or change of explanatory standards that could genuinely move the debate, or is the impasse permanent because the sides begin with incompatible intuitions about what explanation requires?
