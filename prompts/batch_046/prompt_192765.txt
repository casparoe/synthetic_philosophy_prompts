Provide a formal specification of the desiderata that any philosophically adequate theory of self-deception must satisfy, treating the phenomenon as genuinely possible rather than conceptually incoherent. For each desideratum, state the specific motivating case or conceptual tension: (i) resolution of the access paradox—how a single subject can both know and not know the same proposition without splitting into two agents; (ii) preservation of first-person authority over beliefs without requiring total doxastic transparency; (iii) a sharp distinction from adjacent phenomena such as wishful thinking, strategic ignorance, denial, and retrospective confabulation; (iv) consistency with empirical findings on implicit bias and split-brain confabulation without reducing self-deception to mere post-hoc storytelling; (v) accounting for the intentional structure of self-deception—why it is directed at a reason rather than being a pure cognitive malfunction; and (vi) compatibility with a unified subject of experience rather than requiring a fully divided self. Mark which of these are non-negotiable conditions for counting the account as explaining self-deception rather than explaining it away, and which are negotiable commitments of particular camps (e.g., whether unconscious belief is necessary, whether purely motivational mechanisms suffice, or whether a partitioned cognitive architecture is required). Then assess whether any subset of these requirements is jointly unsatisfiable—specifically, whether resolving the access paradox while maintaining both first-person authority and a unified subject necessarily collapses the theory into either ordinary error or interpersonal deception—and identify which single desideratum eliminates the broadest class of existing accounts. Present the result as a structured specification list with brief justifications for each entry; do not provide a bibliography, reading list, or scorecard of named theorists.
