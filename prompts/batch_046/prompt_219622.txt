I am drafting the final examination for an upper-level graduate seminar on cooperative reasoning and decision theory, and I need one essay question that avoids the standard recitation of Hofstadter's superrationality in symmetric Prisoner's Dilemmas. Instead, I want students to confront a deliberately asymmetric variant: suppose we have a Newcomb-like scenario in which the predictor is not an omniscient demon but a bounded computational agent running a lossy, compressed approximation of your decision procedure, and in which you and your algorithmic counterpart share source code but have accumulated divergent sensory histories that undermine common knowledge of rationality. The exam should ask students to determine whether superrational cooperation remains coherent under these conditions—specifically, whether it requires identical reasoning processes, shared priors, or merely mutual transparency about algorithmic structure—and to assess whether, once symmetry is broken, superrationality collapses into standard evidential decision theory or becomes indistinguishable from unjustified optimism about correlation. Please write the exam question itself, followed by a model answer of approximately seven hundred words, and then a marking scheme that distinguishes an A response from a B response by listing the specific conceptual moves that must appear—such as distinguishing algorithmic correlation from evidential correlation, addressing the bounded predictor's limitations, and recognizing the risk that superrationality without symmetry degenerates into naive correlation-tracking—together with the marks or weight each move should carry. The entire response should be rendered in continuous prose only, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, formatted as one might write in a long email. The target audience is graduate students who have about ninety minutes, and the prompt should not ask for bibliographies, citations, or reading lists since the exercise is meant to be answered from conceptual understanding alone. I also want the marking scheme to note, in the same flowing format, the characteristic mistakes that should cost points—such as conflating superrationality with altruism, treating the compressed predictor as Laplacean, or assuming that shared source code automatically restores common knowledge despite divergent histories.
