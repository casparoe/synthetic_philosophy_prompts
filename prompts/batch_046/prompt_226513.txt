Next Thursday I defend a thesis developed in the course of writing my latest novel before the philosophy of action colloquium: that the canonical taxonomy of free will worth wanting—compatibilist hierarchical integration, libertarian agent-causation, and revisionist sourcehood—neglects a fourth variety I call compositional autonomy. By this I mean an agent’s capacity to initiate action through what I term narrative elasticity, the structural tension between an established character (or moral disposition) and an unforeseen situation that permits a deviation neither predetermined by prior causes nor randomly indeterminate, but coherent with the agent’s deeper patterns in a way that surprises without violating them. I claim this elasticity is not merely a literary conceit but describes a real feature of human moral deliberation, and that recognizing it dissolves the standard stalemate because both camps mistake the relevant alternative possibilities. The audience will be about fifteen people: eight compatibilists steeped in Frankfurt and Watson, two libertarians working on agent-causal accounts, one philosopher of art convinced fiction offers no legitimate metaphysical data, a cognitive scientist who studies decision noise, and a moral psychologist interested in narrative identity but suspicious of ontological claims drawn from novels. Give me a Q&A prep sheet with roughly eight objections I will actually receive, ordered by how damaging they are rather than by politeness, each voiced in the vocabulary its speaker would use. For each: what I should concede outright, what I must hold, a reply deliverable in under two minutes, and the precise point where that reply is weakest. Identify the objection I am hoping nobody raises, and flag the weakest structural point in my own argument that I should prepare to defend rather than hope they miss. The thesis being defended is that compositional autonomy, understood through narrative elasticity, is a distinct and genuinely available form of free will, and that its existence undermines the shared assumption that freedom must be located either in determination by an appropriate internal hierarchy or in the absence of such determination.
