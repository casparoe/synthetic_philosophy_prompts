I'm a phd student in political philosophy and was arguing with my roommate over coffee about this stoic revival thing in tech culture. You know the dichotemy of control — Epictetus splits things into what's in our power, like judgments and impulses, and what's not, like body, reputation, or market forces. But in these modern self-help versions I keep noticing people use the split like a moral escape hatch. They say systemic stuff like healthcare pricing or climate policy is "outside my control" so they don't have to think about collective action, but then they treat their morning routine, diet, or career networking as fully within control, even when those are obviously shaped by class and luck. What's weird is the boundary moves depending on whether accepting responsibility would require joining a movement or just optimizing oneself. If a problem can be fixed by willpower they claim full ownership; if it needs structural change they suddenly discover it's an external.

I've been calling it "control laundering" in my notes, but I don't recieve this as just ordinary hypocrisy — there's a specific pattern where the category of externals expands selectively to cover exactly those issues where personal agency feels insufficient. Is there an established concept for this distortion? How does it differ from motivated reasoning, or from the classic stoic mistake of treating externals as goods? And if there's no real name, can you define the phenomenon precisely — including edge cases, like someone who genuinely lacks political power — and suggest a term that distinguishes it from plain equivocation? I don't need a bibliography, just want to know if I'm reinventing the wheel and where this sits relative to the original framework.
