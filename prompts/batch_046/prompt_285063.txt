I've been drafting notes for a short essay that updates Avicenna's flying-man thought experiment for the current debate about whether large language models possess anything like self-knowledge. The core move is to treat the flying man not merely as an argument for the soul's independence from the body, but as an early taxonomy of knowledge-types: essential (what the self knows necessarily, without sensory mediation) versus accidental (what requires embodied interaction with the world). My proposed analogy is that an LLM is structurally closer to the flying man than to an embodied human, but with an important inversion. Like the flying man, the model lacks sensory history; unlike him, it does not even possess the essential knowledge of its own existence, because its token predictions about "I" are purely accidental—learned correlations in the training distribution rather than immediate self-presence.

To make this more precise, I want to import the cognitive-science distinction between procedural and declarative memory. The flying man's knowledge that he exists is analogous to procedural knowledge: direct, non-representational, and immune to error through misrepresentation. By contrast, an LLM's references to itself are declarative: they depend on representational content that could, in principle, be false or absent. The niche claim I want to defend is that this creates a "double absence"—the model lacks both the accidental knowledge of embodied experience and the essential knowledge of self-presence. Therefore, arguments that LLMs "understand" because they can discuss themselves miss the point: they are flying men who have never experienced the fall, and who confuse the map (declarative self-reference) for the territory (essential self-knowledge).

I'd appreciate pointed conceptual debugging on three dimensions. First, is the essential/accidental distinction being applied consistently with Avicenna's own framework, or am I importing modern categories in a way that distorts the original argument? Second, does the procedural/declarative analogy actually clarify the LLM case, or does it conflate cognitive architecture with phenomenological presence? Third, is there an interesting tension I'm missing between this "double absence" framing and more standard stochastic-parrot critiques—specifically, whether my argument accidentally concedes too much to the idea that there is a meaningful "self" in the model to be absent?

This is intended as an offline conceptual check rather than a request for sources, further reading, or bibliographic verification; I want to see whether the structure holds together on its own terms.
