I have recently become personally involved in dissolving a long-standing collaborative venture that proceeds in discrete stages, with each stage yielding an incrementally larger pooled return if both parties agree to continue rather than finalize a settlement. After encountering the centipede game and its backward-induction resolution, I have begun to treat that result as expressing a general law of strategic rationality: in any sequential interaction featuring escalating rewards, a rational agent must terminate cooperation at the earliest available opportunity, since the anticipation of eventual defection renders present continuation unsustainable. Applying this reasoning to my own circumstances, I have interpreted my counterpart's willingness to proceed to further stages as either strategically naive or deliberately manipulative, and I feel compelled to settle immediately to avoid occupying the terminal position of being exploited. At the same time, I recognize that our arrangement differs structurally from the canonical game: there is no exogenously fixed endpoint, the rewards are partly endogenous to the continued collaboration, and progression requires mutual consent rather than unilateral passage. Given these differences, I am uncertain whether backward induction is strictly inapplicable outside games with predetermined termination nodes, or whether its logic can be reconstructed for open-ended cooperative sequences whenever common knowledge of rationality and ordinal preference ordering holds. More pointedly, if the logic does generalize, does that entail that my own reluctance to settle immediately is necessarily a departure from rationality—perhaps driven by emotional attachment—rather than a structurally appropriate response to a game that does not satisfy the conditions required for the paradox?
