I'm preparing a seventy-five-minute seminar for first-year philosophy-of-science students on whether DNA is literally a code. They will arrive with two opposing intuitions: that the metaphor is obviously correct because textbooks speak of a "genetic code," and that it is obviously wrong because they conflate "code" with designed computer software. Plan the explanatory arc: the order in which the conceptual pieces must be introduced—first distinguishing a causal template from an intentional representation, then applying that distinction to transcription and translation—so that the philosophical question arises only after the machinery is in place; the single carrying example, which should involve an identical DNA sequence yielding different phenotypic outcomes across cellular contexts (thereby showing what the metaphor captures and what it suppresses) and must not depend on named synthetic constructs or specific laboratory protocols; the misconceptions in the order they will surface—beginning with "the ribosome is an interpreter," proceeding to "if it is not literal then the metaphor is useless," and ending with "this gap proves intelligent design"—with the precise point in the sequence where each must be defused; a concise check for understanding that reveals whether students grasp that sequence-meaning dependency requires an interpretive context rather than merely repeating that DNA is "like a blueprint"; and what biochemical mechanism or historical attribution should be openly set aside at this level, stated explicitly so the philosophical point is not obscured.
