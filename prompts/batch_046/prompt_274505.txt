I'm developing a specification for theories that claim moral improvement can be achieved through sustained engagement with biographical and hagiographic narrative—particularly texts such as Plutarch's paired lives or medieval vitae that present a life as a structured sequence of moral choices. Rather than asking whether biography is a useful pedagogical tool in general, I want the full list of desiderata that any adequate theory in this space must satisfy: the phenomena it has to explain, the distinctions it must preserve, the results from moral psychology or narrative theory it cannot contradict, and the cases it must get right. I am especially interested in two under-discussed tensions that should shape the criteria. First, hagiographic writing systematically suppresses mundane failure and counterfactual struggle: it rarely shows the subject considering, and then rejecting, a genuinely tempting vice in an unremarkable context. A theory must therefore specify whether this suppression is pedagogically essential—perhaps protecting the reader's affective identification—or epistemically damaging, because it removes the very decision points where practical wisdom operates. Second, there is a distinction between mimetic identification, in which the reader emotionally aligns with the subject's particularity, and structural extraction, in which the reader derives portable patterns of practical reasoning that could apply in radically different circumstances. The theory must state which mechanism it privileges, whether they can operate together, and what each requires from the narrative form.

With those framing concerns in mind, please write the requirements list. For each desideratum, provide the motivating case or conceptual reason behind it, and mark whether you take it to be non-negotiable or a preference of a particular camp—for instance, those who think biography must yield universalizable maxims versus those who think it should preserve irreducible particularity. Make sure the list addresses: (1) the selection problem—how the reader knows which episodes are morally active rather than merely biographical ornament; (2) the distance problem—how temporal, cultural, or heroic distance is bridged without either anachronism or uncritical admiration; (3) the negative-exemplarity problem—how biography teaches what to avoid when hagiography tends to erase failure; (4) the plural-exemplar problem—how a reader should respond to paired or conflicting lives arranged in comparative biography without reducing them to a single scale; (5) consistency with basic moral psychology—what cognitive capacities the theory can presuppose; and (6) whether narrative identification and structural extraction are jointly satisfiable or generate contradictory demands on the text. If any subset of these is jointly unsatisfiable—say, if preserving particularity for identification necessarily undermines portability for extraction—say so explicitly, since that is the part I most want to see. Do not provide a bibliography or reading list; I want the conceptual specification, not directions to literature. You may briefly note, at the end, how a broadly exemplarist approach and a broadly narrative-pedagogical approach tend to fare against your list, but keep the weight on the criteria themselves.
