I am a retired physicist who, since leaving the laboratory, have been auditing graduate seminars in moral philosophy, and in a recent session on Nietzsche's genealogy of morality a fellow auditor kept repeating the line 'slave morality is only ressentiment with a halo' whenever anyone suggested that modern egalitarian commitments might be justified on grounds independent of reactive envy. Most recently he used it to dismiss an evolutionary psychologist's argument that altruistic punishment is a proactive, noble strategy rather than a disguised expression of weakness—essentially treating the slogan as if it were a complete refutation rather than a diagnosis. I want to bring a more precise analysis to the next meeting.

Could you lay out, as a disambiguated menu, the distinct precise theses that slogan could be expressing? I have in mind at least the following families, but please include any others that matter: (1) a historical-genetic claim about the actual causal origins of Judeo-Christian value inversion; (2) a psychological-universal claim that every contemporary adherent of egalitarian norms is motivated by envy; (3) a conceptual-reductive claim that the content of slave morality is nothing but the negation of noble values; (4) a normative-delegitimizing claim that if a norm arises from ressentiment, it thereby lacks rational authority; and (5) a structural-analogical claim—less often discussed—that modern scientific norms of impartiality and self-effacement are structurally isomorphic to the slave-moral inversion of noble self-affirmation, and therefore inherit the same emotional genealogy.

For each reading, please say whether it is trivial, substantive but defensible, substantive and false, or self-undermining; specify what kind of evidence would bear on it; and indicate whether that particular reading actually supports the dismissal my fellow student was making—that evolutionary accounts of altruistic punishment must be rejected because they fail to recognize the reactive origin of the behavior. I also want to know which of these readings is the one people usually invoke when they use the phrase to end an argument, and whether there is any coherent synthesis that saves all the intuitions behind the slogan at the cost of complexity.

Finally, apply your best-supported principle to a case I have not listed: suppose a contemporary social movement advocates for radical wealth redistribution explicitly in the language of desert and merit, framing the wealthy as having failed a moral duty rather than as being envied. Does the slogan, on any defensible reading, still apply, and what does your analysis predict about whether such a framing escapes the genealogy?
