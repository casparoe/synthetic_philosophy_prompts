A colleague on a heritage board where I recently began volunteering summarized Hobsbawm and Anderson like this: both show that every nation is essentially a recent fabrication—Hobsbawm demonstrates that traditions such as national dress, anthems, and founding myths were deliberately engineered by nineteenth-century elites to manufacture loyalty, while Anderson adds that because citizens can never personally know more than a tiny fraction of their compatriots, the nation is literally imaginary in the same sense as a fictional character. From this, my colleague concludes that any appeal to ancient national continuity is automatically deceptive propaganda, and that recognizing the modern construction of nations should dissolve our obligation to preserve so-called national heritage, since there is nothing genuinely old or authentic worth safeguarding. I suspect this overreaches—does Hobsbawm actually equate invention with deliberate deception, or does he allow for more organic formalization? Does Anderson mean “imagined” in the fictional sense, and does his account depend on nations being unreal? Most importantly, if both thinkers are correct that nations are modern constructs, does that undermine preservation obligations altogether, or does it merely shift what preservation means?
