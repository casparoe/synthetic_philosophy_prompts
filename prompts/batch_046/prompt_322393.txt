Having spent thirty years in condensed-matter physics before turning to the philosophy of public policy, I am preparing a seminar defense of the following thesis: Portugal’s 2001 decriminalization and North American cannabis legalization are not points on a single liberalization continuum, but structurally distinct regimes, and evaluating both with an identical harm-reduction metric commits a category error that obscures the normative role of the state-mediated boundary between licit and illicit substances. My audience is a philosophy-of-law reading group consisting of two liberal theorists committed to neutrality, one epidemiologist who has studied the Portuguese model extensively, and a political philosopher skeptical of state paternalism in any form. I need a prep sheet, not a defense: list the six or seven objections I will actually receive, in the order they are likely to arise, phrased exactly as each interlocutor would put them, with a two-minute reply for each, an explicit note on where the reply is weakest, and what I should concede up front. Include the one objection I am hoping nobody raises—specifically, the challenge that my distinction between decriminalization and legalization collapses once we recognize that both regimes still deploy administrative sanctions against unregulated supply. Finally, identify the weakest point in my own position that I should prepare to defend rather than hope they overlook: namely, that I have not yet shown why the preservation of a formal boundary has independent normative value rather than being merely a transitional political compromise. Keep everything self-contained; do not ask for bibliographies, citations, or reading lists.
