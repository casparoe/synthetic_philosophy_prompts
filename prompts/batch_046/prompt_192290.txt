I'm drafting a novel about people stuck in a plane wreck high in the mountains, and I got into this weird debate with my freind about exstantialism—specifically Sartre's thing about radical freedom and bad faith. It made me think about the real 1972 Andes flight disaster, the Uruguayan rugby team stranded in the fuselage at like 12,000 feet. They held a vote, made this pact that if one died the rest could eat him, prayed that God wanted them to live, and then Parrado and Canessa eventually treked out across the mountains. I want to look at that whole episode through Sartre's lens, not just have you explain the philosophy in a vacuum.

For Sartre we're always radically free, even starving in a broken plane, and bad faith is pretending you're just an object shoved around by circumstance. So was their vote an authentic assumption of freedom—choosing the terms of survival—or was it bad faith, using the pact and prayer to hide that each person still had the absolute freedom to refuse and die? And when Parrado said they had no choice but to walk, is that the classic lie of facticity? I'm also stuck on the religious part: they framed survival as God's will, but Sartre's atheism says we're alone in making meaning. Does that faith count as another layer of bad faith, or can it sit alongside authentic choice? Map it carefully—who was deciding what, where Sartre explains the behavior and where he goes silent or feels forced, and whether the extremity of the case actually supports radical freedom or shows that bad faith is kind of necessary just to keep living. Don't just summarize Sartre; use the crash to test him.
