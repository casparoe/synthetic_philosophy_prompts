I am preparing a single fifty-minute seminar for first-year philosophy undergraduates whose biology stops at GCSE level. I want them to understand the units-of-selection debate — genes, individuals, and groups — not as a turf war but as a disagreement about whether 'level of selection' is chiefly a statistical partitioning device or a claim about causal efficacy in evolutionary change. To prevent them falling back on familiar kin-selection stories, the carrying example must be the social life-cycle of Dictyostelium discoideum: cells aggregate into a stalk-and-spore fruiting body, with some dying to form the stalk, despite lacking the close genetic relatedness that makes altruism in bees or ants immediately intuitive. Plan the explanatory arc: first, which conceptual distinction — replication versus interaction, or evolutionary explanation versus physical mechanism — must be in place before the debate even makes sense; second, the precise order in which gene-level, individual-level, and multilevel accounts should be introduced, with the reason that sequence prevents the usual collapse into 'it is really just genes'; third, the misconceptions they will predictably form — I expect confusion between selection level and physical size, the assumption that group selection requires conscious cooperation, and the belief that Price's equation proves gene-centrism — together with the exact point in the sequence where each should be defused; fourth, the properties the Dictyostelium case must possess (group-level differential survival without shared genes, complete absence of intentionality) and the properties it must lack (human-like social contracts, obvious kin structure); fifth, a brief check at the end that reveals whether they have grasped the causal-democracy point rather than merely memorised the three labels; and sixth, what should be openly left out at this level — specifically, the formal derivation of multilevel Price equations and the MLS1/MLS2 distinction — with an explicit statement that these are excluded. In your response, separate what is broadly settled in evolutionary biology from what remains genuinely contested among philosophers and biologists who specialise in this area, and do not present any contested interpretation — particularly the claim that gene-centrism is the only scientifically respectable position — as though it were the consensus.
