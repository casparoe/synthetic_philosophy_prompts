I've been trying to map the logical space of responses to Jaegwon Kim's causal exclusion argument, but every taxonomy I find collapses into the standard quadrants: reductionism, epiphenomenalism, overdetermination, and emergence-with-downward-causation. For a workshop with technically inclined rationalists, I need a finer-grained decision tree that separates positions by what people actually believe when pressed, not by which philosopher they cite.

Please construct a taxonomy that begins from one shared commitment: every bodily movement has a complete causal story at the level of fundamental physics. From there, build branching nodes as concrete yes/no questions that a respondent could answer without knowing the philosophical labels. The branches should cover: whether mental properties are determinables relative to physical determinates or something stronger; whether macro-level causal selection is compatible with micro-level closure; whether interventionist causation applies differently across levels of abstraction; whether mental-physical overdetermination is systematic or only occasional; and whether structural realization can carry causal efficacy that its physical realizer does not.

Your leaves must include, with one-sentence awkward commitments: (A) the view that mental properties are determinable/determinate with physical bases but not type-identical; (B) the view that mental causation is real only as macro-selection within a physically closed domain; (C) the view that exclusion fails because closure is misapplied across abstraction levels; (D) standard epiphenomenalism; (E) the view that structural realization transmits causation that the base properties lack; and (F) a systematically overdetermined but non-reductive view. If any of these collapse into the same branch pattern, flag them as inseparable rather than inventing a spurious split.

Also identify at least one unoccupied logical combination — for instance, mental causation that makes a physical difference but with no corresponding physical property — and diagnose whether it is incoherent, unstable, or merely unexplored. Note any pair of named positions that your tree genuinely cannot separate, and explain whether the difference is rhetorical or substantive.

Keep the deliverable free of bibliographies, author lists, and citation requests; build the tree from the logical space and from concrete diagnostic cases, not from the literature. The audience already knows what downward causation is and does not need a primer.
