I've been working on this novel about the 1848 revolutions and I got into this wierd argument with my writing group that I can't shake. One friend keeps saying Ranke wanted history to show how it actually was, like there's a pure past you can just recover if you read enough archives. Another friend brings up Carr saying historians are basically carpenters selecting and cutting the wood of facts, and then Hayden White shows up with that idea that every history is just a story—romance, tragedy, satire—plastered over the same events. We've been circling for months: we all agree the facts don't change, we all agree historians choose which ones matter, but nobody moves on whether that choice destroys objectivity or just reveals it was never there. Is this stall actually about evidence, or about what we mean by true when we talk about dead people? Or are Ranke and Carr answering totally different questions—one about archives, one about narrative form—while pretending they're fighting over the same thing?

My professor mentioned this concept of emplotment being unavoidable but not totally arbitrary, and I think thats maybe a middle ground, but I'm not sure if it's real or just another way to avoid picking sides. I want to know what kind of disagreement this actualy is—verbal, substantive, hidden intuition about naturalness, whatever—and whether there's some distinction or formal result or even just a clearer question that could break the logjam. Because if I understand why the debate is stuck, it might change how I write the revolution scenes without feeling like I'm just lieing prettily. What do you think is really going on here, and should a novelist even care, or are historians and fiction writers doomed to talk past each other forever?
