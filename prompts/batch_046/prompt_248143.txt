As I sketch the moral geography for my next novel, I have set down my own commitments regarding rural, working-class outlooks and urban, professional-class ones, and I wish to know whether they cohere. Here is my position: (a) I hold that working-class virtues—particularly repairability, local loyalty, and tacit practical mastery—are ethically more defensible than the professional-class emphasis on credentialism, cosmopolitan abstraction, and procedural transparency; (b) I believe these differences arise chiefly from material conditions, with rural dependence on land and kin fostering the former and urban institutional life fostering the latter; (c) I nevertheless reject historical materialism as an ethical theory, maintaining that the material origin of a value does not determine its moral validity and that universal ethical reasoning remains possible; (d) I intend my narrative voice to treat both outlooks with equal sympathy, withholding any authorial ranking despite my personal preference; and (e) I grant that professional culture is structurally parasitic upon rural and working-class material production, though I deny that this dependence amounts to a moral failing. Please analyse which pairs genuinely conflict, which conflicts are only apparent, and rank the real tensions by severity. For each genuine tension, indicate the cheapest revision that would restore consistency, and state which single change to my view would resolve the greatest number of problems. Please respond in British English.
