I am drafting the final examination for my upper-level undergraduate seminar in moral and political philosophy, and I need a single essay prompt together with a model answer and a granular marking scheme. The topic is individual contributions to collective harms and the inefficacy objection, specifically as it arises in climate-related emissions, market-driven animal agriculture, and democratic voting. I want to avoid questions that can be answered by reciting the standard introductory treatments—Parfit’s harm examples, the usual climate-footprint statistics, or generic appeals to Kantian universalization. To that end, please frame the examination around a conceptual distinction I have developed in our lectures: the difference between aggregative inefficacy and structural inefficacy. In aggregative cases, an individual action is causally negligible only because a large number of others are performing the same action; in structural cases, the harm is produced by an institutional or systemic architecture such that an individual’s withdrawal would not disrupt the mechanism even in principle, regardless of what others do. The exam should require students to apply this distinction rigorously across the three domains mentioned.

Please provide the following:

1. The essay question itself, directed at students who have 75 minutes and should write approximately 1,200 words. The question must ask them to explain both forms of inefficacy, to argue whether structural inefficacy undermines the causal version of the objection more radically than aggregative inefficacy does, and to apply this analysis to at least two of the three domains—climate emissions, meat consumption, and electoral participation—showing precisely where the distinction changes the moral evaluation.

2. A model answer of roughly 600–700 words that demonstrates the specific analytical moves a top response must contain: (a) precise, non-circular definitions of aggregative and structural inefficacy; (b) an argument about why structural cases pose a distinct challenge to causal-reasoning-based objections; (c) a comparative application to two domains that does not merely label them but explains how the institutional architecture operates; and (d) a brief critical assessment—either defending or qualifying—the claim that recognizing structural inefficacy generates a positive moral obligation rather than merely excusing inaction.

3. A marking scheme totaling 25 points, broken down by the four moves above, with explicit point allocations. For each component, include a one-line note on the characteristic error that should cost marks—for example, conflating the two forms of inefficacy, treating voting as purely aggregative without attending to electoral-system design, or invoking familiar examples without adapting them to the structural/aggregative framework.

The difficulty should suit students who have engaged with the primary literature but who must now synthesize it with the conceptual framework introduced in our seminar. Do not include a bibliography, reading list, citation instructions, or any request for specific page references.
