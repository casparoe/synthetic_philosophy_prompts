Here is an argument I built for a seminar on disjunctivism and now find suspicious because it seems to export the view far beyond perception. (1) The disjunctivist holds that in veridical perception the object is constitutively present in the experience, while in perfect hallucination nothing is present; because the presence or absence of the object makes a kind-difference, there is no common mental factor shared between the two cases. (2) The driving principle is: if a mental state in case A is constituted by the presence of some item X, and in case B X is entirely absent, then A and B cannot belong to the same mental kind, no matter how subjectively indistinguishable they are. (3) Now apply this to testimonial belief. In case A, I come to believe that p because I am directly in the presence of a reliable expert who asserts p; her authority is actively exercised and she is available for further questioning. In case B, I form the very same belief with the same confidence from an anonymous, fabricated document; no expert is present at all. (4) From the first-person point of view the two beliefs are indistinguishable: same content, same strength, same functional role, and—if belief has any phenomenal coloring—same subjective feel. (5) By principle (2), since the expert is present in A and absent in B, the testimonial belief and the rumor-based belief cannot share a mental kind. (6) So far that only divides belief-types; the destructive step comes next. If the expert's presence is genuinely constitutive of the belief-state in A, then the belief is a relational state that extends beyond my mind to include her. (7) But then, when I later recall that belief in her absence, the recall-state lacks the expert entirely, so by (2) it must be a different mental kind from the original belief—despite being functionally and intentionally identical in every respect. (8) Generalizing, no mental state directed at an absent item can ever be the same kind as one directed at a present item; hence memory, anticipation, and conceptual thought about absent objects are all non-relational internal states that never genuinely relate us to what they are about. (9) That is a sweeping anti-representationalism about all intentionality except present perception, which is plainly too much to swallow. So either (2) is false, or disjunctivism can be saved only by an ad hoc restriction to perception alone. Where exactly does the argument slip? Is there a hidden disanalogy between the way an object is present in experience and the way an expert is present in testimony, or does the fault lie in treating belief and recall as having the kind of phenomenal identity that makes (4) relevant? If you must choose between saving (2) and saving (9), which do you sacrifice, and what does that cost—either for disjunctivism's motivation or for our ordinary account of knowledge through testimony?
