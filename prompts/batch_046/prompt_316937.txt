Analyze the July 2020 European Council agreement establishing NextGenerationEU — in particular the authorization of joint EU borrowing on capital markets to fund pandemic-recovery grants and loans to member states — through the lens of subsidiarity (Article 5 TEU) and fiscal-federalism theory. Do not summarize the macroeconomics; focus on institutional logic. By strict subsidiarity, should fiscal stabilization and sovereign-style debt issuance have remained at the national level, or do the cross-border spillovers of a pandemic justify EU-level action? How does the agreement’s architecture — common debt, national repayment obligations scaled to GNI, temporary exceptionality, and ex-ante conditionality — map onto the fiscal-federalist distinctions among assignment, macroeconomic stabilization, and redistribution? Which features does the theory illuminate (for instance, why the Netherlands and Germany initially resisted direct grants, or why the mechanism was framed as one-off), and which does it misdescribe or leave silent (for instance, the interaction with the ECB’s prior PSPP ruling in shifting German constitutional politics, or the mismatch between new EU borrowing power and the absence of a corresponding democratic fiscal authority)? Does the episode show that subsidiarity in fiscal matters is genuinely crisis-contingent, or does it reveal that fiscal federalism was always latent in the EU’s architecture and merely activated? State where the theory is silent rather than forcing it to speak, and say whether the case ultimately confirms, complicates, or undermines the principle.
