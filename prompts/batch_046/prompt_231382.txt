I review incident reports for a small company that builds adaptive tutoring software for children with learning differences. Several times a week I have to explain to parents and regulators why the program acted in a strange or harmful way—locking a student out of a module, repeating an exercise far past usefulness, or advancing a child who has not shown mastery. I have been thinking about the idea that any explanation of a system’s behavior can come from three stances: the physical stance (hardware, sensors, code execution), the design stance (the pedagogical rules and goals it was built to pursue), and the intentional stance (treating it as if it holds beliefs, wants outcomes, or understands a situation). I need a practical, reusable procedure—a sequence of ordered checks or tests—that I can run in about twenty minutes with no philosophy training, that tells me which stance belongs in which section of my report, and that produces something my non-technical manager can sign off on. The procedure must flag when two stances conflict, identify the points where judgment is unavoidable, and be honest about what it cannot settle. Apply it to three incidents: first, the software gives a dyslexic child phonics drills that are clearly too advanced, and the parent asks whether the program knows the child is struggling; second, a server error skips an entire curriculum block for a whole district, but parents interpret it as the software giving up on their children; third, a case where the design stance and the intentional stance recommend opposite explanations, and the procedure directs me toward the explanation that will anger the parents more. End by stating where this procedure is guaranteed to misfire and what I should do instead.
