Hey, I retired from physics a few years back (mostly statistical mechanics) and lately I've been trying to wrap my head around philosophy of rational choice. I keep running into this argument about iterated dominance in mixed strategies that seems like it proves way too much. Here's my version:

(1) A rational player never uses a strategy that's strictly dominanted, whether pure or mixed.
(2) But in the real world any "choice" is physically noisy - neural firing, hand tremour, measurment error - so what I actually execute is always a distribution centered near my intended pure strategy.
(3) That means every strategy I can physically play is technically a mixed strategy spread over a neighborhood.
(4) Now in a continuous space, for any such neighborhood distribution I can always shift a little weight toward higher-payoff regions and build a new distribution that strictly dominates the first one.
(5) If I apply iterated elimination over and over, every distribution except the one putting exactly uniform weight across the whole convex hull of surviving strategies gets eliminated.
(6) So rationality apparently forces me to randomize uniformly over all remaining actions, even ones with terrible payoffs, just because of execution noise.
(7) That can't be right - it makes rational agents behave like they're throwing darts blindfolded.
(8) So I figure one of (1) through (5) has to break, but I really want to hang onto (1) and (2) since they feel true from a scientific perspective.

Which step is the bad one? Is (4) wrong because dominance requires comparing against the same opponent strategies and shifting local weight doesn't guarantee that? Or is (3) the problem - maybe execution noise shouldn't count as part of my strategic choice? If I reject (3), am I just denying that physical implementation matters? I'd rather not drop (1) if I can help it.
