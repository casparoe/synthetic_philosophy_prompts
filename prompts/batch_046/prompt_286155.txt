Write an essay on the following controversy. Galen Strawson's Basic Argument contends that true moral responsibility is impossible: to be responsible for what one does, one must be responsible for the mental nature from which one's actions flow, but one can never be ultimately responsible for that nature, since doing so would require being responsible for an even prior mental nature, generating either an infinite regress or a stopping point for which one is not responsible. Standard replies deny that responsibility requires such ultimate sourcehood, or they defend a compatibilist conception that requires only that one's actions reflect one's endorsed values and reasoning capacities. Now introduce a less-discussed alternative: a 'modular' theory of agency, according to which the self is not a unitary author but an assembly of relatively autonomous cognitive modules, each capable of generating outputs with its own internal norms of transparency, coherence, and revisability. On this view, a moral-judgment module might bear 'local responsibility' for its outputs—being answerable for them within its own operating parameters—without the global self having authored the module's design. Does this modular reply genuinely defeat Strawson's regress by distributing responsibility downward to locally autonomous subsystems, or does it merely relocate the problem? In particular, consider whether (a) the modules themselves fall prey to the same regress, since they did not design their own architecture; (b) local responsibility is too thin to support the practices of blame, praise, and punishment we actually care about; and (c) the global self remains accountable for integrating, or failing to integrate, these modules. Weigh the standard compatibilist and libertarian responses to Strawson alongside this modular proposal, and reach a justified conclusion: either that modular sourcehood blocks the Basic Argument and preserves a meaningful form of moral responsibility, or that it fails and Strawson's conclusion stands.
