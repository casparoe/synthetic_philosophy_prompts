I'm working through my own reconstruction of Leibnizian ethics for a graduate seminar on rationalist moral philosophy, and I'd like a consistency audit before I commit to the paper. Here is where I've landed. First, I hold the anti-voluntarist thesis that moral truths are necessary truths of reason residing in God's understanding, not products of divine will, so that injustice is as contradictory as a mathematical falsehood. Second, I accept the definition of justice as caritas sapientis—love distributed in proportion to the rational perfection or merit of each being—rather than as equality of treatment. Third, I think the principle of sufficient reason applies without exception to human volition: every moral choice has a complete determining reason, yet I also want to preserve genuine contingency, on the grounds that the contrary of a free choice implies no contradiction and is therefore only hypothetically, not absolutely, necessary. Fourth, I take the normative ought to be fully explained by descriptive facts about which actions maximize the compossible perfection of rational substances in the actual world; I deny there is any irreducible moral property or categorical imperative beyond this metaphysical calculus. Fifth, I hold that because we inhabit the best of all possible worlds, every authentic moral improvement is simultaneously an augmentation of my own metaphysical reality and a contribution to a universal harmony that is already fully determined by pre-established coordination—so moral striving is real, yet causally redundant with respect to altering the world's overall structure.

These commitments feel coherent in isolation, but I'm uneasy about how they interlock. Does the merit-proportional distribution required by caritas sapientis conflict with the aggregate-maximization logic that seems to drive my fourth commitment? Is there a hidden tension between applying the principle of sufficient reason to every volition and maintaining that moral effort is genuinely efficacious, given my fifth claim about causal redundancy? And does reducing the normative to the descriptive in my fourth thesis collapse the distinctive authority of moral reason that my first thesis was meant to protect, or is that only an apparent conflict that dissolves once we recognize that necessary truths can carry prescriptive force within a rationalist framework? Please rank the tensions by severity, indicate for each whether it requires giving up one of the five commitments or can be resolved by refining it, and tell me which single revision would clean up the most inconsistencies with the least damage to the Leibnizian system as I understand it. Be specific about which pairs pull in opposite directions and where I might be smuggling in a non-Leibnizian assumption—perhaps something closer to modern consequentialism or to a compatibilism that Leibniz himself would reject—without noticing.
