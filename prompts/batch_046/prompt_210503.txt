Many contemporary correctional rehabilitation programs operate on two apparently incompatible tracks. The first track treats offenders through structured moral dialogue and reflective exercises designed to awaken an innate capacity for ethical judgment; the second relies on behavioral conditioning, surveillance protocols, and incentive structures that treat self-interest as the primary driver of conduct and seek to reshape it through external control. Program administrators defend both tracks as complementary, yet the institutional design never resolves whether the offender is being guided back toward a natural moral orientation or forcibly reconstructed away from an inherently wayward one. What theory of human nature does this hybrid practice presuppose? Must it assume a layered psychology in which original goodness coexists with acquired selfishness, or can the dual-track structure be explained without committing to either view? Reconstruct the implicit anthropology, distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what it merely accommodates, and say where it is philosophically underdetermined—compatible with several theories—versus where it quietly rules out rival accounts. Then specify which institutional elements—the dialogue-based reflection or the behavioral conditioning—would become redundant or incoherent if one adopted a consistently Mencian view, and which would fail to justify themselves under a consistently Xunzian view.
