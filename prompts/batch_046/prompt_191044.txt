I'm working out what counts as corruption by testing my gut reactions across cases that mix gift-giving, clientelism, and cross-cultural norms, and I want to know whether there's one principle behind them or whether I have to abandon the idea that Western frameworks apply everywhere.

Here are my verdicts. A mayor hires his genuinely best-qualified cousin for a planning post but hides the relation: corruption, though mild. A businessperson gives expensive whiskey to an official two weeks after a license was granted, with no explicit deal: not corruption—this is relational maintenance, not a transaction. A politician sends infrastructure funds to her supporters' district, which genuinely needs a bridge and fits the discretionary formula: not corruption, even if it smells like clientelism. A firm pays an illegal-but-universal "facilitation fee" so goods clear customs in one day instead of months; without it, trade collapses: corruption, yet I hesitate because locally it's treated as a service charge. A tribal elder distributes crisis food first to his clan, as his culture expects kin priority: not corruption—applying a Western universalist standard here feels like a category mistake. A Western NGO refuses all such fees, reports officials, and pulls out, leaving aid undistributed: not corrupt, but wrong about what corruption means in that context.

The case I keep reversing on: a hospital administrator accepts a small annual New Year's gift from a supplier whose fair-priced contract hasn't changed in a decade, and the gift is never mentioned in negotiations. Is this corruption, or just ritual reciprocity?

Is there a coherent principle—perhaps one that turns on a breach of institutional role, or on whether an exchange is transactional rather than relational—that captures all six verdicts? If so, state it precisely, show which case motivates each clause, predict its verdict on the unlisted judicial-patronage case (where judges rule for a patron's allies not because of bribes but because loyalty is the legitimate basis of appointment), and tell me honestly which of my six verdicts it forces me to drop. If no single principle works, say whether the conflict is best resolved by giving up on cross-cultural universalism or by revising my intuitions about the facilitation-fee and tribal-elder cases.
