I am a retired experimental physicist who, since leaving the laboratory, has turned my attention to moral philosophy and the history of science. I have sketched an essay that interprets Benjamin Franklin's program for moral perfection—his catalog of thirteen virtues and his practice of maintaining a weekly accounting ledger—as an anticipatory form of cybernetic self-regulation, though one that operates as an open-loop controller rather than a closed-loop system. Specifically, I contend that Franklin's manual recording of faults lacks the continuous automatic feedback and integral correction term that characterize a true thermostat or servomechanism, which may account for his acknowledged failure to reach steady-state perfection. I further propose that his strategic reordering of the virtues, placing Temperance at the head of the sequence so that it might facilitate the acquisition of the others, functions analogously to the prerequisite conditions in a multi-step chemical synthesis, where the yield of later stages depends on the purity of earlier intermediates. Before I refine these notes into a full argument, I would value your critical comments: does this engineering analogy illuminate Franklin's method, or does it distort the normative and self-cultivating aims that motivated his project? I also wonder whether describing his ledger as lacking an integral term is historically defensible, given that he evidently modified his habits in response to accumulated errors. Please provide your response in American English.
