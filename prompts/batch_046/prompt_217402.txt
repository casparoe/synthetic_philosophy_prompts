I am a doctoral student in philosophy of religion and moral psychology, and I am currently developing a chapter that extends Hume's arguments in his suppressed essay on suicide into contemporary debates about physician-assisted dying. Hume's central claim, as I understand it, is that suicide is not an offense against God—who governs through general laws rather than particular interventions—nor against society, provided one's withdrawal does not harm dependents, and that the customary attachment to life is sustained more by irrational superstition and violent passions than by calm reason. I want to apply this framework to a problem that arose only after his death: modern regulatory regimes—such as those in Oregon, Canada, and parts of Europe—that require psychiatric evaluation to distinguish rational requests for assisted death, typically in terminal physical illness, from pathological requests driven by depression or other mental disorders. This gatekeeping model implicitly treats certain mental states as distorting cognition in a way that invalidates autonomy, which seems to parallel Hume's distinction between calm passions, which reveal our true interests, and violent passions, which mislead judgment; yet Hume also treats the judgment that continued life is a burden as a rational assessment that need not be pathologized, creating a tension I am trying to resolve.

Specifically, I would like you to reconstruct Hume's likely position on whether he would endorse this psychiatric-gatekeeping distinction or reject it as a secularized form of the religious and social prejudice he criticized. How would his empiricism and his theory of the association of ideas bear on cases where a patient with a severe but non-terminal condition—perhaps involving what contemporary Dutch and Canadian frameworks call existential suffering, meaning a loss of meaning rather than physical pain—requests death? Would his commitment to observable harm and social sympathy allow him to recognize existential suffering as a legitimate ground, or would it compel him to dismiss such suffering as insufficiently objective? In your answer, please clearly separate what is well-established in Hume's texts—such as his rejection of theological objections and his emphasis on sympathy and custom—from interpretations that remain genuinely contested among Hume scholars, including whether his moral psychology ultimately permits genuinely autonomous self-destruction or subordinates individual judgment to the preservation of social bonds. Do not present a disputed interpretive claim as the scholarly consensus, and please indicate where your reconstruction depends on speculative philosophical extension rather than direct textual evidence.
