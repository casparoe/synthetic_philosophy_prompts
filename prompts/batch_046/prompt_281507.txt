I have been working through several multi-causal explanations in modern historiography—particularly accounts of rapid institutional collapse that assign causal weight to economic strain, elite defection, and symbolic rupture—when I noticed a recurring explanatory structure that I cannot place precisely within the philosophy of causation or history. The pattern runs as follows. The historian identifies three or four conditions that are each said to be indispensable, yet none is presented as sufficient on its own or as replaceable by the others. Crucially, however, each factor is assigned a relational modal role—one is characterized as the structural precondition, another as the accelerant, a third as the triggering occasion—where the content of that role is defined relative to the presence of the others. Consequently, if one attempts to run a standard counterfactual decomposition—asking, for instance, whether the outcome would still have occurred had only the economic strain been present while the other two were absent—the explanation resists the test, not because the historian claims the remaining conditions were sufficient, but because the assigned role of economic strain as precondition is said to presuppose the presence of an accelerant and a trigger for its causal efficacy to be activated. In other words, the explanation constructs what one might call interlocking indispensability: the causal powers of each factor are specified relationally, so that removing any one does not merely subtract a component from a sufficient set, but collapses the modal architecture that made the others causally operative in the first place.

This seems related to, but not identical with, the familiar problem of causal overdetermination, where multiple sufficient conditions are present and any one would have produced the outcome alone. It also differs from the insufficient-but-necessary-part framework, in which each factor is an indispensable component of a collectively sufficient condition that is itself unnecessary; in that model, the parts are still independently identifiable, and counterfactual dependence can be traced through them. Here, by contrast, the parts are not independently identifiable outside the relational grid. I am also unsure whether this is simply an instance of explanatory pluralism—the view that different explanations answer different why-questions—or whether it represents a distinct failure mode in historical reasoning, perhaps closer to what some methodologists call causal holism applied to narrative explanation. Does this configuration have an established name in the philosophy of history or the metaphysics of causation? If not, how should one classify it: as a variant of overdetermination with relationally defined parts, as a special form of causal selection bias in narrative construction, or as something else? I would particularly value a clarification of where its boundaries lie—under what conditions does a multi-causal explanation cross from legitimate relational specification into this kind of non-decomposable interlocking structure, and how can one tell the difference in practice?
