After a long and somewhat heated discussion with a friend over coffee, I found myself unable to defend a claim I still think is correct: if eliminative materialism about beliefs and desires is right, then it should apply with equal force to institutions and not merely to individual brains. We had been arguing about the September 2008 decision not to rescue Lehman Brothers, and she kept framing the episode in folk-psychological terms — that Treasury and Federal Reserve officials collectively "believed" systemic risk was containable and "desired" to preserve market discipline. I want you to take the eliminativist thesis seriously and apply it directly to that decision. Do not treat institutional language as harmless metaphor; instead, identify the actual organizational information flows, regulatory incentive structures, and embedded policy commitments that produced the outcome, and show precisely how these replace the explanatory work that belief-desire psychology pretends to do.

Then push the analysis in both directions. First, does extending eliminativism to collective agents expose a weakness in the view? Individual brains might eventually yield to neuroscientific replacement, but distributed bureaucracies lack a unified physical substrate, so perhaps folk psychology survives here by default. Or does the Lehman case actually strengthen eliminativism, showing that even in high-stakes institutional choice we never needed mental-state attributions to explain outcomes? Second, address responsibility: if no literal beliefs or desires were present in the decision-making system, on what grounds — if any — can we hold particular actors or the institution itself blameworthy for the cascade that followed? I am not asking for a reading list or citations; I want to know whether this real episode confirms, strains, or silently presupposes the very mental ontology that eliminativism denies.
