I'm putting together a proposal for an independent six-month research project—deliverable a 10,000-word paper suitable for an interdisciplinary philosophy of science or AI-epistemology venue—on an underexplored tension in Alan Turing's writings about machine education. The standard scholarship reads the child-machine analogy in Computing Machinery and Intelligence (1950) and the earlier Intelligent Machinery report (1948) as either proto-behaviorist or as a loose heuristic for connectionist training. I want to press a sharper, more precise question: does Turing's pedagogical model, in which an educator shapes an initially unorganized system through reward, punishment, and curated experience, commit him to the view that a learning machine's internal states are epistemically inaccessible in principle? Or does the analogy actually presuppose that the educator can, and must, attribute representational content to hidden intermediate structures that are not directly observable?

The framing needs to be exact. Turing compares educating a child to educating a machine: both begin as largely unstructured notebooks, and both are modified through environmental interaction rather than explicit rule-writing. Contemporary readers usually take this to mean that knowledge in a machine is reducible to stable behavioral disposition. But if that is the whole story, the educator has no conceptual basis for distinguishing between a machine that has genuinely learned a rule and one that has been conditioned to produce correct outputs by coincidence or spurious correlation. Conversely, if the educator can make that distinction—if they can coherently say the machine represents a concept rather than merely behaving consistently—then Turing's framework requires some form of internalist, representational attribution that survives the opacity of the training process. Which reading does the text actually support, and can a stable third option—weak internalism, in which representations are real but epistemically partial—be reconstructed?

This is genuinely open because the existing literature tends to treat strict behaviorism and explicit representationalism as exhaustive, ignoring the possibility that Turing's educator operates with partial, indispensable attributions of internal structure that are not fully verifiable. Resolving the ambiguity matters for current debates about interpretability and mechanistic understanding in large-scale learning systems. If Turing's own educational philosophy accommodates representational intermediates that the educator cannot fully observe, then the black-box narrative of his view is conceptually misleading, and the modern search for internal explanations has an unexpected historical precedent rather than being a purely contemporary imposition.

The method will be conceptual reconstruction rather than archival discovery. I will work from standard editions of the two texts, mapping every passage in which Turing discusses education, reward, punishment, the suppression of undesirable tendencies, and the limits of explicit programming. I will formalize three interpretations: strict behaviorism, instrumental representationalism (representations as useful fictions), and weak internalism (representations as real but partially opaque). Each will be tested against Turing's remarks on the notebook analogy, his skepticism about writing out explicit rules, and his claim that the educator modifies the machine indirectly through experience. I will avoid importing modern reinforcement-learning vocabulary except where the conceptual parallel is direct and defensible, and I will not produce a citation-heavy literature review—only enough conceptual mapping to show that the standard binary reading is incomplete.

A successful result will defend weak internalism: the educator's practice of guiding training presupposes representational attributions that are indispensable but not fully transparent, which dissolves the false choice between transparent programming and opaque conditioning. Failure will be a demonstration that the texts are genuinely indeterminate between behaviorism and instrumentalism, with no coherent intermediate option available, in which case the project will conclude that Turing's pedagogical analogy is less philosophically determinate than is usually assumed.

The main scope risk is anachronism—reading modern concerns about black-box optimization or reward hacking back into 1940s conceptual vocabulary. I will control for this by restricting comparison to structural features of Turing's pedagogical model rather than to specific technical mechanisms.
