In debates over Thomas Szasz’s well-known claim that “mental illness” is a myth—where the claim is not that people do not suffer, but that their suffering is miscategorized as a medical disease analogous to pneumonia or diabetes—both defenders and critics repeatedly reach for an analogy between psychiatric diagnosis and involuntary commitment, on the one hand, and the Inquisition’s identification of heresy and witchcraft, on the other. In this mapping, the psychiatrist plays the inquisitor; the diagnostic manual’s categorical taxonomy plays the Inquisition’s catalog of identifying marks; involuntary commitment plays the ritual punishment or execution; the patient who denies the diagnosis plays the heretic who refuses to confess; and the underlying harm being prevented is not biological dysfunction but social disorder or spiritual corruption. The analogy is typically used to license a specific conclusion: that psychiatric categories are not descriptive of biological dysfunction but are normative instruments of social control, and that what we call mental illness is therefore better understood as deviant behavior or existential crisis mislabeled by medical authority.

Audit this analogy carefully. First, lay out an explicit correspondence table: for each element on the source side (the inquisitor, the manual of marks, the punishment ritual, the accused, the theory of harm, the institutional church-state), state what plays the corresponding role on the psychiatric side, and note any elements in the source that have no clear counterpart. Second, examine the disanalogies—modern voluntary treatment, the presence or absence of neurobiological correlates, legal safeguards surrounding commitment, the patient’s own subjective experience of suffering, the pluralism of psychiatric schools, and the absence of a unified theological-political authority directing practice. Separate the disanalogies that genuinely undermine the analogy’s intended argumentative work (that psychiatry is purely coercive social control masquerading as medicine) from those that are incidental or cosmetic. Third, state whether Szasz’s core conclusion—that mental illness is a myth in the specific sense of being a non-medical, socially constructed role—survives once these breaks are on the table, or whether the Inquisition comparison was carrying the normative weight of the argument all along. Fourth, if this analogy collapses or is severely weakened, suggest whether a different analogy (perhaps to disability classification, criminal law, or educational tracking) better captures what is genuinely at stake without smuggling in the same coercive, pre-modern imagery.
