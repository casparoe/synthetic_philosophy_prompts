Writing on the history of economic thought, I keep running into what I call a Smithian paradox of scaled specialization. Smith treats the division of labor as the principal source of opulence and extends that logic to international exchange, urging countries to specialize and trade without artificial restriction. By that reasoning, a colony devoted to producing raw materials exclusively for the metropole should appear as nothing more than an extreme geographic instance of specialization—functionally analogous to a distant workshop—rather than as an economic distortion in itself. Yet Smith is sharply critical of the colonial monopoly, the Navigation Acts, and the mercantile system that binds colonies to the mother country. My confusion is whether his opposition can be sustained on strictly economic grounds internal to his framework of natural liberty, or whether it necessarily smuggles in a political or moral premise—perhaps about coercion, consent, or monopoly privilege—that his system is usually read as having rendered redundant. What is the precise constraint in Smith's account that distinguishes legitimate specialization from imperial extraction, and does resolving the tension require going outside political economy?
