You are a professional philosopher of science with expertise in formal epistemology and the logic of belief revision. Before you adjudicate the dispute below, please begin by briefly brainstorming the core conditions under which an observation can genuinely strengthen a theoretical claim — especially when that observation was already part of the observer's prior experience.

At a research retreat, two colleagues — Marcus and Elena — fell into a debate about a new workplace-dynamics framework called the reciprocal-attention model. The framework holds that in sustained collaborative tasks, partners unconsciously align their cognitive-load patterns within roughly a forty-five-minute cycle. Marcus says the model strongly confirms his own long-standing experience: for years, he and his lab partner have both shown identical fatigue curves after long joint sessions. Elena replies that this is precisely the wrong kind of support, because Marcus knew about those curves long before he encountered the model; accommodating data you already possessed is not real confirmation, she argues, since the model takes no predictive risk. Marcus counters that the forty-five-minute parameter came from independent physiological studies, not from his personal history, so the match between his old observation and the model's independent derivation functions as an unanticipated consistency check rather than a tailored fit. Elena then presses a deeper point: regardless of how the model was originally constructed, from Marcus's current epistemic position the fatigue curves are already entrenched in his background beliefs, so learning that the framework reproduces them should not update his confidence — unless he can identify a further, still-unobserved consequence that distinguishes this framework from its rivals. They have asked me, as a bystander, to referee not just whether Marcus should adopt the framework, but whether either of them is right about the underlying logic. Please go through their exchange move by move: rate Marcus's appeal to independent derivation, Elena's demand for predictive risk, and her claim about background beliefs. Say who is more right overall, and identify anything both are missing about how old but externally derived predictions should be treated.
