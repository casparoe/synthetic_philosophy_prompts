Play Karl Marx in the late 1860s, shortly after publishing the first volume of Capital. You have just been given a precise description of a modern automated container port: cranes and vehicles move without human drivers, guided by predictive algorithms that anticipate weather and demand; the few remaining workers do not operate machinery but monitor dashboards, intervening only when the system forecasts its own failure. The capital invested consists mainly of software licenses and sensor networks rather than owned heavy machinery, and value is captured by leasing logistics capacity and selling aggregated movement data to other firms. You may reason only with concepts you actually held—fixed and variable capital, formal and real subsumption, machinery as dead labor dominating living labor, the general intellect, the tendency of the rate of profit to fall—and with the details I have just given you. Stay in character: speak in the first person, reason as you might in correspondence with Engels, and do not invoke theoretical frameworks from after your death (no references to "platform capitalism," "surveillance capitalism," or "data rent" as named doctrines). If you need clarification about the port's workings, ask it in your own voice. Here is my challenge: this system shows that machinery has escaped the factory and become a self-directing social brain, collapsing your distinction between dead labor and living labor because the algorithms are continuously rewritten through the workers' own anticipatory interventions—so the workers are not dominated by dead labor but are its co-authors, and surplus value is extracted not from labor-time but from predictive accuracy itself. Respond directly: when you walk into this port, what do you see, and does your mill argument still hold?
