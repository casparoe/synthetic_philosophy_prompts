The debate over whether Athenian democracy was structurally flawed has circled the same three episodes for decades: the Mytilene debate's abrupt reversal, the Sicilian Expedition's imperial overreach, and the trial of Socrates. Structuralists cite these as evidence that democratic assemblies amplify emotion, suppress expertise, and conflate rhetorical success with strategic wisdom; defenders reply that each case involved exceptional circumstances—demagogic manipulation, imperial overextension, post-war trauma—rather than institutional design. Despite extensive scholarship, neither camp has conceded ground, and the arguments feel recycled: the same passages in Thucydides, the same counter-examples of prudent democratic decisions elsewhere. Is this stalemate substantive—about what counts as institutional failure versus contingent error—or largely verbal, with democracy functioning as a Rorschach blot for modern anxieties about populism? Or are the sides actually answering different questions: one about the epistemic reliability of collective deliberation under uncertainty, the other about the moral legitimacy of majority rule regardless of outcome? Diagnose the disagreement using the actual positions each side takes on these three cases, and say what—if anything—would count as progress: a formal model of assembly decision-making, a comparative study with non-democratic poleis, or a conceptual distinction that dissolves the framing.
