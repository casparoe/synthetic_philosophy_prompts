Please describe the most important implications of Scanlon's contractualist resolution of the aggregation problem—particularly his insistence that the wrongness of an action depends on whether a principle allowing it could be reasonably rejected by each affected individual taken separately—for evaluating institutional policies that distribute probabilistic micro-burdens across very large populations, such as incremental environmental degradation or algorithmic risk-scoring regimes, where each person's individual claim is too weak to justify rejection but the cumulative effect produces catastrophic structural harm. In particular, discuss whether contractualism can consistently condemn these policies without smuggling in an aggregate welfare calculus that it officially rejects, how the strength of individual claims should be calibrated when harm is diffuse and mediated by complex causal chains, and what this reveals about the limits of pairwise justifiability as a foundation for systemic moral evaluation.
