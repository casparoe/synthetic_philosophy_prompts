Design a 90-minute seminar session for twelve graduate students in political philosophy. The session should make participants feel, from the inside, Simone Weil’s structural critique of political parties—specifically, her claim that parties do not merely promote bad beliefs but systematically disable a precise cognitive virtue she called attention: the disciplined, patient suspension of partisan alignment that allows reality to impose itself on judgment, a capacity she linked both to Cartesian method and to the ascetic discipline of manual labor. Do not begin with Weil’s name or with any lecture; the students should encounter the dynamic through action and only learn the framing in the debrief.

Deliverable: a runnable simulation with explicit rules, roles, and material needs, plus a structured debrief.

Simulation design: Divide the group into three parties of four. Assign each party a hidden priority—such as distributive equality, collective security, or cultural continuity—that they must defend through rapid collective decisions about a fictional governance crisis. Introduce a hidden scoring mechanism: each party has a whip who distributes points not for argument quality but for speed and unanimity of alignment. At the same time, give each individual private attention tokens that can be spent to ask a clarifying question that delays the vote and reveals cross-party information embedded in the crisis scenario; spending a token, however, deducts from the party’s collective score. Structure the crisis so that the hidden resolution logic requires information only available through token expenditure, yet the scoring system makes expenditure costly. After 45 minutes, halt the simulation, have the groups vote on a final resolution without discussion, and reveal the hidden logic and the token economy.

Debrief (remaining 45 minutes): First, ask participants to map specific moments—the pressure from the whip, the choice to hoard or spend tokens, the relief of rapid agreement—onto parts of Weil’s analysis. Second, predict what will reliably go wrong when this is run (for instance, one party will likely collapse into strategic cynicism, or students will try to game the token system) and state how the facilitator should respond without breaking the simulation’s logic. Third, be explicit about the feature of Weil’s idea that the simulation cannot reproduce—namely, the spiritual and ascetic dimension of attention tied to factory experience and to a Cartesian discipline of method—and explain how the facilitator must supply this verbally in the closing minutes. Fourth, warn against a likely misreading: that Weil’s critique is merely about inefficient party rules that could be reformed; clarify how the facilitator should correct this and insist that, for Weil, the problem is structural, not remediable by better internal democracy.

Keep the instructions formal. Do not ask for bibliographies, citations, or reading lists. Do not include verbatim quotations from Weil’s texts; if you reference her language, paraphrase without quotation marks.
