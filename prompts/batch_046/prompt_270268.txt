I've noticed a recurring argumentative move in debates about whether the sciences carve nature at its joints, and I lack a precise term for it. Here are two concrete patterns. In biology, researchers defend reproductive isolation or phylogenetic clustering as tracking real divisions among organisms, but when confronted with bacteria—where horizontal gene transfer blurs lineage boundaries—they do not conclude that bacterial "species" are ontologically different kinds from mammalian ones. Instead they preserve the reality of the joint inside each domain and treat the cross-domain divergence as a context-sensitive application, not as a failure of joint-carving itself. In chemistry, bonds are presented as real structural features, yet when asked whether a hydrogen bond in a folded protein and a covalent bond in a diatomic molecule belong to the same ontological category, practitioners typically invoke scale, environment, and functional role to absorb the difference, rather than splitting "bond" into two natural kinds or abandoning its reality. The shared structure seems to be: maintain that nature has joints within a disciplinary boundary, but treat differences across boundaries as pragmatic, scale-dependent, or framework-relative adjustments rather than as evidence that the carving is globally mind-dependent. This is not standard ontological pluralism, since pluralists usually allow multiple equally valid global carvings, whereas here there is apparently one correct carving per box with the boxes themselves drawn by us. It also differs from straightforward anti-realism about kinds, because the internal joints are taken as genuinely discovered. Does this configuration have an established name in philosophy of science? How precisely does it relate to local realism, perspectival realism, and domain-specific naturalism? And if there is no standard label, what term and boundary conditions—for example, whether the pattern extends to the physics-chemistry interface or to psychological versus neural kinds—would best capture it?
