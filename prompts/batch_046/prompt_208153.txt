I'm putting together a 90-minute workshop for about sixteen graduate students in a political philosophy and global justice seminar, and I want them to experience—rather than merely discuss—why Amartya Sen's entitlement framework makes famine intelligible as a political and economic failure rather than a natural disaster. We've been reading his analysis of the 1943 Bengal famine, the Irish famine, and the Great Leap Forward, and the students keep defaulting to the intuitive explanation that famine means there wasn't enough food to go around. I need a structured in-class simulation with assigned roles, hidden information, and symbolic stakes so that the group runs into an entitlement collapse firsthand, realizes only gradually that aggregate supply never dropped, and then sees in debrief why observers routinely misdiagnose such events as scarcity-driven natural disasters.

Design the simulation. Specify the setting precisely: a seminar table arranged into three entitlement groups—direct producers who hold food tokens, wage-laborers whose survival depends on exchanging labor tokens for food at a market rate, and intermediaries with storage capacity who set exchange ratios—plus one or two "outside observers" who receive private envelope cards revealing that the total food-token pool is actually stable or rising. The rules should allow a shock (for example, a sudden labor-market freeze or a speculative price spike introduced by the facilitator at minute 25) to strip one group of survival tokens while leaving aggregate supply untouched. Crucially, build in an information asymmetry: the observers can see the stability card, but the rules should block credible transmission—perhaps they may only communicate in writing through a mediator who can distort messages—so the affected group interprets its loss as a harvest failure or absolute shortage rather than as a breakdown in exchange entitlement. Use only symbolic tokens or course-participation points, never real money, and include a clear payoff scheme that rewards survival within the exercise and records who starved symbolically.

Provide a minute-by-minute timeline for the 90 minutes, list minimal materials (role cards, hidden envelopes, a simple board tracking aggregate supply versus group holdings), and explain how the facilitator introduces the shock without naming it as an entitlement failure. Then lay out the debrief: map the moment when participants blame "bad weather" or "shortage" onto Sen's distinction between direct and exchange entitlement; identify what predictably breaks the first time you run this—likely intermediaries hoarding defensively or informed observers breaking the communication rules—and say how to correct it in the moment; and conclude with an honest admission of what the simulation cannot reproduce, specifically the long-run political dynamics and state-policy choices that turned entitlement failures into mass death in the historical cases I mentioned, so that I supply that context verbally rather than pretending the game captured it. The design should stay firmly within Sen's well-known conceptual vocabulary but inject an unfamiliar institutional twist—this hidden-observer, multi-layer mechanism—so students cannot simply replay a standard famine narrative they've encountered before.
