In a graduate seminar I am running on moral character, the debate over whether virtue ethics survives the situationist critique has stalled in a structurally familiar but unresolved way. The situationist side points to the well-documented empirical finding that cross-situational behavioral correlations are often low—hovering around .30—suggesting that people lack the global, stable character traits that virtue ethics seems to presuppose. From this they conclude that traits such as honesty or courage are largely predictive fictions, and that behavior is driven more by situational features than by internal dispositions. The virtue-ethical response, refined over decades, is that this measurement strategy commits a category error: virtue is not a rigid behavioral disposition but involves phronesis—practical wisdom that integrates reason, emotion, and perceptual sensitivity in a manner genuinely responsive to context. A courageous agent should respond differently to a minor social slight than to a mortal threat; variability across situations is therefore a feature of wisdom, not evidence of its absence.

Yet neither camp has moved in years. The situationists treat the virtue-ethical retreat as an unfalsifiable normative idealization detached from empirical psychology, while virtue ethicists treat the situationist data as measuring the wrong target entirely—descriptive consistency rather than normative excellence. I want to push past this familiar standoff and ask whether the stall conceals a deeper structural problem. One possibility is that the disagreement is partly verbal: "trait" means a predictive behavioral regularity for the situationist, but a normatively structured capacity for practical reasoning for the virtue ethicist, so they are not actually denying each other's claims. Another, more specific possibility is that there is a hidden formal question about whether practical wisdom can be decomposed into stable sub-capacities—such as emotional regulation, perspective-taking, or attentional control—that might show cross-situational stability at some appropriate level of analysis, or whether it is an irreducibly holistic second-order capacity that regulates first-order responses without itself being globally consistent across contexts. There may also be an orthogonal-projects problem: one side asks what predicts behavior, the other asks what constitutes moral excellence, and they treat these as competing answers to a single empirical question.

Given this landscape, I am asking for a diagnosis rather than a verdict. Is the disagreement substantive or verbal? Do the sides differ about hidden empirical claims—perhaps about whether phronesis produces measurable stability when the unit of analysis is practical reasoning rather than isolated action—about standards of evidence, about which cases should count as data, or about bedrock intuitions regarding whether moral evaluation requires predictive consistency? Is there an ill-posed question at the center, such as the expectation that a normative theory of character must be vindicated by the same behavioral correlations that descriptive personality psychology seeks? And if your diagnosis holds, what would actually count as progress: a sharp conceptual distinction between descriptive and normative traits, an experimental design that measures reasoning processes rather than behavioral outputs, a formal argument about how to aggregate context-sensitive responses, or a reframing that separates the predictive project from the evaluative one? Or should we conclude that the impasse is permanent, and if so, why?
