Grade the following examination response. The prompt given to the student was: "Distinguish Hume's is-ought gap from Moore's naturalistic fallacy. Then assess whether thick ethical concepts—descriptive-evaluative terms such as cruel or treacherous—undermine the separation each thinker defends. Finally, evaluate whether an a posteriori identification of goodness with a natural property (for example, evolutionary fitness) escapes Moore's open-question argument in the manner of synthetic identities such as water being H2O."

The student wrote:
Hume's is-ought gap, presented in Book III of A Treatise of Human Nature, holds that no normative conclusion can be validly derived from purely descriptive premises; logic alone never supplies the inferential step from is to ought. Moore's naturalistic fallacy, introduced in Principia Ethica, targets definitional reduction: identifying the non-natural property of goodness with any natural property, such as pleasure or evolutionary survival. The open-question argument reveals the mistake—if good meant pleasant, asking whether pleasure is good would be redundant, yet the question remains genuinely open.

Thick ethical concepts appear to blur this boundary because describing an action as cruel already includes moral condemnation. One might think this embeds evaluation within description, undermining both separations. However, the blending is conceptual rather than logical, so Hume's derivation gap remains intact. Against Moore, one can reply that the evaluative component of a thick concept is merely a non-natural addition to an otherwise descriptive core, preserving his distinction.

More significantly, synthetic naturalism offers a stronger defense. If goodness is identical to evolutionary fitness in the same empirical manner that water is identical to H2O, then the identification is synthetic rather than analytic. Moore's open-question argument applies only to conceptual reductions, so this synthetic identity avoids the fallacy entirely. Consequently, thick concepts show that evaluation can be tied to natural descriptions, and synthetic identification shows that normative properties may be real natural properties discovered by science. Together they suggest that the is-ought separation is a feature of logical form alone, and that Moore's commitment to non-natural properties is superfluous once empirical identification is permitted.
