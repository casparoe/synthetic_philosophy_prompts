Two members of a decision-theory reading group I attend have been arguing about whether coherence arguments genuinely compel expected utility maximization in contexts that matter for prosaic AI alignment. I am reporting this as a bystander and am not signaling which side I take.

The exchange went like this:

Maya opened by saying that Dutch-book arguments are decisive: any agent with probabilistic beliefs that violates expected utility maximization can be pumped for money by a bookmaker who knows the agent's credences. She added that this applies directly to artificial agents—if an AI's reward-modeling produces non-EU preferences over outcomes, it is in principle exploitable by an adversary or by its own environment.

Leo replied that the Dutch-book construction requires precise, shared betting odds tied exactly to the agent's credences. In radical uncertainty—where an AI lacks well-defined probabilities over outcomes, especially over possible specifications of its own reward function—there is no mechanism to construct the requisite bets. So coherence does not mandate EU; it only mandates internal consistency with whatever decision rule the agent actually employs, such as maximin or ambiguity-averse rules.

Maya countered that one can always expand the state space. If the agent is uncertain about probabilities, model that uncertainty with second-order beliefs. Once the state space is expanded, EU maximization over that expanded space is still required by coherence. Rejecting EU, she said, is not escaping coherence but hiding probabilities inside a higher-order structure.

Leo responded that this expansion is not free. It requires a prior over priors, and without independent justification that prior is question-begging. For an AI facing ontological uncertainty about its utility function, imposing a second-order prior is a substantive normative commitment, not a bare coherence requirement. Therefore coherence arguments do not uniquely support EU in prosaic alignment contexts; they support consistency, which maximin can satisfy just as well.

Maya then introduced a further point: even if maximin is consistent within a single decision, there is a diachronic coherence problem specific to self-modifying agents. A maximin agent today could be intertemporally exploited by a future version of itself that adopts EU tomorrow, creating a cross-time money pump across self-modification stages. So EU emerges from coherence across time, not merely within one choice.

Leo said that this misapplies the framework. The intertemporal argument treats a future self as an adversarial bookmaker, which is not warranted. Dynamic consistency requires stability conditions, but those do not uniquely select EU; they could stabilize maximin if the agent binds itself to that rule. He concluded that Maya is conflating dynamic consistency with expected utility maximization.

Neither will concede.
