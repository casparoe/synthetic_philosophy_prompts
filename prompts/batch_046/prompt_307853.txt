I've just come out of a long, exhausting dinner argument with my closest friend, and rather than asking you to declare a winner, I want help mapping the philosophical structure that we're actually fighting over. Every weekend I volunteer at a small nonprofit sanctuary that houses retired laboratory animals—mostly pigs and rats that have completed research protocols and now live out their lives there. From everything I understand about their emotional and cognitive lives, the two groups occupy broadly comparable ranges in the dimensions that seem morally relevant: both appear capable of sustained social bonds, anticipatory distress, and something like preference-formation. Last week a private donor offered the sanctuary a substantial grant, but with an explicit contractual condition: the funds may only be used for the pigs' care, enclosure expansion, and veterinary resources, and the rats are explicitly excluded by name. If we accept, the pigs live noticeably better; if we refuse on principle, both groups remain in adequate but cramped quarters, and no one receives the donation.

My friend argues that signing the agreement makes me complicit in a morally arbitrary hierarchy. She says I'm treating membership in a biological category—pig versus rat—as a legitimate reason to distribute relief from suffering differently, even though the capacities that ground moral concern don't differ in any relevant way between the two groups. I countered that refusing doesn't help the rats at all, since the donor won't redirect the money, and that it feels like a kind of moral vanity to deny real, tangible improvements to some sentient beings just to avoid endorsing a distinction I don't actually believe in. She replied that the harm isn't in the outcome but in the act of legitimizing the boundary: by contracting around it, I'm treating it as administratively valid, which is precisely how arbitrary distinctions become entrenched. I walked away feeling that there's a deeper question about whether working inside a flawed structure necessarily endorses its flaws, especially when the alternative isn't reform but mere absence of benefit.

What I'm asking is not for generic charity advice, but for an analysis of which philosophical commitments actually govern this choice. Is there a defensible distinction between benefiting from an unjust distribution and endorsing the principle behind it? And does that distinction collapse when the injustice is written into the contract itself, rather than being an accidental feature of the environment? I'm particularly interested in whether my resistance to refusing the grant depends on smuggling in the very idea I'm trying to reject—that biological category carries some hidden moral weight—or whether there's a coherent framework that allows me to reject the boundary in principle while still accepting its contingent benefits without hypocrisy.
