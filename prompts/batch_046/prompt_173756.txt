(1) The teleological suspension of the ethical occurs when a single individual stands in an absolute relation to the absolute, such that universal ethical obligations are suspended for the sake of a higher telos.

(2) For this suspension to be genuinely religious rather than mere criminal violence, there must be a structural distinction available—specifically, Abraham's silence. (3) If the justification for suspension is incommunicable, then it is not subject to rational evaluation by others; but if it is communicable and universalizable, it collapses back into the ethical (e.g., a universal rule that fathers ought to kill sons when commanded).

(4) Therefore, the only feature distinguishing legitimate suspension from murder is the private, incommunicable character of the agent's relation to God.

(5) But now apply this structure reflexively: to determine whether Abraham's private relation is genuinely to God or merely to a delusion, we require some criterion—whether internal coherence, transformative effect, or consistency with revelation.

(6) Any such criterion must be either communicable and universal (hence falling back into the ethical and undermining the suspension's uniqueness) or incommunicable and private (hence useless for distinguishing true faith from psychosis in any particular case, including Abraham's).

(7) So Kierkegaard's position requires what we might call a second-order suspension: we must suspend not only the ethical prohibition against killing, but also the very demand for criteria that distinguish authentic religious suspension from arbitrary violence.

(8) Once second-order suspension is permitted, there is no principled stopping point; anyone who claims an incommunicable absolute relation can justify any act, and the framework provides no resources to reject them.

(9) Thus the teleological suspension, if taken seriously, proves too much: it dissolves ethics and simultaneously dissolves the boundary between faith and madness.
