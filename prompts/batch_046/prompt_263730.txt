For a 100-minute workshop with 16 mixed participants (undergraduates and community members) on the ethics of commemoration, design a structured map-making role-play with real but small stakes that makes participants feel why renaming streets or retaining statues is not reducible to weighing present offense against historical merit. Inject the following framing: the city is divided into four quadrants, and any proposal to alter a commemorated name in one quadrant automatically triggers an obligatory audit of all names in the other three quadrants—so removing one statue forces the group either to justify leaving others untouched or to initiate an unpredictable cascade. Give each participant a hidden role card with a distinct payoff structure—municipal archivist (rewarded for traceability of every change), descendant of a commemorated figure (rewarded for visibility of that specific legacy), urban planner (rewarded for navigational continuity), and activist (rewarded for demographic parity across quadrants)—plus a budget of symbolic-capital points they can spend to block or advance proposals. The group must physically redraw a simplified grid of twelve named locations, deciding the fate of each within 60 minutes of negotiation. Afterward, apply a predetermined outcome matrix: the final map is photographed and sent to a community archive pool, with a $5 donation per retained controversial name to one fund and $5 per changed name to another, so that no faction achieves a pure victory and every choice leaves a tangible residue.

Specify the timeline in 10-minute blocks, the rules for spending symbolic capital, the conditions that trigger a quadrant audit, and how hidden payoffs are revealed. The debrief should map specific moments—the cascade audit, conflicts over layered plaques versus blank removal, and the map’s physical layout—onto conceptual distinctions in commemorative ethics such as symbolic erasure versus layered remembrance, collective memory versus navigational utility, and procedural fairness versus substantive justice. Predict where the simulation will collapse into majoritarian voting or paralysis, and advise how the facilitator can intervene without dissolving the stakes. Finally, include an honest note identifying which feature of commemorative ethics—perhaps the non-volitional, intergenerational exposure of public space, or the long temporal arc by which moral judgments about figures shift—the workshop necessarily cannot reproduce and must be supplied directly by the facilitator.
