As a retired physicist who has only recently begun to study philosophy of social science, I should like to submit a cluster of convictions for a consistency audit. They are as follows.

(1) I endorse methodological individualism: every social explanation must ultimately bottom out in the beliefs, intentions, and actions of individual persons.

(2) I accept that social institutions exercise downward causation — a legal system or market architecture, for example, can constrain and enable individual behaviour in ways that are not reducible to an aggregation of the participants' mental states.

(3) I regard emergent properties in physics, such as superconductivity, as ontologically innocent: they are predictive conveniences with no autonomous causal powers over microphysical processes.

(4) I reject explanatory pluralism in sociology: if an agent-based simulation can reproduce a macro-regularity from micro-rules, that exhausts the explanation, and no autonomous social-level account is necessary.

(5) I treat collective intentionality — utterances such as 'we intend to strike' — as a pragmatic fiction with predictive utility but no metaphysical reality, analogous to treating a centre of mass as a point particle.

(6) I hold that statistical mechanics supplies the correct formal template for social science: macro-social states are merely probability distributions over individual micro-states and carry no ontological weight of their own.

(7) I observe that macro-social patterns — the division of labour, for instance — can remain stable across generations despite complete turnover of the individuals who instantiate them.

Please examine these commitments for joint tenability. Which pairs are genuinely in tension, which tensions are only apparent, and what is the least costly revision for each? Rank the conflicts by severity, indicate the cheapest single revision that would resolve the greatest number of problems, and state whether that revision forces me to abandon my individualist core or merely to refine it. I am particularly interested in whether my analogy between physical emergence and social downward causation is sustainable, and whether the persistence of macro-patterns across generational turnover actually undermines (1) or can be accommodated within it.
