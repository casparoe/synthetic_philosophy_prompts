I'm taking this course on political memory and we keep arguing about statues and who gets a plaque. I was trying to figure out my actual gut verdicts case by case, not just slogans, and I keep ending up with results that feel right individually but don't line up neatly.

So: Jefferson wrote equality into founding docs but kept hundreds of people enslaved. I think that makes him seriously wrong, but I also think you can't understand American democracy without engaging his ideas, and those same ideas eventually got turned against slavery. So I'd keep monuments but with heavy context, not tear them down. Churchill led resistance to Hitler, which feels world-saving, but he was openly racist and his government let the Bengal famine happen through policy choices. I still think he's mostly a great leader with a dark stain, not a villain to erase. Columbus personally tortured and enslaved people, and even his own sponsors told him to stop, so the "man of his time" excuse feels hollow to me — he should be judged harshly, maybe not uniquely evil but definitely not celebrated. But then there's that 19th-century surgeon, I think Marion Sims?, who developed important gynecological techniques by experimenting on enslaved Black women without anesthesia. For him I think the achievement is basically tainted and I wouldn't want a hospital named after him. Yet Jefferson also exploited enslaved labor, just not in a lab. What's the difference? Is it that the harm was "built into" the achievement versus "seperate" from it? Meanwhile if some modern senator knows climate change is real and still blocks action for campaign cash, I actually feel that's worse than Columbus, even though nobody dies in front of them personally. So my verdicts are: Jefferson gets contextualized honor, Churchill gets qualified praise, Columbus gets no defense, Sims gets erased, and the modern politician gets the worst judgment. Is there one principle — maybe about epistemic access, or about whether the wrong was structurally necessary to the legacy — that captures all of that? Or am I just making excuses for people I was taught to admire? And what would it say about Linnaeus, who invented modern taxonomy but also classified human races in a hierarchy? I keep going back and forth on him, and I want to know if my principle would have to overturn one of the verdicts above to stay simple.
