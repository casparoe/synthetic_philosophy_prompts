Im trying to sort out whether culture can actually cause things, or if its always just dressing up material forces. I keep going back and forth, and my gut verdicts on specific cases feel all over the place.

Weber's Protestant ethic thesis: I think he was basically right that Calvinist anxiety about salvation causally pushed people toward rational capital accumulation, but only because there was already a capitalist legal framework waiting to be filled. So values were a cause, but only through elective affinity -- they didnt do it alone. I think thats a real causal contribution, not just labeling.

But then I look at Soviet industrialization under Stalin. The official culture was all about the New Soviet Man and collective labor values, but I feel like that was pure window dressing. The real drivers were coercion, planning targets, and the security state. Here culture feels totally epiphenomenal.

Japanese quality circles after the war: the norm of group harmony (wa) made workers report defects instead of hiding them, which genuinely improved manufacturing quality. But I also think this only stuck because lifetime employment made firing people hard, so the cultural norm filled an institutional gap. So culture caused the specific form of quality improvement, but not the fact that improvement happened at all.

The honor-culture story about the American South (Nisbett and Cohen type): I want to say weak state presence caused both the honor norms and the violence, so culture is just a mediator, not an independent cause. But that conflicts with my Weber verdict, where I let values share causal work with institutions. I cant tell if Im being inconsistent.

Then theres the Victorian separate-spheres ideology: the idea that women belonged in the home. I think that came after the economic shift -- rising male wages and mechanization made home production obsolete -- and just justified it. So values followed economics.

But heres one that goes the other way: Scandinavian transparency norms in the 90s. The democratic ethos of open government came first, then actually reshaped administrative law and attracted tech investment. So values changed institutions, which then changed economic outcomes.

So my verdicts are: values can be co-causes with material conditions (Weber), they can fill institutional gaps and shape form (Japan), they can be pure rationalization (Soviet, Victorian gender roles), they can be mediators rather than roots (honor culture), and they can drive institutional change from the top down (Scandinavia).

Is there one principel that captures all of this? Maybe something like values cause outcomes only when they operate as selection environments rather than motivational engines? Or do I have to drop one of my verdicts? In particular, Im stuck on the Weber vs honor-culture pair: in one I let culture share causal credit with weak institutions, in the other I deny culture any independent credit because institutions are weak. Which one should go?

Also, what would my principle say about a case I havent listed: the spread of effective altruism norms in tech and finance since the 2010s? Did those values cause the shift in charitable giving patterns, or are they just rationalizing tax optimization and reputation management?

Please be precise about the principle, state which clause handles which case, say what it predicts for the effective altruism case, and be honest about which of my verdicts it cant save without becoming ad hoc.
