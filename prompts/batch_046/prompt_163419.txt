In my philosophy of mind seminar we have been examining the paradox of phenomenal judgment—the tension between epiphenomenalism and the apparent fact that we form accurate judgments about our own conscious experiences. The critics maintain that if phenomenal properties lack causal efficacy, then judgments about them are causally disconnected from the very states they purport to represent, which seems to undermine phenomenal knowledge altogether. The defenders reply that a judgment need not be caused by the qualia it describes; it need only be reliably correlated with them via neural mechanisms, so there is no paradox—only a missing causal link that never needed to exist. Despite years of exchange, neither camp has moved, and the arguments feel like they are circling the same three moves.

My working hypothesis is that the stall is not really about causation but about an unexamined disagreement over what counts as judgment in the first place: one side treats phenomenal judgment as a direct demonstrative reference that requires causal contact with its object, while the other treats it as an inferential report mediated entirely by subpersonal processing, analogous to confabulation phenomena in clinical neuroscience. But I am unsure whether this is a substantive dispute about mental architecture, a verbal dispute about the word reference, or two groups answering different questions—one semantic, one epistemological—under the single heading of phenomenal judgment.

Please diagnose the structure of this disagreement. Is there a hidden empirical question—perhaps about whether introspective access involves genuine causal tracking or only heuristic correlation—that neither side can resolve without leaving the armchair?
