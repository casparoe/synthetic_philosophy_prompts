The debate over whether counterfactual mugging — in which a predictor correlates your actual choice with a payoff across counterfactual worlds — and XOR blackmail — in which the predictor arranges two mutually exclusive threats so that compliance avoids exactly one — genuinely undermine causal decision theory, or instead smuggle evidential reasoning into the specification of the predictor, has persisted for years without clear resolution. Proponents of the counterexample argue that rational agents must accommodate correlations that hold across worlds even absent direct causation; defenders of causal decision theory reply that without an upstream causal pathway such correlations are decision-theoretically inert, and that treating them as binding mistakes correlation for control. Despite repeated formalization, the dialectic loops: each redesigned predictor scenario reproduces the same opposing verdicts rather than converging on a shared framework.

Diagnose why this debate has stalled. Is it a substantive disagreement about the correct conditioning principle for rational choice; a verbal disagreement turning on ambiguous uses of "prediction," "correlation," and "control"; or a case of two sides answering different questions — one normative, one metaphysical — under a single heading? Might the hidden disagreement actually concern the legitimacy of acausal cooperation: whether treating counterfactual copies of oneself as decision-relevant is rational, incoherent, or a category error?
