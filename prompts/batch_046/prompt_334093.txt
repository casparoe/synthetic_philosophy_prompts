I'm a philosophy of science reader without formal training, and I've noticed a recurring argumentative posture in debates about whether psychology, biology, or social science can be fully reconstructed from physics. The posture works like this. A proponent insists that every feature of the higher-level system must be derivable from the properties and relations of its smallest constituents; if a feature—say, intentionality, functional organization, or normative commitment—can't be so reconstructed, it is dismissed as illusory or as "nothing but" those constituents arranged differently. The proponent treats any reluctance to accept this dissolution as a lapse into mysticism. Yet when the same interlocutor is pressed on the conceptual tools they themselves employ—terms like "information," "causal role," "explanatory success," or even "truth"—they do not subject those tools to an identical reductionist demand. Instead, they treat them as neutral, unreduced scaffolding that can legitimately be used to carry out the reduction of everything else. The result is an asymmetry: the target phenomenon is aggressively dissolved downward, while the explanatory framework that performs the dissolution is protected from the same treatment. I've seen this pattern in arguments about consciousness, evolutionary adaptation, and the ontology of institutions. Is there an established name for this specific asymmetrical stance—reduction that is greedy toward its object but lenient toward its own apparatus? If there is, how does it differ from ordinary auxiliary hypotheses or from straightforward eliminativism? And if it maps onto familiar distinctions—say, between constructive explanations that build level by level and explanations that quietly depend on unreduced high-level capacities—where exactly does it sit on that spectrum? I am less interested in a bare label than in a precise carving of what the phenomenon is, where its boundaries lie, and what it is not.
