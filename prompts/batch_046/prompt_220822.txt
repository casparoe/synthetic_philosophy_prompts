Examination question:
Camus presents the absurd as arising from the confrontation between human longing for meaning and the silent, indifferent universe, and he proposes “revolt” — living with full consciousness of this tension without appeal to transcendent justification — as the appropriate response. Meanwhile, contemporary accounts such as Susan Wolf’s “fitting fulfilment” theory hold that a meaningful life requires both passionate active engagement and contribution to something of objective value, where “objective” means worthy of love independently of any particular agent’s attitudes. Consider the case of an artist who spends her adult life creating large-scale outdoor murals in a coastal region where severe weather destroys them within weeks. She claims this practice exemplifies Camusian revolt: she works with total awareness that nothing she makes will endure, yet she remains passionately committed to the act itself, refusing to treat permanence as a condition for worth. Could this artist also satisfy Wolf’s conditions for a meaningful life? In your answer, address whether Wolf’s requirement of objective value can be interpreted in a way compatible with absurdism, and whether Camus’s notion of revolt genuinely permits the kind of sustained, value-directed commitment that Wolf demands.

Student answer:
The student argues that Camus’s absurd hero, comparable to Sisyphus, finds fulfilment not in the outcome but in the consciousness of his task, so the rapid destruction of the murals is not a deficit but a reinforcement of the absurd condition. The student correctly identifies Wolf’s two conditions — active engagement and objective value — and notes that for Wolf, value must be independent of the agent’s own satisfaction. The student then claims that Wolf’s “objective value” is best understood as intersubjective durability: if a practice is recognised by a reasonable community over time as worthwhile, then it counts as objectively valuable, regardless of whether it has cosmic significance. Applying this to the artist, the student says that even though the murals vanish physically, the local artistic tradition and community recognition provide the durability Wolf requires, so the artist satisfies both conditions. The student also invokes Thomas Nagel’s view that the absurd arises from our capacity to view our lives from an external, detached perspective, and uses this to support the claim that there is no “outside” standard by which to judge value, confirming that intersubjective agreement is all the objectivity available. The student concludes that Camus and Wolf are therefore fully compatible: revolt supplies the passionate engagement, and community recognition supplies the objectivity, with neither requiring transcendent meaning.
