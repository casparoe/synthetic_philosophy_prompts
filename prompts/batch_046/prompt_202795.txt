As a journalist covering the philosophy of public policy, I am drafting a long-form essay on the moral architecture of the welfare state and want to audit my own position before committing it to print. The view I have arrived at is as follows. First, I hold that a just society should provide an unconditional social floor—subsistence-level support without work-search mandates, behavioral conditions, or moral grading of recipients—because I take the state's obligation of provision to be independent of market contribution. Second, I nevertheless accept that there is a genuine moral distinction between those who lack suitable employment through no fault of their own and those who deliberately and persistently refuse available work they are capable of performing; I reject the category of the undeserving poor, but I maintain that the latter violate a duty of fair reciprocity. Third, I believe the state may legitimately employ light-touch verification—such as random audits of income declarations—to prevent outright fraud, yet I insist that heavy conditionality, including mandatory job-search logs and sanctions for non-compliance, produces disproportionate psychological harm and should be abolished. Fourth, I claim that such verification is fully compatible with unconditionality, since it targets intentional deception rather than withdrawal from the labor market. Fifth, I also maintain that if reciprocity is a real moral duty, then a just state ought to possess some institutional mechanism to encourage or enforce contribution, lest the obligation become purely notional.

Taken together, do these commitments form a coherent package, or am I smuggling in a contradiction—particularly between unconditional provision and the enforcement of reciprocity, or between the rejection of conditionality and the acceptance of verification? Please rank the tensions by severity, identifying which pairs genuinely conflict, which only appear to, and what the least costly revision would be if I must surrender one commitment to restore consistency without abandoning the core commitment to unconditional provision. Be specific about whether the distinction between fraud and refusal of work actually does the work I need it to do, and whether my acceptance of reciprocity implicitly commits me to a form of workfare I have already rejected.
