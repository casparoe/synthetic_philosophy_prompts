I was recently discussing with some acquaintances whether the labels we apply to generations—Baby Boomers, Millennials, Generation Z, and the like—actually pick out genuine divisions in reality, or whether they function more like temporary administrative categories, comparable to sorting passengers by the hour they arrived at a train station. One participant proposed that generations might best be understood not as natural kinds possessing intrinsic essences, but rather as what he termed "historically anchored relational kinds": groupings that acquire real causal powers—shaping voting patterns, consumer habits, and linguistic norms—only in virtue of their position relative to specific technological and economic transitions, yet lacking any necessary properties that would persist independently of those conditions. I found this framing compelling but have struggled to apply it consistently without slipping back into treating cohorts as if they possessed fixed internal natures.

Could you please describe the most important implications of adopting this ontological stance toward generational cohorts? I am particularly interested in the consequences for how we ought to interpret cross-generational survey data, design social policies tied to age brackets, and avoid circular explanations in which a cohort's shared behavioral traits are simultaneously invoked as proof of the cohort's reality and then explained away as mere products of the very historical conditions that define the cohort's boundaries in the first place.
