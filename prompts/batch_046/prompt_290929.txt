Design a 90-minute, paper-only seminar activity for twelve graduate students in philosophy of mind, to be run in a standard classroom with no technology beyond pens and notecards. The goal is to make participants feel the force of the skepticism and self-dissolution in Zhuangzi's butterfly dream, but filtered through an injected conceptual problem: "indexical instability" — the idea that the first-person pronoun may fail to pick out the same subject, or any stable substrate, across the waking-to-dreaming boundary, which means coherence criteria used to verify "reality" are available in both states and therefore cannot settle which one is authoritative. Do not open with a lecture; instead, assign roles (Observer, Dream-Recorder, Verification-Judge, Skeptic) and structure the rules so that participants first construct a shared verification protocol, then apply it to a staged sequence of ambiguous experience-reports, and finally discover that the protocol succeeds equally well for reports they have been told are "waking" and "dreaming." Include: (1) the precise step-by-step procedure, materials, and role instructions; (2) a built-in hard case — a report that satisfies every coherence criterion yet is designated as a dream, forcing a choice between privileging waking experience, privileging nothing, or abandoning the first-person subject altogether; (3) a debrief that maps each stage onto the philosophical moves in the butterfly passage — transformation (wu hua), loss of fixed self, and epistemic symmetry — without using verbatim quotations from any text; (4) an explicit statement of what will predictably go wrong (e.g., participants collapse the exercise into a generic "how do we know we're not dreaming?" discussion, or one role dominates the deliberation) and how the facilitator should handle each failure mode; (5) an honest account of which feature of the idea the activity cannot reproduce (for instance, the actual phenomenology of transforming into a butterfly, or the broader Daoist metaphysical context) and how the facilitator must supply it verbally; and (6) a brief statement of what participants should come away seeing about the difference between doubting a specific belief and dissolving the subject that holds beliefs. Do not include a bibliography, reading list, citation, or reference to specific editions or commentary traditions. Write so it depends only on widely known philosophical material.
