I'm a doctoral student in political philosophy, and over the past semester I've been tracking a particular argumentative pattern in discussions about institutional design—both in my seminar on distributive justice and in informal debates with colleagues—that I can't quite map onto the standard conceptual toolkit. I want to know whether it has a specific name, or whether I'm just describing a familiar ambiguity seen from an unusual angle.

The pattern goes like this. In defending a decentralized economic arrangement—say, a labor market without collective bargaining protections—someone will argue that every participant remains fully at liberty because no particular person or office issues a direct command to accept or reject a contract. When critics respond that structural conditions—debt obligations, regional unemployment, information asymmetries—make refusal practically impossible, the defender typically replies that these conditions do not count as constraints on liberty because they result from diffuse, impersonal forces rather than from the will of an identifiable agent who could choose otherwise. So far, this is a standard move. But what interests me is the selective reversal that occurs when the same speaker confronts a centralized policy—say, a mandatory vaccination protocol or a zoning ordinance. There, the speaker will insist that citizens are made unfree not because a specific official is personally coercing them in each instance, but precisely because the rule removes a practical option from their lives regardless of who enforces it or whether enforcement is actually occurring. The criterion shifts: in the first case, freedom requires only that no agent is actively imposing a choice; in the second, freedom requires that a practical option remain genuinely available, even if the obstacle is a generally applicable rule rather than a personal command. I've noticed the same double application in debates about immigration quotas, educational admissions, and housing markets—always the same directional asymmetry, with the agent-absence standard deployed to protect diffuse structures and the option-absence standard deployed to attack centralized ones.

What I'm trying to isolate is not merely that people talk past each other using different notions of freedom. It's the specific rhetorical choreography: the strategic deployment of an agent-centered criterion in one domain and an option-centered criterion in another, without acknowledgment that the two yield contradictory verdicts about both domains if applied consistently. Is there an established concept—perhaps in the literature on the varieties of liberty, or in political rhetoric—that names exactly this pattern of selective oscillation? I know there are well-known distinctions between freedom understood as non-interference, freedom understood as non-domination, and freedom understood as effective capacity, but this configuration doesn't align neatly with any one of those accounts; it seems to be a particular mode of exploiting the gap between them. If there is no precise term, how should we characterize its boundaries—distinguishing it from ordinary conceptual pluralism on one side and from straightforward equivocation on the other?
