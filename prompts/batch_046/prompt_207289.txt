Since receiving direct-to-consumer genetic ancestry results several weeks ago, I have become preoccupied with a conceptual tension that structures how those results are presented: is DNA literally a code, or is the vocabulary of encoding, transcription, and translation purely metaphorical? Among molecular biologists, the genetic code is treated as a genuine system of representation, with correct and erroneous translations, while philosophers of biology often object that DNA lacks the semantic properties—intentionality, arbitrariness, normative standards of correctness—that true codes require. Both sides accept the same biochemical mechanisms: triplet codons, ribosomal decoding, mutation as deviation from a canonical mapping. Yet one camp holds that the genome carries information with genuinely normative content (a given codon ought to specify a particular amino acid, and misincorporation can be literally erroneous), whereas the other treats these regularities as purely causal correlations that we describe with intentional language for predictive convenience.

My working hypothesis is that the debate has stalled not because of missing empirical data, but because the disputants employ different criteria for what counts as a code—specifically, whether a code requires an intelligent interpreter, or whether evolutionary teleofunction can ground semantic normativity without design; and whether arbitrariness in the mapping is essential or merely contingent. But I am unsure whether this diagnosis is complete. Could the disagreement be substantive rather than verbal—perhaps about whether selection history can generate genuine representational content? Or are the sides answering different questions under one name: biologists concerned with engineering predictability, philosophers concerned with ontological status?

Given this, I want a diagnosis supported by what each side actually claims, together with an honest assessment of whether progress is possible. Would a formal result in information theory resolve the ambiguity? Could a synthetic-biology experiment—constructing an organism with a genuinely arbitrary, expanded genetic alphabet—demonstrate that the mapping relation is essentially symbolic rather than causally fixed? Or is the impasse a sign that the question is ill-posed, requiring reconception rather than resolution? Please diagnose the structure of the disagreement, indicate where each side is right or missing something, and say directly whether anything—conceptual, empirical, or formal—should be expected to move it.
