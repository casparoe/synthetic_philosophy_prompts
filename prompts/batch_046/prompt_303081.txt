I am a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy, but I have been auditing an online course on medieval Islamic thought and occasionally help grade practice essays for a small study group I joined there. To avoid having our discussions feel like pure repetition of textbook summaries, I introduced a framing idea of my own—call it “theological interventionism”—which asks whether Al-Ghazālī’s occasionalism can be reconstructed as an early analog to modern interventionist theories of causation, except with God rather than an experimenter as the agent who sustains invariant relations. Here is the exam-style prompt I gave the group.

Consider Al-Ghazālī’s occasionalist argument that fire does not burn cotton by any intrinsic power of its own; rather, God directly produces the burning, and the observed pairing is sustained only by a divine custom or habit (adah) in which secondary causes serve as mere occasions (asbab) rather than genuine agents. Some contemporary philosophers analyze causation in terms of interventions—manipulations of variables that reveal invariant relations under controlled conditions. Evaluate whether Al-Ghazālī’s view can be reconstructed as a form of theological interventionism, in which God is the sole intervening agent, secondary causes are non-modular markers with no invariant efficacy, and observed regularities hold only relative to divine manipulation. First, explain whether this reconstruction accurately captures Al-Ghazālī’s metaphysics or instead imports foreign concepts. Second, identify one specific feature of modern interventionist accounts—such as the distinction between direct and contributing causes, or the condition of modularity—that either usefully illuminates or significantly distorts Al-Ghazālī’s position. Be precise about where the analogy succeeds and where it collapses.

The student begins by correctly summarizing the fire-cotton example and noting that God (al-musabbib al-haqiqi) is the only true cause, while secondary causes possess no independent efficacy and function only as occasions. They correctly observe that the regular pairing is maintained by adah rather than by metaphysical necessity. Turning to the comparison, they propose that God acts like an experimenter who continuously intervenes to preserve invariant relations between variables, while the secondary causes are analogous to control conditions carrying no causal weight. They note that miracles—cases in which the usual pairing is suspended—show that the regularity is genuinely contingent, which they take to parallel the interventionist idea that an invariant holds only under a specific manipulative regime.

The student then distinguishes Al-Ghazālī from Hume by emphasizing that Al-Ghazālī posits an active divine agent rather than reducing causation to mental expectation. Nevertheless, they go on to claim that Al-Ghazālī’s concept of adah ultimately amounts to constant conjunction observed in the mind, with God serving primarily as a theological explanation for why observers anticipate regularity—functionally equivalent to Hume’s skeptical solution, but expressed in metaphysical language. Regarding modularity, the student asserts that Al-Ghazālī treats each causal pairing as governed by an independent divine decree that can be suspended individually, so that God might stop fire from burning cotton in one instance while leaving every other causal chain intact; they take this to parallel the modularity condition in modern interventionism, where an intervention on one mechanism need not disturb others. Finally, they conclude that because God could break the habit at any moment, Al-Ghazālī must hold that human beings lack any reliable knowledge that fire will cause burning in the future, making occasionalism a form of radical skepticism about inductive inference.
