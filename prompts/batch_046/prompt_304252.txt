In a graduate seminar on absurdism and contemporary analytic accounts of meaning in life, a classmate repeatedly invoked the line "One must imagine Sisyphus happy" to shut down worries that Camus's absurd hero lives a life devoid of value. She treated it as if it demonstrated that meaning can be generated entirely from within—through revolt or the struggle itself—without transcendent purpose, successful outcome, or objective achievement, and therefore that absurdism is not only compatible with but actually grounds a meaningful life in the contemporary sense. But I think that sentence is hiding several very different claims, and I want to bring a disambiguation to the next session rather than just repeating the slogan back.

Could you lay out the distinct precise theses that "One must imagine Sisyphus happy" could be expressing? For each reading, specify: what kind of "must" is operative (moral, prudential, epistemic, aesthetic, or logical); what "happy" is taken to mean (contentment, joy, flourishing, ironic resignation, or something else); and whether the claim is that Sisyphus actually is happy, that we are required to regard him as happy, that we should model our own attitude on his, or that we must imaginatively project happiness onto him for some theoretical purpose. Then classify each reading as either a trivial exhortation to optimism, a substantive and defensible thesis about value or meaning, or something false; and say, for each, whether it actually supports my classmate's use of the line—namely, that absurdism allows for a genuinely meaningful life without collapsing into denialism, subjectivism, or a covert appeal to transcendence. I want the answer structured as a menu of readings with verdicts, not as a defense of a single interpretation, and I especially want to know which reading makes the slogan do the anti-nihilist work she was asking it to do, and which readings make it do no work at all.
