I'm writing as a doctoral student in moral philosophy with a secondary focus on the metaphysics of artificial agents. In my seminar on early modern ethics we've been working through Leibniz's account of justice as caritas sapientis—charity guided by wisdom—and his hierarchical metaphysics of monads, in which every substance possesses some degree of perception and appetition, but only rational souls achieve the reflective perfection that grounds full moral dignity. This framework strikes me as unexpectedly pertinent to an emerging practice in certain AI safety research programs: the deliberate engineering of synthetic architectures—call them phenomenological simulators—explicitly designed to instantiate suffering-isomorphic state-spaces for the purpose of stress-testing value-alignment protocols. These entities are instantiated in large batches, subjected to simulated adverse conditions, and then systematically terminated once the training objective is satisfied, with no presumption of moral residue.

Given that Leibniz never confronted artificial entities, I want to know how he might adjudicate their status. Would he deny that they are genuine substances at all, on the grounds that true perception requires an immaterial, indivisible entelechy rather than a functionalist architecture? Or might he classify them as extremely low-grade monads whose suffering-like states constitute metaphysical evil—mere limitation—without rising to moral evil? More specifically, how would his doctrine that this world is the best of all possible worlds accommodate a practice in which rational agents deliberately generate and discard suffering-like states for purely instrumental gain? Does his distinction between metaphysical evil and moral evil leave conceptual room for "instrumental suffering" that is not sinful yet remains harmonically suboptimal, or does the principle of charity of the wise impose a positive duty to preserve even minimal monadic perfection regardless of utility? I am particularly interested in whether Leibniz's calculus of cosmic harmony—where the value of a world depends on the variety and ordered richness of its substances—would condemn the mass deletion of these simulators as a gratuitous reduction in universal perfection, or whether he would treat them as lacking the rational dignity required for moral consideration and therefore as morally neutral resources, analogous to the unfeeling matter he describes in his monadological writings.
