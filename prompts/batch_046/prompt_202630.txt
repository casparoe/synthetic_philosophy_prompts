I work for a regional participatory governance office, and we keep receiving proposals—everything from localized air-quality regulations to redesigned transit corridors—that are presented as technical fixes but actually involve contested values about risk, space, and collective life. I have been studying Dewey’s experimentalist philosophy of democracy, in which democratic life is itself a mode of social inquiry: problems are identified through shared experience, tentative institutional changes serve as testable hypotheses, and consequences are evaluated collectively rather than decreed by experts. I want you to translate this framework into a reusable decision procedure my team can apply to any proposal that comes across our desk, without requiring anyone on the team to be a philosopher.

The procedure should run as an ordered set of tests with explicit stopping conditions. First, specify how to determine whether the proposal addresses a genuinely shared problem—one that affected parties can recognize in common lived experience—rather than a deficit defined solely by technical metrics. Second, lay out what conditions must be met for a pilot or trial to count as an empirical test of the proposal rather than a symbolic or predetermined rollout, including the requirement that the trial must be structured so that it can genuinely fail. Third, establish the protocol for evaluating consequences collectively, especially in cases where quantitative measurements conflict with residents’ qualitative reports; state exactly at what point disagreement over the interpretation of observation itself should halt the inquiry. Fourth, identify the threshold of democratic participation in framing both the problem and the evaluation criteria below which the process is invalid, regardless of the outcome’s technical merit. Fifth, mark the precise boundary where the procedure must yield to explicit value judgment—such as when stakeholders agree on observed effects but disagree on whether a particular harm or risk is acceptable—rather than disguising a normative choice as a procedural result.

Then apply the procedure to two contrasting proposals: vaccinating an urban wildlife population against a painful endemic disease, where epidemiological data is robust but public affective and ethical responses are sharply divided; and converting a residential street to a pedestrian-only zone, where residents possess rich lived knowledge of current conditions but long-term economic and traffic effects are genuinely unknown. For each case, indicate where the procedure directs action, where it mandates a halt, and where it exposes a judgment that cannot be resolved within the logic of experimental inquiry. Finally, state clearly what the procedure deliberately excludes—such as cost-benefit optimization, representative polling, or appeals to bare majority preference—and explain why those exclusions are principled features of the model rather than merely practical limitations.
