Act as a professional philosopher of personal identity and bioethics with experience in graduate seminar design. Before you draft anything, take five minutes of internal brainstorming: sketch three distinct ways a live classroom simulation could make participants feel—before they name it—the conflict between diachronic authority and present autonomy, then choose the one that best forces the tension into the mechanics rather than the discussion.

Design a runnable 90-minute activity for an upper-level undergraduate philosophy seminar of 12 students. The group has read conventional medical advance-directive literature but not the idea of “existential directives” governing non-medical legacy and relationships. Your deliverable is a structured role-play with rules, payoffs, a timeline, a debrief, and an honest limitation statement.

Concept to be embodied: whether an earlier self’s binding authority survives a radical, deliberate, and reversible transformation of values—here caused not by dementia but by an elective neurocognitive enhancement that alters moral and aesthetic preferences—when the directive concerns the fate of a lifetime creative archive and intimate relationships formed before the change.

Mechanics:
- Divide the 12 into three groups of four. Within each group assign: the Archivist (proxy for the earlier self, holding a sealed directive), the Transformed Subject (present self with opposite values), the Continuity Judge (must apply one hidden rule—either “binding authority requires strict psychological continuity” or “binding authority requires only narrative continuity”), and the Silent Witness (records contradictions silently, may speak only in the final 15 minutes).
- Give each group an identical “value-profile” packet describing concrete, irreconcilable preferences: the earlier self demands the archive be destroyed unread to preserve artistic purity; the later self wants it published and monetized to fund a new life. Include a “wild-card” procedural clause: the directive contained an explicit revocation window that closed one hour before the enhancement was completed.
- Impose real, small stakes: points that determine presentation-order privileges for the next unit. Honor the directive fully: Archivist and Judge receive 3 points each, Subject receives 0. Revoke fully: Subject receives 3, Archivist 0. Split the difference: everyone receives 1, but the group loses 1 collective point for violating the directive’s unitary intent.
- Timeline: 10 minutes silent role absorption; 30 minutes closed deliberation under the hidden rule (Judges do not reveal their rule); 15 minutes cross-group comparison in which Judges disclose their rules and groups discover they reached opposite verdicts on identical facts; 20 minutes structured debrief; 15 minutes Silent Witness interventions and facilitator synthesis.

Requirements for the design:
The philosophical problem must arise from the rules and incentives before it is named. The hidden-rule distribution should cause identical cases to diverge. The payoff structure should make participants personally feel the cost of deferring to an “alien” authority. The wild-card clause should make the Subject’s present protest formally irrelevant regardless of merit, so the group confronts procedural authority rather than mere welfare comparison.

Debrief:
Map three specific in-game moments onto philosophical positions—e.g., the Judge’s realization that her hidden rule forces her to disregard present suffering; the Subject’s realization that the closed revocation window renders her current consent moot; the Archivist’s discovery that honoring the directive feels like betrayal of the present person. Predict failure modes: groups will try to meta-negotiate the hidden rule; some Subjects will break character to appeal to fairness; the point system may feel trivial to some. Give concrete facilitator responses for each.

State frankly which feature of the authority debate the activity cannot reproduce—specifically, the metaphysical question of whether the transformed individual is numerically identical to the author of the directive or a successor self—and explain exactly how the facilitator must supply that gap verbally.

Finally, include a brief note acknowledging that the design invents “existential directives” with no legal analog, uses a contrived point-payoff system, and assumes a value-pluralist framing of temporal stages rather than a unitary rational-agent model; also note any other contested philosophical framing you have baked in.

Conclude by stating how confident you are that the hidden-rule mechanism genuinely illuminates the authority problem rather than producing mere procedural confusion, and single out the one point about which you are least sure—whether the artificial revocation-window clause clarifies the bindingness of past consent or instead introduces a confounding artifact that distracts from the core identity question.
