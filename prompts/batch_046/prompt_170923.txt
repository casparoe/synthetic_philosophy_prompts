The result is paradoxical: she can now recover discrete events with photographic accuracy, yet reports that none of them feel "owned." Each memory presents itself as an isolated datum lacking the usual diachronic thread that binds yesterday's agent to today's. In response, she has begun manually weaving these fragments into retrospective narratives, selecting which episodes belong to the "protagonist" she identifies as herself and quietly discarding those that disrupt the arc. She admits, candidly, that she is writing fiction—not falsifying facts, but constructing an authorial center that does not emerge naturally from the data.

She now faces a choice between two paths. One is to abandon all retrospective construction and live entirely in the present, accepting a fragmented, episodic mode of being with no continuous narrator behind it. The other is to embrace this editorial role deliberately, crafting a coherent autobiography even when it requires suppressing veridical but narratively disruptive memories, on the grounds that without such an organizing center she is not a unified person but merely a succession of mental states.

She has asked whether she should preserve her constructed narrative or dissolve it. I want to advise her, but I am unsure whether she has lost something essential, or whether she has merely become lucid about an artifact that was always present. How should I think about her situation, and what should I tell her?
