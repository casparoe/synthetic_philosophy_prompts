I'm a journalist who usually covers science and ideas, and I'm drafting a feature on moral psychology and teh doctrine of double effect, but I got stuck on my own gut reactions. I want to check whether they actually hang together or if I'm just being inconsistent.

Here are my verdicts, going case by case. Flipping a switch to divert a trolley onto a track where it'll kill one person instead of five: that feels totally permissible. Shoving a heavy man off a bridge to stop the trolley: definitely not okay. A surgeon killing one healthy patient to harvest organs for five dying ones: also not okay, and it feels closer to the bridge case than to the switch, even though the numbers are identical. A factory owner who keeps a toxic plant running knowing it'll kill one worker but saves hundreds of jobs: weirdly, that feels okay to me, which already clashes with my surgeon verdict. A self-driving car algorithm programmed to swerve into one pedestrian to avoid hitting five: that feels okay, like the switch. But a hospital AI that automatically withdraws life support from one ICU patient to free up ventilators for five others: that feels wrong, almost like the surgeon case, even though no human hand is on the lever. And telling a dying patient that her estranged son called, just to comfort her, when he actually didn't — I keep going back and forth, but I lean toward not okay.

So is there a single principle — maybe about directness, or harm as a means versus a side effect, or whether human agency stays in the loop — that captures all of these? Or do I have to drop one of my intutions? If there's a coherent account, what should it predict for a case I haven't mentioned: a doctor who programs a robotic arm to administer a lethal dose of pain medication to a suffering patient, with the robot carrying out the injection autonomously once activated? And please be honest about which of my verdicts the best principle can't save.
