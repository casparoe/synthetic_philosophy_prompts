I am preparing a moderated interview with an unnamed philosopher who defends a rigorous interpretation of Erwin Schrödinger’s writings on consciousness—specifically the view that quantum mechanics, read without hidden variables or privileged observers, formally supports the Advaita Vedanta claim that there is only one universal consciousness, and that the multiplicity of individual minds is analogous to the multiplicity of apparently distinct quantum particles that are in fact indistinguishable. She holds that embodied perspective does not create a new subject but operates like a measurement context, selecting particular experiential contents from a single undivided field without thereby dividing the field itself; and she treats the Vedantic analysis of superimposition—the imposition of limiting attributes upon the unlimited—not as a mystical doctrine but as a precise description of how cognitive structures generate the illusion of private ownership over experience. I need you to draft eight to ten interview questions for her, arranged in the order I should ask them, presented entirely in continuous prose woven into flowing paragraphs separated only by ordinary line breaks, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, tables, or numbered lists, written in the manner of a letter or essay, and with each question immediately followed by a brief anticipatory follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to offer. At least one question must press the particle-indistinguishability analogy directly, asking whether the statistical interchangeability of quantum entities genuinely supports the metaphysical identity of conscious subjects, and it should include a follow-up that addresses the objection that my suffering and yours are not interchangeable in any meaningful sense. At least one question should examine the ethical implications of strict monism: if there is literally one subject, what becomes of moral responsibility, of the distinction between harming oneself and harming another, or of interpersonal forgiveness? At least one question should probe her account of illusion—whether she treats Maya as an epistemic limitation, an ontological error, or a necessary structural feature of embodied cognition—and should ask in the follow-up how her view avoids collapsing into eliminativism about other minds. At least one should confront the phenomenological asymmetry between the direct transparency of one’s own mental states and the opacity of others’, asking whether that asymmetry can be explained within her framework without smuggling in a hidden dualism of perspectives. And at least one should ask what specific empirical result in neuroscience or physics, or what conceptual argument, she would accept as genuinely undermining her interpretation rather than being reinterpreted as further evidence for the unity thesis. Do not include bibliographic requests, reading lists, or citations; the questions should turn on the conceptual substance of her position and should be specific enough that they could not be answered by a generic summary of panpsychism or mysticism.
