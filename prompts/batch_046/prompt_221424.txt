I keep running into the same move in arguments about dead politicians and explorers, and I don't know whether philosophers have given it a precise name.

Take Jefferson. When someone raises that he owned slaves, the defense is usually that slavery was the water he swam in—everyone in his class did it, and blaming him for failing to see past it is like blaming a fish for being wet. The historical context eats the blame. But when someone raises that he advocated religious disestablishment, nobody files that under "just another Enlightenment gentleman reflecting the minority progressive current." Instead we treat it as a distinctive personal achievement. The context that dissolved responsibility for the vice suddenly disappears for the virtue.

The same directional switch shows up elsewhere. Churchill's imperial violence and racial views are routinely contextualized as "that's just how British elites thought in 1920," but his refusal to surrender in 1940 is not contextualized away as "that's just how British elites reacted to existential threat"; it is credited to his individual judgment. Columbus's navigational skill and the economic logic of exploration are praised as personal or epochal triumphs, while the enslavement and extermination he initiated are treated as structural outputs of fifteenth-century European expansion that he should not be singled out for.

So the pattern is not simply "judge people by the standards of their time." It is a selective deployment of that principle: historical normalization is applied to absorb agency for moral failures, but suspended to preserve agency for moral successes. I have seen this happen in careful, non-rhetorical writing, not just in lazy debate, which makes me think it might be a recognizable failure mode rather than ordinary hypocrisy.

Does this asymmetry have an established concept? It is not presentism, since presentism concerns whether modern standards apply at all, not whether context is invoked asymmetrically for praise versus blame. It touches the fundamental attribution error, but that bias usually means we over-attribute behavior to personality across the board; here we seem to flip the attributional switch based on the moral valence of the act. It also differs from plain motivated reasoning in that the same person can apply the asymmetry sincerely in both directions without recognizing the contradiction.

If there is no precise term, I want a coined concept with clear boundaries: what distinguishes it from a defensible view that virtues require active agency to transcend norms while vices are often structurally compelled, and what distinguishes it from simple inconsistency? And I want a committed answer, not a survey of options. Is this directional asymmetry best understood as a rhetorical shield with no philosophical merit, or is there a coherent principle—perhaps about the difference between complying with evil norms and transcending them—that genuinely justifies treating praise and blame differently in historical judgment? Give your reasons and take a definite position.
