I have just finished a prolonged and somewhat heated discussion with a friend about Amartya Sen's claim that famines are political crimes rather than natural disasters, with particular reference to Bengal in 1943, the Irish famine of 1845–1852, and the Chinese famine of 1959–1961 during the Great Leap Forward. My interlocutor insisted that Sen's entitlement framework, while illuminating for democratic failures, becomes conceptually overextended when applied to the Great Leap Forward, and that treating the Irish blight as a mere entitlement failure ignores the biological reality of the potato blight. I am now preparing to defend a modified position—one that retains Sen's core insight but supplements it with a mechanism I call anticipatory entitlement foreclosure, whereby bureaucratic forecasting and centralized rationing protocols preemptively dismantle the informal exchange networks that traditionally buffer local populations against harvest shocks. I will present this to an interdisciplinary seminar comprising four political philosophers well-versed in Sen's entitlement analysis but wary of moralized causal language; two economic historians, one of whom specializes in the Irish famine and maintains that export continuity proves political causation was secondary to crop failure; a historian of Maoist China who regards the democratic-accountability component of Sen's thesis as anachronistic when applied to the Great Leap Forward; and a group of graduate students concerned with comparative moral responsibility across the three cases. The audience values conceptual precision over rhetorical force, and they will press me on whether my supplementary mechanism is genuinely distinct from ordinary auxiliary hypotheses in entitlement theory. I need a prep sheet: the objections in roughly the order they will arise, phrased as these specific interlocutors would phrase them, with what I should concede, what I should hold, how to reply within a two-minute window, and the weakest point in my own position that I should prepare to defend rather than hope they overlook. Please include the objection I am most hoping nobody raises—specifically, the one that targets the empirical claim that informal exchange networks existed in a form robust enough to be preemptively dismantled in 1959 rural China.
