I serve on the board of a small community garden—roughly half an acre, run entirely by volunteers—where we are debating whether to install an automated drip-irrigation system. The manufacturer’s data show that large agricultural operations reduce water waste by about forty percent with this technology, yet a study of small urban plots under one acre reports a sixty percent failure rate within two years, largely due to inconsistent maintenance. A regional survey of gardens in our climate zone shows more mixed results, but it combines both volunteer-run and professionally staffed sites into a single category. Two neighboring gardens in our network adopted the same system, but only after hiring part-time managers, and they now report excellent outcomes. Some board members argue that since we are considering hiring a manager ourselves, we should classify our project with those successful, professionally supported sites; others insist that our present identity as an all-volunteer collective is the only honest basis for prediction, and that selecting the category after seeing the data is a form of self-deception. I sense that the disagreement is not merely about statistics but about a deeper question: there may be no objective fact about which group we truly belong to, especially because the very act of adopting the system and hiring staff would change the conditions that define our category. How should we think about which numbers apply to us?
