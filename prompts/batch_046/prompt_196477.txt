I'm working through material on supererogation for a seminar, and I've kept noticing a pattern I can't place precisely. It goes like this: someone performs an act that is clearly beyond what morality demands—say, a researcher who spends years in dangerous field conditions to complete work that benefits strangers, with no institutional reward—and the act is rightly praised as exceptional. But then, in subsequent moral assessment, that exceptional act seems to function not just as an upper bound but as a hidden benchmark. People who fulfilled every explicit obligation—who went to the safe site, met deadlines, protected dependents—are evaluated as if they have fallen short of a standard that was never officially theirs. The exceptional case hasn't changed the rules; it has cast an evaluative shadow in which ordinary duty-fulfillment looks like a failure to grasp what the situation "really" required. I have seen this in discussions of medical ethics, in debates about charitable giving, and in workplace norms around going above and beyond. What strikes me is that the shadow applies selectively: it lowers the standing of the merely dutiful, but it does not seem to impose a new obligation going forward—nobody says "now you must do what she did"—yet the comparison lingers.

Is there an established concept for this specific dynamic—this retroactive recasting of an explicit duty as an inadequate approximation of a hidden, stricter standard revealed only by a supererogatory performance? I know the literature distinguishes acts beyond duty from ordinary obligations, and I am familiar with discussions of moral sainthood and with the demandingness objection, but none of these quite capture the asymmetry I am describing: the way the exceptional act is used to judge others without being adopted as a universal prescription. If the concept does exist, how is it usually distinguished from simple moral comparison or from the idea that obligations become more stringent over time? If it does not exist, I would want a term and a boundary: does the phenomenon require that the exceptional act be visible to the evaluators, does it depend on shared social context, and how should we distinguish it from ordinary moral hypocrisy or from an equivocal retreat between demanding and modest moral claims? I am not asking for a bibliography or reading list—just the conceptual carving, with attention to where the edges blur and what it is not.

Please say how confident you are in your identification or in any term you coin, and single out the specific point about which you are least sure—whether that concerns the relation to moral sainthood, the necessity of visibility for the shadow effect, or the distinction from retroactive obligation revision.
