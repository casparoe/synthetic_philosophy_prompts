Read Ursula K. Le Guin's short story The Ones Who Walk Away from Omelas through the philosophical problem of causation by omission, but with a specific conceptual framing: distinguish between direct omission, meaning an individual citizen's failure to free the imprisoned child upon learning of its suffering, and systemic omission, meaning the collective failure to restructure the social conditions that require the child's imprisonment for the utopia's existence. The narrative presents both those who stay and enjoy the benefits, and the few who walk away into an unknown darkness. Rather than treating the story as a simple allegory for moral complicity, examine whether Le Guin dramatizes the citizens' continued presence as an omission that actively sustains the system, and whether the walkers' departure counts as an omission of resistance—an absence of reform—rather than a positive act of liberation. Does the moral geometry of the story depend on treating absence, silence, and departure as causally loaded in different ways? In particular, draw on the philosophical debate about whether omissions can function as causes only against a background of prior causal commitments or institutional roles: does the story implicitly rely on such a background to assign complicity to those who remain, and does it withhold that background from those who leave, making their walking away causally inert? Please take seriously the ambiguity of the walkers' destination and resist resolving it into clear moral triumph or failure. Conclude by saying what the story contributes to the philosophical discussion of negative causation that pure argument tends to miss—perhaps about the phenomenology of recognizing an omission without being able to locate the corresponding positive duty.
