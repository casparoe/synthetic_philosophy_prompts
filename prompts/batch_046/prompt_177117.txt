I spent the last two hours arguing with a friend about whether evolutionary biology has actually moved past the adaptationist program, and we kept circling back to Gould and Lewontin's spandrels paper without resolving whether modern genomics changes the picture. For a course I'm taking on philosophy of biology, I need to think through how a dead thinker might respond to evidence that emerged only after their death.

So: imagine Stephen Jay Gould—who died in 2002—were confronted with the ENCODE consortium's 2012 claim that about eighty percent of the human genome exhibits biochemical activity, followed by the fierce rebuttal from evolutionary biologists who insisted that biochemical noise is not the same as evolutionary function. Would Gould treat ENCODE as a resurgence of the Panglossian paradigm he criticized, arguing that most of that biochemical activity is spandrel-like—byproducts of selection for other traits, or structural necessities with no direct adaptive value? Or would he grant that the prevalence of regulatory elements forces a revision of the spandrel metaphor, perhaps distinguishing sharply between biochemical 'function' and selected-effect 'function'? I'm not asking for a bibliography or citation list—just a reasoned reconstruction of his likely position, written in American English, that takes seriously both his anti-adaptationist commitments and the specific genomic evidence that arrived after he was gone.
