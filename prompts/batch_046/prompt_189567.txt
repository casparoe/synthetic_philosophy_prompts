Write a dialogue between Maya, who holds that practical reasons are fundamentally internal—meaning a consideration counts as a reason for an agent only if it is capable of motivating her through some sound deliberative route from her existing psychological states—and Julian, who holds that reasons can be genuinely external, obtaining for an agent in virtue of objective normative facts regardless of whether she can currently access or be moved by them. Their exchange should center on the concrete case of an agent whose preferences have been deeply adapted through prolonged structural coercion: Maya should argue that attributing external reasons to such an agent amounts to an unacceptable epistemic paternalism, because those considerations cannot play any guiding role in her actual deliberation without being imposed from outside, thereby violating her autonomy. Julian should respond that confining reasons to internal accessibility effectively treats the coerced psychology as normatively authoritative, thereby legitimizing the very structures that shaped it, and that recognizing external reasons is essential for the critical self-reflection and social transformation needed to overcome such coercion. Require genuine back-and-forth: each philosopher must directly address the other’s specific claims about autonomy, paternalism, and the normative status of adapted psychology, rather than delivering parallel monologues. Neither should be a strawman; both should display equal philosophical competence and concede shared distinctions where appropriate—perhaps agreeing on a separation between justifying reasons and motivating reasons—before showing precisely where and why that distinction fails to resolve their disagreement. The dialogue should remain conceptual and self-contained, with no appeal to bibliographies, reading lists, or citations.
