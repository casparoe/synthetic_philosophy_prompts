I have been discussing the moral status of invertebrates with colleagues in moral philosophy and biology, and I find myself caught between two intuitions that I cannot easily reconcile. On one hand, the evolutionary and neurobiological evidence suggests that phenomenal consciousness in insects is far from established—likely low in probability for any particular species, though perhaps non-negligible when aggregated across taxa. On the other hand, the sheer numerical scale of insects affected by ordinary agricultural and pest-management practices is so vast that even a modest per-individual probability of sentience might, through straightforward multiplication, generate an enormous expected moral burden.

This tension is sometimes called the scale argument, and I want to test whether it genuinely produces an all-things-considered moral obligation rather than merely a pro tanto consideration. Specifically, I am asking for your own credence—stated as a rough numerical range with a brief justification—that we currently have a stronger moral reason to restructure large-scale agricultural pest-control practices, accepting modest costs and some reduction in human welfare, than to pursue certain competing moral priorities of comparable cost but far lower numerical scale, purely on the grounds of this expected insect suffering.

But I want the reasoning decomposed, not compressed into a single number. Please address the following sub-questions, assigning a rough probability or confidence level to each and providing a one-line rationale: first, the base-rate question—what is your credence that a representative insect species possesses phenomenal consciousness rather than merely functional nociception or reflexive behavioral response? Second, the weighting question—if consciousness is present, does the moral weight of an individual insect scale continuously with cognitive complexity, or is there a sharp threshold that would render insect experiences morally negligible regardless of aggregation? Give me a number reflecting your confidence in continuous scaling being the correct normative framework here. Third, the empirical magnitude question—with what confidence do you judge that the number of insects affected by standard practices is large enough that, multiplied by even a low per-capita moral weight, it dominates obligations of comparable cost affecting far smaller populations of uncontroversial moral patients? Fourth, the aggregation-rule question—should we combine these uncertainties using standard expected-value reasoning, or does radical epistemic uncertainty about cross-species moral status call for a risk-averse rule, such as maximin or some precautionary principle, that either suppresses or amplifies the scale argument?

Then combine these terms—explicitly or implicitly—to arrive at your overall credence in the obligation-claim I stated above. Tell me which sub-question carries the greatest weight in your final number, and identify the specific kind of evidence—conceptual, empirical, or normative—that would most move your overall assessment. I am especially interested in whether your answer changes if we treat moral status as binary rather than graded, and whether that binary framing is itself defensible or merely a convenient way to avoid the aggregation problem.
