In my philosophy seminar this term we have been working through the second book of Plato's Republic, and I have tried to formalize Glaucon's challenge in a way that now seems to me to prove either far too little or far too much. I imagine not merely the ordinary ring of Gyges, which grants invisibility and thus removes all social punishment, but an enhanced variant that also suppresses every internal check—no guilt, no anxiety about reputation, no sympathetic identification—so that the bearer is left with purely instrumental reasoning intact. From this I build the following chain: (1) If an act is morally wrong, then the agent has a decisive reason not to perform it. (2) If an agent has a decisive reason not to perform an act, that reason must be capable of motivating them; otherwise it is not genuinely a reason for that agent. (3) For the bearer of this enhanced ring, injustice serves their interests perfectly, and nothing within them is moved toward justice. (4) So for the ring-bearer there is no motivating reason not to be unjust. (5) Therefore, by (1) and (2), injustice is not wrong for them. (6) But wrongness is an objective property and cannot depend on an agent's psychology; hence (2) is false, and reasons may exist without motivating. (7) If reasons can exist unmotivated, then the ring-bearer still possesses decisive moral reasons to be just, despite gaining nothing and suffering no penalty. (8) By parallel reasoning, whenever justice conflicts with self-interest—even returning a wallet to a dying enemy who can no longer use it, at the cost of one's own life and with no benefit to anyone—morality still demands the just act. (9) But morality cannot require guaranteed, pointless self-annihilation. (10) Therefore (1) is false: an act can be wrong even when there is no reason, not even an unmotivating one, not to do it.

I find this suspicious because the conclusion undermines the very link between wrongness and practical reason that makes Glaucon's challenge intelligible in the first place, yet I am unsure which step is the real culprit. I would most prefer to keep (1), since it captures the idea that moral demands are genuinely reason-giving, but I also want to avoid (9) and its apparent demand for total self-sacrifice in every conflict. Is the error in my inference from (6) to (7), where I treat unmotivated reasons as still decisive? Or is there a hidden equivocation in (8) between the special conditions of the ring and ordinary moral conflicts? Or should I instead reject (2) differently—perhaps by distinguishing normative reasons from motivating ones—without letting that distinction collapse into the absurdity of (9)? I want to know exactly which premise to surrender, why it initially looked acceptable, and what philosophical price each available rejection exacts—particularly whether saving (1) forces me to accept some form of moral rationalism that applies with equal force inside and outside the ring.
