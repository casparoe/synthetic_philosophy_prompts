I hold a qualified substrate-independent view inspired by Hofstadter's dialogue in The Mind's I: a physical book encoding the complete functional architecture of a conscious brain could itself be conscious, but only stroboscopically—its mental states exist solely during discrete intervals of active causal engagement (being read or computationally processed) and vanish entirely in the static gaps between them, with the consciousness belonging not to the paper substrate but to the transient organizational dynamics that mirror the brain's invariants. What would count against this specific position? I want an honest map of its exposure: which of its three core commitments—(i) that consciousness is organizational rather than substrate-bound, (ii) that a static encoding can realize the relevant organization upon activation, and (iii) that discontinuous, interval-bound realization constitutes genuine mental life rather than mere simulation—are most vulnerable, and to what kinds of counterevidence (empirical findings about neural temporal continuity, conceptual arguments about binding and presence, formal results on information integration, or phenomenological considerations about the stream of consciousness)? Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, distinguish those that defenders of this view should concede as genuinely damaging from those they could dismiss by further qualification, and assess whether the stroboscopic clause insulates the position so thoroughly that it risks unfalsifiability—and whether such insulation is a defect for a claim of this kind.
