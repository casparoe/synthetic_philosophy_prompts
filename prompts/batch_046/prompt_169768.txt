I’ve been trying to formalize a concern that keeps surfacing in discussions about AI and scientific practice—what I think of as “explanatory inversion,” a state in which a research community treats an AI-derived predictive framework as its primary reference point not because anyone has reconstructed its core results from first principles, but because the outputs are consistently accurate, and over generations junior researchers learn to navigate the model’s interface rather than its underlying logic. The conceptual boundary I can’t fix is whether this represents a genuinely novel form of epistemic dependence—one that erodes collective understanding in a way that using a telescope or adopting a statistical black-box does not—or whether it is merely an accelerated, scaled-up version of ordinary specialization and instrumental opacity.

I want your actual credence, expressed as a number between 0 and 1, that explanatory inversion (defined precisely as a sustained field-level condition where the dominant working model is AI-originated, lacks any human-comprehensible derivation for its central predictions, and is maintained primarily through predictive success rather than explanatory integration) constitutes a categorically distinct kind of epistemic harm rather than a quantitative extension of distributed cognition. Please decompose the reasoning: assign rough weight to how much depends on whether “understanding” requires individual reconstructive ability versus reliable collective access; how much on whether present or near-future architectures are in principle interpretable but practically opaque to human cognition; how much on whether scientific communities possess sociological stabilization mechanisms that can sustain such dependence without eventual collapse, correction, or silent degradation; and identify which single sub-question, if resolved with high confidence, would move your number the most. I am not asking for citations or a literature map—just the decomposition with its weak points marked. And please flag the objection I am hoping nobody raises: that I have smuggled in a normative premise by measuring “erosion” against an idealized model of science—transparent, derivation-first, individually reconstructible—that may never have described actual working communities.
