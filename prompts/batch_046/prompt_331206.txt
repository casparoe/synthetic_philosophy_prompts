As a novelist working on a historical fiction project set in a nineteenth-century Bengali intellectual circle, I have been reading extensively in comparative moral philosophy, and I find myself stalled by a conceptual disagreement that mirrors arguments I want my characters to have. The dispute concerns whether karma, specifically in its non-theistic, non-dualistic formulations, should be interpreted as a theory of moral desert—that is, a normative framework in which an agent's present suffering or flourishing is fitting in light of past moral actions—or rather as an impersonal metaphysical mechanism of psychophysical causation in which normative language is merely instrumental. Proponents of the desert reading maintain that without a normative dimension, karma collapses into a morally arbitrary cosmic determinism: if suffering is not in some sense appropriate to prior wrongdoing, then the doctrine offers no ethical guidance and becomes indistinguishable from brute fate. Their opponents insist that importing the concept of desert, particularly its backward-looking, retributive structure, constitutes an anachronistic projection of Western juridical morality onto a system originally concerned with liberation from the illusion of a persistent moral self. Both camps cite the same commentarial traditions, agree on the textual evidence, and even accept that both causal and normative vocabularies appear in the sources; yet the disagreement has persisted for decades without resolution.

My own suspicion, shaped by conversations with a philosopher friend, is that the stall occurs because the two camps are not actually disputing the same thesis. One camp asks whether karma can fulfill the functional role of moral desert in justifying differential outcomes, while the other asks whether karma requires a substantive moral self that persists across actions to make sense of causation at all. These may be orthogonal questions that have been conflated under a single heading. Yet I wonder whether there is also a hidden disagreement about the standards of explanation—whether an ethical theory must provide a normative justification for suffering, or whether it is sufficient to describe the conditions under which suffering ceases. I would like a diagnosis rather than a survey: is this disagreement verbal, substantive, or a case of answering different questions? And given that diagnosis, what would actually move it—a conceptual distinction that separates desert from fittingness, a comparative argument drawing on another tradition, or an acknowledgment that no single interpretation can dominate? I am particularly interested in whether there is a third framing, perhaps one that treats karma as a diachronic equilibrium condition rather than either desert or mechanism, that could dissolve the opposition or at least reveal what is genuinely at stake.
