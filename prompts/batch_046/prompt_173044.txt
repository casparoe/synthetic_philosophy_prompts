I sit on an advisory board for a municipal social-services agency, and we are drafting a reusable protocol for supervisors who must decide whether to enforce standardized risk-assessment and reporting rules on frontline caseworkers. The philosophical framework we want to use combines Bernard Williams's integrity objection—some personal commitments are ground projects that partly constitute the agent's identity, and moral demands that require abandoning them are illegitimate—with the related concern that in certain close relationships, inserting a calculative or justificatory step between oneself and the other introduces one thought too many and corrupts the bond. Write a step-by-step decision procedure my supervisors can run when a caseworker resists a directive on these grounds. The steps should be ordered; for each, specify the question to ask, what counts as a sufficient answer, and whether the step can be answered with facts or requires a value judgment that must be flagged and handed to a person. The procedure must distinguish a genuine ground project from a mere preference, habit, or rationalized avoidance of duty, and it must identify the point at which institutional accountability legitimately overrides individual integrity. Apply the procedure to three proposals: (a) a veteran caseworker refuses to initiate removal proceedings for a child because she has promised the grandmother to keep the family intact; (b) a worker declines to record a client's immigration status, citing a personal ethical opposition to cooperation with federal enforcement; (c) a worker insists on delivering aid to a family that fails eligibility criteria because he grew up in the same neighborhood and considers them part of his community. Include at least one case where the procedure yields a result the caseworker will not like but the framework requires, and explicitly state what the procedure cannot settle—such as conflicts between two ground projects or between worker integrity and statutory obligations.
