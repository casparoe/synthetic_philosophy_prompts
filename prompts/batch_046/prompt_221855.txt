I'm prepping a discussion for my philosophy of perception study group — like six grad students and one professor who's been patient with us — and I want to use H.G. Wells's "The Country of the Blind" to think about Molyneux's problem, but not the tidy textbook version. I'm interested in the messier recent debate about whether cross-modal recognition fails because of missing concepts or because of missing sensorimotor couplings, and whether sensory substitution devices (like those tongue-electrode arrays that let users navigate via tactile patterns) change the framing entirely. In the story, Nunez falls into a valley where blindness is universal and generations-old; he expects sight to make him dominant, but the villagers treat him as delusional because he keeps describing things he "sees" that they only know through touch and sound. Read it as an inverted Molyneux case: instead of asking whether a person who gains sight can match a cube and sphere, Wells asks whether visual knowledge can ever be translated into a tactile-auditory scheme, and whether the breakdown is cognitive, social, or both.

I want a reading that doesn't just map plot onto philosophy — so don't reduce Nunez to "the empiricist" and the villagers to "the rationalists" — but that takes both seriously. Where does the story illuminate the idea that sensory modalities carry their own conceptual frameworks, and where does it resist that reading? There's a moment where Nunez nearly agrees to have his eyes removed to marry Medina-saroté, and that feels like it dramatizes something about embodiment and identity that the standard lab-version of Molyneux misses entirely. Also, if we bring in modern sensory substituion research, does the valley start to look less like a proof about incommensurable minds and more like a parable about institutional resistance to new data? I'd like you to say what the story gets right about cross-modal recognition that the philosophical arguments usually don't capture, where the metaphor breaks down, and whether Wells ultimately sides with anyone in the debate — or whether the story's real contribution is showing that the debate asks the wrong question.
