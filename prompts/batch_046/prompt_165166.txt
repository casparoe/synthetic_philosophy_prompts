I am finalizing the assessment for an upper-level philosophy of mind seminar that has spent the term on personal identity, distributed cognition, and Douglas Hofstadter’s strange-loop account of the self—particularly the idea that an “I” is not tied to a single biological substrate but might persist wherever its causal-pattern structure is properly instantiated. For the final exam, I want one essay question that students could answer in roughly 45 minutes, but it must be impossible to answer well by simply summarizing Hofstadter’s view that the self is a pattern capable of being copied or shared. Instead, the question should embed a deliberately engineered crux: present a hypothetical case of asymmetric co-instantiation, in which a strange-loop pattern is gradually duplicated across two living brains that maintain partial causal integration—intermittent shared memory streams, coupled emotional feedback, but no unified global workspace—yet develop divergent first-person reports (Brain A consistently reports “I am here in the left structure,” Brain B reports “I am there in the right,” and each occasionally accesses fragments of the other’s experiential stream without ever merging into a single point of view). Students must be required to determine whether Hofstadter’s framework commits him to recognizing one distributed “I,” two numerically distinct “I”s, a single fractured self, or no self at all in this configuration, and—crucially—they must pinpoint exactly where in his apparatus (pattern identity conditions, causal-density thresholds, or first-person accessibility constraints) the framework either resolves the case or remains silent. Please write the exam question itself, then provide a model answer of approximately 500 words, and conclude with a marking scheme organized around four must-hit conceptual moves (total 20 marks) that specifies the marks assigned to each move, the precise reasoning step a top answer must contain, and a one-line description of the characteristic mistake that should cost marks on that point—such as conflating functional equivalence with pattern identity, or smuggling in a Cartesian theater to break the symmetry. The assessment should specify time and length constraints within the question framing, should avoid asking for bibliographies, citations, or reading lists, and should not depend on any obscure biographical or textual detail that a well-prepared student could not be expected to know from the seminar’s common material.
