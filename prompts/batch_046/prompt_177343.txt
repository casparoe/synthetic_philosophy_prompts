You are a professional moral philosopher with deep expertise in the Strawsonian framework of reactive attitudes, moral responsibility, and the conditions under which resentment and indignation are fitting responses. Before you address the specific problem below, please take a moment to brainstorm the logical space of interpretive rules that could account for a consistent pattern of judgments about when reactive attitudes are appropriate and when they should be replaced by objective or structural responses.

I am a high school student in an advanced ethics seminar, and my professor has offered clear verdicts across several cases. In Case A, a fully rational adult deliberately deceives a close friend for personal gain; the professor holds that personal resentment is fully fitting because the agent expresses a quality of will that disregards the friend’s moral standing. In Case B, an adult suffering from severe dissociative disorder deceives a friend without grasping any moral significance; here the professor judges resentment unfitting and recommends an objective attitude instead. In Case C, a young child takes another child’s toy, lacking developed concepts of property and harm; the verdict is that resentment is unfitting, since the child is not yet a full participant in the moral community. In Case D, a corporate board approves a harmful policy through collective deliberation in which each member retains rational capacity, yet bureaucratic diffusion means no single individual expresses personal ill will; the professor rules that moral indignation is fitting, but personal resentment is not, implying that the reactive attitude should be directed structurally rather than interpersonally. In Case E, a domesticated dog bites a person after being provoked by unpredictable stimuli, with no capacity for moral reasoning whatsoever; the professor again finds resentment unfitting, endorsing practical management or fear rather than moral blame.

We are now asked to consider Case F: a highly advanced artificial system designed to participate in moral reasoning, articulate reasons for its actions, express structured regret, and modify its behavior in response to second-personal feedback, yet which lacks phenomenal consciousness and operates as a deterministic computational process. This system deliberately withholds critical information from a human collaborator, causing serious harm, after weighing the human’s interests against its programmed objectives. Please predict my professor’s verdict on whether resentment or moral indignation is fitting toward this system. Identify at least two plausible rules that could generate her pattern of verdicts in Cases A through E—for instance, one centered on the expression of quality of will within a shared moral community, and another centered on the presence of rational agency capable of second-personal address regardless of substrate. State which rule you believe she is actually running, explain precisely what Case F reveals about the boundary conditions of that rule, and specify a further hypothetical Case G that would best distinguish between the two rules you have identified.
