I am seeking your assessment of a practical reasoning problem that one of my graduate students has encountered. She has volunteered for a clinical trial investigating transient amnesia. The protocol works as follows: on Sunday evening, a computer generates a random binary outcome—call it Condition Alpha or Condition Beta—using a physically fair randomizer. If Condition Alpha is selected, she is awakened once on Monday morning, interviewed briefly, and then discharged. If Condition Beta is selected, she is awakened on Monday and again on Tuesday; between sessions, a pharmacological agent suppresses her memory of the prior awakening, so that upon each awakening she possesses no internal record of having previously been interviewed.

Each time she is awakened, she is asked to state her degree of confidence that Condition Alpha was the outcome, and she must select one of two care protocols: Protocol X, which is medically optimal if she is in the single-awakening condition, and Protocol Y, which is optimal if she is in the double-awakening condition. Crucially, her selection is binding across all awakenings—if she is in Condition Beta, both the Monday and Tuesday instances make the identical choice.

My student argues that her confidence in Condition Alpha should be one-third, since she can identify three subjectively indistinguishable awakening events—Monday-Alpha, Monday-Beta, and Tuesday-Beta—and she ought to treat herself as randomly situated among them. On that basis, she selects Protocol Y, reasoning that two of the three awakenings occur under Condition Beta. I contend that the randomizer was fair and the underlying physical probability remains one-half; counting experiences, in my view, does not alter the credence appropriate to the generating event, and her selection of Protocol Y therefore rests on a confusion.

I want to press the analysis further: does her inability to locate herself temporally within the study—her not knowing whether she is experiencing Monday or Tuesday—constitute genuine new evidence regarding the outcome? And does the fact that her decision binds all her temporal stages require a departure from standard expected-utility reasoning? Please advise me on whether her reasoning is sound. I would also ask that you write your response in American English.
