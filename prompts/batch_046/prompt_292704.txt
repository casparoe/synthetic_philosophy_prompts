As a software engineer without formal philosophy training, I have been trying to understand whether DNA is literally a code or only metaphorically described as one, and I am stuck on which conceptual disagreements actually matter. The familiar comparison notes that triplet codons are mapped to amino acids via the ribosome—an apparently arbitrary symbol-to-meaning assignment rather than a direct chemical template. But the analogy quickly becomes unstable: the genetic code is degenerate (multiple codons specify the same amino acid), functional frameshifting means what would be a syntax error in software can be biologically meaningful, and the “execution environment” (the cell, with its chaperones, epigenetic state, and metabolic context) co-constructs the output rather than passively interpreting instructions. Meanwhile, some philosophers of biology argue that DNA lacks true semantic information because it has no interpreter-independent meaning—its role is purely causal—while others treat the cellular machinery as an interpreter that makes the code genuinely literal.

Rather than asking for a survey, I want a dependency-aware prioritization of the open questions, practical implications, and research directions in this area. Specifically:

1. Among the unresolved conceptual issues—whether degeneracy and context-dependent frameshifting undermine the claim of arbitrariness; whether “semantic information” requires an external interpreter or can be grounded in functional selection; and whether Shannon-theoretic measures capture anything essential about genetic regulation— which is the upstream question? If one of these is settled, which others collapse or become tractable, and which remain independent?

2. For practice at the biology–computation boundary—synthetic gene editing, AI-driven protein design, and governance of engineered organisms—which conceptual disputes actually change outcomes? Does it matter whether we call DNA “code” if we can already synthesize and edit sequences reliably? Are there specific regulatory or ethical implications—around intellectual property analogies to software licensing, liability for engineered phenotypes, or the moral status of “rewriting” an organism—that genuinely hinge on resolving the literal-versus-metaphorical debate, and which are merely rhetorical dressing?

3. Which lines of work are tractable with current tools? For instance, can formal information-theoretic distinctions (Shannon versus semantic or algorithmic measures) be applied experimentally to genetic regulation in a way that resolves the interpreter problem, or is that a conceptual dead end? Should limited effort go toward clarifying the ontology of biological information, or toward systematically mapping analogies and disanalogies between genomic editing and software engineering?

4. What should be deprioritized? Are there debates—say, over whether Crick’s central dogma should be interpreted as a unidirectional computational pipeline, or whether the “code” metaphor commits us to a specific philosophy of mind about biological intentionality—that sound important but have little downstream effect on either synthetic biology or philosophy of science?
