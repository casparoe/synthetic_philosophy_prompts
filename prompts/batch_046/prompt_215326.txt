You are an expert in comparative constitutional design and multi-level governance, with working familiarity across the US, German, Swiss, and EU federal traditions. Before you draft the activity, take a moment to brainstorm: how could a classroom simulation make participants feel—through the rules and incentives they actually face, not through explanation—the structural tension between democratic proximity and meta-competence allocation, specifically the problem that the authority to draw the boundary of local autonomy is itself always held by the level that benefits from centralization?

Design a structured 90-minute classroom activity for 14 graduate students in a seminar on federalism and subsidiarity. The setting is an advanced political-theory or law seminar; participants should come away having experienced what we might call the reverse-subsidiarity trap: lower-level autonomy is justified by democratic proximity, yet the decision about which level holds a given competence is made at a higher level, and lower-level exit rights are structurally weaker than voice rights within the center.

Construct a role-play or structured deliberation with explicit roles—e.g., local municipal delegates, state or Länder representatives, national federal representatives, and a supranational coordination body—plus rules for proposing, blocking, or opting out of competence allocations. The payoff scheme should use status points or symbolic capital rather than real money, arranged so that defending local autonomy requires accepting outcomes that undermine the equal representation and transparency conditions that justify autonomy in the first place. Inject a niche but defensible twist into the rules: for example, an asymmetric opt-in/opt-out mechanism in which lower levels may reject central frameworks only if they bear the full external cost of that rejection, while joining requires unanimous consent from all other levels, or a hidden-information condition in which representatives do not know the true intensity of local preferences until after the allocation is locked.

Provide a clear timeline with phases (e.g., 10 minutes framing, 35 minutes first-round allocation, 25 minutes crisis/reallocation under revealed preferences, 20 minutes debrief). The rules should be arranged so that participants run into the coordination and legitimacy failure themselves before it is named; do not front-load the philosophical vocabulary.

Then provide a debrief that maps specific moments in the simulation onto real-world features: the US anti-commandeering doctrine and its limits, German Länder administration of federal law, Swiss cantonal direct democracy, and the EU subsidiarity protocol. Be frank about which feature of the philosophical problem—likely the infinite regress of who decides who decides—the activity can reproduce through the rules, and which feature—perhaps the normative justification for breaking the regress—must be supplied verbally by the facilitator without turning the session into a lecture.

Include an honest note on what will predictably go wrong the first time the activity is run—such as role confusion, premature strategic convergence, or participants treating the payoff scheme as purely competitive rather than normative—and tell the facilitator exactly how to handle it in the moment. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations. Do not ask participants to research external sources. Write in American English.
