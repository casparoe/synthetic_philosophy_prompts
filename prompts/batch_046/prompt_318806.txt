I'm working through a problem in philosophy of science with a study group, and we've reconstructed an argument about chaos and determinism that seems to turn determinism against itself, yet I keep returning to it without finding the precise slip. Here is the argument as laid out: (1) A world is deterministic just in case every state is uniquely fixed by prior states together with the laws of nature. (2) Chaotic systems are deterministic in exactly that sense—the governing equations are fully deterministic and contain no stochastic terms. (3) Yet because of exponential divergence of trajectories, any initial-state specification with physically achievable precision is compatible, within a short time, with a vast range of radically different future states. (4) Therefore, relative to any actual specification we can ever produce, the distant future of a chaotic system is not uniquely fixed. (5) If determinism is to be a substantive metaphysical thesis rather than an empty ontological decoration, it must be specification-relative: it must connect what we can actually specify to what will happen. (6) So in chaotic systems, determinism is not specification-relative, and therefore fails to be substantive. (7) Since chaos is pervasive in physical dynamics—weather, turbulence, orbital mechanics—this failure is widespread. (8) Hence determinism is either false or substantively empty everywhere that matters, and we should abandon it.

The student who presented this says it shows causal determinism refutes itself in any realistic physical setting. I feel like something slipped between (4) and (5), or perhaps between (2) and (6), but I can't name the equivocation or scope shift. Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? If determinism survives by being purely ontological and not specification-relative, does it lose all empirical content? Or is the flaw elsewhere—perhaps in treating measurement limitations as metaphysically relevant to what the laws fix? If I have to preserve anything, I want to keep (1) and (2) intact—the idea that chaos is genuinely deterministic—because giving those up feels like conceding too much to indeterminism.

Please tell me which premise or step is weakest, explain why it looked plausible, and price the alternatives precisely. In particular, say what argument or consideration would change your verdict: for instance, if someone showed that determinism was always intended to be purely ontological with no epistemic conditions, or if they demonstrated that chaos actually imposes hidden constraints that restore specification-relative predictability despite divergence. I also want you to identify where your own reasoning is most likely to fail—whether in assessing the cost of an ontological determinism, in treating physical measurement limits as metaphysically decisive, or elsewhere.
