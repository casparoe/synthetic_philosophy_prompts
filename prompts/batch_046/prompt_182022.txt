I spent the better part of this evening arguing with a close friend about Quine's "Two Dogmas of Empiricism" — not about whether anyone still defends the analytic-synthetic distinction in its classical form, but about whether Quine's critique genuinely dissolves the boundary or merely relocates it to questions of centrality within the web of belief. We kept returning to the same sticking point: if confirmation holism is correct, then even logical truths are in principle revisable, yet we still behave as though "all bachelors are unmarried" and "there is water in this glass" differ in kind rather than merely in position within our total scheme. Our discussion ended without resolution, and I should like to close it with a poem rather than a further lecture.

Could you write a poem of exactly sixteen lines, divided into two octaves with a volta at line nine? The first octave should present the two dogmas as distinct pillars: the belief that analytic truths are reducible to meaning, and the belief that every meaningful statement can be reduced to statements about immediate experience. Use a rhyme scheme of ABABCDCD, and let the rhymes pair terms that belong to the analytic side with terms that belong to the synthetic side — for instance, "definition" with "sensation", "logic" with "experience". The second octave should preserve the identical rhyme scheme and the same rhyming sounds, but should invert the conceptual assignment: the word that rhymed with the analytic term in the first stanza should now rhyme with its synthetic counterpart, so that the sonic architecture itself enacts Quine's claim that the distinction has not been erased but has migrated into the topology of belief. Include an image of the web of belief, treat logical truths as revisable without mockery, and leave the tension genuinely open at the end — no sermon about intervention, no shrug that everything is relative. I should prefer loose iambic pentameter or blank verse, but the rhyme scheme must hold precisely. The poem should be accurate enough that someone familiar with Quine recognises both dogmas and the holistic picture, and should feel like a philosophical impasse rendered in form rather than a summary of a well-known essay.
