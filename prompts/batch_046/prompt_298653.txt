I am drafting an essay—likely for a literary quarterly rather than an academic philosophy journal—on a peculiar conceptual migration I have observed in fiction workshops. The term "voice" appears to slide among three distinct nodes without anyone marking the border crossings: first, the acoustic reality of a character's literal speech; second, the stylistic fingerprint of an author's prose rhythm and diction; and third, the ethical-political claim of "giving voice to the voiceless." Rather than treating these as mere polysemy, I want to examine them as a small analogical domain, somewhat in the spirit of Hofstadter's Copycat project and his broader work on fluid analogies, where elasticity is governed by structural correspondences rather than by fixed definitions. To that end, I propose a "conceptual triangle": each vertex exerts pressure on the others, so that when an instructor praises a manuscript for having "found its voice," the praise may simultaneously invoke acoustic verisimilitude, stylistic consistency, and moral authorization, with the speaker rarely knowing which vertex is doing the work.

The essay would ask whether this triangle operates like a genuine fluid concept—where analogical mapping allows the same relational structure to be projected across domains—or whether it is simply a cluster of homonyms that workshop culture has allowed to blur for rhetorical convenience. I plan to test the analogy by constructing variations within the triangle: for instance, substituting "silence" for "voice" in the ethical-political vertex and asking whether the structural mapping to the acoustic vertex still holds, or whether it collapses. I also want to examine whether the normative urgency of the third vertex corrupts the analogical fluidity of the first two, since moral weight might freeze a concept that should otherwise remain pliable, creating a tension between descriptive analogy and prescriptive commitment that I have not yet resolved.

I would be grateful for your comments on the essay's conceptual framing rather than on its prose style. Specifically: does the comparison to analogical reasoning in fluid-concept domains hold water, or am I imposing a technical vocabulary onto ordinary linguistic ambiguity? Is there a hidden contradiction between treating "voice" as an analogically fluid concept and insisting that its ethical-political usage carries non-negotiable moral weight? And if you see a cheaper restructuring—perhaps by dropping one vertex of the triangle, or by replacing the Copycat analogy with a different model of conceptual change—please indicate it. I am not seeking bibliographic recommendations or a reading list; I am only interested in whether the commitments I have sketched hang together, and where the weakest joint lies.
