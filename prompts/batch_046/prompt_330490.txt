I have been thinking lately about those mental pictures scientists use to explain things—like Galileo dropping weights from a tower, Einstein imagining he is travelling beside a beam of light, or Schrödinger placing a cat in a sealed box with a quantum trigger. I understand they are meant to make complicated theories easier to picture, but I keep wondering whether they actually teach us anything new, or whether they are simply very clear stories. The view I have settled on, though I am unsure of it, is that a thought experiment can never produce real scientific knowledge by itself. At best, it pulls out something that was already hidden inside the theory or inside our everyday observations; at worst, it relies on conditions that could never happen—perfectly smooth surfaces, total vacuums, exact measurements—and then pretends that what happens in that imaginary world tells us about the real one.

What I want to understand is exactly what it would take for me to be wrong about this. I do not want a list of books or a summary of what philosophers have said; I want to know which kind of result or argument would genuinely defeat my scepticism. In particular, I am struck by cases in which scientists construct scenarios that violate physical laws—perfect vacuums, infinite planes, instantaneous measurements—and then draw conclusions about messy, real-world systems. Is there a specific structural feature, perhaps the isolation of a single causal variable while everything else is held fixed, that allows such an impossible setup to yield knowledge rather than fiction? Furthermore, I want to know whether defenders of the illustration-only view themselves concede any conditions under which a thought experiment could be genuinely epistemic, or whether they think the distinction is always illusory. And if my position is mistaken, is there a well-known historical case in which a mental exercise led to a prediction that was later confirmed in the laboratory, where the result was genuinely unexpected rather than merely a verification of what was already suspected? I would also like to understand whether removing that structural feature—allowing several variables to change together—would collapse the case back into a simple analogy, and what that tells us about why some thought experiments feel convincing while others feel like just-so stories.
