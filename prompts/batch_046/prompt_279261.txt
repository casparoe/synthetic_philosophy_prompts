You are composing the opening remarks for a memorial symposium honoring Dr. Elena Vasquez, a moral philosopher whose work centered on the boundary between impartial moral reasoning and personal integrity. The audience is divided: moral philosophers and graduate students familiar with Bernard Williams’s integrity objection and with the idea that certain deliberations involve one thought too many; and family members, including her sister and nieces, for whom these concepts are unfamiliar. The remarks should run about ten minutes and must treat the philosophical idea as structural rather than decorative: specifically, they must explain why Vasquez held that there is a distinct kind of moral failure—not mere partiality or weakness—when universal cost-benefit reasoning is permitted to colonize personal attachments, and they must use this framework to illuminate two documented choices in her life. First, her refusal to justify prioritizing her sister’s care during a prolonged illness through impartial moral calculus; second, her quiet resignation from a university ethics board when it required her to evaluate funding for her own department using the same metrics she applied to strangers. Craft the speech so that the philosophers recognize the precise distinction between ground projects that confer meaning on a life and the demands of agent-neutral moral theory, while the family hears a coherent defense of her loyalties that does not reduce them to selfishness. Include one concrete, non-technical anecdote that illustrates the boundary she defended. Do not provide citations, page numbers, or a reading list; the argument must stand entirely on its own reasoning. The remarks should conclude with a formulation of the objection that is technically accurate—distinguishing it from a general license for partiality—yet fully intelligible to listeners with no background in moral philosophy.
