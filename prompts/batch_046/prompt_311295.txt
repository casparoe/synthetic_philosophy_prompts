I've been trying to work out whether my moral intuition is actually principled or just a post-hoc story I tell myself, and I keep running into this weird asymmetry that I can't shake. On the one hand I have this absolute, hard-stop rule about confidences: if someone tells me something in private, I treat that as a constraint I don't violate, period. Even if staying silent means they walk into a disaster I could have prevented with one sentence — like if I knew my roommate was about to sign a lease with a landlord I have proof is a scammer, I'd still keep my mouth shut because it was told to me in confidence. I see that as a negative duty, not up for cost-benefit analysis.

But on the other hand, I don't feel any obligation to do small positive things that would barely cost me. If a friend asks me to spend ten minutes helping them proof a grant proposal that could get them funded, and I have the time but just don't feel like it, I'll say no and I don't think I'm doing anything wrong. It's my time, my option, not my problem. Recently a coworker called me out on this: she said I'm "morally lopsided" because I'll maintain an iron wall of silence that might let real harm happen, but I won't lift a finger to prevent harm when the lift is tiny. She thinks if negative duties are that strong, then cheap positive duties should be strong too, or else I'm just picking seperate categories to excuse being lazy. I told her there's a real distinction between actively breaking a promise and merely failing to be a hero, but she said that distinction collapses once you admit that harm can come from omission just like commission. Is she right that there's a tension here? Or is there a principled way to hold both views without being a hypocrit?
