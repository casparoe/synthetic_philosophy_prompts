Draft the joint statement that an informational self-determination camp and a democratic-transparency camp could both sign after an adversarial collaboration on the question: How much should citizens hide from the state?

Define the first camp as holding that citizens possess a default right of broad informational opacity to state agencies, anchored in post-war German constitutional jurisprudence—specifically the census-decision framework that treats data protection as an aspect of personality rights—and extending this resistance to centralized profiling, cross-agency data fusion, and predictive scoring. Define the second camp as holding that democratic legitimacy requires a structural asymmetry in which state agencies are maximally transparent to citizens while citizens are minimally hidden from legitimate democratic oversight, drawing on American open-government and sunshine traditions; on this view, Chinese social-credit architectures are not categorically illegitimate but represent a miscalibrated extreme of this asymmetry, with individual hiding reserved for narrowly defined vulnerabilities rather than treated as a default status.

The deliverable should be formatted as a numbered agreed-claims section, followed by a disagreements table, followed by a crux-and-prediction appendix and a brief diagnosis of verbal versus substantive dispute. Specifically:

1. Agreed claims (numbered): Include claims both camps accept about (a) the institutional architecture of German data-protection law, American FOIA and sunshine regimes, and Chinese social-credit scoring mechanisms; (b) the empirical correlations between high citizen-state information asymmetry and outcomes such as corruption, discrimination, or participatory efficacy; and (c) the conceptual distinction between an individual’s concealment of personal data and an institution’s concealment of its decision-making processes from public view.

2. Disagreements table: Two columns, one for each camp, containing the precise formulations of residual disagreement phrased so that each side would accept the wording in its own column. Cover at least: whether informational opacity is a default constitutional right or a narrowly granted exception; whether American openness norms apply symmetrically to citizens and to state bodies, or only unidirectionally; and whether social-credit systems are categorically illegitimate regardless of calibration, or illegitimate only when they violate specific procedural safeguards.

3. Cruxes: Identify the sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one camp. For each crux, specify a concrete case, institutional feature, or comparative governance mechanism—such as a particular data-use scenario, an oversight-board design, or a profiling-threshold rule—that both sides agree bears on the crux, and record in advance each camp’s prediction about its outcome or effect.

4. Diagnosis: State whether any portion of the dispute is verbal rather than substantive—particularly regarding whether “privacy” denotes a property of individuals, of information flows, or of institutional boundaries between citizen and state—and explain exactly why that portion should be treated as a terminological divergence rather than a disagreement about facts or values.

Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citation requests. Do not quote specific texts or rulings verbatim; if referencing particular doctrines or decisions, paraphrase without quotation marks. Write in a formal, precise, and polished style.
