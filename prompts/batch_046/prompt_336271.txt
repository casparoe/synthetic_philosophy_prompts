(2) But people miss a distinction: there's resource inheritance, which is just cash and property, and then there's relational inheritance—the networks, the norms, the confidence that comes from knowing you have a backstop if things go bad. (3) If we're serious about neutralizing dynastic advantage, we have to tackle both kinds. Taxing cash is administratively easy, but neutralizing relational inheritance would mean either banning parents from teaching thier kids social skills or constantly auditing family culture, which is incoherent and creepy. (4) So the principle that justifies taxing resources also justifies regulating family life, which nobody actually accepts. (5) That makes inheritance taxes arbitrary: they hit the measurable part of dynastic advantage but leave the deeper part untouched, and if you try to go deeper you destroy family autonomy. (6) Therefore we either accept dynastic wealth as inevitable or we accept a totalizing state; there's no stable middle ground that actually delivers equal opportunity. (7) So inheritance taxes can't be justified on equal-opportunity grounds without also justifying something monstrous. Where does this slip? I'd honestly rather keep (1) and the distinction in (3) than (7), but I want to know what giving up (7) costs me.
