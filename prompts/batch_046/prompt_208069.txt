I run an upper-level phil of math seminar, about 10 students, 75 minutes. I want them to feel the specific vertigo of discovering that a basic axiom system they trusted might actually be inconsistent—not just learn Gödel's theorems, but experience the destabilization firsthand. Design a simulation with roles: each table of 3 gets an "axiom kit" (4 rules that seem harmless, involving basic sets and a restricted choice principle) and has 25 minutes to derive "theorems" and post them on a shared board, competing for the longest valid chain. Then you (the facilitator) reveal that Rule B and Rule D, used together in a specific way they've probably already employed, generate an object that violates Rule A—using only steps they've already endorsed. They have 15 minutes in-character to decide: reject the object, reject one axiom, or accept contradiction and continue. Include payoffs (points for derivations, heavy penalty for contradiction), a timeline with exact minites, and a debrief mapping their reactions—denial, bargaining, splitting the system—onto actual philosophical responses to a foundational crisis. Be frank about what feature this can't reproduce, like mathematicians continuing to work despite unproven consistency. Don't ask for bibliographys, reading lists, or quotes from specific texts; the rules should be self-contained in the room. Write casual, and don't worry if there's a typo or two.
