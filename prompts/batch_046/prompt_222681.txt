I've come around to thinking the discursive dilemma is mostly an artifact of forcing groups through a two-stage pipeline—vote on premises, then derive the conclusion—when they could just evaluate whole scenario-bundles directly. If each person assigns continuous credibility weights to every possible world-state that includes both the premises and the conclusion together, and we aggregate by summed coherence, there's no separate premise-level vote to clash with the conclusion-level one. The paradox never gets started, and we don't have to default to some hierarchical authority either.

But I want to know what would actualy count against this. Which formal results show that any coherence-maximizing rule still ends up structurally equivalent to the dilemma? In real deliberative settings, what hidden costs does "bundle voting" have—strategic manipualtion of the scenario space, or just cognitive overload? And have I qualified this into unfalsifiability: if every failure gets blamed on poor scenario design, is that a fatal flaw for an institutional proposal?

Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward. Tell me which ones I'd have to concede even if I stay committed, and whether there's a version of my view that risks nothing at all—which might itself be a problem.
