I am a philosophy professor who advises a non-partisan legislative drafting office in a pluralistic jurisdiction. Our staff must routinely evaluate whether the normative justifications in proposed policy briefs satisfy the constraints of public reason: that is, whether they rely on premises that citizens holding reasonable but incompatible comprehensive doctrines could not reasonably accept. Because the staff have legal training but limited background in political philosophy, I need a reusable, step-by-step decision procedure they can apply without consulting me on every brief. The protocol should proceed in ordered stages—identifying the normative premises, testing for dependence on comprehensive doctrines, assessing whether a secular translation or proviso is available, determining whether the argument remains coercive once religious or metaphysical premises are bracketed, and specifying the point at which the evaluation must be handed back to human judgment because the issue turns on contested interpretations of reasonableness rather than on determinable facts. Please include explicit stopping rules that yield a clear pass, a clear fail, or an escalation. Then run the procedure on three briefs: (a) a proposal to expand welfare benefits grounded in a theological account of human dignity; (b) a proposal to restrict immigration justified by a secular thesis about cultural preservation that implicitly tracks a comprehensive doctrine; and (c) a proposal for open borders justified exclusively by economic-efficiency premises that are themselves disputed within the domain of reasonable pluralism. In your response, apply the protocol rigorously to each case, include at least one instance where the procedure produces a result that conflicts with the staff's likely intuitive political judgment, and conclude by stating precisely what categories of disagreement the protocol cannot resolve and where it is liable to misfire.
