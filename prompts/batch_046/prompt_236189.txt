I’m preparing a long-form feature on judgment aggregation and the discursive dilemma—the problem, formalized in social-choice theory, that a group can hold consistent majority judgments on every premise of an argument yet reach an inconsistent collective conclusion, and vice versa—and I keep returning to the classic jury-room drama 12 Angry Men as an unexpectedly precise dramatization of it. Read the film through that lens: treat each juror’s private views on the evidentiary premises—whether the switchblade was genuinely unique, whether the elderly witness could have reached the door in time given his limp, whether the defendant’s alibi about being at the movies held up—and on the final conclusion, guilty beyond reasonable doubt, as inputs to an aggregation procedure. Does the opening 11-to-1 vote for guilty reflect a conclusion-based aggregation that may conflict with the majority judgments on the individual premises? As one juror forces the room to examine each piece of evidence separately, is the group effectively shifting to a premise-based rule, and does that shift resolve the discursive dilemma or merely relocate it?

Point me to the scenes where the philosophical idea illuminates a character’s move—the demonstration with the identical knife, the reconstruction of the old man’s walking pace, the debate over the boy’s relationship with his father—while also saying where the film resists the framework. In particular, does the drama treat deliberation as a static voting rule applied to fixed beliefs, or as a dynamic process of belief revision, emotional persuasion, and social pressure that transforms the premises themselves? Finally, what does the work add to the philosophical debate that a formal model of premise- versus conclusion-based aggregation misses—does it suggest that the discursive dilemma is not merely a logical puzzle solved by picking the right procedure, but a symptom of treating collective judgment as something to be averaged rather than discursively produced?
