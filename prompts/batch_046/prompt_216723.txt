As a novelist accustomed to reconstructing a character's past from fragmentary letters, I notice that historiography is often likened to forensic reconstruction — the historian as detective weighing testimony. I want to press a stricter analogy that circulates at the margins: the source as palimpsest. Rather than treating Herodotus and Thucydides as witnesses whose contradictions must be reconciled, treat them as successive inscriptions over an irrecoverable original, where institutional pressure does the overwriting and the contradictions themselves are the primary data — not noise to be eliminated, but the visible trace of deformation. Give me the correspondence table: what plays the role of the original manuscript, the overwriting scribe, the chemical reagent, and the historian-reader? Then show me where the palimpsest image breaks, particularly regarding deliberate fabrication versus organic distortion, and tell me whether the methodological conclusion — that we ought to study contradiction rather than resolve it — survives once those disanalogies are on the table, or whether the analogy was carrying the argument all along.
