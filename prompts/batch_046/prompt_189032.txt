I am preparing for a seminar on distributive justice, and I want to check my understanding of a disagreement that arose between two members of my study group—call them A and B. They constructed a thought experiment to pressure-test luck egalitarianism: two individuals, R and S, are born with identical talents and resources and grow up in the same neighborhood, but during adolescence they are randomly assigned to different peer groups. R’s friends valorize entrepreneurial risk; S’s family and friends are shaped by witnessing a bankruptcy and valorize security. Both internalize these values, then choose matching careers. R launches a startup that later fails because of an unforeseeable market shift; S becomes a civil servant with stable but modest earnings. The question is whether justice requires redistributing some of R’s remaining assets to S.

A defended the non-redistribution view by arguing that R’s career choice was option luck: she voluntarily embraced risk, so she must bear the cost. B objected that the preference for risk itself was brute luck, since R never chose her peer group. A replied that tracing choices backward through every preference-shaping contingency dissolves option luck entirely, collapsing luck egalitarianism into full outcome equality; the theory must draw a line somewhere. B proposed drawing it at the boundary of social influence: peer assignment is a correctable social brute-luck factor, whereas innate risk-tolerance is not. A then charged that B was smuggling in a prior theory of legitimate social structure, making luck egalitarianism circular—its application depends on a prior account of justice that it was supposed to deliver. B conceded the circularity worry but insisted it was unavoidable, and added that A overlooked what she called “recursive preference revision”: after the failure, R might update her risk preferences, but that update is itself structured by the prior brute-luck-laden framework, so the option-luck assessment remains contaminated even ex post.

Please adjudicate this exchange move by move. Rate each of the five moves—(1) the option-luck defense, (2) the brute-luck-of-preferences objection, (3) the social-boundary reply, (4) the circularity charge, and (5) the recursive-revision point—from clearly mistaken to clearly correct, with brief justification for each rating. Then say what both parties are missing, especially regarding whether luck egalitarianism can be non-circular if it must presuppose which preference-forming conditions are just. Be explicit about what argument or empirical finding would change your verdict, and identify where your reasoning is most likely to fail. Keep your entire response under 450 words.
