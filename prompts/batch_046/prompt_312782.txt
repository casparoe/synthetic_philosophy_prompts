I've been thinking on my own about what people call peer disagreement — the question of whether you should change your belief just because someone you respect believes the opposite. I don't have a philosophy background, but after long conversations with two friends, Jordan and Riley, I want an outside referee to go through their exchange point by point. Here is exactly what happened, in four rounds.

Jordan opened by saying that if two people share the same evidence and the same reasoning ability but reach opposite conclusions, rationality demands they split the difference. Otherwise, he said, each is just treating herself as infallible. Riley replied that this ignores hidden asymmetries: one person might have missed a study, be tired, or be influenced by an unstated bias. She said steadfastness — holding your ground when you have reason to think your reasoning was sound — is often the rational response, and that splitting the difference can just average truth with error.

Jordan came back that Riley was sneaking in a self-serving assumption. Without some independent check, declaring "I'm the careful one" is just rationalizing. He said that if both parties can do that, disagreement becomes useless and nobody ever updates. Riley answered that we do have independent checks — track records, institutional roles, who actually did the primary work — and that a climate scientist shouldn't split the difference with a lay denier just because they both have brains. She added that Jordan's view, taken seriously, would force moral compromise in value-laden cases: if Jordan thinks factory farming is monstrous and an equally reflective friend finds it acceptable, conciliationism pushes him to weaken his stance just because of disagreement, which feels wrong.

Jordan then raised what he called a "second-order" problem for Riley: to claim you're not peers, you need meta-evidence about whose reasoning is better, but that meta-evidence is itself open to disagreement. So Riley's appeal to hidden asymmetries collapses into an infinite regress of peer-checking. In practice, he said, people invoke "peer disagreement" precisely when they want an excuse not to update. Riley conceded the regress worry but said people solve it with practical heuristics — credentials, past accuracy, division of labor — not with infinite loops, and that Jordan's split-the-difference rule is just as vulnerable, since deciding how far to split requires knowing how far apart the peers really are, which brings the same problem back.
