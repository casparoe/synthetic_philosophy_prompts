Consider the institutional practice in higher education of evaluating faculty for tenure and promotion through bifurcated rubrics: in service and governance there is a clearly defined minimum—committee work, advising, administrative tasks—whose fulfillment is necessary for advancement but whose overfulfillment is treated as genuinely optional, praiseworthy, and never as a hidden requirement; in research and scholarship, by contrast, there is also a formally defined baseline of peer-reviewed publications, funded grants, and disciplinary recognition, yet in practice that baseline functions as a necessary condition that is rarely sufficient, with candidates expected to demonstrate impact or distinction well above the threshold and with performance near the baseline typically judged inadequate rather than adequate-but-non-exemplary. What philosophical commitments about the structure of professional obligation does this asymmetry presuppose? Does it assume that duty has an upper bound in some domains but not in others, or that service and knowledge-production carry different modal statuses regarding what can legitimately be demanded? Reconstruct the implicit theory of obligation and supererogation embedded in this evaluation architecture, distinguishing what the practice strictly requires from what merely fits it well. Is the bifurcation coherent, or does it depend on conflating instrumental value with intrinsic value, or on treating autonomy-based reasons differently across domains? Finally, describe what a tenure-review system built on a consistent, domain-neutral theory of supererogation—one that either treats all excellence as genuinely optional or treats all formal baselines as genuinely sufficient—would have to change in actual committee practice.
