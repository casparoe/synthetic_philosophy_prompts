I'm giving the commencement address next Friday for the engineering and robotics programme at my old university — about three hundred grads, plus family and a few philosophy of science people who always sit in the front row. The dean asked me to avoid career advice and talk about "what you're actually building for," but honestly that phrase feels a bit hollow unless there's a real idea behind it.

I was reading about Von Neumann's view that technology doesn't really ask permission once it reaches a certain scale — he seemed to think the question isn't whether something gets built, but what kind of survivor it becomes after the people who made it are gone. I want to use that to shape the whole speech: not a warning, but an argument that these graduates are in a strange counterfactual position. If they refuse to build a particular system, some version of it still gets built elsewhere, just without their values in it. So the ethical weight isn't on stopping progress — that's not even available — it's on deciding what values survive inside the technology.

Can you write a ten-minute address that lands this without turning into a philosophy lecture? The family in the room shouldn't need to know decision theory, but the robotics people should feel I'm not just saying "be nice." I want the Von Neumann idea to do real structural work: it should determine what I can and can't claim about optimism, responsibility, and regret. Oh, and please don't include any reading list or citations — half the audience wouldn't understand them and it breaks the flow.
