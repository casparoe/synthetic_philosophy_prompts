In the philosophy of machine learning, the dispute over whether deep neural networks literally possess representations—rather than merely being usefully described as if they do—seems to have reached an impasse that I find genuinely puzzling. Realists point to mechanistic interpretability results: feature visualization in vision transformers, linear probes that decode semantic properties from hidden states, causal interventions showing that ablating specific directions changes behavior in concept-specific ways, and recent sparse autoencoder work isolating apparently monosemantic features. They take these findings as evidence that internal vectors function as representations with combinatorial structure, approximate error conditions, and causal efficacy. Anti-realists or deflationists accept the same empirical record but deny the ontological inference, arguing that predictive success localized in parameter space does not entail representational content unless one already imports a functional or teleological framework—such as decouplability, counterfactual robustness, or error-correction mechanisms—that feedforward architectures trained on classification or next-token loss do not obviously satisfy.

What makes the stall frustrating is that both sides agree on the data—including on phenomena like superposition, where a single neuron encodes multiple conceptually unrelated directions—yet neither side has shifted. My working hypothesis is that part of the disagreement is verbal, turning on whether "representation" requires something stronger than reliable correlation with environmental features, such as being deployable in reasoning tasks that run counter to the training distribution; but I also suspect a substantive divergence about what counts as an explanation in cognitive science versus engineering. I am unsure, however, whether there is a hidden empirical question doing the work: for example, whether representational status should depend on whether a feature supports out-of-distribution generalization that tracks structural properties of the world rather than surface statistical patterns, a distinction that standard probing experiments rarely isolate cleanly, or whether it requires evidence of compositional recombination under novel task demands.

Given this, I want a diagnosis of the disagreement’s structure rather than an adjudication of which side is correct. Is this best understood as two groups answering different questions—one about the ontology of computation, the other about the pragmatics of scientific modeling—under the single heading "do networks represent"? Or is there a genuine, unresolved empirical disagreement about necessary conditions for representational content that could in principle be settled by a specific conceptual distinction, a formal result about functional architectures, or an experimental design testing counterfactual deployment? And if the impasse is largely bedrock—rooted in incompatible intuitions about what a system must do to count as representing—what should count as progress: a refined taxonomy of functional roles that both sides accept, a demonstration that certain interpretable features systematically fail in reasoning contexts, or an honest argument that the question is ill-posed for artificial systems lacking the relevant evolutionary or normative history?
