I'm prepping notes for my grad seminar on epistemic circularity and got to thinking about my old advisor's grading rubric for bootstrapping hypotheticals — she's been gone for years but her verdicts are still weirdly consistent. Here are the ones I reconstructed from her old exams and some marginalia:

- The classic Cohen-style case: you use knowledge that you have hands to derive you're not a BIV, then license further perceptual beliefs. She said illegitimate — you don't know you're not a BIV that way.
- Thermometer loop with an independent anchor: check a digital thermometer against melting ice (a physical constant you access without instruments), then measure boiling water. Full knowledge, she said.
- Testimonial circle: witness A confirms B's reliability and B confirms A's, no outside entry point. No knowledge gained of either's reliability.
- Memory-recalibration: recall that you recalled correctly yesterday, then trust today's recollection. The today-belief can still be knowledge, but the meta-belief about your memory's reliability is not.
- Mathematical induction bootstrap: use a restricted, intuitionistically safe fragment of arithmetic to justify full PA consistency, then apply PA. The consistency claim isn't known; the applied result can be.

Next week's case is one I cooked up — a reflexive calibration through a synthetic mediator. A researcher trains a bias-correction algorithm on her own past judgments, including ones made before she knew about the bias. Once trained, it flags which current beliefs are distorted. She uses one belief independently verified by a colleague (outside the loop) to check the algorithm works, then applies it to correct her other beliefs, including the belief that her original training data was unbiased. The intermediate belief — that the algorithm is reliable — is true, formed reliably, true in nearby worlds, but part of the training data are the very judgments it's now correcting.
