A colleague summarized the philosophical view on art forgeries and originals as follows. The reason an original painting is more valuable than a perfect visual duplicate is that only the original stands in a direct causal-historical relation to the artist's own act of creation—what might be called an authentic causal chain—whereas the copy does not. Because aesthetic appreciation is essentially an appreciation of that specific intentional act, a forgery is aesthetically defective even when no observer can detect the difference, and knowledge of its origin should change our evaluation. The colleague then extended this reasoning to digital reproduction, claiming that a high-resolution museum scan is therefore also a kind of forgery, since the pixels were not arranged by the painter's hand, and that displaying such a scan in place of the canvas is ethically equivalent to hanging a copied painting. I suspect this overreaches but I am unsure where the philosophy ends and the extension begins. Which parts of this summary fairly represent the causal-history or intentionalist account of artistic value? Where does it conflate aesthetic properties with provenance, market value, or the ethics of display? And does the argument about digital scans actually follow from the premises about hand-painted forgeries, or does it confuse different senses in which an object can fail to be identical to an original?
