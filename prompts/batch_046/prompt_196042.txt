I've been putting together materials for an advanced philosophy of mind seminar I'm co-teaching this term, and we've reached the unit on affective ontology—specifically the old triad of judgmental, perceptual, and feeling theories of emotion. The students keep sorting themselves by philosopher names rather than by commitments, so I want a diagnostic decision tree that forces them to navigate by concrete claims. The tree should begin with whether emotions possess intentionality in the same robust sense that beliefs do, then split by whether that intentionality takes propositional form (the judgmental line associated with Nussbaum and Solomon), non-propositional but world-directed form (the perceptual line running through Prinz and Tappolet), or is altogether absent in favor of pure bodily phenomenology (the feeling line from James through Damasio). At the next level, each branch should split on whether emotional experience necessarily includes a distinctive bodily phenomenology that is constitutive rather than merely causal. Then I want a further branching criterion I'm calling affective framing: does the state function primarily as a structuring of the agent's practical field—opening and closing possibilities for action—without being reducible either to a representational content or to a bare qualitative feel? Every leaf should be labeled with the actual position name if one exists, or marked unnamed/unoccupied if the combination is logically coherent but philosophically abandoned. Each leaf must carry one sentence describing its most awkward commitment—something about emotional rationality, transparency, or cultural variability that the view must swallow. The tree should contain six to eight leaves, should be navigable by yes/no answers to the branch questions without knowing any philosopher's name, and should explicitly flag any pair of positions that the tree cannot genuinely separate. Finally, please note one branch combination that remains empty and diagnose briefly whether its emptiness is due to incoherence, unattractiveness, or mere neglect.
