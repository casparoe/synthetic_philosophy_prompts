I have noticed a recurring argumentative structure that sits near this cluster but does not map neatly onto any of the standard labels. Here are two concrete instances.

First instance: A researcher trusts her laboratory instruments for measuring local chemical concentrations, but when the same instruments are used in large-scale ecological surveys she dismisses the results. She does not attack the instruments globally; instead, she posits domain-specific interference—micro-climatic noise, sampling bias—that applies only to the ecological context. Having protected her laboratory beliefs from this failure mode, she then cites her continued, unproblematic laboratory successes as evidence that instrument-based reasoning is generally sound, and therefore treats demands for independent, non-instrumental ecological verification as unnecessary.

Second instance: A philosopher accepts perceptual knowledge about ordinary mid-sized objects but denies it in dreams. When asked how she knows she is not dreaming now, she does not invoke a general skeptical argument. Instead, she introduces a contextual reliability clause—perception is trustworthy only in appropriate contexts—and then uses her successful ordinary perceptual judgments as evidence that she is currently in such a context. The beliefs being protected are not used to validate perception directly; rather, the method’s successes in one domain justify a boundary that shields beliefs formed in another domain, while failures in the shielded domain motivate the boundary itself.

In both cases, there is a loop: successes of method M in domain A justify a restriction that protects beliefs formed by M in domain B; failures of M in domain B motivate the restriction rather than undermining M globally; and the protected beliefs in B are then treated as secure without independent validation. This is not the classic bootstrapping loop, because the protected belief is not used to establish the reliability of M. It is also not a standard auxiliary hypothesis, because the boundary is not added to save a specific theoretical prediction but to partition M’s epistemic status across contexts.
