I'm drafting a technically precise Q&A for a podcast episode on the architecture of practical reason. The guest is an unnamed philosopher defending what she calls layered indirect normativity: normative reasons do not apply directly to an agent's actual mental states, but only to the outputs of successive stages of idealized deliberation, where each stage corrects for the informational and motivational limitations of the one below. She argues that this explains why we can legitimately blame someone for a decision that was, at the first-order level, cognitively unavoidable—the failure is located in the pipeline's architecture rather than in the local choice. I want nine questions that avoid exposition and instead press where the literature presses, with each major question paired with a follow-up keyed to the reply she is most likely to give.

First, ask how her mechanistically specified corrector layers differ from standard omniscient ideal-observer theories, and include a follow-up anticipating her answer—that the layers are algorithmically defined rather than epistemically perfect—asking whether that algorithmic specification itself smuggles in normative commitments about which biases count as correctable.

Second, ask about the regress of correction: if every layer requires a further corrector to validate its outputs, do we ever reach a normatively authoritative base, or does the pipeline extend infinitely? The follow-up should anticipate her reply that the regress is benign, analogous to coherence conditions in belief revision, and then press whether that collapses her view into direct normativity at the limit.

Third, ask a question connecting her framework to concerns familiar from rationalist decision theory: if an agent evaluates her own past reasoning using this model, she must apparently run the full layered simulation, which seems computationally intractable in the same way that exact Bayesian updating is. Ask whether she accepts that self-evaluation is therefore always approximate, with a follow-up probing whether approximation undermines the normative authority she assigns to the pipeline's final output.

Fourth, ask about moral worth on her account: does an agent ever act with full moral worth, or is worth always derivative on the alignment between first-order motivation and the final-layer verdict? Include a follow-up asking what happens in cases of systematic misalignment—when the pipeline outputs a verdict that contradicts the agent's considered reflective equilibrium—and whether she has a principled reason to privilege the pipeline.

Finally, ask what kind of conceptual counterexample or empirical finding could actually dislodge layered indirect normativity, specifically inviting her to consider whether there are domains where first-order normative intuitions should override the idealized verdict, and if she denies this, whether her view risks unfalsifiability.

The audience is comfortable with Parfit-style reasoning about identity and with LessWrong-style vocabulary around coherence and bounded rationality, so the questions can use that register without dilution. No requests for bibliographies, citations, or reading lists—just the questions and their paired follow-ups, clearly delineated.
