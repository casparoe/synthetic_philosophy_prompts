Draft the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between two opposing interpretive camps on Plato’s views on drunkenness. Camp A, the Symposium-optimists, holds that Plato treats controlled intoxication as a philosophical pharmakon that reveals stable character through the basanos of wine, with the symposiarch serving as a rational regulator who ensures the test remains diagnostic rather than destructive; they read the Symposium’s drinking party as a genuine, if elite-confined, endorsement of moderated inebriation for self-knowledge. Camp B, the Laws-pessimists, holds that Plato consistently treats drunkenness as a civic poison that dissolves to logistikon and therefore demands legal prohibition in the well-ordered polis, reading the Symposium’s apparent praise as either dramatic irony, strictly restricted to an exceptional philosophical elite, or ultimately subordinate to the Republic and Laws’ broader program of temperance (sophrosyne).

The document should contain:
1. A numbered section of claims both camps can sign—agreement on the textual evidence in the Symposium’s account of Alcibiades’ intrusion and the Laws’ extended discussion of wine-drinking and civic regulation; agreement on Plato’s broader distinction between beneficial and harmful pleasure; agreement on the institutional role of the symposiarch; and agreement that neither camp denies the textual presence of both celebratory and prohibitory passages.
2. A disagreements table in which each camp’s core position is stated in wording its own members would accept, paired with the opposing camp’s neutral formulation of that same disagreement—wording each side could accept as describing its own view. Identify whether any portion of the dispute is verbal rather than substantive.
3. The cruxes—sub-questions such that resolving them would shift at least one camp. One crux must involve the hypothetical “Magnesian civic symposium”: a state-sponsored, moderated drinking ritual proposed within the institutional framework of Magnesia, designed not for philosophers but for ordinary citizens, intended to test temperance through regulated consumption rather than to foster philosophical insight.
4. For each crux, specify a concrete argument, textual comparison, or hypothetical institutional prediction both camps agree is relevant, and record in advance what each camp predicts about it—particularly what each predicts the Magnesian civic symposium would produce in terms of civic character versus civic disorder.

In drafting, avoid bibliographies, reading lists, or citations to secondary scholarship; do not quote verbatim from Plato unless you retrieve and copy the exact passage—otherwise paraphrase without quotation marks. Ensure the residual disagreement is genuine and precisely located, and say explicitly if any part reduces to emphasis rather than substance.
