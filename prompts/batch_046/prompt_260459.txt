As a novelist who constructs characters in conditions of extreme isolation, I have been turning over Adam Smith's account of sympathy and the impartial spectator, and I find myself caught in an argument that seems to prove too much. (1) For Smith, sympathy is not the transmission of feeling but an imaginative exchange: I represent your situation to myself and form a proportionate sentiment about its cause. (2) The impartial spectator is formed by internalising the corrective judgments of actual social others, so that moral self-assessment is never merely private. (3) If sympathy requires an accurate experiential representation of the other's situation, then I cannot sympathise with beings whose consciousness is structurally alien to mine—say, a synthetic mind with no continuous memory, or a creature for whom duration is experienced as simultaneity—because I lack the experiential basis for the imaginative exchange. (4) Yet Smith clearly extends sympathy to animals and even to the dead, cases in which literal occupation of the situation is impossible. (5) Therefore sympathy cannot depend on accurate experiential representation; it depends only on constructing a proportionate sentiment about whatever cause I attribute to the other. (6) But if the spectator is formed from these constructed sympathies, then he is not an independent social check on my passions but merely a rationalisation of sentiments I have already manufactured for myself. (7) Hence Smith's theory either excludes sympathy from precisely the alien or inaccessible cases he wishes to include, or it collapses the normative authority of the impartial spectator into solipsistic self-approval, undermining the claim that morality originates in social sentiment rather than reason. Which step should I surrender, and why did it look plausible? Then tell me what giving it up costs each of the standard readings—whether one preserves sympathy at the expense of social correction, or preserves correction at the expense of universal sympathy—and please answer in British English.
