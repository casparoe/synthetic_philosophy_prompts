As a retired biologist who has only recently begun working through philosophy of mind, I have tried to settle on a coherent package of views about consciousness, and I would like a consistency audit before I present them to a discussion group. Here is where I stand: (1) I am a physicalist—every conscious state is identical to or realized by some neurobiological process. (2) I accept a higher-order representational theory: a mental state becomes conscious only when it is targeted by a suitable meta-representation. (3) I take phenomenal concepts to be genuinely special, in that they open an explanatory gap between third-person physical descriptions and first-person phenomenal knowledge; still, I deny that this gap undermines physicalism. (4) I regard the philosophical zombie—physically identical to a conscious human but lacking inner experience—as genuinely conceivable, though I treat conceivability as only a fallible guide to metaphysical possibility. (5) I deny consciousness to any system that lacks recursive self-monitoring, so I hold that there is nothing it is like to be a thermostat or a present-day large language model. (6) I also hold a more specific, empirically flavored constraint: the temporal integration window required for conscious access is metabolically scaled rather than fixed, which means a substrate with sufficiently slowed neural processing could, in principle, integrate representations across several seconds rather than the usual 200–500 milliseconds and still generate conscious experience.

Please go through these commitments pair by pair. Tell me which pairs are in genuine tension, which conflicts are only apparent and dissolve on inspection, and rank the real tensions by severity. For each genuine tension, say how badly it threatens the package, what the cheapest revision would be, and what that revision costs. Finally, identify the single change to one commitment that would clean up the most problems overall.
