After a long and somewhat exhausting argument with a friend last night about whether consequentialism can survive its own epistemic limitations, I tried to organize my scattered thoughts into the outline below for a short essay. The familiar first-order cluelessness worry—that agents cannot predict the distant, cascading effects of their actions well enough to justify consequentialist calculation—strikes me as only half the problem. I want to argue that there is a deeper, second-order cluelessness embedded in the very remedies we propose: prediction markets, expert aggregation, and scenario planning all depend on prior framing choices about which outcomes count as relevant and how near and far futures should be weighted. Because these framing commitments determine the evaluative landscape before any calculation occurs, they lie outside the scope of consequentialist assessment, making the attempt to reduce ignorance structurally self-undermining. I then plan to contrast this with the precautionary principle, suggesting it suffers a mirror-image failure—by halting action to avoid unknown harms, it implicitly privileges present goods over future ones without any consequentialist justification for that weighting. The essay would close by claiming that both consequentialists and their critics mistakenly treat epistemic humility as a procedural patch rather than recognizing it as a substantive normative commitment that already decides the moral question in advance. I am particularly unsure whether this second-order framing is philosophically coherent, whether the comparison to precautionary reasoning holds, and whether my conclusion overreaches by collapsing epistemology into ethics. Please provide exactly five numbered comments, each one or two sentences long, with no introduction and no conclusion.
