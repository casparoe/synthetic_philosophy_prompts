As a retired physicist who has only recently begun studying philosophy of gender and social ontology, I have been examining the gender-equality paradox: the empirical regularity that societies with stronger institutional gender-equality measures tend to display greater sex-based differentiation in occupational choice. The standard interpretation holds that cultural suppression masks true preferences, and that removing constraints allows an underlying biological baseline to emerge—much as removing an external field permits spins to align with internal anisotropy. To explore this rigorously, I have modeled career selection as relaxation within a potential landscape, where egalitarian institutions lower inter-basin barriers and allow the system to settle into deeper, gender-correlated minima. From this framework I conclude that the observed clustering represents an intrinsic equilibrium state rather than a socially constructed artifact. Given this, I wish to ask a normative question: if distributive justice requires correcting for arbitrary social influences, yet the clustering is the unperturbed equilibrium of the system, does justice demand that we actively engineer asymmetric potential barriers—specifically raising the effective energy cost for individuals to enter occupational basins correlated with their own sex—in order to restore statistical parity, or does justice instead require preserving a symmetric landscape and accepting the resulting distribution as fair?
