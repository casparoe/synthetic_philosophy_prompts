In reporting on the recent revival of interest in Benjamin Franklin's ethical self-engineering—specifically, his autobiographical scheme of tracking thirteen virtues on a nightly ledger and pursuing what he called moral perfection—I keep encountering a reductive one-liner among historians and essayists: that Franklin treated virtue as a bookkeeping problem rather than a spiritual transformation. The phrase is usually offered as an interpretive key, yet I have seen it pulled in opposite directions—once to dismiss any role for grace or innate character in Franklin's ethics, and once to recruit him as a proto-behaviorist who believed self-management could manufacture morality from scratch. Before I use the framing in my own writing, I need to know exactly what precise philosophical claims it could be advancing.

Could you lay out the distinct readings of that slogan as a conceptual menu, running from the trivial observation that quantitative tracking can aid habit formation, through intermediate theses about whether virtue reduces to the statistical absence of recorded vice, up to the radical claim that moral ontology itself collapses into accounting? For each reading, please indicate whether it is defensible, false, or anachronistic when applied to Franklin's actual writings, and specify which version is actually required to support the two argumentative uses I described—the dismissal of grace and the behaviorist recruitment. I am writing for an audience familiar with Franklin's autobiography in outline but not with the secondary literature, so I need no bibliography or citation list, only a precise cartography of what that one sentence might be saying.
