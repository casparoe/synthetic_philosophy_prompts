In my philosophy of science reading group, we have been deadlocked for months over whether the Principal Principle collapses into an untenable demand for epistemic surrender whenever belief-formation is causally entangled with the chance-generating process itself. The deferentialists hold that if you know the objective chance of event E is ch, then your rational credence in E should equal ch, with deviation permitted only when you possess genuinely inadmissible evidence—evidence that bears on E without bearing on the chance of E. The autonomists counter that there are structurally transparent cases—call them Reflexive Calibration Cases—where you know both the model's published chance (say, 70% rain) and the structural fact that in this region the forecasting system updates its weights using aggregated forecaster credences, which systematically depresses actual rainfall frequency by roughly twenty percentage points relative to the model output. They claim this structural self-knowledge is admissible, not chancy, and therefore should pull your credence downward despite the published figure. Both sides invoke Lewis's original formulation, both accept that inadmissible evidence is the official escape hatch, and neither denies the formal statement of the principle; yet after a year of examples neither camp has budged. I am trying to understand what kind of disagreement this actually is. Is it a hidden disagreement about the extension of "admissible"—a disagreement about standards of evidence, about which cases count as data, or about whether structural knowledge of a forecasting loop counts as bearing on the world-level chance? Or is it a verbal disagreement in which "objective chance" means the model output for one side and the actual world-level propensity for the other, with the parties answering different normative questions—one about deference to an external authority, one about total belief given full information—under a single heading? Alternatively, could the impasse indicate that the question is ill-posed, because any attempt to state a universal deference norm runs into reflexive instability? I want an honest diagnosis supported by what each side actually says, together with what, if anything, would count as progress—perhaps a precise distinction between first-order and second-order chance information, a formal result about reflexive calibration, or a reconception of the question itself. Please separate what is settled about the Principal Principle's core formulation from what is genuinely contested among specialists, and do not present any particular interpretation of "admissible evidence" or "objective chance" as though it were the consensus view.
