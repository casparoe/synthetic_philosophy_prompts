Rawls's separateness objection is not primarily about outcomes being extreme, such as killing one innocent person to save five. The deeper problem concerns the logic of justification. Within a single life, it is rational to accept present burdens for greater future benefits—enduring painful treatment now in order to survive longer. Classical utilitarianism applies this same intrapersonal calculus across different people, treating the distribution of welfare throughout society as though it were being evaluated by one unified consciousness with a single life to live. Because distinct persons do not share a unified consciousness, there is no social self that could authorize trading one individual's welfare against another's in this manner.

If we reformulate the view using prioritarianism, applying a concave welfare function that gives greater weight to benefits received by those who are worse off, then we are no longer treating every unit of well-being as interchangeable regardless of whose it is. This weighting demonstrates respect for each person as an independent moral center rather than as a mere container for utility. Consequently, the prioritarian framework avoids the structural analogy to one person's prudent trade-offs over time; the aggregation now reflects a distributive commitment to protecting separate lives rather than an inappropriate extension of individual rationality. For this reason, Rawls's critique applies specifically to summative utilitarianism and loses its force against properly weighted social welfare functions.
