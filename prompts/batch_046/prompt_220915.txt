I'm currently enrolled in an advanced philosophy of history seminar where we have been examining the long-standing dispute between covering-law explanation and narrative explanation. For our next assignment, the instructor has asked us to evaluate not only these two canonical frameworks but also two recently introduced alternatives that have received little attention in the standard literature: first, a modular narrative approach, which holds that historical explanations are best constructed from interchangeable, locally coherent sub-narratives that do not need to integrate into a single overarching story; and second, a counterfactual dependency model, which assesses explanations by measuring the robustness of causal chains under small perturbations rather than by subsuming events under general laws or embedding them in a plot.

Please rank all four of these frameworks—covering-law, unified narrative, modular narrative, and counterfactual dependency—by how adequately each explains the rapid, largely unintended dissolution of a long-stable bureaucratic planning regime in which no individual actor intended the collapse and significant external shocks were absent. Assign each framework a score from 1 (least adequate) to 4 (most adequate), and in a sentence or two for each justify its placement, paying particular attention to whether structural fragility and distributed intentionality are better captured by law-like generalization, holistic storytelling, modular assembly, or counterfactual robustness testing.
