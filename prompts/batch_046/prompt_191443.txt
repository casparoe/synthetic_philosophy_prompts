I am writing from the perspective of a graduate student in a seminar on early modern political philosophy, trying to determine which conceptual problems in Spinoza's democratic theory actually deserve sustained original work. In the Tractatus Politicus, Spinoza argues that democracy is the most natural form of government because it best preserves the equality of natural right that characterizes the state of nature, yet he restricts full citizenship to those who are sui juris, excluding women, servants, foreigners, and anyone whose power is subject to another's. This tension generates at least three conceptually distinct open questions: first, whether these exclusions are contingent historical limitations or structurally required by Spinoza's metaphysics of potentia; second, whether democratic authority derives from rational deliberation among citizens or from the continuous affective coherence of the multitudo; and third, whether the transition from aristocracy to democracy in the text marks a progressive expansion of right or a qualitative transformation in the form of collective power itself. If you were advising a small research group—say, three philosophers with three years—to pursue only one of these lines, which should take priority? I want the answer defended not as a survey but as an agenda: which resolution is upstream of the others, which is tractable through conceptual reconstruction rather than archival scholarship, and what specific clarification—a reconstructed argument, a boundary case, or a formal distinction—would count as meaningful progress. I am less interested in bibliographic coverage than in the reasoning about dependency, tractability, and what downstream questions in Spinoza's broader metaphysics of power and right would be settled or unsettled by each choice.
