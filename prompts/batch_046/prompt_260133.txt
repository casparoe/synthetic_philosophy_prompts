I'm preparing to lead a discussion section in my animal ethics course for first-year philosophy students who have read Singer but haven't worked through the structural analogies between speciesism and other arbitrary boundary conditions. I want to move them past the familiar "equal consideration of interests" formulation and instead have them examine why species membership operates as a defective proxy for moral status—using a carrying comparison that juxtaposes a hypothetical non-sentient but highly intelligent artificial system with a sentient but cognitively minimal farmed cephalopod. The goal isn't to settle AI ethics or invertebrate rights, but to isolate exactly where the species boundary does explanatory work and where it collapses.

Plan the session's explanatory arc: the dependency order—what idea (sentience gradients? the arbitrariness of birth membership? ecological embeddedness?) must be secured before the analogy can be introduced without collapsing into "all discrimination is identical," and what must come after so the example functions as a precision tool rather than a confusion; the specific properties the carrying case must have and must lack—why the artificial system needs to be non-sentient despite its intelligence, and why the invertebrate needs to be sentient despite its alien cognition—so that the intuition pump targets species rather than cognition; the misconceptions students will predictably form—I'm especially anticipating they'll conflate cognitive complexity with moral weight, and that they'll invoke ecological role to justify leaving wild predators alone—along with the exact point in the sequence where each should be defused; one diagnostic question I can ask at the end that reveals whether they've grasped the arbitrariness thesis rather than just adopted the slogan that "speciesism is wrong"; and what I should explicitly bracket—perhaps the wild-animal-suffering extension or the rights-based versus utilitarian framing—and announce as beyond the session's scope.
