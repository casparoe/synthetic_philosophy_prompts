I have reached the following verdicts about whether consent is genuinely valid in particular situations, and I want to know whether a single principle, or a small family of competing principles, can capture the pattern behind them. If no one principle saves every judgment, I want an honest statement of which verdict must be dropped and why.

Here are my cases and verdicts:

1. A clinical-trial recruiter tells a prospective participant that the study "monitors sleep patterns," and lists an injected psychoactive compound only in an appendix to the consent form that the participant does not read. The participant signs. I judge the consent invalid, because a material feature of the intervention was effectively concealed.

2. A heavily indebted graduate student agrees to a four-week sleep-restriction study for $800. She later says she felt she had "no real option" to refuse given her debt, yet the payment is standard for the time required and the risks are minimal. I judge the consent valid, despite the economic pressure.

3. A surgeon explains an elective spinal procedure using only quantitative risk statistics ("2% infection rate, 5% nerve damage") without describing what those outcomes mean for daily life. The patient has average health literacy and signs. I judge the consent invalid, because qualitative understanding of the stakes is absent.

4. A person with well-managed, high-functioning autism agrees to sell a long-owned apartment at fair market value. She understands every financial and legal detail but does not experience or weigh the emotional and social significance of leaving her home. I judge the consent valid; emotional resonance is not required for transactional consent.

5. A government tax office requires citizens to check a box consenting to cross-departmental data sharing in order to receive their legally owed tax refund. The disclosure is explicit and clearly worded. I judge the consent invalid, because refusal is incompatible with receiving a basic entitlement.

6. A premium banking service requires customers to consent to algorithmic profiling for "enhanced personalization" in order to retain a specialized investment account they have held for years. The profiling carries no direct harm, the disclosure is explicit, and standard banking services remain available elsewhere. I judge the consent valid, even though the customer faces a real loss of a valued service.

Cases 2 and 5 both involve serious pressure, yet I treat them differently. Cases 1 and 3 both involve information deficits, yet they differ in kind—omission of a material fact versus absence of qualitative framing. Cases 4 and 6 both involve agents who lack some form of full contextual appreciation—emotional in 4, long-term preference stability in 6—yet I accept both without demanding it.

Please state the most coherent principle (or two competing candidates) that explains these verdicts, specifying the clause motivated by each case. Then apply it to two further cases I have not considered: first, a patient with mild cognitive impairment who consents to a routine vaccination after a nurse explains it using only metaphors ("it teaches your body to recognize the enemy"); second, an employee told "sign this non-compete agreement or we will not promote you this cycle," where the promotion is discretionary rather than guaranteed and the employee genuinely wants it. Finally, tell me which of my original six verdicts your preferred principle cannot accommodate, and whether that verdict should be revised.

Please write your answer in British English.
