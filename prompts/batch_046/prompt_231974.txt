I've been thinking about how anomaly detection in mechanistic interpretability differs from statistical outlier detection, and I've landed on a fairly strong position that I'd like stress-tested. The view is this: an observation should count as anomalous not because it lies in a low-density region of some feature distribution, but because it was generated by a mechanism that differs in its counterfactual structure from the mechanism that generated the reference data. More precisely, call two mechanisms the same if they preserve identical interventionist counterfactual dependencies among their constituent parts—that is, if intervening on part X in mechanism M1 produces the same change in part Y as the corresponding intervention in mechanism M2, holding other parts fixed—regardless of differences in parameter values, input distributions, or surface-level behavior. On this account, distribution shift that preserves the underlying causal architecture (say, a neural network evaluated on out-of-domain inputs but with unchanged internal computation graphs) produces no true anomalies, whereas a subtle adversarial perturbation that reroutes computation through a different subgraph does, even if the statistical deviation is smaller.

What would have to be true for this mechanism-identity criterion to be wrong, or at least seriously damaged? I want the defeaters sorted by how decisive they'd be. First, are there cases in actual ML systems where the counterfactual dependencies remain intact but we still have good reason to treat the outputs as anomalous or untrustworthy—and conversely, cases where the internal mechanism has clearly changed but the outputs are still reliable and should not be flagged? Second, does the criterion risk collapsing into unfalsifiability if defenders can always claim that any apparent counterexample involves approximate or partial mechanism identity? Is that insulation a virtue or a vice for a framework meant to guide practical detection? Third, which philosophical accounts of mechanism identity—say, interventionist versus entity-activity views—does this criterion implicitly commit me to, and are there results in philosophy of science or in interpretability research that undermine those commitments? I'd particularly value an honest assessment of whether this view is well-insulated but useless, or genuinely exposed—and if it's the latter, which kind of evidence (a formal impossibility result, a well-documented engineering failure, a conceptual reduction to statistical anomaly detection) would actually move me.
