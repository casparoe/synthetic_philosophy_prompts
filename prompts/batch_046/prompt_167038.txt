In discussions of insect sentience, advocates of what is sometimes called the scale argument frame the problem like a cosmic lottery: there are trillions of insects, each assigned a small but nonzero probability of being sentient, so the expected number of conscious subjects is vast — as if one held billions of tickets, each with a one-in-a-million chance of being a winning "sentient being" ticket. Critics counter with a different analogy: treating this aggregation as morally significant is like claiming that because there are countless grains of sand on a beach, and each grain has a nonzero chance of consciousness, beach erosion should be treated as a mass-casualty event. The critics say both analogies confuse statistical expectation with actual victims, and that the lottery framing illegitimately treats independent possibilities as interchangeable moral units.

Audit both analogies. For each, lay out the correspondence table: what plays the role of the ticket or grain of sand, the prize or casualty, the holder or beach, and the aggregation mechanism. Then identify where the mappings break: do insects share the independence conditions of lottery tickets or grains of sand? Does the "expected value" of suffering map onto anything in moral theory, or does it smuggle in a consequentialist calculus that the opponent rejects? Which features of the source domain — randomness, physical independence, fungibility — does each analogy quietly import that the target domain lacks? Finally, say whether either analogy can actually carry its intended conclusion — that insect welfare should be a top priority, or that the scale argument is fallacious — or whether both are doing rhetorical work that collapses once the disanalogies are on the table. If neither survives intact, suggest what a more honest framing of the probability-multiplication problem would look like, and whether the core ethical tension between vast numbers and low confidence can be articulated without analogical crutches.
