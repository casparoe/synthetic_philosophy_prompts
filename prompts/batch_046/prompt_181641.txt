Consider the view that consciousness is a user illusion, sharpened as follows: phenomenal experience operates as a lossy, compressive interface layer—functionally analogous to a codec—interposed between high-dimensional predictive processing in the brain and the low-dimensional control structures that govern behaviour. On this account, what we take to be a rich, unified phenomenal field is a heavily compressed representation optimised for behavioural coordination rather than veridical depiction of neural dynamics.

This framing generates several open lines of inquiry pulling in different directions. Imagine you are advising a small interdisciplinary group—two post-doctoral researchers and one doctoral student, with three years of funding—whose objective is to produce results that either (i) reshape clinical assessment of disorders of consciousness or (ii) inform whether artificial predictive systems with structurally similar interface layers warrant moral consideration. You must allocate limited effort across the following four lines. Rank them from 1 (highest priority) to 4 (lowest) for this specific group, and defend the ordering.

The lines are:

A. Developing formal, information-theoretic criteria—such as rate-distortion bounds applied to neural dynamics—to determine when a biological system is running this compressive interface, and testing whether clinical populations in minimally conscious states exhibit degradation of the codec itself rather than failure of the underlying predictive machinery.

B. Investigating whether first-person reports retain epistemic authority if consciousness is purely an interface representation: specifically, whether introspective access tracks properties of the codec or of the machinery it compresses, and what this implies for the reliability of clinical interviews.

C. Examining whether an artificial system implementing a functionally equivalent compressive interface—without biological substrate—should be treated as possessing a user illusion of consciousness for ethical purposes, and designing conceptual or empirical tests capable of settling the question without invoking biological naturalism.

D. Mapping the dependency relations between this interface model and existing predictive-processing accounts of anaesthesia and sleep, to determine whether disruptions in conscious state are best explained by corruption of the codec or by degradation of the generative models it compresses.

For each line, state its rank. Then explain the criteria governing your ordering—tractability within a three-year window, current neglect in the literature, dependence of other questions on its resolution, and direct relevance to the group's dual aims—together with the principal trade-offs. Finally, specify what argument or empirical result would most likely lead you to revise this ranking, and identify the weakest point in your reasoning where it is most liable to fail.
