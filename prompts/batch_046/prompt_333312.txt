As a doctoral student working on personal identity and philosophy of technology, I recently observed a seminar disagreement about staged neural replacement that I have been unable to resolve. Two colleagues—let me call them Alex and Blake—were debating a protocol in which a subject's brain is gradually replaced with synthetic analogues over several years, but with an unusual constraint: after each module is replaced, the subject spends six months in a fully biological state (with the synthetic module temporarily deactivated) before the next replacement begins. Alex opened by arguing that this discontinuous protocol actually strengthens the case for preservation of identity, because if the subject remains psychologically continuous across each biological interlude, then the synthetic stages are merely instrumental intermediaries rather than breaks in the chain. Blake responded that this misses the point: personal identity depends on first-personal continuity of concern, and a synthetic module that is deactivated for six months cannot inherit the biological subject's self-concern, so the chain is broken at every interlude regardless of functional equivalence. Alex countered that concern is a relational psychological property—if the synthetic module, upon reactivation, genuinely cares about the biological stage that preceded it, then inheritance succeeds, and there is no principled reason to deny this except an unmotivated biological essentialism. Blake then pressed that genuinely caring is doing all the work: without phenomenal consciousness in the synthetic stage, there is no fact of the matter about whether it cares, only behavioral mimicry, and treating mimicry as sufficient collapses the distinction between replacement and replication. Alex replied that this assumes phenomenal consciousness is necessary for concern, which is precisely the functionalist thesis under examination, and that Blake is therefore begging the question by insisting that only biological phenomenal states can ground the inheritance relation. They asked me to adjudicate. Please go through their exchange move by move—rating each argument from clearly mistaken to clearly right and briefly justifying each rating—and then say who is more right overall and what both are missing, particularly regarding whether intermittent deactivation introduces a metaphysical gap that continuous replacement avoids.
