Having recently accepted an overseas research post that conflicts irreducibly with my duty to care for an aging parent, I have begun to wonder whether some conflicts are not merely tragic choices but structurally dilemmatic. Give me your credence, expressed as a number between 0 and 1, that genuine moral dilemmas are metaphysically possible—where an agent is necessarily wrong no matter what they do, even after full rational deliberation—presented as exactly five numbered items with no introduction or conclusion and each item one to two sentences: item 1 should state your overall credence; item 2 should assign a probability to whether moral reasons can be strictly incommensurable rather than hierarchically ordered, with brief justification; item 3 should assign a probability to whether such incommensurability entails unavoidable wrongdoing rather than a permissible selection among evils; item 4 should assign a probability to whether the persistent phenomenology of moral residue in unavoidable-choice cases is best explained by real dilemmatic structure rather than by violated pro tanto obligations alone; and item 5 should show how these terms combine, identify which is least certain, and specify what philosophical argument—such as a proof that all conflicts reduce to a hidden lexical priority—would move that term most.
