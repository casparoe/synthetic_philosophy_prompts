For a feature I am writing on the philosophy of artificial minds, I want to examine Denis Villeneuve's Blade Runner 2049 through Daniel Dennett's framework of real patterns and the three stances—physical, design, and intentional. The film asks whether a replicant's grief, loyalty, or self-sacrifice are genuine mental states or merely well-engineered behavioral outputs that invite an intentional-stance prediction we ought eventually to reduce to manufacturing specifications. Read the film through this tension: in K's attachment to Joi, in the baseline-test scenes that probe for emotional deviation, and in Wallace's attempt to engineer reproductive consciousness, where does the narrative present replicant subjectivity as a real pattern that resists reduction to the design stance? Conversely, where does it trade on a confusion between our epistemic inability to distinguish replicant feeling from human feeling and the stronger ontological claim that such feeling exists? I am particularly interested in whether the film's refusal to resolve Deckard's nature—human or replicant—suggests that if the intentional stance works identically regardless of origin, the difference between them is not a real pattern but a design-label artifact. Do not treat the film as a mere illustration: identify scenes where cinematic form—perhaps the treatment of Joi's holographic presence or K's final snow-covered death—does philosophical work that conceptual argument alone cannot. Where the philosophical lens forces a distortion, for instance by flattening the film's religious or aesthetic symbolism into cognitive taxonomy, acknowledge the resistance honestly. End by stating what Blade Runner 2049 adds to the debate over real patterns in artificial beings that Dennett's framework, on its own, does not settle.
