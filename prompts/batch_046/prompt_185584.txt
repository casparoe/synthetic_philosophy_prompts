I've noticed a recurring split in how people emotionally engage with suffering inside interactive fiction—video games, RPGs, and similar media. When a companion dies in a scripted cutscene, I feel sadness but no guilt; I don't think I've done anything wrong. But when an equivalent death results from my own strategic choice—say, I diverted scarce resources away from that character—the feeling is closer to genuine moral responsibility, even though I know the character doesn't exist. There's also an asymmetry in how "fixing" it feels: reloading a save to undo my choice-driven death seems like correcting a mistake, whereas reloading to undo a scripted death feels like skipping the narrative. So the weight of the response seems to depend not on fictionality itself, but on whether my own actions within the fiction's rules caused the outcome. Is there an established concept for this distinction between scripted and player-caused moral engagement? How does it relate to the paradox of fiction, and if no specific term exists, what exactly separates the two cases and where are the boundary conditions—such as highly scripted games with illusory choice?
