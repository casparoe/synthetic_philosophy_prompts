You are grading a graduate exam in philosophy of science. The exam asks: "Schrödinger was deeply influenced by Advaita Vedanta, particularly the idea that individual consciousness is an illusion and that there is only one universal consciousness. Some readers take his essay Mind and Matter to defend this view as a direct scientific conclusion drawn from quantum mechanics. Assess this interpretation. What does Schrödinger actually claim about the unity of consciousness, and how does he relate it to physics?"

Here is a student's answer:

"Schrödinger was clearly influenced by Vedanta, and in Mind and Matter he argues that quantum mechanics proves individual minds are illusions. The wave function's non-separability shows that all apparent separation between observers is physically groundless, so there is literally only one mind that experiences everything. He adopts the traditional Advaita view that the self (Atman) is identical with Brahman, and he thinks physics confirms this metaphysical identity. He also rejects the problem of other minds entirely, since if there is only one consciousness, there are no other minds to be skeptical about. His view is therefore a form of scientific pantheism that derives religious truth from the Schrödinger equation."

Grade this answer (letter grade or points out of 10) and provide specific, point-by-point comments justifying the score. Note where it is right about the more obvious points—Schrödinger's Vedanta influence, his argument against a plural ontology of minds, and the distinction between scientific description and metaphysical commitment. Then identify one or two subtle mistakes a hasty grader might miss: for example, conflating quantum non-separability with the metaphysical identity of Atman and Brahman; attributing to Schrödinger an unmodified traditional religious thesis rather than his naturalized framing; or claiming he outright rejects the problem of other minds rather than reconceiving it in light of unified consciousness. Write in American English.
