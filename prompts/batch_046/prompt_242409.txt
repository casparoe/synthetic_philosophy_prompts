I am preparing a long-form feature for a science-and-ideas magazine on why the argument from illusion continues to structure debates in philosophy of perception, and I need a diagnostic instrument rather than a standard literature survey. Please construct a decision tree—built from the logical space of commitments rather than from a list of famous names—that sorts a reader’s actual beliefs about hallucination and veridical experience. Begin with one concrete scenario: a subject alone in an empty white room reports seeing a small, brightly dyed elephant; the experience is subjectively indistinguishable from a genuine sighting of a similarly colored animal at the same distance. From that starting point, build four levels of binary or ternary branch nodes, each phrased as a concrete judgment about the case or about structurally analogous non-visual cases—never as “do you accept disjunctivism?” The first split should ask whether subjective indistinguishability entails that the two experiences share at least one intrinsic mental property. If yes, the second split should ask whether that shared element is best understood as representational content, a non-representational qualitative feel, or a mode of awareness. If no, the second split should ask whether the absence of a shared element is a metaphysical difference (no common object or relation) or an epistemic one (a common mental kind is present but carries a different justificatory status). At the third level, introduce the niche cross-modal check I want emphasized: if one denies a common mental kind for vision, is one also committed to denying it for spatially unstructured modalities such as olfactory hallucination—phantosmia—or can one defend a modular view in which disjunctivism applies only where relational spatial structure is available? Use this to generate a fourth-level split that distinguishes uniform cross-modal positions from modular ones.

The leaves should carry named positions where they are genuinely defended—naïve direct realism, metaphysical disjunctivism, epistemic disjunctivism, sense-datum representationalism, adverbialism, and modular cross-modal disjunctivism—while logical combinations that appear unoccupied should be labeled “unnamed” and given one sentence each diagnosing whether they are incoherent, merely unattractive, or unexplored. Explicitly flag any pair of named leaves that the tree cannot reliably separate—such as metaphysical disjunctivism and a strictly vision-restricted modular variant—explaining which branch judgments they share. The instrument should require no bibliographies, reading lists, or citations, and should avoid invoking hyper-specialized recent debates that depend on memorized journal details; the olfactory-modality injection should serve as the primary element that keeps the taxonomy from being a generic encyclopedia entry. Keep the tone suitable for an educated general audience, with every node answerable without prior knowledge of the labels.
