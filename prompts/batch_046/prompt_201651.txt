I was discussing with a friend over coffee why her neighbor — who pays local taxes, sends her children to the district school, and relies on the town's water system — still cannot vote in municipal elections because she holds a different passport. That led us to the broader puzzle of who really counts as part of "the demos." Citizenship seems too narrow, since it excludes people whose lives are genuinely governed by local institutions. But the opposite idea — that anyone affected by a decision should have a say — seems to expand the boundary so far that local democracy becomes impossible, pulling in distant trade partners, downstream water users in other countries, and people who will be born a century from now.

I'd like you to make a sustained argument for a specific position on this boundary question. Defend the view that belonging to the demos should be determined not by citizenship papers alone, nor by the broad "all affected interests" principle, but by what we might call a standard of continuous institutional interdependence over time: you belong if your practical life is shaped by a particular set of shared rules that you can meaningfully influence, and if the consequences of those rules will bind you and those for whom you are responsible across generations — but only within a functional and geographic scope where collective decision-making remains coherent and actionable. Make the case that this criterion properly includes people like cross-border residents who are embedded in local systems without being citizens, while excluding distant stakeholders whose connection is too indirect. In particular, argue that admitting intergenerational responsibility does not collapse the boundary into global democracy, and explain what practical difference this standard makes for how we should think about emigrant voting rights and the representation of future generations in long-term constitutional or environmental planning.
