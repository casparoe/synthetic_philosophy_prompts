I'm moderating a colloquium Q&A with an unnamed philosopher who defends the view that genuine moral dilemmas are not merely apparent conflicts resolvable by all-things-considered reasoning, but are structurally endemic to institutional roles. She argues that roles can carry irreducible agent-relative permissions—such as a judge required to apply an unjust statute or a physician caught between confidentiality and mandatory reporting—that generate objective normative conflicts. On her view, the moral remainder of appropriate regret or guilt after the optimal choice is not merely psychological but epistemically tracks a real feature of the normative landscape. She rejects both deontic-logic resolutions that translate conflicts into priority rules and the claim that institutional redesign could eventually eliminate all such conflicts.

Please draft eight to ten interview questions that press well beyond a restatement of the position. Include at least one question on how she distinguishes genuine dilemmas from high-cost tragic choices that have a clear resolution; at least one on whether her appeal to role-based agent-relative permissions collapses into a form of rule-consequentialism once institutional design is optimized; and at least one on the implications for moral progress—specifically, whether institutional reform is itself always dilemmatic or whether some reforms can be genuinely dilemma-free. For each question, provide a follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give: for instance, if she insists on the irreducibility of role-based reasons, press her on whether this commits her to a pluralist ontology of normative reasons; if she claims reform cannot eliminate dilemmas, ask whether this entails a pessimistic or even quietist stance toward institutional ethics.

The tone should be rigorous yet accessible to a philosophy colloquium audience. Do not request bibliographies, reading lists, or citations. Build the framing from recognizable philosophical concepts rather than obscure named doctrines.

End with a single line beginning 'Verdict:' summarizing the central tension these questions are meant to expose.
