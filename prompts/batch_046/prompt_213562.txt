I've been arguing with some mates on the rationalist forum about Bostrom's presumptuous philosopher thing, and one of them walked me into this argument that seems to collapse SSA and SIA into each other in a bad way. Here goes:

(1) By the Self-Indication Assumption, if I exist I should update towards theories that predict more observers who are relevantly like me. (2) So if Theory Big predicts 10^40 conscious minds and Theory Small predicts 10^11, I should massiavely favour Big. (3) But now apply the Self-Sampling Assumption inside Big: I pick one observer at random from all 10^40. (4) Under Big, the overwhelming majority of observers are post-biological or live in the far future, not carbon-based humans in 2025. (5) So the SSA probability I'm a present-day human under Big is vanishingly small. (6) But I clearly obsereve that I am a present-day human. (7) Therefore, if I accept SIA and favour Big, I must also accept SSA and conclude I'm almost certainly not who I observe myself to be — contradiction. (8) So either SIA or SSA has to go, and since Bostrom needs both for the presumptuous philosopher argument, the whole framework is broken.

I think the slip is somewhere between (5) and (7) — maybe I'm mixing up the referance class? — but I can't pin it down. Which premise should I actually reject, and why did it look reasonable? Also, what do the standard positions — the ones that keep SSA pure, the ones that keep SIA pure, and maybe the ones that restrict the reference class to observer-moments capable of this reasoning — each have to pay to drop it?
