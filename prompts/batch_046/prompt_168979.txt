For a graduate seminar I am running on philosophy of mind and artificial systems, my students have begun treating the intentional stance as if it were a single toggle—either we adopt it toward a large language model or we do not—without asking what conditions must be met for the attribution to be philosophically defensible rather than merely predictive. I want to press them on a more demanding specification: consider not only standard cases of task-specific success, but also situations in which the system’s behaviour is systematically destabilised by adversarial perturbations of its inputs—what engineers call prompt injection—which should serve as a stress test for whether an attributed belief remains stable across distributional shifts. The theory must also distinguish between attributing beliefs to a unified agent and attributing distributed belief-like states to subsystems that lack any integrative mechanism analogous to a global workspace, without presupposing phenomenal consciousness.

Write the desiderata that any adequate theory of belief attribution to artificial systems must meet. Specify the cases it must handle correctly—predictive success in narrow domains, cross-domain stability, adversarial failure, and distributed architectures—together with the distinctions it must preserve, such as that between instrumental prediction and explanatory commitment, and between having a belief-state and having the kind of integrated cognitive architecture that warrants treating that state as belonging to a single subject. Indicate which requirements are non-negotiable—consistency with the absence of consciousness in current architectures, for instance—and which are negotiable preferences of particular camps, and flag any pair of desiderata you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied, particularly if requiring both pragmatic utility and adversarial robustness collapses the attribution into something ontologically committed. Then, briefly, assess how Dennett’s intentional stance, an eliminativist view that rejects all such attribution, and a functionalist account that ties belief to specific computational roles fare against your list. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then provide the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question. Write in British English.
