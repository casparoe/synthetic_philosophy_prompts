I have recently become involved with a local wetlands-conservation initiative, and through that work I have been drawn into a sharp disagreement over whether habitat preservation actually serves wild-animal welfare. Some participants invoke ecological-intervention reasoning: because many wetland species are strongly r-selected, reducing breeding habitat might lower the number of individuals born into lives that are, on average, net-negative in welfare. At a recent forum, a fellow volunteer dismissed this line of reasoning by insisting that "nature knows best," claiming that any human disruption of the wetland's equilibrium necessarily increases aggregate suffering among its inhabitants. I want to understand exactly what that slogan is committing him to. Please disambiguate "nature knows best" into a menu of precise claims—ranging from definitional tautologies about evolutionary adaptation, through empirical hypotheses about ecosystem homeostasis and suffering-minimization, to normative or metaphysical theses about the authority of natural processes. For each reading, indicate whether it is a platitude, a defensible but substantive scientific claim, or false; and specify which interpretation, if any, genuinely supports the inference that preserving this particular habitat minimizes net wild-animal suffering, as opposed to merely protecting biodiversity or ecological function for independent reasons.
