In philosophical discussions about whether ideas can be genuinely owned, there has long been an impasse between defenders of intellectual property on Lockean labor-mixing grounds and anti-propertarian critics who stress that ideas are non-rivalrous and non-excludable. Both sides grant that physical labor can generate property rights in material objects, both grant that copying an idea does not deprive the originator of it, and both grant that attribution and reward are socially valuable. Yet the debate has barely shifted in decades: defenders keep insisting that mental labor is continuous with physical labor and therefore generates entitlement, critics keep replying that non-rivalry breaks the analogy and makes exclusionary rights either incoherent or unjustified, and neither camp treats the other’s central reply as demanding a substantive revision of its own position.

My working hypothesis is that the stall is not a single disagreement but at least two running together under one heading. One concerns the metaphysics of ideas—whether an idea is best understood as an abstract type, a token mental event, or a socially instantiated practice—since each model implies different conditions for “mixing” labor with it. The other concerns whether ownership necessarily requires the possibility of physical exclusion, or whether it can be sustained by pure normative convention, which in turn affects whether non-rivalry is fatal or merely a practical complication. I am also unsure whether hidden bedrock intuitions about creation versus discovery are doing the work, or whether “ownership” is being used ambiguously across moral-desert, efficiency, and attribution-based questions.

Please diagnose what kind of disagreement this actually is. Examine whether it is substantive or partly verbal; whether there is a hidden empirical or formal question—for instance, about the ontology of abstract objects or the coherence of rights over non-excludable goods—doing the real labor; whether the sides differ about what counts as a moral reason rather than about facts; and whether they are answering different normative questions—such as whether creators deserve control, whether regimes should maximize innovation, or whether attribution is a separate moral claim—while treating them as one. Support your diagnosis by engaging concretely with what each side actually says about labor, mixing, non-rivalry, and exclusion, not by surveying the literature.

Then, given your diagnosis, say what would actually count as progress: a conceptual distinction that dissolves part of the dispute, an empirical finding that bears on the ontology or economics of information goods, a reframing that separates desert from efficiency from attribution, or an argument that no further progress should be expected because the disagreement rests on irreducible normative intuitions. In doing so, clearly separate what is settled or widely agreed upon among philosophers who work on this topic—such as the basic non-rivalry of information goods, the idea-expression distinction, and the historical contingency of particular IP regimes—from what remains genuinely contested, and do not present any contested view as if it were the consensus.
