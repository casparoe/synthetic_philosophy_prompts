I am a doctoral candidate in political economy, and in two weeks I will defend a chapter of my dissertation at our department’s formal seminar. The thesis is that the Kalai-Smorodinsky bargaining solution—modified so that its utopia point is not exogenous maximal payoffs but is endogenized via a climate-vulnerability-weighted capability index—should serve as the normative benchmark for international emissions-abatement burden-sharing. I contend that the Nash solution fails in this domain because its proportional-scaling property treats the status quo symmetrically and thereby permits reductions in assigned burden for high-capability states even when low-capability, high-vulnerability partners have not improved; by contrast, my modified KS framework respects individual monotonicity in a way that tracks non-deprivation, so that an expansion of one party’s feasible abatement frontier (say, through green-technology transfer) does not reduce that party’s obligation relative to more vulnerable counterparts.

The audience is specific and skeptical: six applied climate economists who regard axiomatic bargaining as normative window-dressing with poor empirical traction; three political philosophers of global justice who know the formal literature but treat it as largely decorative; and one formal micro-theorist who will scrutinize uniqueness, consistency, and whether the axiom system collapses into a known solution. None are decision theorists; all will suspect I am smuggling distributive commitments into mathematics.
