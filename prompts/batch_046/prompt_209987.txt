I've been preparing a long-form feature on whether P.F. Strawson's classic essay on reactive attitudes actually furnishes a complete theory of moral responsibility or merely relocates the problem from metaphysics into social psychology. In discussions with philosophers and in the literature I have surveyed, the same examples recur—the negligent parent, the psychopath, the brainwashed agent, the agent raised without moral education—but rarely does anyone lay out the full specification sheet: the conditions a Strawson-inspired account must satisfy before it counts as adequate rather than merely evocative. Before I commit to a thesis, I want the engineering spec. If reactive attitudes—resentment, gratitude, indignation, and the like—are taken as foundational to moral responsibility, what exactly must a theory built on them be able to deliver?

Write the desiderata. I want the phenomena it must explain (for instance, why we withhold goodwill from some agents but not from young children or those with severe cognitive impairments; why reactive attitudes feel structurally different from predictive or instrumental adjustments to behavior; why group-directed resentment can be appropriate even when no individual member of a collective is separately culpable), the distinctions it must preserve without collapse (such as the difference between being a proper target of resentment and being blameworthy in a practice-independent sense; between emotional withdrawal and moral condemnation; and—this is the point I care most about—between exemption, which removes an agent from the moral community altogether, and excuse, which keeps them inside it while defeating particular attributions), and the conceptual constraints it cannot violate (for example, that reactive attitudes have intentional objects directed at agents rather than at events, and that they are not reducible to behavioral dispositions or to mere affective states). For each requirement, provide the motivating case or consideration—the specific scenario or argument that makes it load-bearing—and flag which are genuinely non-negotiable and which reflect camp preferences, such as whether determinism must be false for resentment to be rational, or whether the theory must accommodate collective reactive attitudes directed at institutions or groups. I also want your judgment on which single desideratum eliminates the largest number of existing accounts; my own suspicion is that it is the demand to preserve the asymmetry between excuses and exemptions without importing metaphysical libertarianism or a capacity-based desert condition, but I want your assessment rather than confirmation.

Then, briefly score the main contenders against this list: Strawsonian compatibilism, a libertarian reconstruction that retains reactive attitudes but denies determinism is compatible with them, and a recent capacity-based desert account that attempts to ground blame in rational capacities rather than in the emotional dynamics of interpersonal relationships. I do not need a bibliography, reading list, or citations—just whether each survives the hard requirements or falls at a specific one. Keep the weight on the criteria and their motivating cases rather than on a detailed history of each position; I am writing for an audience that understands the basic distinction between reactive and objective attitudes but needs to see which parts of Strawson's framework are doing the philosophical labor and which are decorative.
