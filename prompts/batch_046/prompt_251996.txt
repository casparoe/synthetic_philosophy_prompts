I'm moderating a podcast interview with a philosopher who defends a specific variant of perceptual disjunctivism. On her view, hallucinatory experiences are not representational states with false content, but what she calls "failed reachings"—attempts at direct perceptual contact that lack any intentional object altogether, which is why they confer no justification on beliefs about the world. She routinely deflects the causal argument by insisting on a sharp distinction between causal dependence and phenomenal dependence, and she treats the argument from illusion as conflating metaphysics with epistemology. I want a sequence of interview questions that makes these deflections progressively harder. Begin by pressing her on how the "failed reaching" account avoids collapsing into a sense-datum theory once we grant that the hallucinator is in some determinable mental state; include her likely reply that the state is purely relational and fails to obtain, and then ask what positive characterization remains that doesn't implicitly invoke an internal object. Next, ask what her causal-versus-phenomenal dependence distinction actually achieves against the brain-in-a-vat worry, anticipating her claim that causal history is irrelevant to present phenomenal character, and then follow up by asking whether this insulation makes her view unfalsifiable with respect to neuroscience. Then ask about the epistemological payoff: if hallucinations carry no evidential weight, how does she explain the intuition that a subject in a perfect hallucination is rationally situated just like a veridical perceiver, and include the follow-up about whether she can consistently deny that there is any common factor even at the level of rational accessibility. End with a question about what empirical finding—say, a demonstrated neural isomorphism between veridical and hallucinatory perceptual states—could count against her view, given her tendency to reinterpret such results as explaining only causal conditions rather than phenomenal nature.
