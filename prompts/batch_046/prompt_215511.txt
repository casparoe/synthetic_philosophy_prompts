The student submitted the following answer. In Plato's Euthyphro, Socrates presents two options. The first horn says the gods love an action because it is already pious, which means piety exists independently of divine opinion—this is the objective reality view. The second horn says an action is pious only because the gods love it, which makes piety arbitrary and dependent on divine will—this is divine command theory. Euthyphro initially picks the first horn but cannot define piety, so Socrates demonstrates that his answer is circular and ultimately fails. I believe Plato clearly endorses the first horn, using the dialogue to establish that genuine morality must be objective and independent of any authority. When the realist adds that rational nature itself makes cruelty wrong, this is not a metaphysical claim about what constitutes wrongness; rather, it describes the epistemic pathway by which rational beings discover the pre-existing wrongness. Therefore, there is no circularity, and the secular view successfully escapes the Euthyphro trap. The analog actually collapses the dilemma because the dependence mentioned is only about how we come to know moral truths, not about what makes them true.
