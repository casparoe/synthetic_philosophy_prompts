I was recently in a reading group where two colleagues—let's call them A and B—got into a sustained disagreement about Charles Darwin's account of morality in his later writings on human evolution, and about how that account relates to his broader rejection of revealed religion and design-based ethics. I stayed out of the exchange, but I would like an outside adjudication of the four moves they actually made.

(1) A opened by claiming that Darwin reduces human morality entirely to the evolutionary refinement of social instincts—sympathy, habit, and the desire for social approval—so that for Darwin there is no categorical difference between the herd loyalty observed in social animals and human ethical commitment. On A's reading, Darwin is a consistent naturalist: once you explain the origin of moral feelings biologically, you have dissolved any claim to normative authority that is not reducible to adaptive utility. (2) B replied that this misses Darwin's explicit distinction between the unreflective operation of instinct and the supervisory role that develops with human reason. B argued that Darwin allows reason to evaluate and even suppress immediate social instincts in favor of wider welfare—such as extending sympathy to strangers or to future generations—and that this reflective capacity introduces a genuinely normative dimension rather than merely a more complex instinctual calculus. (3) A pushed back by conceding that Darwin describes reason playing this supervisory role, but insisted that Darwin never treats reason as autonomous or non-natural. For A, the evaluation B describes is still driven by evolved neural structures and by culturally transmitted conditioning; Darwin's well-known skepticism about supernatural design in nature shows that he saw no transcendental ground for ethics, only increasingly sophisticated mechanisms of selection operating on behavior. (4) B's final move was to grant that Darwin denies any supernatural foundation for morality, yet to argue that Darwin's account of cultural transmission—where moral habits are inherited, modified through education, and stabilized within social practices—creates a form of normative binding that outstrips its biological origins. B compared this to language: just as grammatical rules are not reducible to the physical properties of individual vocal cords, moral norms can become genuinely authoritative within a social practice even if they originally arose from evolved sympathy. A dismissed this as a sleight of hand, saying B had smuggled in a non-reductive social ontology that Darwin himself never endorsed.

Please adjudicate these four moves point by point. For each, rate it from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with brief justification. In particular, address whether B's analogy to language and cultural transmission is a legitimate extension of Darwin's view or an anachronistic imposition, and whether A is correct that Darwin's naturalism necessarily eliminates normative authority rather than relocating it within evolved but reflectively governable capacities.
