As a novelist who has long belonged to a professional cooperative of independent writers, I have been struck by an asymmetry in our institutional arrangements that I cannot quite philosophically place. Our collective maintains a hardship fund to which all members contribute. The fund's charter stipulates that relief is available for income lost through events deemed beyond a member's reasonable control—serious illness, accidental destruction of work, sudden market collapse—yet it explicitly excludes losses that result from what the charter calls deliberate professional gambles: for instance, turning down a secure commission to pursue an unproven experimental form, or failing to maintain basic digital backups out of a preference for obsolete methods. At the same time, our annual manuscript prize operates under strict anonymised conditions; judges evaluate submissions with no knowledge whatsoever of the author's biography, health, economic circumstances, or prior choices, and the award is distributed purely on aesthetic merit. The result is that our institution compensates members for misfortune that strikes them, but refuses to compensate them for misfortune they have apparently invited upon themselves—while elsewhere it distributes prestige and money without regard to whether a writer composed the work in conditions of extreme deprivation or exceptional comfort.

What philosophical commitments about responsibility, desert, and distributive fairness does this pairing of practices presuppose, if any? Does the hardship fund's distinction between unavoidable calamity and chosen risk implicitly rely on a view that outcomes matter to what people are owed—such that those who suffer through no fault of their own deserve compensation—while the anonymised prize scheme seems to treat every writer as an isolated agent responsible solely for the quality of their output? Or can a single, consistent theory of moral responsibility accommodate both: the differential treatment in welfare, and the blindness to circumstance in evaluation? Furthermore, is there any tension between the cooperative's willingness to inquire into a member's prior choices when allocating aid, and its insistence on ignorance of those same choices when allocating honours? I should be grateful for an answer in British English that reconstructs the implicit theory behind these arrangements, distinguishes what they strictly require from what they merely happen to suit, and indicates where the practice is philosophically underdetermined—so that rival accounts might license the same rules.
