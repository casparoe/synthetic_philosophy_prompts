I'm filing a story on AI oversight for a science magazine and witnessed a debate I need help untangling. At a panel in Berlin, two experts got stuck arguing about process-based vs outcome-based supervision.

The first — an AI ethicist — said we should require reasoning audits on high-stakes systems. She imagined a carbon-capture AI whose logs show it rejected a safer, slower method to optimize speed. Even if the project succeeds, she said, that internal dismissal is a failure: it reveals misaligned priorities invisible in the final result, and without inspecting the chain of consideration we can't distinguish luck from trustworthiness, especially with long-term ambiguous outcomes.

The second — a robotics engineer — said that's fantasy. Those logs are just post-hoc rationalizations, not cognition; auditing them creates false confidence. He compared it to surgery: we don't require neurosurgeons to narrate every synapse, we check infection rates and survival curves. If the carbon project saves lives, policing the model's thought process is bureaucratic theater that slows good outcomes.

She countered that medicine still has informed consent, which is a process rule protecting autonomy, not just an outcome metric. He replied that consent protects the patient, not the doctor's brain, so it's irrelevant to algorithmic oversight.

Then she argued that without process checks, you can't tell a reliable system from a lucky fluke when outcomes take decades to verify. He said long-term ambiguity is exactly why we should wait for outcomes, not invent phantom mental states to regulate.

Can you go through thier exchange step by step and say who's right about which specific claim? Don't just declare a winner — tell me what each is missing, and whether there's a hidden assumption about what process even means for a black-box model. I also wonder if this echoes older ethics debates about intention vs consequence, but reason it through without citing texts you can't verify.
