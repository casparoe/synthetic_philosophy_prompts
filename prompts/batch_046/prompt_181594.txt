I'm writing an essay for my course on the political economy of empire, where we've been working through Adam Smith's Wealth of Nations—particularly his argument that colonies are costly military outposts sustaining private monopolies rather than public wealth, and his distinction between productive labor that reproduces capital and unproductive labor maintained by revenue. I want to read Joseph Conrad's Heart of Darkness through that Smithian lens rather than through the usual Marxist or psychoanalytic frames. Apply Smith's taxonomy of labor and his critique of mercantile monopoly to the Belgian ivory company, to Marlow's employment within it, and to Kurtz's station at the Inner Station: does Conrad depict colonial extraction as Smithian unproductive labor scaled into systematic atrocity, or does the novella reveal that moral corruption is not an incidental byproduct but a structural necessity that Smith's economic calculus cannot register? I want a committed answer—take a definite position on whether Conrad's horror is essentially an aesthetic transposition of Smith's economic indictment, or whether it introduces a philosophical excess that actually undermines Smith's assumption that imperial costs are quantifiable and correctable through policy. Use specific scenes—the accountant's immaculate ledgers, the grove of death, Kurtz's report for the International Society for the Suppression of Savage Customs, and the intended's drawing-room conversation—to show where the Smithian framework illuminates the action and where it forces the text into a frame it resists. Conclude by saying what the novella contributes to the philosophical debate about empire that Smith's argument alone cannot supply, and whether that contribution strengthens or weakens the anti-imperial case.
