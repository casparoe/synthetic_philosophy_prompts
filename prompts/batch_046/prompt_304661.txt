I am convening an advanced undergraduate seminar in intergenerational ethics with approximately twelve participants for a single ninety-minute session, and I want you to design a structured deliberation that makes the nonidentity problem felt rather than merely stated. Invent a specific institutional scenario that is not the standard depletion-conservation or teenage-pregnancy framing—perhaps a long-range archival preservation board choosing between two technological diffusion pathways, one that accelerates climate-adaptation research with faster but riskier outcomes and one that prioritizes ecological stability with slower but safer conditions—so that participants must vote and justify a collective choice knowing that each pathway creates conditions in which an entirely different set of future scholars, citizens, and researchers will come to exist two centuries hence. Assign concrete roles with hidden obligations: an archivist bound to institutional continuity and fidelity to the original mission, a climate-adaptation researcher holding data about risk profiles and welfare distributions, an advocate speaking for unspecified future generations who cannot be named, and a trustee accountable solely to present stakeholders. Structure the rules and evaluation scheme so that appeals to harming particular individuals systematically collapse, because whichever pathway is chosen, the people who would exist only under the other pathway never come into being to be wronged, yet the aggregate welfare and opportunity structures across the two possible futures diverge sharply. Arrange the timeline so that participants commit to arguments and cast preliminary votes before they discover, through guided disclosure of demographic projections, that the identity of every future person depends on which pathway is selected. Include a payoff framework that rewards logical consistency rather than emotional appeal, and ensure the deliberation rules forbid compromise solutions that blend the two pathways, forcing a binary choice. Then provide a debrief that maps each stage of participant frustration—when harm-talk fails, when aggregate comparison feels morally inadequate, when rights-language seems inapplicable to people who do not yet exist—onto the corresponding logical component of the problem, specifically existence-dependence, the comparative harm principle, and the asymmetry between harming and failing to benefit. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time this is run, such as participants inventing a false middle-ground option, treating future populations as interchangeable welfare containers, or conflating the choice with a standard risk assessment, and explain how to correct each failure without delivering a lecture. Be frank about which precise metaphysical claim regarding personal identity over time and cross-world individuation this simulation necessarily cannot reproduce and that I must supply in my closing remarks. Present all of this in continuous prose only, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, tables, numbered lists, or other visual formatting; write it as one flowing account, the way one might compose a detailed letter describing a classroom design.
