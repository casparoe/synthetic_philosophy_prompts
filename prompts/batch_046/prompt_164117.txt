I've been comparing how three societies answer the same question: how much should an ordinary person hide from the state?

In Germany, citizens have a legal right to demand that companies and government delete personal data, and tracking in public spaces is sharply restricted. The practice feels like it treats information about you as something you own, similar to your home or your body.

In the United States, court filings, property records, and campaign donations are public by default. The idea seems to be that corruption thrives in darkness, so democratic health requires citizens and officials to remain visible to one another.

China's social credit system monitors behavior and assigns scores that affect travel, employment, and housing. Here, hiding looks less like a protected right and more like an obstacle to correct social order.

I'm trying to understand what view of citizenship each system quietly assumes. Does the German model presuppose an informational self that belongs to the individual? Does the American model presuppose that visibility is a civic duty? Does the Chinese model presuppose that moral conduct is fully observable and can be shaped through transparent incentives?

Are these three practices expressions of one deeper principle about the state and the person, or do they rest on incompatible pictures of dignity, control, and knowledge? And if we replaced the underlying view with an alternative — say, that privacy is not property but a form of social trust, or that transparency should run asymmetrically upward to the state but not downward among citizens — which parts of each system would collapse?

Reconstructed commitments (about 250 words): For each practice, state the implicit theory of citizenship, value, or responsibility it requires.

Coherence check (about 200 words): Say whether one philosophical view can license all three, identify the exact point where they conflict, and name which commitment must be dropped to unify them.

The replacement test (about 200 words): Choose one alternative conception (not ownership-based privacy) and describe precisely what would have to change in each of the three systems.
