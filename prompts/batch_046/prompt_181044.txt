Argue that the standard unitary model of indirect normativity—according to which normative authority derives from what a single, fully idealized version of oneself would choose—should be replaced by a "deliberative lattice" framework. On this view, idealization is not an all-or-nothing correction of cognitive flaws but a graded parameter, generating a partially ordered set of idealized deliberators each indexed by which biases and informational deficits they remedy. The normative prescription for an actual agent is then determined not by any single maximal element but by the meet (greatest lower bound) of the relevant subset of nodes, with normative silence occurring precisely when no meet exists. Defend this framework against two objections: first, that the lattice collapses into a conventional weighted-attribute model of a single agent; second, that yielding silence in cases of incompatible pulls is a failure rather than an accurate reflection of practical reason's limits. In your argument, explain how this structure accommodates the phenomenon of rational regret—recognizing that differently corrected versions of oneself might endorse incompatible courses—without abandoning the core commitment that normative force is mediated through counterfactual self-reflection. Be explicit about which distinctions within the lattice must be preserved to maintain indirect normativity and which can be collapsed without loss.
