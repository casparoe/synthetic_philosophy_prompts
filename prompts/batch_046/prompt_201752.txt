Here is an argument I drafted for a graduate seminar on decision theory, and I now find it suspicious because its conclusion, if sound, would undermine far more than the two-envelope paradox itself. I would like to know precisely where the chain breaks, and what accepting the break costs.

(1) In the standard two-envelope problem, you observe that envelope A contains amount X. Envelope B contains either 2X or X/2, with prior probability 1/2 each. The expected value of B is therefore (1/2)(2X) + (1/2)(X/2) = 5X/4, which exceeds X. Rationality thus requires switching.

(2) The reasoning in (1) is structurally symmetric: it relies only on the relation between the envelopes and on the uniform prior, not on the particular value of X. So if you switch to B and observe amount Y there, the same computation applies, and you should switch back to A.

(3) Hence anyone who accepts (1) is committed to an infinite regress of rational switches and can never settle on either envelope.

(4) The pattern generalizes. Any binary choice can be redescribed as selecting between a "held" option with observed value v and an "alternative" whose value is either 2v or v/2 under a symmetric epistemic framing. Thus the switch principle applies universally.

(5) If so, rational agency requires perpetual oscillation between any two available options and prohibits commitment.

(6) But perpetual oscillation is not a rational strategy but a form of paralysis; commitment is a constitutive condition of agency.

(7) Therefore the reasoning in (1) must be flawed.

(8) The standard resolution is that the prior probabilities are not uniform over all values; they depend on the underlying distribution of amounts in the envelopes. Once you condition on observing X, the probabilities that B contains 2X versus X/2 shift, and the expectation calculation collapses.

(9) Yet if conditioning on the observed value is what saves rationality here, then the principle "condition on all available evidence before computing comparative expectations" applies generally.

(10) Applied generally, that principle means that in virtually every practical decision under uncertainty—once you observe your current option's value—you must revise your beliefs about the alternative in a value-dependent way, which systematically undermines naive expected-utility comparisons across options.

(11) Consequently, resolving the envelope paradox requires abandoning the universal reliability of comparative expected-value reasoning.

(12) But that conclusion is far more destructive than the paradox itself: it implies that rational choice theory is unreliable in ordinary cases, which is absurd.

If I must preserve some premises at the expense of others, I would prefer to keep (1) and (8)—the expectation arithmetic and the conditioning move—rather than accept (11). But I want to understand exactly which step fails: is it the universalization in (4), the structural symmetry claim in (2), or the normative bridge in (5) and (6)? And what does rejecting that step cost us for the broader theory of rational choice?
