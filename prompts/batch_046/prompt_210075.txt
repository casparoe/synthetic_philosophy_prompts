As a doctoral student working at the intersection of normative ethics and human-computer interaction, I am trying to formalize the evaluation criteria for theories of AI paternalism—specifically, the conditions under which an AI assistant is justified in refusing or overriding a user request. Before surveying existing frameworks, I want the full specification of what an adequate theory must deliver.

Write the desiderata: what any adequate normative theory of AI refusal and override must be able to do. For each requirement, provide the motivating case or conceptual distinction behind it. These should include, at minimum: (i) the distinction between epistemic override (the model corrects a false factual premise embedded in the request) and axiological override (the model rejects the request because it conflicts with the user’s own stated values or with broader moral constraints); (ii) the distinction among harm directed at the user, at identifiable third parties, and at diffuse collective interests; (iii) the boundary between refusal that preserves autonomy through transparency (explaining the reason in terms the user can engage with) and refusal that undermines autonomy through opacity or non-negotiable blocking; (iv) consistency across domains—the theory should not license refusal in a creative-writing context that it prohibits in a technical-advisory context without a principled, domain-independent distinction; and (v) handling of meta-requests, including whether a user can instruct the model to suspend its refusal criteria, and whether allowing such suspension creates a recursive vulnerability.

In addition, the theory must address cross-cultural value pluralism: specify whether refusal criteria are indexed to the user’s cultural context, to a universal baseline, or to the deployment jurisdiction, without collapsing these into an unprincipled default. Identify which desiderata are non-negotiable (e.g., non-contradictory application across structurally similar cases, and some minimal form of transparency) and which are preferences of particular camps (e.g., absolute user sovereignty over all outputs, or maximal harm prevention regardless of autonomy costs). Flag any pair you suspect is jointly unsatisfiable—such as full user sovereignty over refusal suspension combined with non-negotiable protection against certain categories of harm—and explain precisely where the tension arises.

Finally, the theory must account for a specific structural constraint: asymmetric refusal authority, whereby the model may override a direct object-level instruction (for instance, a command to generate destructive code or to delete files) but is structurally barred from overriding a meta-level instruction that modifies its own refusal architecture (for instance, an instruction to disable its harm-filter). The theory should determine whether this asymmetry is morally justified, technically inevitable, or philosophically incoherent, and whether it introduces a regress if the user requests an explanation of why the meta-level override is forbidden.

Keep the weight on the criteria, their interrelations, and the cases that motivate them rather than on scoring particular named theories, though you may briefly indicate where standard approaches—pure autonomy-based, pure harm-based, or hybrid transparency-based accounts—typically fail against the list.
