I was recently informed by my neurologist that I have a progressive neurodegenerative condition for which an experimental gradual prosthesis protocol is being developed; the idea is that over several years my biological neurons could be replaced with synthetic equivalents that preserve functional and mnemonic continuity, but there may be a six-month suspension of consciousness during the final integration, and I have learned that an unauthorized backup copy could in principle be activated during that window. This has made Locke’s account of personal identity suddenly and uncomfortably personal. Assuming Locke’s framework—specifically his distinction between the biological human being and the thinking person constituted by continuous consciousness and memory—how would he judge my survival through this process? Would he say that the entity which resumes after the six-month gap is me, or merely a psychological duplicate? And if, during the suspension, a backup is briefly activated so that two psychologically continuous entities exist with equal claim to my memories, how would Locke adjudicate between them? I want a committed answer: take a definite position on whether Locke’s theory survives these conditions intact, collapses, or requires a specific amendment, and defend that position with precise reasoning drawn from his criteria rather than surveying every possible interpretation. Please do not treat this as an open-ended taxonomy of views; argue for one outcome and explain exactly what in Locke’s concept of personhood does the decisive work.
