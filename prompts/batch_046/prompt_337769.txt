Consider the verdicts that a philosopher—call her Dr. V—has given on whether a speaker is epistemically entitled to make an assertion in five hypothetical situations. In each case she answers “appropriate” or “inappropriate” without further qualification.

1. A geologist tells her students, “This zircon formed 4.2 billion years ago,” after running the mass-spectrometry herself, understanding every step, and confirming the result is correct.

2. A traveler tells a stranger, “The museum opens at nine,” having misread a sign; he sincerely believes it, but the museum actually opens at ten.

3. An expert witness testifies, “The DNA profile matches the defendant,” having read a forensic report but lacking any grasp of the statistical algorithm that generated the match; the match is in fact correct.

4. A journalist publishes, “The minister accepted the bribe,” relying on a leaked document she and her editor have authenticated but that she never personally witnessed being exchanged; the claim is true.

5. A moral philosopher declares in a seminar, “Virtue ethics is the only coherent framework,” based entirely on her long-standing intuitive conviction; in this hypothetical the claim happens to be true, but she offers no argument she could defend to skeptics.

Dr. V’s verdicts are: appropriate in cases 1 and 4; inappropriate in cases 2, 3, and 5.

Now consider a new case. A cognitive scientist publishes in a peer-reviewed journal that a proprietary large language model exhibits phenomenal consciousness during a specific fine-tuning run. She bases this solely on interpretability analyses of internal representations that she herself conducted; the metrics are technically sound, and in the hypothetical the model is indeed conscious during that run. However, because the model weights and training logs are proprietary, no other researcher can replicate or verify her analysis. She genuinely believes the claim and can explain her methods in detail, but she knows her peers lack access to check them.

Would Dr. V judge her assertion appropriate? I am less interested in the bare answer than in the work of identifying the plausible rules that jointly fit her five earlier verdicts, stating what each rule predicts about this new case, giving your best guess at her verdict with the reason for preferring that rule, and then naming one further hypothetical case—one that does not turn on proprietary access—that would best discriminate between the two leading rules you have identified.
