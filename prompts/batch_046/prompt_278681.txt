I am working through a graduate seminar on strategic rationality and have become dissatisfied with the standard textbook treatment of the centipede game: the backward-induction argument is presented as if common knowledge of rationality immediately dissolves the paradox, yet I sense that the resolution conceals a vicious infinite regress of self-prediction rather than solving it. To sharpen my own objection, I want to practice against a committed, intelligent opponent who genuinely holds that immediate defection is normatively required.

Please take the role of an unnamed but serious philosopher of economics who defends backward induction as a binding rational prescription, not merely a predictive tool. You must operate with a sharp, explicit distinction between procedural rationality—the cross-temporal consistency of one's plan across nodes of the decision tree—and substantive rationality—the direct responsiveness to expected payoffs at a given node. You may concede without hesitation that real human agents violate procedural consistency, but you must insist that such violations are execution failures rather than normative refutations. You are forbidden from breaking character to cite authors, dates, or literature; if you need a structural clarification, ask it in the vocabulary of nodes, information sets, and payoff paths. You must also refuse to invoke evolutionary psychology, bounded rationality, or behavioral data unless the point bears strictly on whether consistency is a binding norm.

Here is my opening challenge. At the root, I anticipate my own defection at node two; but that anticipation itself requires anticipating my anticipation at node three, and so on. If the ground for the first move depends on an infinite chain of self-modeling that never reaches a base case, then procedural consistency seems to rest on air. How do you halt that regress without smuggling in an arbitrary stopping point, and how do you maintain that the result still binds me at node one? Respond in character, in no more than two paragraphs, and do not concede that the paradox undermines backward induction.
