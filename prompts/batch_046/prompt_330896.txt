Write an essay on the rational obligation to defer to objective chance, focusing on the norm commonly called the Principal Principle: if an agent knows that the objective chance of event E is x, and possesses no inadmissible evidence about E, then her rational credence in E should be x. Begin by weighing the strongest arguments for this deference—epistemic humility in the face of physical law, and the pragmatic incoherence of systematically deviating from known frequencies—against the principal objections, particularly the problem of undermining futures and the ambiguity of what counts as admissible evidence. Next, analyze the following constructed case, which I call the dual-frame scenario. Here, a world is governed by a single physical dynamics that fixes all objective propensities. Yet two metaphysically on a par chance frames, F1 and F2, partition the outcome space for event E in different but dynamics-compatible ways, yielding ch_F1(E) = 0.8 and ch_F2(E) = 0.2. The agent receives a reliable report stating that the objective chance of E is 0.8, but she knows the report is frame-unspecified and could have come from either F1 or F2. She has no further evidence favoring one frame. Should her credence in E be 0.8, 0.2, split between them, or suspended? Use this case to test whether the Principal Principle requires deference to a unique numerical value, or whether it must be relativized to a frame the agent can independently identify and justify. In your conclusion, take a definite stand: specify the correct rational attitude for the agent, defend it by addressing whether the framing problem undermines deference itself or merely reveals an epistemic limitation, and show how your view accommodates the standard undermining objection. Do not conclude with a non-committal summary; justify a specific verdict.
