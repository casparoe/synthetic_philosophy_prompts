I'm putting together a proposal for this tiny 3-month independent study grant I found online—its only like two grand and they don't care if you have a phd or whatever. Lately I've been stuck on those Frankfurt cases where someone does a thing but there's this hidden controller who could've stopped them but never actually did. Every philosophy thing I read treats it like a solo free-will problem: is this one guy still responsible? But I keep wondering what happens when you put the hidden controller inside a group, like a five-person committee where one member secretly has veto power over the final decision and the other four don't know who it is. The usual debate about the principle of alternative possibilities—basically that you're only blameworthy if you genuinely could've done otherwise—assumes a clean split between one agent and one outside manipulator. I want to ask whether PAP breaks down differently in these embedded-group cases, and whether the hidden veto holder's presence undermines collective responsibility for the group decision even when nobody exercises the veto. For the proposal I need the question stated sharp: does distributed agency with an unknown internal veto preserve alternative possibilities for the collective, and if not, does blame shift only to the veto-holder or dissolve entirely? I think the existing Frankfurt literature leaves this open because it never models multi-agent structures where the controller is part of the social unit being evaluated. My approach would be to build a set of structured thought experiments—varying group size, transparency of roles, and whether the veto is exercised—then map where ordinary intuitions about blame and "could have done otherwise" stay stable versus collapse. A useful result wouldn't be "free will is dead" but a taxonomy showing which structural features of hidden veto power actually erode PAP in collective contexts. What'd count as failure is if the intuitions turn out totally noisy with no pattern, or if the framing just collapses back into the standard single-agent case. Honestly three months is probably too short to solve moral responsibility, but I think it's enough to show the problem space is real and not just the old debate in new clothes. I won't ask for citations since I'll fill those in later, but I want to check: is this framing actually different enough from the standard Frankfurt stuff, or am I just describing the same thing with more people in the room?
