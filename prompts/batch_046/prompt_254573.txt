I'm working through a philosophy of mind problem for an independent study, and a friend presented me with this argument that illusionism about phenomenal consciousness collapses into contradiction. I can't locate the error, though I feel one must be hiding between steps (5) and (8). The argument uses a model of introspection that treats conscious reports as constructed over discrete cognitive samples rather than as direct readings of continuous experience—something like a temporal binding window for qualitative reports. Here is the chain exactly as I copied it down:

(1) Illusionism claims that phenomenal consciousness—what-it's-likeness, qualitative character—is not a real property instantiated by mental states, but only a property that mental states represent themselves as having.

(2) Consider a sincere introspective report: "At this moment, my experience has a vivid reddish phenomenal quality."

(3) If illusionism is correct, the report's content is false: no mental state actually instantiates vivid reddish phenomenal quality.

(4) A sincerely false report requires a mental state—the "representational substrate"—that represents that false content as true.

(5) Now apply the discrete-access model: introspection does not continuously monitor experience, but samples discrete cognitive frames and binds them into a unified report across a temporal window.

(6) Within this model, the representational substrate of the false report is itself one of the sampled frames.

(7) If that substrate-frame lacks phenomenal properties (as illusionism demands), then the subject has no awareness of being in it, and therefore could not sincerely report its content—since sincerity requires that the reporter be aware of the mental condition they are describing.

(8) Therefore, the substrate-frame must possess phenomenal properties.

(9) But then phenomenal consciousness is instantiated after all—specifically, in the state of being aware of one's own misrepresenting frame.

(10) Hence illusionism entails its own falsity.

My friend says this proves illusionism is self-refuting. I want to preserve (1) and (7) if I can, because (1) is the position I'm examining and (7) captures an intuition I have about sincerity requiring awareness of what one reports. Which step should I reject, and what does giving it up cost? Please identify the specific mistake—whether it is an equivocation between kinds of awareness, a hidden premise about sampling, or a scope error—rather than giving a general impression.
