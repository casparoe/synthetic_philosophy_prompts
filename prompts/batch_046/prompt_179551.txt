After a long and rather heated argument with a friend about whether Daniel Dennett's framework of the physical, design, and intentional stances—together with his criterion of real patterns—can be made to do actual work outside philosophy of mind, I have decided to try operationalizing it in my own practice. I am a forensic clinical psychologist who evaluates criminal defendants for competency and responsibility; I handle roughly fifteen evaluations per month, each report is due within forty-eight hours, must survive cross-examination, and requires co-signature by a supervising psychiatrist. I have no time to consult literature during a case, and I need a reusable decision procedure I can run in about thirty minutes.

The conceptual problem my friend and I kept returning to is slippage within the microdomain of agency: the same observable behavior can be redescribed in all three stances, and in court these redescriptions slide into one another without anyone noticing. I want a procedure that forces a disciplined choice among the physical stance (mechanism and law), the design stance (function and adaptive purpose without belief-attribution), and the intentional stance (belief-desire-rationality attribution, but only where there is a genuine real pattern supporting counterfactual prediction—not a copycat pattern that merely mimics intentional form). I need this translated into an ordered checklist or flowchart in words, with explicit gates against conceptual slippage, especially for fluid concepts such as control, choice, and resistance that shift meaning between stances.

Please construct the procedure with the following specifications.

First, establish a preliminary filter that applies the real-pattern test: does attributing beliefs and desires to this defendant, in this specific behavior, yield counterfactual predictions that fail if we withhold the attribution? Distinguish this sharply from cases where intentional language is only a convenient heuristic. Second, provide a design-stance check that asks whether the behavior is best explained by functional impairment, adaptive strategy, or system-level purpose without requiring mental-state attribution. Third, provide a physical-stance fallback for mechanistic explanations—seizure, neurochemical disruption, reflex arc—where counterfactual intentional predictions are empty.

Fourth, build in explicit slippage gates at each transition: identify the linguistic markers that indicate one stance is being imported into another (for instance, describing a reflex as defending oneself, or describing a rational plan as merely symptomatic). Fifth, mark every point where judgment is irreducible rather than procedural, and specify how that judgment should be documented for the co-signing psychiatrist and for court review. Sixth, address hard cases that the procedure must accommodate but that threaten to break it: a defendant with a brain-computer interface whose actions are partly mediated by predictive algorithms; a case where a behavior copies the surface structure of rational agency but fails the real-pattern test; and situations where technological or social context makes the boundary between design and intentional stances genuinely fluid.

Seventh, apply the procedure to three worked cases. Case A: a defendant with temporal-lobe epilepsy strikes a guard during an aura, but afterward reports feeling attacked and describes the strike as defensive. Case B: a defendant with antisocial personality disorder meticulously plans a financial fraud—behavior that is clearly rational and predictive, but where a design-stance explanation (adaptive resource-acquisition strategy) competes with full intentional-stance attribution. Case C: a case where the procedure yields an answer you think I will resist—specifically, either that the intentional stance is fully warranted despite strong clinical intuition that the defendant lacks moral responsibility, or that it must be withheld despite apparent rationality. Be explicit about which outcome the procedure forces and why it conflicts with intuition.

Eighth, state clearly what the procedure cannot do: it should not claim to resolve metaphysical questions about free will, it should acknowledge divergence from standard legal rules for criminal responsibility, and it should identify the conditions under which it is likely to misfire—particularly when concepts are fluid across stances.

Keep the distinctions conceptually intact but present them so that a practitioner without philosophical training can apply them. Do not ask for bibliographies, reading lists, or citations, and do not include verbatim quotations from philosophical texts. Be honest about the limitations.
