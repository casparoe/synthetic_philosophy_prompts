I am a high school student writing for an advanced ethics seminar on moral psychology and technology. I find Bernard Williams's integrity objection compelling: the idea that requiring an agent to perform an impartial moral calculus can insert one thought too many between that agent and their ground projects, thereby alienating them from the very commitments that make their life meaningful. I want to test this objection against a novel, conceptually rigorous scenario. Suppose we develop integrated cognitive systems—call them moral co-processors—that do not merely advise but are embedded in an agent's deliberative architecture, continuously computing optimal outcomes in real time, yet the agent experiences these computations as transparent extensions of their own deepest values rather than as external demands. In this hybrid case, does the alienation Williams describes still occur, or does the fusion of impartial reasoning with ground projects dissolve the objection? More generally, playing it straight: what would have to be true for Williams's integrity objection to be wrong or seriously weakened? Which of its premises—about the psychological reality and non-derivativeness of ground projects, about the necessary alienation caused by impartial calculation, or about the normative priority of integrity over aggregate welfare—could fail, and what kind of theoretical development, empirical finding, or thought experiment would genuinely defeat the objection rather than merely complicate it? I want an honest map of its exposure: where is it well insulated, and where does it risk collapsing into a defense of unreflective partiality or into the rival theories it rejects? Please distinguish what defenders of Williams themselves would concede as damaging from what only critics allege, and indicate whether any escape routes are live or merely logical possibilities.
