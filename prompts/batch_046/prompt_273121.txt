I run a philosophy workshop, about 12 grad students, 75 minutes. I want an activty that makes them feel the dogmatism paradox in their bones — specifically that protecting a well-supported belief by tweaking background assumptions starts to look like stubborn dogmatism, but revising the core belief every time something weird happens makes you look like you have no convictions at all.

Design a card game with small real stakes (like snack points or picking the next reading). Each player gets one "core theory" card and three "auxiliary" cards describing background conditions. There's a deck of "anomaly" cards that challenge the core. When hit, a player can either spend an "integrity token" to revise the core (gain open-mindedness points) or burn an auxiliary card to absorb the blow (keep consistency points). The niche twist: include "meta-credibility" cards that let a player claim their auxiliary tweak was independently motivated, but there's no objective rule to check this — the other players vote "ad hoc" or "legit" and the votes determine payoffs. By round three the group should be punishing rational conservatism and rewarding either obvious stonewalling or flaky revisionism.

Then a debrief that maps core/auxiliary/anomaly onto belief/auxiliary assumption/counterevidence, and the meta-credibility vote onto the problem of ad hocness. Say exactly what will go wrong when you run it: people vote on charisma not logic, someone hoards tokens and becomes a scapegoat, and the ambiguity of the anomaly cards will make one faction think it's obvious they should revise. Be frank that the game cant reproduce the actual epistemic status of background assumptions — the facilitator has to supply the point that there's no algorithm for ad hocness, and that in real life there's no peer jury with perfect access to your reasoning. No lecture up front, dont name the paradox until after they've played, and keep everything runnable without web access.
