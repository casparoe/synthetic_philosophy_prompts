For a philosophy assignment, I need to analyze a well-known historical episode through the framework of just war theory, with particular attention to the doctrine of the moral equality of combatants and to the revisionist critique that challenges it. I have chosen the International Military Tribunal at Nuremberg, held in 1945 and 1946. The salient facts, as I understand them, are these: the tribunal convicted high-ranking officials for crimes against peace—planning and initiating aggressive war—while also recognizing war crimes and crimes against humanity committed in the course of hostilities. At the same time, it generally did not prosecute ordinary soldiers merely for fighting on behalf of what was later judged an unjust cause, though it placed strict limits on the defense that a soldier was only obeying superior orders.

Please examine this episode in both directions. First, map the tribunal’s distinctions onto the traditional just war framework: who bore responsibility for failures of jus ad bellum, who could be held liable for violations of jus in bello, and what the decision to shield rank-and-file soldiers from liability for the injustice of the war itself implies about the moral equality of combatants. Second, consider the revisionist challenge, which denies that unjust combatants possess any moral permission to kill even if they fight with perfect discipline under jus in bello rules. Does the prosecution of leaders for the injustice of the war support the revisionist claim that cause fundamentally matters for moral permission? Or does the protection of ordinary soldiers support the traditional equality thesis that combatants on both sides share the same rights and obligations in battle regardless of their cause? Third, identify precisely where the theory is silent or strained—for instance, whether a criminal court’s practical need to assign individual blame can ever fully capture the collective moral status of combatants, and whether the tribunal’s hybrid practice reveals that legal institutions must combine elements of both views. Reach and defend a clear conclusion about whether Nuremberg ultimately reinforces the moral equality of combatants, undermines it, or demonstrates that neither pure framework can accommodate the demands of postwar justice.
