I have been trying to formalize why moral biography works, and the result looks like a reductio that I cannot locate. (1) If a life story is to serve moral education, it must present its subject as possessing a stable character that the reader can emulate. (2) But real human lives contain contradictory actions and moral failures; to present a stable, exemplary character therefore requires selecting, ordering, and smoothing events so that contradictions disappear or are explained away. (3) Any such narrative smoothing is hagiography in the precise sense: not necessarily falsehood, but a structural removal of the friction that actual moral failure provides. (4) Therefore every biography that succeeds as moral instruction is hagiographic. (5) Yet hagiography undermines moral learning precisely because it conceals the subject's real errors, which are the very material a reader needs to recognize and correct in herself. (6) So successful moral biography teaches nothing usable, and unsuccessful biography—one that preserves all the contradictions—teaches nothing clear. (7) But Plutarch's parallel lives are widely taken to be paradigmatic moral instruction without being pure fables, and I am reluctant to dismiss them. I want to keep (1) and (7); I would rather not surrender (5). Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost?
