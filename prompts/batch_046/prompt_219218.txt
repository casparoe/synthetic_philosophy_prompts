I've been trying to understand Nick Bostrom's singleton hypothesis from a systems-engineering perspective. As I understand it, the claim is that as technological development accelerates, competitive pressures will drive all independent decision-making agents—states, corporations, AI systems—into a single globally coordinated architecture, much like how distributed nodes in a consensus protocol eventually converge on one leader to avoid split-brain failures. From this angle, the singleton looks less like a political theory and more like an emergent property of scale-free coordination networks: once the cost of non-coordination exceeds the benefit of autonomy, the system naturally collapses to one root authority. My question is: if the singleton is essentially a convergence result about network topology rather than a normative prescription, why do discussions of AI risk frame it as a contingent future we might avoid? Shouldn't it be treated as an inevitable structural endpoint—like TCP/IP standardization—rather than a scenario that requires active prevention?
