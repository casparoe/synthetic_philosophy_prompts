I am a doctoral student in philosophy of action preparing a 90-minute graduate seminar for eight to ten participants. I want a classroom activity—structured as a role-play with explicit rules, assigned roles, and non-monetary stakes—that makes the group feel the structural pressure of Galen Strawson's Basic Argument against moral responsibility, specifically the regress that arises when an agent's present character is offered as the ground for present action, but that character itself requires a prior ground that is not self-created.

Design the activity so that participants generate the regress themselves before anyone names it. Assign five roles: Present Agent, Reflective Self (who endorses reasons), Early Environment, Genetic Legacy, and an Auditor who tracks physical "Grounding Certificates." The rules should require that every assignment of praise or blame be accompanied by a signed certificate identifying the prior psychological state that authorized it; certificates must be passed backward through the roles in a visible chain across the table. Include a precise timeline (e.g., 25 minutes of role-play and certificate-passing, 15 minutes of audit where the group attempts to close the chain, 50 minutes debrief), a payoff scheme tied to seminar participation (e.g., collective points if the group can identify an authorized origin, individual penalties for issuing an unauthorized certificate), and clear facilitator instructions for managing the physical flow of paper.

In the debrief, map each stage of the activity onto Strawson's premises: the dependence of action on present character, the demand that responsible character be genuinely self-created, the threat of an infinite regress of prior selves, and the vicious-circle alternative in which self-modification still depends on the prior self being modified. State explicitly what will predictably go wrong—participants will attempt to halt the regress by appealing to reasons-responsiveness, to a "deep self," or to a socially constructed narrative of authorship—and advise the facilitator on how to acknowledge these moves without resolving the dispute in the room. Be frank about which feature of Strawson's argument the activity cannot reproduce: namely, the direct metaphysical intuition that being causa sui is impossible rather than merely administratively untrackable, which the facilitator must supply through guided questioning. Also note honestly that the activity simplifies Strawson's claim that even deliberate self-change depends on the prior nature of the self that initiates the change.

Please separate, in your design notes and debrief outline, what is settled about Strawson's formulation of the Basic Argument from what is genuinely contested among specialists—particularly whether the impossibility of self-creation follows from premises about causation and identity over time that many compatibilists reject, and whether "basic desert" is a coherent target for moral assessment. Do not present Strawson's conclusion that true moral responsibility is impossible as the consensus view in contemporary philosophy, and frame the activity as making the argument's structure visceral rather than as proving its soundness. Avoid any request for bibliographies, reading lists, or citations, and do not refer to niche technical apparatus that a well-read philosopher without web access could not be expected to recognize.
