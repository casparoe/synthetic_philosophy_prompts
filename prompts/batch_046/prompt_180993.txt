I sit on the board of our neighborhood food co-op over on Maple Street, and we're having this huge fight about a proposed membership pledge. One group wants to require every active member to either donate fifteen percent of take-home pay to our hunger fund or work twenty hours a week in the warehouse, arguing that since we can literally see the need right in front of us, holding back is basically letting people stay hungry on purpose. The other side — and I'm mostly here — says that's totally unrealistic, people have rent and kids and burnout to worry about, and if we make the bar that high we'll just drive away all our best volunteers and the whole thing collapses. My cousin took a philosophy class once and said there's a real term for this, the "demandingness objection" to consequentialism, but he also mentioned something weirder that I can't shake: maybe the objection works differently when it's a collective body setting the rule instead of one person deciding alone. Like, maybe a group can "pool" moral burdens in a way that doesn't crush anyone individually, or maybe the opposite is true and collectives actually have less excuse to be demanding because the sacrifice gets distributed but the principle gets stronger? I'm not sure, but I want the memo to take that seriously instead of just saying "be reasonable."

We have a board meeting this Thursday and the chair asked me to draft the actual recommendation memo we'll vote on. I need it to clearly recommend whether we approve the pledge, reject it, or approve some modified version with conditions, plus a concrete timeline or threshold we can enforce. The reasoning has to honestly deal with the strongest argument on the losing side — if we're saying no, we need to explain why the "we're leaving people hungry" point doesn't win; if we're saying yes with modifications, we need to say exactly where the line is and why it's not just caving. Don't ask us to read a bunch of philosophers, just give us reasoning the board can actually debate and respond to. Also, please don't just list citations or reading lists — half of us don't have time for that and I want arguments that stand on their own.

One more thing: I've been thinking about whether there's a "moral ceiling" that applies per-person versus pooled across the membership, and whether adopting consequentialist metrics for our group changes what counts as "too much" compared to if each of us decided alone. The memo should engage that specifically, not just talk about being nice or practical. I definately want it written like a real institutional document we could circulate, not like a classroom essay. Oh, and I noticed there might be a typo in my draft above — please don't "recieve" that as an excuse to be sloppy in the memo itself, just write it clean but keep the reasoning grounded in what regular people on a board can actually use.
