As a retired experimental physicist who has recently turned to philosophy of science, I am attempting to map the logical geography of the many-worlds interpretation's measurement problem—specifically, the derivation of the Born rule—without relying on literature I cannot access. I want you to draft the joint statement that an adversarial collaboration might produce between two camps: (A) defenders of the decision-theoretic derivation, who hold that rational preference axioms applied to branching agents uniquely determine Born-rule weights; and (B) defenders of the self-locating-uncertainty approach, who hold that the Born weights arise from symmetric epistemic uncertainty about which decohered branch an observer occupies, independent of decision-theoretic rationality constraints.

The document should have four parts. First, a numbered list of claims both sides can sign—restricted to the formalism (the unitary Schrödinger dynamics, the structure of decoherence, the absence of collapse in the bare theory), to what needs explaining (why observed frequencies match the squared-modulus weights), and to what the standard no-go theorems actually rule out. Second, a disagreements table in which each row states a precise point of divergence, with the wording for side A and the wording for side B each rendered so that the respective camp would accept it as an accurate statement of its own position; include at least one row addressing whether a disagreement is substantive or partly verbal. Third, the cruxes: identify the sub-questions such that resolving them in one direction would cause at least one camp to revise its core commitment—specifically addressing the status of the epistemic separability principle, the legitimacy of treating branch weights as probabilities prior to any rational-choice framework, and the explanatory relevance of a hypothetical three-branch decoherence-isolated observer who receives a reward contingent on correctly identifying her own branch without any prior branching record. For each crux, state what each side predicts or requires. Fourth, record in advance what arguments, formal results, or constructed cases both sides agree would actually bear on these cruxes, and for each specify whether side A or side B (or both) regard it as potentially decisive against their own view; if either side believes no conceivable evidence could move them, say so explicitly, and if both agree that part of the dispute reduces to a verbal choice about how to use the term probability, identify that part precisely.

Write in a formal, polished register appropriate to a philosophical collaboration protocol. Do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations. Do not invoke obscure technical results that are not part of the standard discussion of this problem. Where you need to refer to specific thinkers, use only the widely recognized pairings associated with the decision-theoretic program without attributing specific paper titles or dates. Avoid direct quotation; if you reference a well-known formulation, paraphrase it without quotation marks.
