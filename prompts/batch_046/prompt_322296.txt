In many technology firms, structured behavioral interviews rate candidates on stable moral dispositions—integrity, grit, resilience—then aggregate these ratings into a single composite character-index score that is weighted equally with technical assessments in hiring algorithms. The practice treats these traits as cross-situationally consistent, causally primary, and measurable independently of situational context. At the same time, the situationist literature in moral psychology—drawing on experiments in which trivial contextual shifts, such as being told one is late or encountering an unexpected obstacle, predict prosocial behavior better than self-reported trait measures—challenges precisely this ontology of character. Audit the philosophical commitments embedded in this institutional practice: what theory of moral agency and trait ontology does it presuppose? Is it specifically committed to an Aristotelian account of hexis, or can it be sustained on a thinner dispositionalist or behaviorist framework? How does its aggregation method—collapsing context-sensitive responses into a scalar score—presuppose a particular relation between part-traits and global character? Finally, distinguish whether the situationist critique undermines the practice only on empirical-predictive grounds, or whether there is also a distinct normative failure in treating persons as bundles of stable, quantifiable dispositions regardless of context.
