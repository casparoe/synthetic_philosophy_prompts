A philosophy-of-biology forum post summarizes Gould and Lewontin's 1979 essay The Spandrels of San Marco and the Panglossian Paradigm as follows: Gould and Lewontin borrow the architectural term spandrel—the triangular spaces between arches in San Marco basilica—to argue that evolutionary biologists wrongly treat every trait as a direct adaptation shaped by natural selection. They claim many biological features are inevitable structural byproducts rather than functional solutions, and that the adaptationist program therefore invents just-so stories for traits that are really non-adaptive leftovers. The post also says that in this same essay they introduced the concept of exaptation, in which a spandrel is later co-opted for a new function, to show that even apparently adaptive traits may have originated as accidents. From all this, the poster concludes that natural selection is only a minor evolutionary force, with structural constraints and random drift explaining most biological form, and therefore that adaptationist explanations should be rejected by default unless a trait can be shown to have no possible structural origin. I have a nagging sense that this overstates their anti-adaptationism and that the exaptation attribution may be wrong, but I cannot locate the specific errors. How fair is this summary? Please assess it point by point: which claims accurately represent the 1979 argument, which are overstatements or misattributions, what a defender of adaptationism would object to, and whether the poster's conclusion—that adaptationist explanations should be rejected by default—actually follows from Gould and Lewontin's premises. I am especially unsure whether their spandrel argument applies with equal force to molecular-level features—say, the specific amino-acid sequence of a histone protein—or whether the architectural metaphor loses its grip at smaller scales.
