Having retired from experimental physics after a career spent calibrating high-precision interferometric instruments, I have lately been studying philosophy of science, particularly Quine's critique of the analytic-synthetic distinction and his thesis of confirmational holism. In the laboratory, one routinely faces a decision: when an instrument yields anomalous data, should one revise the theoretical model being tested, or should one adjust the calibration constants—thermal coefficients, geometric alignment parameters, electronic gain factors—that translate raw signals into measured values? The standard methodological view treats these calibration adjustments as purely empirical corrections to the measuring apparatus, preserving the theoretical framework intact, whereas revising the model is seen as a conceptual or theoretical change. I want you to argue that this distinction collapses under rigorous Quinean holism. Specifically, defend the position that calibration parameters are themselves embedded in a network of auxiliary theories—about material properties, noise distributions, and engineering design—so that adjusting a calibration constant is never a theory-neutral empirical fix but always involves holding some parts of the web of belief constant while altering others. From this, derive the stronger claim that there is no principled boundary between correcting the instrument and revising the theory in experimental practice, and that treating calibration errors as falling on the purely observational side of the ledger implicitly resurrects the very analytic-synthetic dichotomy Quine sought to dismantle. Address the obvious objection that calibration adjustments are local and pragmatic, confined to a single device, whereas Quine's holism concerns global systems of belief; explain why the locality of the adjustment does not rescue the distinction, perhaps by noting that even local auxiliary hypotheses carry theoretical commitments that can be redistributed across the broader network if one chooses to preserve them. Write your response as a sustained philosophical argument, not as a literature review, and do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations to specific editions or page numbers.
