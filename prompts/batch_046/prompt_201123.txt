I have been studying Du Bois's argument that art should serve as propaganda for justice, and I want to defend a stronger, more structural version of it: for art produced within systematically marginalized groups, aesthetic judgment is inseparable from evaluation of political efficacy, so that applying "pure" aesthetic standards does not remain neutral but misrecognizes the conditions under which the work is made and received. What would genuinely count against this claim? I am not asking only for standard counterexamples, but specifically which formal properties of artworks, which institutional practices of art criticism, and which cross-cultural judgments would undermine the structural link between evaluation and politics. Would discovering a genuinely apolitical work by a marginalized artist be fatal to the view, or could a defender absorb it by redefining what counts as political? Please rank the potential defeaters from decisive to merely awkward—considering, for instance, whether analogies to mathematical or scientific beauty sever aesthetic evaluation from politics; whether the position collapses into the claim that all art everywhere is propaganda; and whether it renders certain kinds of aesthetic disagreement impossible. Which of these objections would a committed defender concede as genuinely damaging, and which would they dismiss as missing the point? I want to know whether this position is genuinely exposed, or whether it can be insulated without becoming unfalsifiable.
