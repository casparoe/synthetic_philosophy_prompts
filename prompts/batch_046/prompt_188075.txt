I'm trying to isolate a specific historiographical move I keep encountering, and I want to know whether it already carries an established label or whether I need to coin one. The pattern unfolds in two stages. First, the historian translates a past figure's conceptual framework into contemporary vocabulary—rendering, for example, a seventeenth-century natural philosopher's talk of living forces as an inchoate version of kinetic-energy conservation, or recasting an ancient ethical account of self-preservation and social extension as proto-evolutionary altruism. Second, having made the past thinker speak in modern terms, the historian scores that thinker for failing to resolve questions that only arise inside the translated framework—criticizing the ancient ethicist for not addressing mechanisms of kin selection, or the natural philosopher for lacking a variational principle. The result is not straightforward Whig history, since there is no triumphal narrative of progress toward the present, and not mere presentism, since the past is not judged directly by modern values but via an intermediate translation layer. Rather, it resembles a rigged comparison: the past figure is entered into a competition whose rules were written after he left the field, then faulted for not winning it. I have observed this in reconstructions of early modern science, in philosophical genealogies of political concepts, and in histories of moral psychology. Is there an established concept for this precise two-step—translation followed by deficit-scoring—distinct from ordinary anachronism, from charitable conceptual genealogy, and from standard Whiggishness? If such a concept exists, what are its precise boundaries and its closest relatives? If not, can you propose a term together with a rigorous definition, and supply boundary cases that clearly separate it from legitimate retrospective interpretation on one side and from ordinary historical misunderstanding on the other?
