Student answer:

Kim's exclusion argument is designed to show that non-reductive physicalism cannot consistently maintain both the causal closure of physics and the causal efficacy of mental properties. The argument begins with two premises that non-reductive physicalists typically endorse. First, causal closure: every physical event with a cause has a complete sufficient cause within the physical domain. Second, supervenience: mental properties depend on physical properties such that there cannot be a change in the mental without a corresponding change in the physical.

The critical third premise is the principle of causal inheritance. This principle, which was first articulated by Donald Davidson in his defense of anomalous monism, holds that if a mental property M is realized by a physical property P, then M derives its causal powers entirely from P. Consequently, when P causes some physical effect E, and M is also instantiated, M does not contribute any independent causal force. If we allow M to cause E as well, we face systematic overdetermination—two distinct sufficient causes for one effect.

Because Kim treats mental properties as genuinely distinct causal contributors—properties that are not identical to their physical realizers—his argument effectively treats them as if they were non-physical in the Cartesian sense, competing with neural processes for control over behavior. Only on this assumption does the threat of overdetermination become acute.

Kim applies this specifically to downward causation, where a mental state is supposed to cause a bodily movement or a neural event. Because the physical realizer of that mental state already provides a sufficient physical cause for the bodily movement, the mental property is excluded from doing any causal work. The argument does not necessarily apply to horizontal mental-to-mental causation—say, one belief causing another—because in those cases we might still need mental descriptions to capture the pattern, especially if mental properties are not law-governed in the way Davidson suggested.

A non-reductive physicalist might respond by arguing that mental properties are not ontologically distinct enough from their realizers to produce overdetermination. If mental properties are merely ways of describing the same causal process captured by physics, then there is no second cause. But this response risks collapsing into reductive physicalism or an identity theory, because it denies that mental properties have autonomous causal profiles. Alternatively, one might embrace overdetermination and treat mental and physical causes as complementary descriptions of a single causal pathway. Yet this seems to violate parsimony.

Therefore, the exclusion argument demonstrates that non-reductive physicalism is unstable: it must either abandon the causal efficacy of mental properties or reject physical causal closure. The only fully consistent positions are reductionism, which identifies mental properties with physical ones, or a form of substance dualism that denies closure altogether.
