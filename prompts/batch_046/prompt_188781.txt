I am preparing an essay for a graduate seminar in the philosophy of social science, and I would like substantive conceptual feedback on its architecture and on the accuracy of its reading of Weber. The essay is not intended as a commentary on The Protestant Ethic and the Spirit of Capitalism in the usual historical sense; rather, it uses Weber's thesis as a test case for a broader problem: whether cultural values can operate as causes in the same explanatory register as material conditions, or whether they must always be relegated to either heuristic devices for selecting explanatory problems (Wertbeziehung) or objects of normative evaluation (Werturteil).

My central claim is that Weber's framework implicitly requires a third category—what I call value-causation—in which a normative structure does not merely supply individual motives but constrains the causal pathways through which material interests can be realized. On this reading, the ascetic Protestant ethic is not just one psychological motive competing with economic interest; it functions as a selective filter that determines which forms of capital accumulation are culturally viable and which are blocked, thereby altering the causal topology of early modern economic development rather than simply coloring it.

To make the claim concrete without relying on pure historical reconstruction, I draw a deliberately provocative parallel—explicitly not an identity—between Weber's Calvinist entrepreneur and what I term secular ascetic capital in contemporary effective altruism: a normative framework that directs surplus accumulation into specific long-term philanthropic instruments rather than into consumption or display. The purpose of this parallel is strictly philosophical: to ask whether, if such a framework genuinely reshapes the causal trajectories of wealth, we ought to treat values as structural causes rather than as epiphenomenal rationalizations of underlying material dynamics.

The essay then considers three objections. First, that this tripartite scheme collapses Weber's careful separation of causal explanation and value judgment. Second, that value-causation is merely multicausal overdetermination redescribed in normative language. Third, that the modern parallel fails because effective altruism lacks the specifically theological anxiety—predestinarian uncertainty—that Weber identifies as the psychological engine of ascetic accumulation.

I would welcome focused comments on the following: (1) Is the distinction among value-relevance, value-judgment, and value-causation defensible within Weber's methodological writings on multicausal explanation and elective affinity, or does it misrepresent his insistence that cultural factors can only be assigned causal weight in conjunction with material conditions? (2) Does the comparison with secular philanthropic norms illuminate the causal claim, or does it import anachronistic categories that obscure the historical specificity of Weber's argument about unintended consequences? (3) Are there significant inaccuracies in my characterization of how Weber treats the relationship between subjective meaning and objective causal outcomes? I am especially interested in whether the conceptual argument retains philosophical force if the modern parallel is abandoned entirely.

Please treat this as a request for substantive conceptual feedback rather than for bibliographic suggestions, reading lists, or citations.
