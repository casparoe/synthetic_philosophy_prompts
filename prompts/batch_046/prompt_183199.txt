I was discussing Dennett's multiple drafts model with a friend on a rationalist forum last week, and we got stuck on a point that feels under-examined in the standard literature: even if we reject the Cartesian theater's single timeline and central audience, don't the editorial processes in Dennett's own account reintroduce something like a distributed homunculus? There is no literal stage, but there seems to be a functional selector that determines which draft gets fixed for report — and if that selector is not "me," then the unity of consciousness looks like an ex post facto construction rather than an illusion fully dissolved. I am preparing the closing session of a philosophy-of-mind course and want a poem that does real philosophical work on this tension, not decorative name-checking.

Write a villanelle — six tercets and a final quatrain — with the refrains "No audience waits in the darkened hall" and "The draft revises what came before," kept in roughly iambic pentameter unless technical language forces a deviation. Structure it so that stanza one sets up the naive theater with its single stream; stanzas two and three distribute editing across time and neural substrate; stanza four asks who reads the report if no one is present; stanza five undermines the idea of a canonical final version; and the closing quatrain should leave open whether the missing audience is a loss of self or its true dissolution. It should be precise enough that a reader familiar with Dennett's book-length treatment of consciousness recognizes the mechanism — distributed revision, probe-dependent reportability, no master narrative — without requiring any citation, bibliography, or verbatim quotation from a source. Please paraphrase the conceptual machinery in your own lines, and make the poem stand on its own for someone who has never read the theory.
