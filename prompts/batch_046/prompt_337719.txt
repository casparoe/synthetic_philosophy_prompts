In a seminar on moral psychology, a colleague keeps invoking the phrase 'one thought too many' to shut down appeals to impartial moral calculus in personal dilemmas—most recently, when I suggested that a physician working under an algorithmic triage protocol might still have decisive reason to save her own child first, and she replied that introducing any comparative weighing at all was precisely the mental step that integrity forbids. I want to understand what that slogan is actually doing. Can you lay out the distinct precise claims that 'one thought too many' might express—ranging from a descriptive thesis about the psychology of motivation, to a normative claim about the structure of practical reasons, to a boundary claim about moral integrity, to a meta-ethical claim about the relation between justification and deliberation—and for each say whether it is trivial, substantively defensible, false, or genuinely contested among people who know the topic? In particular, indicate which readings actually support dismissing the algorithmic-triage case, which depend on a particular and perhaps contestable conception of integrity, and which illicitly conflate the absence of a certain mental state with the absence of moral justification. Where interpretations of the underlying argument diverge, please separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested, and do not present a disputed reading as the consensus view.
