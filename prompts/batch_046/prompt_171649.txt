I'm drafting a short forum post arguing that coherent extrapolated volition (CEV) conceals a coordination problem that its standard formulation—what you'd want if you knew more, thought faster, and were more the person you wished to be—treats as purely individual. I want to press a Lewis-inspired framing: if coherence across people requires convergence on shared meta-preferences, then the dimensions of idealization (say, epistemic humility versus moral urgency) face a salience problem. Without a prior convention about which values to weight, different extrapolations may be equally rational but mutually incompatible, so coherence risks either circularity—defining agreement as the output of a process that already assumes agreement on weights—or a hidden normative choice by whoever specifies the extrapolation procedure. I'm asking whether this is a genuine problem or just a restatement of the aggregation problem we already discuss; whether treating idealization as iterative rather than one-shot dissolves the circularity; and whether Lewis's emphasis on the arbitrariness of conventions undermines the normative authority we want CEV to carry. Specifically: if our idealized preferences depend on an arbitrarily salient coordination point, should we regard them as authoritative, or merely as stable? I'd welcome point-by-point comments on the argument structure, especially on whether I'm mischaracterizing the relationship between individual extrapolation and intersubjective coherence.
