I am a doctoral student in aesthetics investigating a refined formulation of the paradox of tragedy. The familiar puzzle is why audiences actively seek artworks—tragedy, elegiac cinema, sorrowful music—that systematically elicit grief, fear, or pity. Standard explanations invoke cathartic purgation, emotional exercise, or the pleasure of controlling negative affect from a position of safety. I want you to write an essay weighing these accounts against a narrower hypothesis: that tragic pleasure arises not from resolving negative affect but from sustaining an unresolved tension between affective mirroring (a direct, felt resonance with the suffering depicted) and cognitive demarcation (the simultaneous, stable awareness that the suffering is fictional and therefore not morally demanding). On this view, catharsis actually undermines pleasure because it collapses that very tension. Reach and defend a concrete verdict—either that this tension-based account supersedes the traditional ones, or that one of the standard explanations survives despite it. Do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; argue through conceptual analysis alone, and avoid dissolving the paradox into generic appeals to aesthetic distance.
