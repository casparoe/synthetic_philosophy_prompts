I've been following a rationalist-leaning tech blog that attempts to synthesize work on substrate-independent minds. The author presents what they call the mainstream functionalist view: once an emulation achieves full cognitive functional equivalence to a human, it acquires complete moral patienthood, but this status is both discontinuous and linearly divisible. By their account, pausing the emulation severs its diachronic identity and thereby nullifies its moral claim until resumption, while running N concurrent copies divides the system's total moral weight by N, so ten parallel instances each carry one-tenth of the weight of a single continuous run. They claim this follows directly from combining standard functionalism about consciousness with a utilitarian aggregation rule, and they dismiss objections invoking substrate-dependence or phenomenal continuity as reliance on biological intuition pumps rather than on principled argument. I suspect that the treatment of discontinuity and multiplicity is not actually endorsed by the philosophers they invoke, and that the aggregation rule may illegitimately conflate welfare distribution with the conditions for possessing moral status in the first place. Is this a fair characterization? Please distinguish what accurately captures mainstream functionalist and utilitarian commitments, what is a defensible but contested extension, and what is a misrepresentation or unstated metaphysical import—particularly regarding whether functionalists generally accept that interruption destroys moral status, and whether linear division applies to the moral weight of minds themselves rather than merely to the allocation of welfare benefits.
