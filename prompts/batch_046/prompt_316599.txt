As a journalist covering science and ideas, I am developing a feature on psychiatric taxonomy and want an expert audit of a position I am weighing. You are a professional philosopher of science with expertise in psychiatry and social epistemology. Please think carefully, begin with brief brainstorming, and deliver your answer in British English.

The position is this: in modern psychiatric classification—particularly since the operationalised criteria era that began with DSM-III—Hacking-style looping effects are not merely present but dominant over neurobiological discovery and clinical pragmatism in explaining why diagnostic categories expand, shift shape, and acquire an appearance of empirical robustness. I apply this especially to more recent or reconceptualised conditions such as prolonged grief disorder and complex PTSD, where I claim a two-stage loop operates. First, individuals reinterpret ambiguous affective and somatic states through the new diagnostic lens; second, clinicians and researchers selectively attend to symptoms that confirm the category’s coherence, while institutional incentives—such as reimbursement structures and research funding streams—reinforce this selective attention. The taxonomy therefore looks descriptively grounded but is largely institutionally self-fulfilling.

What would count against this view? I want the evidence, arguments, and possible developments that should genuinely move me, ranked from decisive to merely awkward. Please include: (a) empirical findings or historical cases that have arguably already occurred; (b) conceptual distinctions that might collapse my two-stage loop into a simpler rival explanation; (c) what defenders of looping-effect dominance—including myself—would be forced to concede as genuinely damaging; and (d) an honest assessment of whether the position can be qualified so heavily that it becomes unfalsifiable, and whether that insulation is a defect for a claim of this kind.
