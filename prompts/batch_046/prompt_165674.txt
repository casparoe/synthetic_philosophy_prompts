A blogger summarizing work on analogical microdomains writes: "In problems like ABC → ABD, concepts such as alphabetic successor, repetition, and phonetic shape do not stay fixed; they slide into one another depending on context. This proves that concepts have no stable core—'letter' can mean position, sound, or visual form—and that there is never a uniquely correct answer, only temporary coalitions of association. For example, AABC → ? can become AABD if the double-A is read as an error, or remain AABC if it is read as intentional intensity, and nothing in the problem structure decides between them. The same fluidity appears in a variant microdomain where strings represent color gradients rather than alphabet symbols: 'red-red-blue' can slide to 'red-blue-blue' or stay doubled, with no structural constraint selecting one resolution. Therefore formal rules are impossible, and conceptual boundaries are purely conventional." The blogger applies this to claim that analogy has no normative structure whatsoever. Is this summary fair? Identify which parts accurately describe conceptual slippage, which parts conflate fluidity with the absence of all constraint, and whether the examples actually show there is no principled answer or merely that several weighted constraints compete.
