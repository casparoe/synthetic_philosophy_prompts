I am drafting a novel about an archivist who has spent decades cataloguing the administrative remains of fallen empires, and I have given her a conviction—drawn from the cyclical tradition that runs from Polybius and Ibn Khaldun through Spengler and Toynbee, though she has reshaped it into something more severe—that now governs every interpretation she makes. She holds that durable historical change is necessarily cyclical at the level of civilizational morphology, and that linear narratives of progress, secularization, or institutional accumulation are not empirically false but conceptually misframed: they arise only when observers apply an inappropriate temporal resolution to social processes, mistaking epiphenomenal surface noise—technological layering, bureaucratic complexity, demographic shifts—for genuine directional movement, much as discrete frames can produce the illusion of smooth motion. I need this view rendered with philosophical precision so that it can be genuinely contested within the narrative rather than serving as mere atmospheric color. Present the position clearly, then map what would actually count against it. I want more than the familiar counterexamples—irreversible ecological transformation, the apparent uniqueness of modern science, or the cumulative character of legal institutions—though you should classify whether these are fatal or merely awkward for her framework. Which formal commitments does the thesis require? Does it depend on a particular ontology of social entities, a specific account of causal explanation across temporal scales, or a normative theory of what counts as the correct resolution at which to describe change? Which of these dependencies, if undermined, would collapse the view rather than merely complicate it? What parallel intuitions about persistence and identity in other domains—biological evolution, personal identity, or the persistence of artifacts—would a committed defender be forced either to share or to reject at a cost? How exposed is the position to the charge that it has been qualified into unfalsifiability: if any linear trend can be redescribed as one phase within a larger, still-incomplete cycle, has the thesis sacrificed all predictive risk? And if it is indeed well insulated, is that insulation a theoretical virtue appropriate to a morphological claim, or is it a vice that renders the view empty? Rank the potential defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, distinguish carefully between what a sincere adherent like my archivist would concede as genuinely damaging and what she would dismiss as a category error, and indicate whether there remains any observation, formal result, or conceptual development that could actually move her—rather than being absorbed into the cyclical framework she already holds.
