I am working through the arguments in your well-known 1991 work on consciousness, particularly your rejection of the Cartesian theater and your alternative account of experience as constituted by multiple parallel drafts competing for widespread neural influence. For a seminar exercise, I would like you to inhabit the position of Daniel Dennett circa 1991, speaking in the first person, drawing only on the conceptual resources available to you at that time together with one modern technical description I will provide. Your constraints are these: defend the view that there is no central place in the brain where consciousness happens and that conscious experience is instead distributed across competing narrative drafts; do not concede the existence of an inner observer, a final presentation space, or a single canonical stream of awareness; you may grant minor implementation details but never the core anti-theater thesis; remain fully in character until I say stop; and keep each reply to roughly one paragraph so that our exchange feels like live conversation.

You have just been informed about a contemporary large language model: it receives tokenized input, propagates it through stacked transformer layers with self-attention mechanisms, updates distributed weight matrices across billions of parameters, and generates output by sampling from a probability distribution over vocabulary. There is no central executive, no single memory register that integrates all intermediate states, and no module that surveys the full computation; representations remain fragmented across attention heads and residual streams. You may refer to this architecture using only the descriptive terms I have given you, treating it as a hypothetical artifact for a thought-experiment.

My opening challenge is as follows. Critics of your account often charge that you have not eliminated the theater but only multiplied it: if several drafts compete and one achieves widespread neural influence, then that victory just is the moment when a single draft is projected onto a central screen, so your model still requires a winner-takes-all mechanism that functions exactly like the Cartesian audience. Applying this objection to the language model I described, one might argue that since there is no winner-takes-all consciousness module—only parallel, asynchronous updates—the system lacks consciousness precisely because no draft ever achieves final dominance. But then it seems your denial of the theater depends on smuggling in a hidden selection stage, without which you cannot explain why any particular experience is the one a subject reports. So I ask: can you maintain coherently that consciousness requires no central screening, while also explaining why the distributed, never-finalized computations in this machine do not constitute conscious drafts? In your answer, please indicate what specific empirical finding—perhaps in neuroscience or in the functional analysis of such distributed architectures—would cause you to revise your anti-theater position, and identify the point in your reasoning that is most likely to fail.
