I was talking with a friend who does formal verification in Lean, and we got into this weird tension with how logic gets taught in different places. In your standard math class you're given modus ponens and proof by contradiction and told to apply them, but nobody asks you to justify why those rules are legitimate — the textbook just lists them like they're part of the game. Meanwhile in philosophy courses we read about that Carroll dialogue where Achilles and the tortoise get stuck in an infinite regress trying to justify applying a rule of inference, and if you took that seriously you'd never prove anything. But then in Lean every step is mechanically checked by the kernal, and the kernal's inference rules are just hardcoded — they aren't defended philosophically, they're executed.

So what does that whole pedagogical and technological practice actually presuppose about the justification of deduction? Does treating inference as something you run rather than defend mean Carroll's regress gets solved by just stopping at bedrock, or by saying practical success is enough? Is the practice even consistent, since we make students justify induction via well-ordering but never ask them to defend modus ponens? And if a math department actually took the tortoise's challenge seriously, what would have to change — would they need a different foundation, or just admit they can't start?
