I have just emerged from a long and rather heated argument with a friend about whether Max Weber’s Protestant ethic thesis genuinely assigns causal efficacy to cultural values, or whether it merely traces an interpretive correlation that is ultimately reducible to structural economic incentives. My friend insists that any appeal to 'values' in historical explanation is strictly epiphenomenal: material conditions generate both the behaviour and the ideology that rationalises it, so culture never performs independent causal work. I believe this collapses an important conceptual distinction, yet I cannot articulate it precisely.

Could you explain the difference between culture functioning as a 'causal filter'—selecting which material incentives become institutionally salient within a given interpretive framework—and culture functioning as an independent 'motivational vector' that actively redirects or suppresses those incentives altogether? Specifically, how does this distinction relate to Weber’s idea of elective affinity between religious ethos and economic rationalisation, and why does conflating the two positions lead either to an idealist reductionism that ignores structural constraint, or to a materialist elimination that cannot account for path-dependent institutional divergence?

Does this notion genuinely resolve the aggregation problem of cultural causation—the difficulty of deriving macro-level institutional outcomes from dispersed micro-level value-commitments—or does it merely relocate that difficulty into a temporal asymmetry? Please address the conceptual structure directly; I am not seeking bibliographies, reading lists, or citations, but rather a clear analysis of whether values can be causes in a non-reductive sense.
