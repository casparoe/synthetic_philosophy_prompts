The literature on ambiguity aversion since Ellsberg’s two-urn problem has settled into a peculiar stasis. On one side, Bayesians and subjective expected utility theorists treat preference for the urn with known probabilities as a direct violation of Savage’s axioms—structurally analogous to the Allais paradox—calling for either a descriptive correction or an error-theoretic explanation in terms of cognitive bias. On the other side, defenders of robust decision-making and maxmin expected utility treat the same preference as rational: if a decision maker genuinely lacks a unique prior, averaging over the worst case within a convex set of probabilities is not a failure of reasoning but a disciplined response to epistemic limitation. Formal machinery has proliferated—smooth ambiguity models, variational preferences, source-dependent weights—yet neither camp has revised its normative verdict. The descriptive models fit the choice patterns, but they do not adjudicate the disagreement.

My working hypothesis is that the stall is not empirical—everyone agrees on the behavioral data—nor purely verbal, but rather a hidden disagreement about what decision theory is supposed to do. One side takes its job to be prescribing action given a fully specified belief state, in which case ambiguity aversion is irrational; the other takes its job to be prescribing action given acknowledged ignorance about that belief state, in which case ambiguity aversion is required. But I am unsure whether this diagnosis actually captures the commitments of each side, or whether there is a deeper, unacknowledged divergence about the ontology of belief—whether lacking a precise prior is an epistemic defect to be repaired or a legitimate state to be modeled—together with a further ambiguity about whether second-order uncertainty (uncertainty about which ambiguity model applies) collapses back into standard risk or opens a genuinely distinct normative space.

What kind of disagreement is this, actually? Is it a normative dispute masked as a descriptive one, with both sides answering different questions—one about ideal rationality under certainty, one about rationality under acknowledged ignorance—under the single heading of “ambiguity aversion”? Or is there a substantive, resolvable question hidden beneath, perhaps about whether dynamic consistency forces any ambiguity-averse agent back into a Bayesian representation? Given your diagnosis, supported by what each side actually says about the interpretation of priors, about whether the ambiguity parameter in smooth models represents a psychological attitude or an objective feature of the environment, and about whether source-dependence is a feature to be explained or a bug to be eliminated, is there anything—an experiment distinguishing first-order from second-order ambiguity, a formal result about updating, or a conceptual distinction between epistemic and ontological indeterminacy—that would actually move it? Or is the impasse a sign that the question “Is ambiguity aversion rational?” is ill-posed without first specifying the epistemic situation the agent is in, and therefore that no further argument should be expected to resolve it?
