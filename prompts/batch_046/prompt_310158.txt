Construct a detailed example of a conceptual tension in defining corruption that arises specifically in institutional contexts where ritualized gift reciprocity and bureaucratic merit procedures are structurally intertwined—such as a public procurement oversight body whose members are drawn from kin networks with enduring seasonal exchange obligations. The scenario should illustrate a case in which an allocation decision is driven by relational loyalty that clearly subverts formal procedural criteria (satisfying one standard definition of clientelism), yet cannot be adequately described as a discrete quid pro quo bribe because the obligation is diffuse, temporally extended, and culturally constitutive of the officeholder's social role. Your task is to present the case so that it exposes exactly where Western transactional models of corruption fail to capture the harm without thereby licensing a purely relativist conclusion; in particular, identify the structural feature—whether it lies in the normalization of expectation, the absence of an explicit exchange term, or the collective rather than individual nature of the benefit—that makes the practice resistant to both straightforward prohibition and straightforward cultural accommodation.
