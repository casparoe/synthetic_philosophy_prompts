I'm putting together the final exam for my upper-level ethics seminar (about 12 students, 90 minutes, essays should be around 800-1000 words). I want one essay question on moral compromise between value systems — specifically whether "gains from trade" can apply when what's being exchanged aren't preferences but deep normative commitments. To keep it from being just another pluralism-vs-monism recitation, I'm baking in a distinction they won't have seen in textbooks: integrative compromise (where the outcome expresses both value systems in a transformed but still recognisable form) versus aggregative compromise (where it's just a split-the-difference bundle and each side loses coherence).

Please write the exam question, a model answer (about 600 words), and a marking scheme totalling 20 marks. The marking scheme should name five specific moves a top answer must make, with marks for each, and a one-line note on how students usually mess each up.

The scenario to embed: two groups share a watershed. Group A sees water as a sacred communal good; private allocation is sacrilege. Group B sees efficient use requiring individual property rights with market exchange. They settle on "stewardship shares" — individuals hold transferable rights, but every transaction requires a communal ritual affirming the watershed's sacred status, and a cut of each trade goes to collective maintenance.

The question should ask students to:
(a) Say why the economic gains-from-trade analogy either illuminates or misleads here, focusing on whether moral values can be "traded" like preferences.
(b) Classify the stewardship-share system as integrative or aggregative, and say whether that depends on the groups' own interpretation or on some external standard.
(c) Tackle the "subsumption" objection: if Group A starts describing their duty in terms of "stewardship rights," have they been absorbed into Group B's framework, or can they still see the ritual-transaction structure as an expression of sacred communal care?
(d) State a precise condition for when moral compromise can yield a result that isn't just tolerable to both sides but genuinely better for both.

The model answer should show what a strong answer looks like — not just summarising the scenario but working through the conceptual tension. The marking scheme should seperate an A answer from a B by naming exactly which moves are present, and include one common mistake per point (like treating values as fully fungible, or assuming integrative compromise requires literal translation of one language into the other).

No bibliography needed — just the question, answer, and scheme. A typo or two in my prompt is totally fine, so don't worry about polishing the wording to perfection.
