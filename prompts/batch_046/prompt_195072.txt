I'm a retired ecologst who spent thirty years tracking bird migration and then suddenly had time for philosphy — mostly Dennett's work on real patterns and the three stances. Next Thursday I'm giving the opening remarks at a small workshop called Patterns in Nature; the room is split between working scientists (climatologists, biologists, one physicist) who live in mechanisms and equations, and philosophers of science who worry about reduction and whether explanations are real. I want to frame the whole five-minute talk around the physical, design, and intentional stances, but I want them to do actual structural work, not just sit there as decoration. Specifically, I want to argue that when we talk about a climate model predicting a hurricane path, we're not just doing physics with approximations added on. The design stance — the engineered grid sizes, the purpose of the model — does real explanatory labor, and there's even a kind of intentional stance in interpreting what the model 'believes' about pressure gradients, though I know that sounds like anthropomorphism. I have to say all this without the scientists rolling their eyes or the philosophers accusing me of smuggling in vitalism. The talk should feel casual, like I'm talking over bad coffee, not a lecture, and please don't include citations or a reading list — half the room wouldn't read them anyway. One more thing: in my handwritten notes I keep writing 'intensional stance' instead of 'intentional.' If you can weave that slip in naturally — maybe as a joke about confusing purpose with logical intension — do it, but don't overplay it.
