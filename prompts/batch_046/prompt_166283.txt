As a retired physicist recently turned to epistemology, I have been struck by how little movement there seems to be in the long-standing dispute between reductionists and anti-reductionists about testimonial knowledge. The reductionists—following the Humean line developed by philosophers such as Coady—insist that any warrant a hearer acquires through testimony ultimately traces back to the speaker’s reliability, which itself must be grounded in the hearer’s own perceptual or inferential capacities; testimony is therefore a mediated, derivative source, analogous to reading a calibrated instrument whose calibration chain must eventually terminate in direct observation. The anti-reductionists—drawing on Reid’s common-sense tradition and refined by thinkers such as Lackey—counter that hearers routinely acquire knowledge through testimony without possessing any independent positive reason to trust the speaker, which makes testimony a basic source on a par with perception or memory, not a mere pipeline for empirical data. Every standard move is familiar: the reductionist demands an account of how we calibrate trust without circularity; the anti-reductionist points to the cognitive impossibility of independently verifying every claim in a complex society; the reductionist replies with tracking-reliability models; the anti-reductionist distinguishes global from local justification; and the debate cycles without resolution.

I hypothesize that the stall is not merely a shortage of evidence but a hidden structural mismatch: the reductionist is asking a normative question about the ultimate provenance of epistemic warrant—like asking whether a thermometer reading is justified only if one understands the physics of thermal expansion—while the anti-reductionist is asking a functional or descriptive question about the architecture of human cognition—like asking whether temperature measurement can serve as a basic input for a control system that never opens the instrument. If that is right, they may be answering different questions under the single heading “the epistemic status of testimony,” with one side treating “basic” as “foundational in the order of justification” and the other treating it as “irreducible in the cognitive economy.” But I want a sharper diagnosis: is the disagreement verbal, turning on competing uses of “basic source” and “justification”; is it substantive, with each side committed to incompatible claims about what actually happens in the transmission of belief; or is there a concealed empirical question—perhaps about the actual cognitive load of trust calibration, or about whether distributed scientific knowledge can be reconstructed from individual observation—that neither side has formalized? Please diagnose the impasse using what each camp actually says, not by adjudicating who is right. Then tell me what, given that diagnosis, would count as genuine progress: a conceptual distinction that separates the two questions; an empirical result from cognitive science about how hearers actually monitor speaker reliability; a formal model of epistemic dependence that captures both calibration and direct uptake; or an argument that the question is ill-posed and should be dissolved. Lead with your bottom-line diagnosis in the first sentence, then develop the reasoning, with no preamble and without restating my question.
