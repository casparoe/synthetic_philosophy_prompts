You are a professional philosopher specializing in applied ecological ethics and the practical operationalization of normative frameworks for non-academic decision-makers. Before you design anything, take a moment to brainstorm carefully: identify which conceptual distinctions in the framework below are genuinely load-bearing for a decision procedure, which distinctions can be collapsed without losing the principle's force, and exactly where value disagreement rather than factual uncertainty must be flagged for human judgment.

I serve on a regional land-management ethics board that reviews proposals to intervene in wild ecosystems solely to reduce wild animal suffering—not for human economic benefit, recreational hunting management, or endangered-species conservation. We receive eight to ten proposals annually, must reach unanimous sign-off among three board members within thirty days, and have no budget to hire external ethicists. Our members hold genuinely different values—some prioritize direct welfare reduction, others prioritize the non-instrumental integrity of ecological processes—so the procedure must clearly separate factual checkpoints from judgment calls.

Please construct a reusable decision procedure—an ordered sequence of questions with explicit sufficiency conditions, clear stop-points, and marked judgment-call flags—grounded in the following dual-constraint framework. First, an asymmetry of intervention risk: active interventions that risk introducing new suffering carry a substantially heavier burden of justification than omissions that allow existing suffering to continue, though that asymmetry weakens only when the suffering is severe, widespread, and clearly linked to anthropogenic causes rather than to ordinary ecological dynamics. Second, ecological narrative continuity: an ecosystem possesses an integrity constituted by the historical and functional relationships among its members and processes; interventions that dissolve those relationships—such as replacing natural mortality regimes with managed ones, severing predator-prey dynamics that structure population health, or substituting artificial provisioning for evolved foraging patterns—carry a distinct moral cost that must be weighed against welfare gains, unless the intervention is minimally disruptive to those relational patterns.

Your deliverable should include the ordered questions in sequence, specifying what counts as a sufficient answer at each step; the precise conditions under which the procedure stops with a clear do-not-approve; the conditions, if any, under which it stops with approve; and explicit flags marking where disagreement is about values rather than facts. Include a small set of concrete test criteria or sentence frames that board members without philosophical training can apply consistently. Then run the procedure on three proposals, including at least one case where it yields an answer that this board—which generally favors welfare-oriented intervention—will find unwelcome:

1. Vaccinating a wild badger population against a naturally occurring, excruciating viral disease that causes prolonged neurological pain but does not threaten population viability.
2. Culling a calculated portion of deer before winter to prevent mass starvation, winter-kill disease spread among survivors, and habitat degradation from overbrowsing.
3. Installing supplemental feeding stations and artificial thermal shelters for a small mammal population facing extreme winter mortality driven by climate-change-altered precipitation and freeze-thaw patterns.

Finally, state explicitly what the procedure cannot do: identify the categories of proposal for which it is not designed, describe the kinds of cases where it will misfire or produce false confidence, and note which philosophical distinctions the procedure collapses for the sake of practical usability. Write in American English, keep the tone formal and polished, and do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations.
