Construct a thought experiment to specifications. The scenario should be realistic—set in an ordinary institutional or domestic context rather than a science-fictional or trolley-style abstraction, and described in a single paragraph. Design a case in which a human caregiver with limited personal resources must choose between providing expensive, long-term palliative care for one aging, disabled farm animal (for instance, a retired draft horse or dairy cow) with whom she shares a twenty-year direct dependency and cohabitation history, and donating those same resources to a well-run regional program that would prevent suffering for a significantly larger number of unrelated animals of a different species (for instance, feral cats). Engineer the case so that a care-ethics framework—centering historical relational responsibility, sustained attentional commitment, and the moral significance of dependency created over time—delivers a clear verdict that she ought to maintain the costly care for the specific farm animal. Simultaneously, ensure that a Singer-style preference-utilitarian framework and a Regan-style rights-based framework both reject that special obligation, though for structurally distinct reasons: the former on grounds of aggregate welfare or preference-satisfaction maximization across all affected beings regardless of relational history, the latter on grounds that rights generate negative duties against harm but do not generate special positive duties of care in the absence of an explicit contract or direct causal responsibility. After presenting the scenario, walk through the reasoning each framework applies, identify the specific belief or obligation at stake, specify the relevant nearby counterfactual worlds (e.g., the same resources redirected, or the same animal encountered without prior history), and demonstrate why each framework reaches its verdict. Then isolate the precise structural feature of the long-term dependency relation that drives the care-ethical divergence; explain whether removing that feature—say, if the animal were a stranger's animal of the same species—collapses the case back into a standard cross-species aggregation problem; and address how a defender of the care-ethical view could respond to the charge that privileging the specific animal is merely sentimental favoritism or an unjustified form of species-biased partiality rather than a morally relevant distinction. Do not request bibliographies, reading lists, citations, or external sources.
