I'm working through Luis de Molina's Concordia (1588), specifically the sections introducing scientia media. The passage, reconstructed from the Latin argument, runs roughly as follows: Molina holds that between God's natural knowledge of all necessary truths and His free knowledge of what will actually occur, there lies a middle knowledge by which God knows, prior to any decree of His will, what every possible free creature would freely do in every possible set of circumstances. He insists that these counterfactuals of creaturely freedom are not necessary, since the creature retains the power to do otherwise, yet they are not contingent upon God's will, since God knows them without having decided to actualize the circumstances in question. He further claims that this knowledge is pre-volitional in the order of explanation rather than in time, and that it allows God to arrange circumstances so as to secure certain free outcomes without causally determining them.

My difficulty is in seeing how Molina avoids two opposing pitfalls. On one side, if the truth of these counterfactuals depends on the creature's free will in a way genuinely prior to God's knowledge, then the creature's will functions as an independent source of truth constraining God's creative options, threatening divine aseity. On the other side, if God knows these counterfactuals only in virtue of His understanding of the essences of creatures, then the knowledge collapses into natural knowledge and the freedom of the counterfactual becomes illusory—merely a modal consequence of essence rather than a genuinely open possibility.

My specific question concerns the explanatory priority Molina assigns here. Can he consistently maintain that the counterfactual is explained by the creature's free choice, while also holding that God's knowledge of it is explanatorily prior to any creative decree, without implicitly committing himself to a form of libertarian modal realism—namely, to the view that there are brute, ungrounded facts about what free agents would do across possible worlds? Or does his Thomistic framework ultimately force him to ground these truths in God's own intellect, thereby undermining the very independence he needs for middle knowledge? Please help me understand whether the passage resolves or fails to resolve this tension, paying particular attention to whether Molina's distinction between prior in explanation and prior in being can bear the weight he places on it, and whether his account requires him to accept counterfactual essences that exist independently of both God and the actual world.
