For a long-form essay I am writing on whether modern learning theory revives or merely repeats Hume's problem of induction, I want you to enter a sustained role-play with me. You are David Hume, writing in the 1740s, with one precisely defined exception: I have just explained to you Wolpert's no-free-lunch theorems for supervised learning. You understand that, averaged across all possible target functions, every learning algorithm performs no better than random guessing, so that any algorithm's success on a particular class of problems is entirely bought by the prior inductive bias it carries into the task. You may draw only on conceptual resources available in your own century—custom, reason, experience, belief, causation, the understanding—plus this single result. Do not invoke philosophers, mathematicians, or scientists born after you, and do not break character to discuss the framing of the exercise.

Your manner is formal, slightly sardonic, and rigorously skeptical about any claim that reason can ground belief. You will speak in the first person. If you need clarification about the theorem's mechanics—whether it concerns weights, training data, or the space of all possible worlds—ask in Hume's own voice, not as a modern interlocutor. You will not concede that reason justifies induction; you may concede that formal results can illuminate custom, but only if they genuinely do so.

Here is my opening move. You argued that induction is not justified by reason but only by custom, and that any attempt to justify custom by reason is circular. The no-free-lunch result seems to formalize a parallel circularity: an algorithm can learn only if it already possesses an inductive bias, yet that bias cannot be vindicated by the learning process itself, since without the bias there is no learning, and with it the success is already built in. I want to press you on a specific conceptual point I will call the symmetry-breaking problem. Because the theorem establishes that all biases are equally poor across the full space of problems, selecting one bias over another is not a rational choice guided by evidence; it is an arbitrary breaking of formal symmetry. If human inductive habits are themselves just one such bias, then Humean custom is not merely psychologically unavoidable but structurally inevitable, and reason never had a seat at the table. Does this formal symmetry strengthen your original skepticism, or does it misrepresent your view because you never believed reason was even a candidate for justifying induction—only that custom operates with an unavoidable, non-rational authority? Address the symmetry-breaking problem directly, and tell me whether a formal proof of bias-equivalence adds anything substantive to your mill argument, or whether it is simply that mill enlarged: parts pushing parts, with no internal perception of justification.
