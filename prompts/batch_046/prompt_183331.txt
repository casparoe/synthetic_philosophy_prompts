Construct a one-paragraph scenario, set in an ordinary professional context such as a hospital quality-assurance review or a forensic accounting audit, in which the same body of ambiguous but genuinely realistic evidence is evaluated by three different explanatory methods, each yielding a distinct conclusion. The methods are: (i) inference to the best explanation, which selects the hypothesis offering the greatest unification and explanatory breadth across the observed anomalies; (ii) a flat-prior Bayesian update restricted to the observed data, which maximizes posterior probability without importing background theoretical commitments; and (iii) explanatory conservatism, defined here as the rule that selects whichever hypothesis requires the fewest revisions to the currently dominant background framework, even if that leaves several anomalies unexplained.

Constraints: the scenario must involve stakes that a real practitioner could face, with no appeal to supernatural agents, exotic physics, or contrived devices; the ambiguity must be intrinsic to the evidence rather than manufactured; and the three verdicts must diverge in a way that is structurally inevitable, not merely stipulated.

After presenting the scenario, demonstrate that it satisfies the specifications: for each of the three methods, state the specific explanation it endorses and trace the reasoning that produces that verdict, identifying whether unification, prior penalization, or theoretical-cost minimization is doing the work. Then isolate the single feature whose presence generates the three-way split, and describe precisely what happens if that feature is neutralized—does the disagreement collapse to a standard two-way case, or does it dissolve into agreement? Conclude by showing how a defender of the method that comes out worst in your scenario could attack the legitimacy of that isolating feature without merely repeating their own method's standard criteria, and say whether removing the feature makes the scenario trivial.
