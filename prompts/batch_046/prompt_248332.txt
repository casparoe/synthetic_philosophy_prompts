Since discovering my great-grandfather’s unpublished correspondence from the 1890s, I have been forced to reckon with the possibility that my family was not merely a passive beneficiary of the Jim Crow order but an active participant in its commemorative architecture; this personal reckoning has led me to defend, at next month’s Comparative Memory Studies colloquium, the thesis that the American South should not be analyzed as a deficient or delayed case of Germany’s Vergangenheitsbewältigung, but rather as a distinct mnemonic regime that I term “hermeneutic capture,” in which perpetrator narratives are not suppressed but systematically reinterpreted as victimhood through decentralized, non-state commemorative practices—what I call “plural denial”—and that this structure aligns the South more closely with specific mechanisms in Japanese postwar victim-consciousness and Turkish state historiography than with the German model of coordinated moral working-through, even though the South’s lack of a centralized archival apparatus produces a comparative anomaly that challenges both Germanist frameworks and state-centric theories of denial. The audience will be interdisciplinary and skeptical: Germanists who treat Erinnerungskultur as a state-coordinated moral project and will resist analogies that ignore the Bundesrepublik’s institutional specificity; Japanese historians of rekishi ninshiki who will object that local commemorative pluralism in the South cannot be equated with Japan’s state-mediated ambiguity over war responsibility; Turkish sociologists of official historiography who will insist that denial requires archival centralization and therefore that “plural denial” is conceptually incoherent; and American historians of the post-Reconstruction South who will see the comparison as an imposition of foreign theoretical vocabulary on regional particularity and who may treat my family papers as anecdotal rather than structurally illustrative. Prepare me for the questions they will actually ask, ordered by how likely and how damaging they are: for each objection, phrase it in the vocabulary that specific audience member would use, indicate whether it has real force and should be conceded in part, provide a concise reply appropriate for the time available in a seminar Q&A, and flag the weakest point of that reply. Include the objection I am most hoping nobody raises—likely an empirical challenge that private family documents cannot ground a collective mnemonic theory—and specify what I should concede up front to maintain credibility. Most critically, identify the single reply that must land for the proposal to survive: my defense of using analogical comparison across Germany, Japan, Turkey, and the American South without collapsing the specific violence and institutional form of each case into a universal model of denial or remembrance. I want a speaker’s prep sheet, not a full defense of the thesis.
