I help run a small community mediation circle that handles non-criminal harms—broken promises, careless damage, betrayals of trust—where everyone usually agrees on what happened but not on what should happen next. Over the past year I have been thinking about karma not as reincarnation or cosmic force, but strictly as a theory of moral desert: the idea that what someone owes in repair should match the moral quality of what they did, where that quality depends on both their intention and what they actually understood about the consequences. I need a reusable decision procedure that my co-mediators and I—none of us lawyers or philosophers—can run in about ninety minutes with both parties present, to test whether a proposed repair falls inside, below, or above the range that is morally deserved. The deliverable should be a fixed-order sequence of questions or tests, with clear criteria for a sufficient answer at each stage, explicit stop-points where the procedure rejects a proposal, and honest flags marking where judgment replaces measurement rather than delivering a mechanical result. To prevent the framework from collapsing into pure subjectivity or a single “correct punishment,” please build it around an injected working concept—call it a desert interval—defining moral desert not as one point but as a bounded range with a floor (the minimum acknowledgment or restoration required) and a ceiling (the point where further burden stops being restorative and becomes destructive or self-abasing). Then apply the procedure step-by-step to three cases: first, a tenant who ignored a slow leak they had noticed but never reported, eventually flooding a downstairs apartment and causing serious property damage; second, someone who deliberately spread a private secret for social gain, causing deep non-material harm; and third, a case designed to yield an answer I will personally resist—perhaps where the wrongdoer offers an extravagant, self-punishing repair that exceeds the ceiling, or where a victim demands a burden that the framework shows is disproportionate—so that the procedure’s limits are visible. For each case, show exactly where the mediator must make a value call the framework cannot settle. Finally, state clearly what the procedure cannot do: whether it determines legal liability, whether it works when basic facts are disputed, whether it applies across relationships of radically unequal power, and whether it can ever override mercy when desert and compassion conflict. The procedure should require no citation of texts, no bibliographic knowledge, and no expertise beyond careful listening and consistent application of the tests.
