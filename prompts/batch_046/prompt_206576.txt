I work as a backend engineer for a multilateral development fund, and I recently joined an internal audit of the data pipeline that feeds our "Cultural Foundations of Growth" evaluation rubric. The rubric takes regional survey responses—dimensions like secular-rational orientation, individualism, and deferred gratification—and treats them as predictive inputs in a causal model that controls for institutional quality and resource endowment. At the same time, when a region scores low, the rubric triggers recommendations for "value-alignment" interventions—media partnerships, curriculum adjustments—treating the same survey dimensions as malleable outcomes that funding can reshape. A colleague mentioned Weber's thesis linking Protestant asceticism to the rise of capitalism, and I see a parallel vocabulary in our framework's baseline cluster, which is calibrated against nineteenth-century Protestant-majority European regions. But I have no formal philosophy training, and the tension confuses me: the framework uses culture to explain growth, yet also proposes to engineer that culture, without clarifying whether values are autonomous causes or products of material conditions.

The rubric's technical appendix is more specific: it measures "cultural distance" by aligning present-day regional responses with that historical Protestant-European baseline, and it identifies causal effects by treating historical missionary presence as an exogenous instrument for current value orientations. A "malleability score" then estimates how quickly survey responses shift after targeted campaigns, and the final funding recommendation combines the predicted growth coefficient with this engineering potential. The framework never addresses whether the values it isolates are genuinely independent of the economic structures they predict, or whether assigning them causal weights in a regression simply re-describes correlations that institutions or resources could explain. It also borrows Weber's language of ascetic self-discipline without adopting his multicausal caution that economic and religious factors interact rather than reduce to one another.

I am trying to understand what philosophical theory of culture, causation, and value explanation would make this practice coherent, if any. Does the rubric presuppose that cultural values can simultaneously serve as autonomous explanatory causes and as direct targets of policy intervention without contradiction? Must it assume that culture is causally prior to institutions, or is it compatible with materialist or institutionalist accounts that treat values as epiphenomenal? How does its instrumental use of historical missionary exposure sit with Weber's refusal to reduce capitalism to a single cultural driver? And if the implicit theory were replaced—say, by a view that only institutions or material conditions explain development—which elements of the rubric (the exogenous instrument, the malleability score, the Protestant-European baseline) would become philosophically indefensible? Please treat the strongest counter-argument seriously: that the framework may not be committed to any consistent theory at all, but is merely an incoherent combination of statistical correlations and normative aspirations.
