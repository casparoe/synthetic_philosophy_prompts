I'm teaching a grad seminar on collective moral psychology — about 14 students, one 80-minute block — and we've been wrestling with Margalit's ethics of memory alongside Rieff's skepticism about therapeutic culture's demand that we never let anything go. I want them to feel the structural tension, not just talk about it. So design a classroom simulation, kind of a role-play with chips, that forces them into a 'mnemonic triage' situation.

Set it up like this: there's a small community with exactly ten 'attention slots' for preserving detailed records of past wrongs. Every student gets a role — some are 'custodians' (they earn reputation chips when a slot is filled with precise testimony, but lose chips if the archive overflows), some are 'descendants' (they hold claim-tokens that only pay out if thier specific record stays public), and some are 'future citizens' who inherit whatever remains after round one and must reach unanimous agreement on a collective decision using only that inherited material. The twist is that preserving every truthful record is morally required — that's the Margalit side — but every filled slot generates social-friction points that make the future citizens' task literally impossible past a certain threshold. Meanwhile, clearing slots improves solidarity but erases claim-tokens. Give them real but small stakes: each person wagers one of thier three course-participation chips, and the group outcome determines whether they keep them or pool them.

The rules should make the crisis happen mechanically. Round one they file or seal; round two half are replaced by 'children' who only see the archive; round three the children vote. They should hit the wall — probably around minute 35 — where adding one more victim's accurate testimony breaks coordination among the inheritors. Only after they've felt that do we name it.

Then give me the debrief: map the overflow moment onto Margalit's idea that shared memory is a moral bond to the dead, map the sealing incentives onto Rieff's point that forgetting can be a form of social health, and map the children's ignorance onto intergenerational justice. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong — I bet people try to bargain outside the rules, or descendants refuse to play the children — and how to handle it. And be honest: this can't reproduce the phenomenological weight of remembering as an obligation that doesn't depend on social utility, so I'll need to supply that in the closing discussion. Definately include which feature of the idea the chips just can't capture.
