I'm working on this for my diss... well, a side project in value theory, and I'm trying to figure out what I actually think about tranqulism. The basic idea is that only the absence of craving and suffering matters — everything else is just window dressing. But my gut verdicts on cases are all over the place, and I want to know if there's a principle behind them or if I just have to drop one.

Here are my takes:

1. A meditator in deep jhana with zero craving, zero suffering, and basically no self-model running. Verdict: perfect state, can't get better.

2. Someone on a hypothetical drug that wipes out all affective valence — no suffering, no craving, but also no joy, no curiosity, no sense of achievement. Verdict: still perfect, maybe even more stable than case 1 because there's no risk of backsliding.

3. A person who lives through intense intellectual discovery, lots of frustration and craving for answers, occasional suffering, but eventually reaches a permanent tranquil state where all questions dissolve. Verdict: the discovery part was net negative (all that cravving and suffering for nothing), but the final state is perfect. The journey doesn't add value.

4. A being that was never conscious — like a very complex rock — with no suffering and no craving because it has no mental states at all. Verdict: not perfect, because there's nothing there to have the absence. Tranquilism applies only to subjects that could suffer.

5. Someone who is in a tranquil state but only because they're in a perfectly engineered simulation that suppresses all negative affect. They don't know they're simulated, and they have no craving to know. Verdict: still perfect. The deception doesn't matter.

6. A person who has eliminated suffering and craving but retains a kind of 'background hum' — not painful, not craved-against, just a neutral emptiness they describe as 'like being a very satisfied rock that can think'. Verdict: this is where I wobble. Sometimes I think it's fine, sometimes I think there's a hidden deficit — maybe the absence of positive orientation matters after all?

7. Someone who voluntarily accepts intense suffering now to guarantee permanent tranquil absence for themselves and others. Verdict: the suffering is bad, but if the result is genuinely permanent and widespread, the act is good? Or is the present suffering just a cost with no redeeming feature except producing a state that was going to happen anyway?

8. A philospher who argues that 'craving' should be defined narrowly — only explicit desire — so that unconscious drives don't count. On that definition, someone with unconscious resentment that fuels their work but never surfaces as felt suffering is in a perfect tranquil state. Verdict: I reject that definition; unconscious drives that generate suffering-potentials should count even if suppressed.

I'm especially stuck on case 6 vs case 2. Both are flat, but in 6 there's this weird residual 'something' — not suffering, not craving, but not quite the clean absence I think tranquilism celebrates. Is there a principle that distinguishes 'pure absence' from 'absence with residue'? Or should I bite the bullet and say 6 is perfect too, which feels wrong?

Also, what does this all say about a case I haven't listed: a community that eliminates all suffering and craving through genetic engineering, but in doing so eliminates the capacity for moral agency? Is that a tranquilist utopia or a loss?

Please tell me what principle, if any, gets all eight (or tells me which to drop), and be honest about the tension between 2 and 6. I don't need citations or reading lists — just work with the cases, and say what your principle predicts for the genetic-engineering scenario.
