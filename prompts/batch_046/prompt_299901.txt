I'm working up a seminar response for a philosophy of biology workshop, and I've constructed what I take to be a reductio of the common move that evolutionary continuity forces us to take insect sentience seriously at scale. The audience will include comparative cognition researchers who accept that hymenoptera and diptera have some form of nociception, plus normative ethicists skeptical of speciesism, so I want to be ready for them to defend the conclusion I'm treating as absurd. Here is the argument: (1) If evolutionary continuity is real, then there is a non-zero, non-negligible probability p that any given insect possesses some minimal form of sentience, however simple; (2) with roughly 10^19 insects alive at any moment, the expected number of sentient insects dwarfs the expected number of sentient humans by orders of magnitude, even if p is small; (3) anti-speciesism requires that sentient beings count in moral calculus regardless of phylogenetic distance, so each expected sentient insect carries at least some non-zero moral weight; (4) conventional agriculture—including mechanized planting and harvesting, routine pesticide application, and soil disturbance—causes direct or indirect mortality to trillions of insects each year; (5) if expected moral harm aggregates linearly across individuals, then the total expected harm inflicted on insects by global agriculture vastly exceeds all human suffering and death combined; (6) therefore, anyone who accepts evolutionary continuity and rejects speciesism must regard conventional agriculture as among the gravest systematic moral catastrophes, demanding immediate abolition or radical restructuring that prioritizes insect welfare over human food security; (7) but that conclusion is wildly implausible—no credible moral theory requires us to halt farming to save beetles, and it collapses the distinction between intentional killing and incidental, replaceable harm; (8) so either p is effectively zero (denying evolutionary continuity), or linear aggregation of expected harm is illegitimate, or we must bite the bullet on agricultural abolition. The argument feels suspicious because the conclusion is absurd, yet premises (1) through (4) each look independently defensible. Where does it actually break—if anywhere? I would prefer to preserve (1) and (3), since those capture my evolutionary and anti-speciesist commitments, and I am willing to surrender linear aggregation or the move from aggregate harm to direct individual obligation, but I want to know exactly what that surrender costs me. Please diagnose the weakest step, explain why it seemed plausible, and identify the specific consideration—whether about risk versus actualized harm, the replaceability of short-lived populations, the non-identity of future insects, or the structure of moral aggregation across non-interacting agents—that would most change your assessment of where the failure lies. Also, please be explicit about where your own reasoning is softest or most likely to depend on a contested assumption about moral mathematics.
