Write the desiderata that any adequate transcendental argument for the authority of domain-specific rational norms—whether epistemic, moral, or practical—must satisfy, where adequacy is measured by the argument's ability to justify those norms without collapsing into either circular reasoning or mere empirical description. Specify, for each requirement, the case or puzzle that motivates it: how the argument must address global skeptical scenarios without presupposing the very norms it seeks to establish; how it must draw and defend a distinction between genuinely transcendental conditions of possibility and contingent psychological or sociological regularities; how it must solve the scope problem—whether its conclusion licenses particular judgments or only the overarching framework within which judgment operates; how it must remain consistent with fallibilism and the phenomenon of persistent rational disagreement; and how it must navigate the move from descriptive premises about what thinking or acting requires to normative conclusions about what one ought to think or do, without committing a category error in that transition. Identify which pairs of these desiderata appear jointly unsatisfiable, and mark which are non-negotiable versus which may be relaxed if necessary. Finally, score three strategies briefly against your list: a Strawsonian descriptive-metaphysical approach, an Apelian pragmatic-transcendental approach, and a hypothetical localized approach restricted to norms internal to a single domain—indicating for each where it succeeds and where it must sacrifice one desideratum to preserve another.
