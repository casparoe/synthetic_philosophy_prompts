Consider the position I will call asymmetric co-agency in historical explanation. It holds that non-human environmental forces—pathogenic microbes, volcanic aerosol events, long-duration climate shifts—ought not to be treated as mere background conditions for human history. Rather, they possess structural efficacy: a capacity to actively restructure the field of possible human actions (for example, by rendering specific agricultural practices impossible or by reshaping labor organization through altered immunological landscapes) without requiring consciousness, intention, or representation. Human collectives retain reflexive intentionality, but the causal partnership is genuinely joint. To explain the collapse of a particular agrarian regime, on this view, one must attribute to a volcanic winter not only causal influence but a form of negative agency—the active elimination of options—rather than treating it as an inert trigger to which humans merely reacted. The framework aims to steer between traditional environmental determinism, which reduces human choice to climatic or pathogenic response, and anthropocentric exceptionalism, which treats nature as purely passive. What would genuinely count against this view? I want an honest map of its exposure. Which conceptual distinctions—say, between causal efficacy and agency—would it have to survive, and does it risk collapsing them into vacuity? What historical patterns or institutional responses would show that these forces were never more than constraining conditions that human collectives absorbed or redirected? Which predictions or commitments distinguish it from rival frameworks, and are there observations that would be decisive rather than merely awkward? Finally, has the position been qualified into unfalsifiability, and if so, is that a defect for a framework of this kind?
