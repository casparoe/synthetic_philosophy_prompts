Read William Golding’s Lord of the Flies through the interpretive debates that frame the French Revolution: whether revolutionary violence is structurally inevitable (the thesis that revolutions devour their children), driven by class formation and material interest, or contingent on particular ideological commitments and accidental circumstances. Focus on the boys’ overthrow of Ralph and ask whether Golding depicts it as a change in the form of sovereignty itself or merely as a change in the personnel who hold it—does the conch represent an institutional authority that can survive a transfer of power, or is its fragility built into the kind of sovereignty it symbolizes? Treat Piggy’s death and Simon’s killing as analogues to the Thermidorian elimination of the revolution’s own: are they structurally required by the new hunter-sovereignty, or contingent on Ralph’s failure to secure symbolic authority and on the misidentification of the beast? Point to specific scenes—the conch’s destruction, the hunt’s ritualization, the rescue’s ironic reversal—and say where the novel resists the Marxist class-struggle reading (the hunters lack a coherent material interest distinct from the rationalists), where it resists pure contingency (the island’s conditions seem to make some form of domination likely), and where it complicates both by making the boys’ ideological commitments to the beast and to rescue inseparable from their material survival. End by saying what Golding’s dramatization adds to the philosophical question of whether revolutions necessarily turn on themselves—something that historical argument about 1789 and its aftermath alone does not capture.
