I'm writing a long-form essay on the rhetoric of "Western values" in contemporary institutional discourse—not merely in electoral politics, but in university accreditation standards, immigration integration courses, and foreign-aid conditionality clauses. I keep noticing that the phrase operates as what I'd call a normative placeholder: it seems to do substantial argumentative work (justifying exclusion, demanding assimilation, or defending liberal institutions) without anyone specifying whether it denotes a historically contingent cluster, a philosophically coherent axiological core, or a strategic branding device. Before I settle on one reconstruction, I want to understand what philosophical commitments this institutional practice actually presupposes.

Specifically, when a state conditions visa extensions on adherence to "Western values," or when an accreditation body audits a curriculum for its alignment with them, what view of value-coherence must be in the background? Does the practice presuppose that there is a unified set—say, individual autonomy combined with secular reason and democratic legitimacy—that these institutions happen to share, or is it compatible with a much thinner, family-resemblance view in which "Western" is purely geographic and "values" are whatever that tradition has contingently endorsed? Further, does the practice quietly rule out anti-universalist or pluralist meta-ethical theories—for instance, views that treat liberal democracy as one formation among many—or can it be rendered consistent with them by interpreting "Western" as purely descriptive? I also want to know whether the practice is philosophically underdetermined: could the same rules be justified by a realist theory of cultural identity, a contractarian theory of social cohesion, or even a purely strategic theory of soft power, without any deep commitment to value-coherence at all?

Can you reconstruct the implicit philosophical theory, or competing theories, behind this institutional use of the phrase? Distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what merely fits it well. Then defend your answer to this further question: if we replaced the underlying view with a pluralist, geographically neutral framework—one that rejects the idea that values come in civilizational clusters—what specific institutional mechanisms (integration exams, curriculum audits, aid-conditionality clauses) would have to change, and which could remain intact with only rhetorical adjustment? I'm less interested in a survey of positions than in a defended agenda for which philosophical crux actually carries the weight in these policies.
