In climate ethics, present generations are frequently cast as trustees holding the Earth in trust for those not yet born: natural capital is the trust property, future people are the beneficiaries, and depleting it is analogised to a trustee embezzling from an account the beneficiary is too young to defend. This framing is regularly invoked to justify stringent preservation duties and to reject discounting future welfare as morally equivalent to theft. Against it, some critics propose an alternative analogy: intergenerational relations are less like fiduciary custody and more like co-authorship of an unfinished manuscript, in which earlier writers establish plot and vocabulary but later authors retain full editorial authority—including the right to reject earlier chapters—so that holding earlier writers strictly liable for every narrative outcome misrepresents the autonomy of those who come after. Audit both analogies systematically. For each, provide an explicit correspondence table (trustee / beneficiary / trust property / depletion versus co-author / earlier-later author / manuscript / revision rights), then identify precisely where the mapping fractures: whether future people can be said to exist as claimants with property rights; whether the climate system is the kind of asset that can be held intact; whether later generations can genuinely “rewrite” climate damage rather than merely inherit it; and what normative assumptions about reversibility, the value of the whole versus its parts, and the legitimacy of risk each analogy quietly imports. Separate the disanalogies that undermine the intended normative conclusion from those that are merely decorative. Finally, assess whether either analogy genuinely supports its conclusion—strict fiduciary preservation on one side, or relaxed obligations on the other—or whether the conclusion was being carried by the analogical machinery all along. If both analogies collapse under scrutiny, sketch what a more adequate analogical framework might need to include.
