In my philosophy of religion seminar we have been studying William James’s essay on the will to believe alongside W. K. Clifford’s essay on the ethics of belief. During a discussion of James’s claim that we sometimes face options that are live, forced, and momentous—options that, by their nature, cannot be settled on purely intellectual grounds—a classmate invoked Clifford’s familiar maxim, paraphrased in our course materials as the claim that it is wrong always and everywhere for anyone to believe anything upon insufficient evidence, in order to dismiss James’s defense of passional belief as automatically illegitimate. I am trying to determine exactly what work that slogan can do. Could you lay out the distinct precise theses that Clifford’s maxim might be expressing? For each reading, specify what kind of wrongness is involved (moral, epistemic, prudential, or some combination), what counts as “sufficient” evidence and who judges it, whether belief is treated as a voluntary act to which obligations apply, whether the universal scope genuinely permits no exceptions for practical urgency or existential risk, and how the reading interacts with James’s conditions for a genuine option. For each thesis, indicate whether it is trivial, substantive and defensible, false, or plainly incompatible with James’s view, and say specifically which readings would actually license my classmate’s rejection of James’s examples and which would leave James’s position intact. Present the analysis as a menu of interpretations rather than selecting a single one.
