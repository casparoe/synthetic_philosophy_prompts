hey so im drafting a short essay comparing Browning's study of the police battalion with Goldhagen's argument about ordinary germans and the holocaust. my central claim is that both miss a mediating mechanism: the way the killings were organized bureaucratically didn't just distance people from harm but actively reshaped their moral self-image. i call it procedural self-effacement — the perpetrators came to treat their own moral agency as delegated upward through command and downward through technical division of labor, so they saw themselves less as killers and more as maintainence workers in a system.

against Browning i say situation matters but it's not mainly peer pressure or fear of isolation; it's the fragmentation of the task into loading, guarding, shooting, collecting that made refusal feel like breaking a machine rather than saving a life. against Goldhagen i say culture made the men available but it didn't have to be specifically eliminationist antisemitism; any strong in-group loyalty plus procedural fragmentation could have sustained it, though in this case the antisemitism supplied the content.

then i add this weirder idea, reverse moral distancing. usually we think physical closeness makes killing harder, but in Battalion 101 the intimate violence of face-to-face shooting actually forced them to adopt the procedural frame faster — the closer the blood, the more you need to convince yourself you're just repairing equipment. i think this explains the alcohol use and why some broke down while others hardened.
