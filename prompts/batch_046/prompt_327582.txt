As a novelist working on a historical philosophical fiction, I am trying to grasp a difficult passage in David Hume's A Treatise of Human Nature, published in 1739-40. In Book II, Part III, Section III, Hume argues that reason is inert in itself and can never by itself produce or prevent an action, and he illustrates the point by observing that it is not contrary to reason to prefer the destruction of the whole world to the scratching of one's finger. He further distinguishes between violent passions and calm passions, noting that the latter are often mistaken for rational judgments precisely because they lack the agitation of the former.

My difficulty is not merely textual but conceptual, and it bears directly on my protagonist: a woman who believes she is acting from reasons that originate entirely outside her own psychological history—normative demands that she experiences as authoritative yet that do not trace back to any prior desire or sentiment. I want to know whether Hume's framework can accommodate reasons that are structurally external to an agent's existing passions, objective obligations that do not arise from within her motivational set, while still being experienced by her as genuinely her own, or whether Hume's account necessarily dissolves all apparently external reasons into hidden internal passions, perhaps calm ones disguised as objective demands.

Could you explain the mechanics of Hume's distinction between reason and passion in this section, clarify how he thinks reason can serve but never command the will, and then address whether there is any room in his psychology for a motivation that is externally constituted in its origin yet internally felt in its phenomenology? I am not asking for a reading list or for citations to secondary literature; I need only a careful unpacking of the argument as it stands, applied to this specific conceptual tension.
