I've been digging back into that old philophy-of-mind spat about whether dreams actually count as experiences — you know, the one where Norman Malcolm argued that dreaming can't be an experience proper because there's no behavioral criteria for being asleep and having it, and Dennett fired back with that cassette theory idea where the brain basically writes the story only when you wake up. I cover science and ideas for a living, and I was chatting with some researchers after a panel last week, and it struck me that this debate is totally frozen. Everyone still rehearses the same two positions: Malcolm tying experience to reportability in the moment, Dennett saying unreliable recall doesn't mean nothing happened.

But I think there might be a hidden layer neither side touches. There's this notion — maybe pulled from more recent sleep work, maybe just my own speculation — that REM involves an 'offline self-model' that lacks the indexical 'I-am-here-now' structure we take for granted in waking life. So maybe Malcolm and Dennett aren't even fighting about the same thing: Malcolm is asking whether there's a verifiable occurrent state, Dennett is asking whether there's a retrospective narrative that can be true, and both miss thier metaphysical premise that an experience needs a present subject at all. Is the stall because 'experience' is ambiguous between occurrent phenomenology and reconstructed memory? Or is it a deeper disagreement about what counts as evidence for consciousness? And if it's the latter, is there actually anything — a neuroimaging result, a formal distinction, a thought experiment — that could move it? Or are we just staring at bedrock intuitions about being 'there' when something happens? I don't want a winner; I want the diagnosis, and I want to know what progress would even look like, or if we're just not going to recieve one.
