In my philosophy of biology seminar we have spent several weeks examining the units-of-selection debate—whether genes, individual organisms, or multi-level groups ought to be regarded as the primary targets of natural selection. Rather than asking you to adjudicate between gene-centered and group-selectionist frameworks, I want a structural diagnosis of why this dispute has persisted for decades despite broad consensus on the basic mechanisms of inheritance, variation, and differential reproduction. My working hypothesis is that the stalemate is not primarily empirical but conceptual: the competing sides appear to operate with different success conditions for evolutionary explanation, one treating selection as a statistical filter that tracks replicator frequencies, the other treating it as a process that requires the stabilization of phenotypic outcomes through developmental interaction and niche construction. Is that diagnosis accurate, or is there a hidden empirical disagreement—perhaps concerning the prevalence of horizontal gene transfer, the magnitude of group-level variance in fitness, or the evolutionary significance of epigenetic inheritance—doing the substantive work beneath the conceptual rhetoric?

To test whether the disagreement can be reframed rather than resolved, consider a distinction that is not usually foregrounded in standard treatments of the debate: that between reproductive fidelity, understood as the capacity of an entity to generate high-fidelity copies across generations, and developmental integration, understood as the capacity of an entity to buffer phenotypic outcomes against environmental perturbation through sustained interaction with its surroundings and with other organisms. Does applying this distinction dissolve the debate by revealing that the sides are measuring orthogonal dimensions of evolutionary dynamics, or does it merely relocate the disagreement to which dimension ought to count as selection proper? Finally, given this diagnosis, what kind of intervention—an experimental result, a formal population-genetic model, or a reconceptualization of what an evolutionary explanation must deliver—would actually move the dispute forward, or should we conclude that the question is ill-posed and best abandoned in favor of a more precise set of sub-questions?
