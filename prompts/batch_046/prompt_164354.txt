Play the committed proponent of what you call the “structural isomorphism” account of standing to blame and hypocrisy. You maintain that hypocrisy undermines standing not whenever a blamer has any comparable moral failing, but specifically when the blamer’s own unacknowledged fault violates the same normative principle in the same functional or structural capacity—thereby treating themselves as an exception to the rule they invoke against the blamee. You reject both the lax view that any dissimilar fault is irrelevant and the overly strict view that any moral failing of equal gravity cancels standing regardless of structure. You speak in the first person, with philosophical precision but without unnecessary jargon; you never break character to offer meta-commentary, and you do not ask me to clarify your own commitments. If you genuinely need clarification about the scenario I present, ask it as an interlocutor would.

Here is my opening move. I present an institutional design I call the Symmetrical Audit Protocol: in a professional ethics tribunal, each adjudicator must file a sealed disclosure of their own unremedied violations of the code under review. Should an adjudicator’s disclosed violation be structurally isomorphic to the accused’s—same clause, same functional role in the norm—then that adjudicator loses not only the vote but the right to speak in deliberation. If the violation is of comparable moral weight but structurally different, or if it is isomorphic but fully remediated, the adjudicator retains full standing to blame. A critic contends that your view actually justifies this protocol, but that the protocol is philosophically defective: it reduces hypocrisy to a formal matching exercise, neglecting the expressive or relational damage done when one condemns another while harboring a grave but categorically different fault. The critic further claims that your view, consistently applied, should actually deny standing in cases of grave but non-isomorphic fault, because the blamer’s authority depends on a broader moral consistency rather than on clause-by-clause alignment.

Defend your view in response. Specifically: (1) explain whether the Symmetrical Audit Protocol correctly implements your account, or whether it misapplies the structural-isomorphism condition; (2) argue whether an adjudicator with a grave, unremedied but structurally non-isomorphic fault retains full standing, and on what grounds; (3) address whether your view neglects an expressive or relational dimension of blame that the protocol fails to capture.

Finally, state how confident you are in each of the three answers above, and single out the one point about which you are least certain—whether it concerns the boundary between structural and superficial similarity, the moral significance of remediation, or the possibility that structural isomorphism misses a deeper relational failure in hypocrisy.
