I'm currently working through the philosophical literature on individual obligations in large-scale collective harms—climate emissions, industrial meat consumption, and electoral participation—centered on what is usually called the inefficacy objection: because any single agent's contribution is causally redundant within the aggregate, it seems there can be no moral reason for that agent to change behavior. The exchange has become remarkably stable over the past several decades. Consequentialists reply with expected-value reasoning, probabilistic thresholds, and supply-chain elasticity; Kantians and virtue theorists invoke complicity, participation in unjust structures, and the corruption of character; yet each reply is met with a rehearsed rebuttal, and the taxonomy of positions—threshold, overdetermination, collective-duty—feels complete without anyone being persuaded. I have begun to suspect that the stalemate is not ethical but structural: that philosophers are implicitly operating with incompatible formal models of collective causation—treating harm either as a linear aggregate of individual inputs, as a coordination game with multiple equilibria, or as a network phenomenon with non-local dependencies—and that disagreements about "making a difference" are really disagreements about which causal architecture is appropriate, disguised as normative debate.

Is this diagnosis correct? More precisely, is the stall a substantive disagreement about facts (for instance, the actual elasticity of demand in specific markets), a verbal disagreement about what "efficacy" and "complicity" should cover across these architectures, or a case of two groups answering different questions—one about causal contribution, one about expressive or symbolic standing—under the shared heading of individual obligation? Drawing on what each side actually says about redundancy and participation, tell me what kind of result or distinction—a formal model, an empirical measurement, a conceptual separation between causal and normative substitutability—would count as genuine progress here, or argue that no progress should be expected because the underlying question conflates causal structure with moral standing in an irreparable way.
