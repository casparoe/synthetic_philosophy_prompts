Read Ridley Scott's Blade Runner through Daniel Dennett's framework of real patterns and the three stances—physical, design, and intentional. Concentrate on the Voight-Kampff test and on Rachael's implanted memories: the test treats replicants as design-stance artifacts engineered for labor, yet demands an intentional-stance response (empathy, moral standing), while Rachael's first-person experience of those memories seems to straddle the two. I want you to ask whether the empathy the test detects is a real pattern anchored in the replicants' physical architecture, or only a predictive stencil—projected by human testers, pragmatically successful, but tracking nothing robust at the substrate level. Where does the film itself stand? Does Roy Batty's final monologue vindicate an intentional stance that the design stance cannot capture, or does it warn us against mistaking a high-fidelity engineered pattern for genuine intentionality? Offer a reading faithful in both directions—one that does not reduce the film to an illustration of Dennett, nor distort Dennett to fit the plot—and conclude by saying what the film adds to the philosophical debate that argument alone cannot, particularly on whether a being can be morally significant even if its patterns are, in the end, designed.
