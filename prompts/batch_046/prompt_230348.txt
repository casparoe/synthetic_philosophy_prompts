I'm trying to determine whether accepting what others tell me is ever justified on its own, or only ever through background reasoning about reliability. Here are my gut judgments.

A stranger warns me the bridge ahead is out; I turn back immediately, with no knowledge of her track record. That feels justified. My physician explains a diagnosis using terms I don't grasp; I accept it based on institutional credentials I barely understand. Also justified, though the mechanism feels different from the stranger case. I read in an encyclopedia that a town was founded in 1847; I accept it without investigating the author's sources—justified, but weaker.

Now the harder ones. A speech-synthesis system with no sensors, trained only on weather sentences, outputs "it will rain"; I happen to know it's true, but I reject the claim, since there's no speaker behind it with relevant access. Meanwhile, a friend with a known tendency to exaggerate tells me she saw a car accident; despite having general reason to distrust her and no specific corroboration, I find myself believing her, yet that feels like I'm being irrational. Then there's the chain: ten strangers independently tell me a museum closes at five, and I accept it; if I later learn they all heard it from the same unreliable tour guide, my belief should collapse—but if I never learn about the shared source, was my original acceptance still justified?

I'm also torn on a pair. A peer-reviewed historian corrects a well-known battle date, offering reasoning I can partly follow, and I accept the revision. An anonymous forum user offers the exact same correction with the same visible reasoning, and I reject it. Is prestige or institutional vetting doing hidden work, or is there a principled distinction?

What principle distinguishes the cases where testimony functions as a basic source from those where it requires prior inductive support? Does anti-reductionism capture the passerby and physician without licensing the synthetic-voice rejection or the exaggerated-friend belief? Or does reductionism explain the synthetic-voice case but fail for the physician? State the principle precisely, show which clause governs each of my cases, predict the verdict for a warning uttered by a parrot conditioned to say "fire" only in the presence of smoke, and be honest about which of my judgments—especially the synthetic-voice rejection, the exaggerated-friend belief, or the forum-versus-historian split—the principle must overturn.
