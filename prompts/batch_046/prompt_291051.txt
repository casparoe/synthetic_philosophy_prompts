Consider the regulatory asymmetry that prevails across most American states and in several comparable liberal democracies: parents who choose to homeschool generally need only file a one-time notification of intent, face no requirement to submit a curriculum for approval, need not employ state-certified teachers, and are rarely subject to standardized assessments of student progress, whereas private schools—even those operating with explicit religious or pedagogical autonomy—must ordinarily satisfy accreditation benchmarks, maintain certified staff ratios, and submit to periodic state evaluation. The family unit is thus treated as a self-certifying educational institution that requires no external scaffolding, while any collective educational body is presumed incompetent unless embedded within state-recognized structures of oversight, credentialing, and accountability.

What philosophical commitments about embedded agency does this pattern quietly presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory: does it treat parental authority as a pre-political sphere of agency that exists prior to and independently of social institutions, so that embedding in bureaucratic structures can only corrupt or constrain authentic development? Or does it assume, conversely, that agency is genuinely constituted only through collective institutional practices, but that the family is itself a natural institution whose internal norms already provide sufficient embedding, whereas private schools are artificial associations lacking such intrinsic normative structure? In your reconstruction, distinguish carefully what the practice strictly requires from what merely fits it well—could the asymmetry be justified instead by considerations of tractability, political power, or historical path-dependence without committing to any deep view of how agency is formed? Also say whether the practice is philosophically underdetermined, so that rival conceptions of embedded agency might license the very same regulatory split for different reasons.

Finally, indicate what a school-choice policy built on a genuinely rival conception—say, one holding that all human agency is equally embedded in and enabled by external practices of accountability and recognition, or one holding that autonomy requires active institutional mediation rather than insulation from it—would have to change in its treatment of homeschooling versus private schooling. Would such a view collapse the distinction, subject both to identical oversight, or perhaps reverse the asymmetry? And if the current practice is indeed anchored in a particular theory of embedded agency, is that theory defensible on its own terms, or does it depend on treating the family as an exception to the social constitution of the self?
