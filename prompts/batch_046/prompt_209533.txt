I'm drafting a chapter on whether genuine moral dilemmas are actually possible and I cooked up this argument that feels like it proves too much but I can't find the crack. (1) A real dilemma means O(A) and O(B) are both all-things-considered with A and B incompatble. (2) If O(A) holds, then every reason against A is already defeated. (3) By parity, O(B) means every reason for A is defeated. (4) So the same reasons are both defeated and not defeated, violating the asymetry of defeat. (5) So genuine dilemmas don't occure—what looks like one is just not knowing which reason wins. (6) But apply this to collective agency: if a legislature faces an agent-neutral duty to protect rights and an agent-relative duty to represent voters, and both genuinely conflict, (4) says one was never binding, which makes constitutional design for unavoidable conflict impossible. (7) That definately seems wrong—institutions are built expecting this. I want to keep (1) and (4) but I need to know the cost. Is the error a scope shift between individual and collective practical reason, or does defeat not work symmetrically? Where should I give ground?
