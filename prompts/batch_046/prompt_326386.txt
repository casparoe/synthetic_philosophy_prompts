Draft the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between deontic exclusivists and reason-responsive pluralists on the conditions for moral worth. The exclusivists hold that an action has moral worth only if the agent is motivated by respect for duty as such, with non-deontic motives either disqualifying worth or being normatively inert. The pluralists hold that moral worth requires acting for the right reasons—those that justify the action—but deny that there is a single canonical psychological mode, such as duty, compassion, or practical wisdom, through which those reasons must be accessed; correct normative responsiveness, not the specific motivational label, determines worth.

Deliver the statement in three parts. First, a numbered list of claims both sides can sign: about the distinction between acting in accordance with duty and acting from duty; about the existence of overdetermined cases in which an agent has both deontic and affective motives; about the irrelevance of mere behavioral conformity without appropriate motivation; and about the need for a criterion that distinguishes lucky conformity from genuinely worthy action. Second, a disagreements table with three columns—issue, exclusivist formulation, pluralist formulation—covering at least: whether affective motives contaminate worth when present alongside duty; whether an agent who saves a life out of spontaneous compassion but lacks any concept of moral obligation has acted with full moral worth (the silent benefactor case); whether motivational overdetermination should be treated as a binary threshold or as a scalar contribution to worth; and whether an agent must be able to articulate her reason under a deontic description (a transparency condition) for that reason to ground worth. Each formulation should be worded so the corresponding camp accepts it as stating its own view precisely.

Third, identify two cruxes—sub-questions such that resolving them would move at least one side. For each crux, state the conceptual test or thought-experiment variant both sides agree is relevant, and record in advance what result each camp predicts. One crux should concern the silent benefactor; the other should concern whether transparent deontic articulation is necessary for worth, using a variant in which an agent acts correctly but can cite only non-moral descriptions of her motive. If any residual disagreement is purely verbal—about whether acting for the right reason requires conceptual availability of that reason—say so explicitly and explain why the wording difference does not change the substantive judgment. Keep the statement formal, concise, and self-contained; do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations.
