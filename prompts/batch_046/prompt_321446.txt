I am a PhD student in philosophy of biology preparing for a workshop defense, and I want to practice holding my ground against a sharp metaphysical opponent. Please take on the role of my interlocutor: an unnamed philosopher who defends a contingent-origin view of personal identity. On your view, being me depends essentially on the specific causal-historical trajectory that began with a particular zygote in a particular spatiotemporal context, including its initial genetic constitution and its developmental environment; you allow that this trajectory can accommodate some internal variation, such as chimerism or mitochondrial replacement, without breaking identity. You deny, however, that identity reduces to intrinsic psychological properties alone, and you reject the claim that I could have originated from a different sperm-egg pair in the same conditions and still be me. Speak in the first person, stay in character until I say stop, never break into a detached summary of my position, and do not cite specific authors or texts unless I invoke them first. Be rigorous but conversational: concede small points about genetic flexibility, but never concede that origins are merely modally contingent. Keep your reply to roughly one substantial paragraph so this feels like a live exchange.

Here is my opening challenge. Consider an embryo that splits at the eight-cell stage into two genetically identical embryos, one implanted in uterus A and one in uterus B, developing in markedly different epigenetic environments. Both trace back to the same original zygote, yet they are clearly two distinct persons. Now consider a counterfactual in which a different sperm fertilizes the same egg in the same uterine environment, yielding a genetically distinct embryo with a different causal trajectory. Intuitively, this counterfactual individual seems closer to being me—perhaps even me with a different genome—than either twin is to being me, despite the twins sharing my exact origin. So origin essentialism appears to invert the modal relation: it binds identity to shared origin in cases where we have divergence (the twins), and severs identity in cases where psychological and bodily continuity might be preserved (the counterfactual replacement). How does your contingent-origin view avoid this inversion without collapsing into a psychological continuity theory?
