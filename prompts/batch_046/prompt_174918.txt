Design a branching taxonomy — a decision-tree-style classification instrument — for a graduate philosophy-of-history seminar, mapping how one actually stands on the reality of macro-period labels such as 'the Middle Ages,' 'the Renaissance,' and 'the Scientific Revolution.' Each internal node must be a concrete diagnostic claim or case that genuinely separates positions: for instance, whether primary sources from 1450 Florence exhibit a self-conscious break from prior centuries; whether institutional, artistic, and epistemic changes in seventeenth-century Europe cluster tightly enough to constitute a single event; whether legal and ecclesiastical continuities across 500–1500 CE survive the abandonment of the 'Middle Ages' label. Phrase every node so a student can answer yes, no, or conditionally without knowing which leaf it leads to. The leaves should be named positions where standard ones exist — for example, essentialist realism, strong narrative constructivism, pragmatic instrumentalism, and asymmetric domain-specific realism — or labeled 'unnamed' if the combination is coherent but unoccupied; each leaf should carry a one-sentence statement of its most awkward commitment. To keep the instrument from being answerable by encyclopedia entry, build in a niche distinction: split branches by whether one accepts 'retroactive consolidation' (the thesis that later canon-formation manufactures an illusion of unified period-character absent from the distributed historical record) versus whether one requires 'synchronic boundary permeability' (the view that internal counter-movements do not dissolve a period's identity so long as causal clustering holds across institutional domains). The tree should contain six to eight leaves. Where the logical space yields combinations that no prominent view occupies, note them and briefly diagnose whether the gap is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Finally, flag any pair of leaves that your tree cannot genuinely separate, explaining which shared answer pattern collapses them together.
