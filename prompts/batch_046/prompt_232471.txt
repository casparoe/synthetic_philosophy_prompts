I've been trying to figure out whether visitors actually have moral obligations to follow local norms like tipping or queueing, and I built this argument that now feels like it collapses everything into either "everything is obligatory" or "nothing is." Here are the steps:

(1) A visitor has a pro tanto moral duty to follow a local convention C iff three things hold: (a) C solves a real coordination problem for residents, (b) complying costs the visitor very little, and (c) breaking C harms specific people who depend on the convention.
(2) American tipping clearly hits all three - waiters literally depend on tips for wages, so skipping it hurts a specific person.
(3) British queuing and Singaporean anti-jaywalking norms hit (a) and (b) easily, and (c) at least weakly - cutting line takes a spot, and jaywalking in dense traffic puts pedestrians and drivers at risk.
(4) So visitors should tip, queue, and obey crosswalk signals.
(5) But now apply this to a greeting ritual I read about in some ethnographies - there's a reciprocal bow where teh depth signals social rank. Refusing to do the deep bow to an elder causes direct emotional harm to them, complying costs the visitor nothing physical, and the system solves coordination (everyone knows where they stand).
(6) By (1)-(4), the visitor is morally required to perform the bow even if she rejects rank-based deference.
(7) That seems wrong. I don't think she's morally obligated to perform a symbolically degrading act just to spare someone's feelings.
(8) So (1) must be false, which means visitors don't have to tip either.
(9) But I want to keep (2) - stiffing a waiter feels genuinely wrong, not just culturally weird. And if I drop (c) to save queuing, then queuing survives but tipping's justification gets weird.

Where does the chain actually break? Is there a hidden distinction between conventions that allocate material resources (tips, queue spots) and those that allocate symbolic status? Or did I smuggle in an equivocation between "harm" in (c) - economic harm vs emotional harm? I feel like I'm paying some hidden price no matter which premise I reject, but I can't name the bill.
