I have been working through the formal problems in moral-uncertainty theory with a graduate seminar, and we keep returning to what seems to be a structural difficulty in the aggregation problem. Suppose an agent distributes, say, sixty percent credence over consequentialist theories and forty percent over deontological ones, and now faces a classic trolley-type case in which diverting a threat saves five lives but actively kills one. Under the consequentialist framework, the intervention is strongly required; under a strict deontological prohibition on instrumental harm, it is categorically forbidden. If we attempt to compute an expected moral value by treating both verdicts as points on a single cardinal scale—assigning, for instance, a very large negative magnitude to the forbidden act and then taking the credence-weighted average—we either encounter undefined arithmetic, when the deontological assignment behaves like an infinite veto, or we watch the deontological constraint be averaged away whenever the consequentialist weight is nonzero. Several students have concluded that moral uncertainty is therefore incoherent for anyone who assigns nonzero credence to constraint-based theories, because linear expectation presupposes that all competing theories describe the same underlying value dimension.

What I want to press is whether this incoherence charge actually reveals a flaw in the uncertainty framework, or rather a misapplication of expected-utility machinery to normative domains that are structurally incommensurable. Specifically, is the error that we are treating moral theories as competing descriptions of one cardinal utility function, rather than as competing decision rules—some of which specify thresholds, vetoes, or lexical priorities that do not translate into comparable payoffs? If the correct response involves a non-linear or rule-governed aggregation—perhaps analogous to ambiguity-aversion mechanisms in descriptive choice theory—does that preserve the core insight of moral uncertainty, or does it collapse the view into a form of normative pluralism with an ad hoc tie-breaker? I am particularly interested in whether there is a principled, theory-internal reason why a deontological veto should resist being smoothed out by credal weighting, and whether recognizing that resistance forces us to abandon the credence framework altogether in favor of something like a hierarchical choice architecture.
