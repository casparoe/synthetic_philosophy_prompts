I spent several hours last night arguing with a close friend about G. E. Moore’s pairing of friendship and aesthetic appreciation as the supreme intrinsic goods in Principia Ethica. My friend defended the standard reading: that both are organic unities valued for their own sake, with friendship being essentially a complex mental state shared between persons but still assessable from within a single consciousness. I found myself resisting this, and I have sketched the following notes for an essay I want to write.

The core claim I want to defend is that Moore’s classification of friendship alongside beauty collapses if we take seriously the mental-state criterion that governs the rest of his axiology. Beauty, on Moore’s account, is a mental state directed at an object; its intrinsic value can in principle be isolated in a single mind contemplating a sunset or a painting. Friendship, however, is constituted by reciprocal recognition, shared intentions, and responsive emotional adjustments that are not fully present in either party alone. This creates an asymmetry: the value of a friendship is distributed across at least two centers of consciousness, which means that assessing its intrinsic worth requires epistemic access to mental states that are not one’s own. If Moore insists that intrinsic value must be located in mental states, then friendship should not sit in the same category as beauty; it should be treated as a relational composite whose value is partly external to any individual evaluator. Yet Moore treats both as the highest goods without distinguishing their ontological structure. I suspect he implicitly relies on a non-hedonistic, relational ontology that his own methodological individualism should reject. A further consequence is that the independence of friendship’s value from pleasure becomes harder to maintain: if the good is distributed, then my judgment that the friendship is valuable depends on my belief about the other person’s mental states, which introduces an evidential dependency that Moore’s direct-intuition model of value judgment does not accommodate.

Could you comment on these notes? I am particularly interested in whether the asymmetry between single-mind and distributed goods is as damaging to Moore’s pairing as I think it is, and whether there is a charitable reading of Principia Ethica that blocks the argument without simply ignoring the relational structure of friendship. Please also indicate how confident you are in your assessment, and explicitly identify the point about which you are least certain.
