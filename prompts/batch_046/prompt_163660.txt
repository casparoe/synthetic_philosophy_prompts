hey, I'm a retired materials scientist — spent about thirty years looking at fracture mechanics and crystal lattices — and lately I've been poking around philosophy of history with no formal training at all. I wrote a short essay comparing four "lost cause" myths: the Confederacy's Lost Cause, Masada, the Kosovo Field narrative, and Dunkirk. My central claim is that these defeats don't just glorify failure but act like symmetry-breaking events in national identity. Before the event, a culture's self-image is kind of continuous and undirected; afterwards it suddenly orients around a single axis of shared loss, giving it a "direction" that shapes politics and memory for generations. I draw the analogy from how applied stress picks a direction in an otherwise uniform lattice. I also wonder whether there's something like a "mnemonic half-life" — why some defeats fade in two generations while others persist for millennia — and whether that belongs in the argument or is just a distraction.

I'd love your comments on a few things. First, am I flattening important historical diffrences? I treat Dunkirk as functioning like the others in British memory even though it was technically a retreat, and I'm not sure I'm doing justice to how Masada operates in Israeli identity versus how Kosovo works in Serbian nationalism. Second, does the symmetry-breaking analogy actually do philosophical work, or am I just importing physics metaphors where they don't belong? Is there a conceptual distinction I'm missing between a lost-cause narrative that serves liberation or resistance versus one that serves oppression and slavery?
