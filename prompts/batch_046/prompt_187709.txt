I am a student in an advanced philosophy of mind seminar, and after working through Churchland's arguments I have adopted eliminative materialism about propositional attitudes: beliefs and desires, as folk psychology describes them, do not exist. I want to map where this view is genuinely exposed. Present exactly five considerations that would count against it, numbered 1 through 5, each expressed in one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion outside the list. Order them implicitly from the most structurally damaging to the merely awkward. At least two should move beyond the familiar self-refutation and phenomenological-objection tropes: one should ask whether clinical taxonomies of cognitive distortion—for instance, catastrophizing or mind-reading—presuppose representational content that eliminativism must either reconstruct without belief-desire architecture or deny as illusory, and another should examine whether predictive-processing architectures in neuroscience genuinely eliminate propositional attitudes or merely rename them in functional vocabulary. The remaining three should cover first-person authority over mental states, the predictive and coordinative success of folk psychology in everyday social interaction, and the difficulty of preserving normative rational constraint without smuggling belief-desire psychology back in through dispositional or dynamical redescriptions. I want the list precise enough that a specialist in the debate can recognize the stakes, not a generic rehearsal of anti-eliminativist slogans.
