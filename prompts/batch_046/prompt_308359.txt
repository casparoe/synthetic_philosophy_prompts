You are serving as the grader for a final examination in an advanced philosophy-of-mind seminar. The exam presented the following prompt, and the student submitted the answer reproduced below in full.

Exam question (30 points): Global workspace theory (GWT) holds that conscious access requires information to be broadcast globally across specialized cortical modules, typically involving sustained prefrontal-parietal ignition. Ned Block has argued that phenomenal consciousness can occur without such access—phenomenal consciousness without access consciousness—citing the Sperling partial-report paradigm and related phenomena of apparent "overflow." In approximately 500 words, address three points: (1) Explain Block's overflow argument, specifically how the Sperling paradigm suggests subjects experience more than they can report; (2) Present the GWT response: that rapid decay of globally broadcast representations, rather than unbroadcast phenomenal content, explains why only a subset is available for report, being precise about the temporal dynamics involved; (3) Evaluate whether this response genuinely addresses Block's challenge or merely assumes that consciousness requires broadcasting by defining any unreported content as pre-conscious or decayed. In your evaluation, also consider a modified scenario: subjects view a 3×3 grid of textured quadrants, are cued to report texture from one quadrant only, yet claim in post-task interviews to have experienced the overall textural layout of all nine quadrants simultaneously, despite being unable to identify any specific quadrant's texture except the cued one. Discuss whether this case strengthens Block's position, and whether a GWT advocate can accommodate it without collapsing phenomenal consciousness into access consciousness.

Student answer:

Block distinguishes phenomenal consciousness—what it is like to have an experience—from access consciousness, which involves availability for reasoning, report, and rational control. The Sperling paradigm shows that when subjects briefly see twelve letters arranged in three rows, they can report only about four if asked for the whole display, yet if cued immediately afterward to report any single row, they can do so for whichever row is requested. Block takes this to mean that subjects phenomenally experience all twelve letters—otherwise they could not selectively access any row—but only have access consciousness to roughly four at a time.

The global workspace reply is that all twelve items may enter the workspace briefly, but their representations decay within about 200 to 400 milliseconds before they can be consolidated into working memory for verbal report. So there is no "overflow" of phenomenal content beyond access; there is only briefly accessed content that fades before report. This seems to explain the data without positing mysterious unbroadcast states.

However, Block can reply that phenomenal consciousness might exist in local recurrent loops—specifically in early visual cortex such as V1 or V2—that never couple with prefrontal-parietal networks at all. If that is right, then the decay model misses the point: it treats all conscious content as workspace content that fades, whereas Block allows content that never enters the workspace in the first place. The modified quadrant-texture case supports this. Subjects experience the overall layout without being able to report specific quadrant textures, suggesting rich phenomenal structure that outstrips item-level access.

That said, Dehaene's model does not obviously deny that local loops exist; it denies only that they are conscious. So Block's opponent can concede the existence of local processing and still maintain that consciousness requires broadcasting. The charge that GWT begs the question is therefore somewhat overstated, though not entirely without merit.

There is also a narrower problem with the student's framing in this exam: access consciousness is treated as equivalent to verbal reportability. Block explicitly defines access more broadly, including availability for reasoning and rational control, not just speech. Still, the decay reply does not clearly rule out the quadrant-texture case, because even if representations decay rapidly, one might ask why the overall textural layout remains phenomenally present if none of its constituent representations are globally broadcast. The GWT advocate needs an independent argument for why local loops cannot support consciousness, not merely a temporal decay curve.

Overall, Block's overflow argument survives in modified form, though the quadrant case introduces complications that neither side resolves cleanly.
