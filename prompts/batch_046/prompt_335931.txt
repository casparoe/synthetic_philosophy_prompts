Read One Flew Over the Cuckoo's Nest through Thomas Szasz's claim that mental illness is mostly a myth we use to medicalize social deviance and control people who don't fit in. The novel is usually read as an anti-psychiatry manifesto, but I want to push on whether Kesey actually ends up vindicating Szasz or accidentally undermining him. Nurse Ratched clearly runs a coercive instutition, and McMurphy's rebellion fits Szasz's picture of the manufacture of madness—he's labeled sick for being unruly. But then you've got Chief Bromden's actual trauma, Billy Bibbit's stutter and suicide, and the lobotomy at the end that feels like real harm, not just a label being applied. So does the novel dramatize Szasz's myth thesis, or does it show there's something genuinely wrong with the patients that psychiatry is (badly) trying to address? And if it's both, where exactly does the work resist a pure Szaszian reading—maybe in the fact that McMurphy's insanity is perfomative but the other patients' suffering isn't? I'd like you to take both the book and Szasz seriously, not reduce one to the other, and say whether Kesey adds something to the debate about the therapeutic state that Szasz's argument alone misses. Don't just summarize the plot—focus on the tension.
