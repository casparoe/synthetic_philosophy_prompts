Hey, I'm a retired materials scientist who only started reading philosphy of science after I stopped working. At our little meetup group on tuesdays, two other regulars — Dave and Maria — got into it about Popper and the demarcation problem. I couldn't tell who was winning, so I'm asking you to referee move by move.

(1) Dave said falsifcationism is useless because every theory comes with auxilliary assumptions, so a failed experiment just makes you tweak those instead of ditching the core. He mentioned Newton and Mercury's orbit — they invented Vulcan to save the mechanics, and later Einstein replaced the core altogether. So there's always an escape hatch.

(2) Maria said Popper already accounted for auxilliaries. The key is whether your fix is ad hoc or makes risky new predictions. Neptune was okay because it predicted an actual planet; Vulcan was bad because it predicted something that never showed up. So falsifiability works if you check whether the adjustment is independantly testable.

(3) Dave replied that 'ad hoc' is just a label we slap on after we know the result. If Vulcan had been found we'd praise it; since it wasn't we call it ad hoc. There's no principled way to tell in advance, so the whole thing is circular.

(4) Maria then brought up something she calls 'predictive insulation' — a theory designed so its core never touches data directly, only through a flexible buffer of sub-models. She said this is different from old-fashioned ad hocness because the insulation is built in from the start, not patched later. Dave said that's just ad hocness spread out over time, and if Popper's criterion can't catch built-in insulation, then it misses the real problem with modern theoretical frameworks.

Please adjudicate each of the four moves: rate them from clearly wrong to clearly right and briefly justify. Then say who's more right overall and whether predictive insulation actually breaks Popper's criterion or if Maria is overreaching. Don't just say it's all grey — I want actual verdicts.
