I was talking with a friend in my philosophy of action seminar and she called me out for being inconsistent about reasons, so I want to check if my package actualy holds up. Roughly, here's where I've landed: (1) I'm a Williams-style internalist about reasons — for me to genuinely have a reason to φ, there needs to be a sound deliberative route from my actual motivational set to φ-ing, not just that φ-ing is objectively good. (2) At the same time, I think there are real external reasons — if I'm about to hurt someone just for kicks and I have absolutly no motivation to stop, there's still genuinely a reason not to do it; it's not just that I'm evil, it's that I'm failing to respond to a reason that applies regardless. (3) I think internalism is more capacious than critics allow, because rational reflection can generate entirely new motivations, not just instrumentally serve old ones, so the "sound deliberative route" includes being rationally convinced that you should care about something new. (4) But I also think the true amoralist — someone who genuinely sees no reason to avoid harming others and can't be rationaly brought to see one — isn't irational, just morally defective; there's no rational failure in lacking that motivation. (5) Finally, I think practical rationality is always relative to some motivational set, even one that's been revised through reflection, so there's no such thing as being irrational full-stop, only irrational-relative-to-S.

Can you audit this? Specifically: which pairs pull against each other, and how badly? Is (3) doing enough work to bridge (1) and (2), or am I just smuggling external reasons in through the back door? What's the tension between (4) and (2) — if there's an external reason not to harm people, why isn't the amoralist irational for ignoring it? For each conflict, say how severe it is, what the cheapest fix is, and then tell me which single revision would clean up the most mess. Also, please separate what's pretty settled in the literature from what's genuinely contested among people who know this stuff — don't present one contested view as the obvious consensus.
