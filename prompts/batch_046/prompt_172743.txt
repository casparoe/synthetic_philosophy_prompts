Here are notes for an essay I am drafting on G. E. Moore's ethics, and I would like your comments on them. The central claim is unconventional: I want to see whether Moore's doctrine of organic unity, which he develops in Principia Ethica (1903) primarily for aesthetic objects and mental states, can be applied consistently to friendship—one of the supreme goods he identifies—without generating a fragility problem that undermines its status as a supreme good.

The argument runs as follows. For Moore, the value of a whole need not equal the sum of the values of its parts; a beautiful painting or a pleasant consciousness can be an "organic unity" whose total value exceeds the additive value of its colors or sensations. Meanwhile, Moore lists personal affection and the appreciation of beauty among the greatest intrinsic goods. Most commentary treats friendship as falling under personal affection in a straightforward, non-structural way: two people each have affection, and the relation is valuable because it instantiates that affection. I want to propose that, on Moore's own principles, deep friendship might be better understood as an organic unity in its own right. The relation does not merely combine two affections; it transforms them, producing a qualitative value that neither friend could possess in isolation. If this is right, then the value of the friendship is not reducible to the value of each friend's mental state.

But this raises a tension. Organic unities are notoriously fragile: alter or remove a part, and the value of the whole can collapse in a way disproportionate to the loss of the part's independent value. A small change in a painting's composition can destroy its beauty entirely. If friendship is an organic unity of this kind, then betrayal, estrangement, or even gradual divergence might destroy the value of the friendship far more radically than the mere reduction in affection would suggest. That makes friendship look structurally unstable—perhaps too unstable to be one of the supreme goods, which Moore describes as things that ought to be pursued for their own sake regardless of circumstances. On the other hand, Moore might reply that the parts of a friendship—namely, the friends themselves and their capacities for affection—retain intrinsic value even when the whole is dissolved, so the collapse of the unity does not entail a total loss of value, only a change in its form.

I am particularly interested in three questions. First, is there textual support in Principia Ethica for extending organic unity beyond aesthetic and mental wholes to interpersonal relations, or does Moore implicitly restrict it? Second, does the fragility objection genuinely threaten Moore's evaluation of friendship, or does it rest on a misunderstanding of how parts contribute to organic wholes in his account? Third, does this reading risk importing a later, perhaps relational-structuralist conception of value back into Moore's early twentieth-century intuitionism?

Please do not provide a bibliography or suggested readings; I am asking for conceptual analysis only. I would especially value a direct assessment of whether the proposed analogy between aesthetic organic unity and friendship holds up, and whether a defender of Moore's original view could block the extension without ad hoc stipulation. If you think the case fails—if the feature that makes friendship look like an organic unity is in fact absent or mischaracterized—please say exactly which feature does the work and what happens if it is removed.
