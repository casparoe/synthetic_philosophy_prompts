I am drafting a six-month fellowship proposal on consequentialist cluelessness. Rather than revisiting the standard question of whether empirical ignorance about distant outcomes undermines expected-utility reasoning in climate or health policy, I want to examine a structurally different variant: evaluative instability across long time horizons. The precise question is whether our inability to predict which moral frameworks will prevail centuries from now generates a form of cluelessness that is categorically different from ordinary outcome-ignorance, or whether it should be collapsed into the same high-variance parameter space. Existing philosophical responses tend to treat value-uncertainty as just another variable to be probabilized, but I suspect that if future agents might reject the very metrics we now use to rank outcomes, the decision problem is not merely uncertain but framework-dependent in a way that standard decision theory does not capture. Write the one-page proposal: state the question precisely and explain why current answers fail to settle it; propose a philosophical rather than empirical method for making progress—perhaps by rigorously distinguishing first-order outcome ignorance from second-order framework ignorance, and explaining why the obvious alternative of assigning probabilities over present values misses the structural point; describe what a useful deliverable would look like for people actually designing long-horizon interventions, and specify what result would count as failure; and outline a realistic scope for six months of conceptual work. The framing should be accessible to a general philosophy review panel but precise enough that the distinction does real work. I will supply all citations and references myself, so do not include a bibliography, reading list, or literature review by citation.
