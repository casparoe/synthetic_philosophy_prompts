I am a science journalist filing weekly on environmental history and public affairs, and I need a reusable fifteen-minute editorial checklist—auditable by my editor—that operationalizes the conceptual tension between treating germs, climate shifts, and volcanic eruptions as genuine causal actors in history and slipping into environmental determinism. Frame it as an ordered decision procedure with explicit tests: first, whether the non-human factor is being granted explanatory primacy it has not earned; second, whether human institutional responses or adaptations are being rendered invisible; third, what vocabulary of agency, if any, is defensible in the lede and body. Mark clearly which steps are unavoidable judgment calls rather than rules. The procedure must work for a generalist without requiring specialist historiography, and it must specify where editorial sign-off should override it. Apply it to three hypothetical assignments: a plague reshaping a medieval trade network; a drought correlating with a state collapse; and a dramatic volcanic-eruption narrative my editor wants to foreground, where the procedure instead forces me to subordinate the eruption to subsequent policy and social responses. Include the hard cases—necessary-but-not-sufficient triggers, minimal local adaptation, and competing archival silences—and end by stating exactly what the checklist cannot do, especially when archives about human response are thin.
