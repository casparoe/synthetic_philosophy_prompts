Since I began apprenticing in precision instrument repair six months ago, I have become obsessed with the gap between being able to calibrate a lathe and being able to explain how one calibrates it. That personal preoccupation led me into the philosophical literature on knowing-how versus knowing-that, and I am struck by how the debate has been stuck for decades. On one side, intellectualists maintain that knowing how to do something is ultimately a form of propositional knowledge—perhaps knowledge of a practical instruction under a practical mode of presentation, or a belief-desire pair structured by a functional role. On the other side, anti-intellectualists insist that knowing-how is an irreducible ability or skill: a master machinist may lack any articulate proposition that guides her hand, and a novice may memorize every manual yet remain incompetent. Both camps agree on the core cases—the expert who cannot verbalize, the novice who can recite rules but fails in practice—yet neither has conceded an inch in years.

What I think is missing from both sides is an acknowledgment that modern craft knowing-how is rarely, if ever, an individual mental state or bare physical disposition. In my workshop, calibration depends on distributed scaffolds: haptic feedback gauges, mnemonic jigs, and collaborative checklists that partly constitute the skill itself. I want to propose that the stalemate persists because both sides implicitly accept an individualist epistemology. They ask whether knowing-how resides in an agent's beliefs or in her muscles, ignoring the possibility that it is a relational property of an agent-artifact system. Under this distributed knowing-how framing, the standard data—silent expertise versus articulate incompetence—no longer forces a choice between propositional reduction and irreducible ability; instead, it shows that the scaffold can carry propositional content while the agent carries the responsive adjustment.
