I'm trying to work out what I actually believe about evolutionary units of selection, because my judgments feel consistent in pairs but hard to unify, and I'd like to know whether there's a real principle behind them or just framing effects. Here are my verdicts on concrete cases. In eusocial hymenoptera with fully sterile castes, I think the colony is a genuine unit of selection—selection is operating at the group level, not merely reducible to gene-level kin selection—because reproductive division of labor creates an evolutionary individual with its own heritable fitness differences. In cancer, where a mutation proliferates inside an organism and eventually kills it, I think selection is clearly operating at the cell or gene level within the organism, and describing the outcome as "the organism failing to adapt" is a category error that ignores decoupled replication. In bacterial biofilms where cells secrete public goods and cheaters are suppressed by toxin-antitoxin policing mechanisms encoded in the genome, I still think selection is fundamentally gene-level or at most single-cell-level: the policing is just another gene-level strategy, not evidence that the biofilm itself has become an evolutionary individual. Now consider a hypothetical scaffolded symbiosis I invented for this exercise: a horizontally transferred gene builds a shared digestive chamber, that chamber becomes heritable at the consortium level, and after several generations the original genes become dispensable because the structure itself mediates replication—here I think the unit of selection genuinely shifts over evolutionary time from gene to consortium, not just that weights change. I also think that when two populations face identical within-group dynamics but differ in how often groups go extinct, cooperative traits fix in one but not the other; so the "level" isn't intrinsic to the trait but depends on the ecological regime weighting replication at different scales. But then there's a tension: in human cultural evolution I resist calling societies units of selection even when they compete, conquer, and replace one another, because transmission is too horizontal and boundaries too porous—whereas in the hymenoptera case I accept group-level selection despite gene-level mechanisms still being present. Is there a coherent principle—perhaps about causal decoupling of replication, or about heritability of fitness differences being localized at a specific boundary—that captures all of this? Which of my verdicts, especially the one distinguishing cultural groups from insect colonies, does it force me to overturn? And apply it to a pair I haven't mentioned: a viral gene integrates into the host genome and increases host fertility specifically to boost viral transmission, versus a host gene that suppresses viral load and thereby also boosts host fertility—do both involve gene-level selection, or does one shift the unit to the virus or the organism? Please state how confident you are in the principle you propose, and explicitly identify the point you are least sure about—particularly whether the scaffolded symbiosis case involves a true shift in unit rather than just multi-level selection with changing weights.
