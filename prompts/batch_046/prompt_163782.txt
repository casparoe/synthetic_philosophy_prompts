I'm facilitating a 90-minute seminar for sixteen upper-level philosophy undergraduates who have already encountered the general shape of evolutionary debunking arguments in metaethics—specifically the worry that if natural selection shaped our moral beliefs for reproductive fitness rather than for tracking mind-independent moral truths, then those beliefs lack epistemic justification. Rather than reviewing familiar cases, I want them to inhabit a structurally novel scenario that makes the debunking pressure visceral before we ever name it. Here is the framing: imagine a small-scale cooperative foraging group in which survival depends on rapid, binding resource-allocation decisions. Each student receives a hidden role—either a kin-group member, an outsider, or an observer—and a concealed payoff card that rewards kin-partial allocations with "status points" (non-monetary tokens that determine who selects the next round's scenario and who must publicly defend the group's endorsed rule in a justification round). The group must collectively ratify one of two written norms—strict impartial division or kin-weighted division—before making three timed allocation choices. Crucially, the payoff cards are engineered so that kin-partial choices always yield more status points regardless of which norm was verbally endorsed, but participants cannot see others' cards. After the rounds, everyone reveals their cards and calculates how far their actual choices deviated from their endorsed norm. Then they vote, anonymously, on whether they should still trust the moral principle they endorsed, given that they now know the choice environment was systematically biased by evolutionary-style incentives.

Please design the full classroom activity: explicit rules for the three rounds, the role and card distribution (including what the observers do and whether they speak in the justification round), the status-point payoff scheme and how it feeds back into scenario selection, the materials needed (cards, scenario sheets, timer), and a structured 20-minute debrief that maps specific moments in the game onto the logical parts of an evolutionary debunking argument—the causal-origin claim, the alleged epistemic gap between evolutionary usefulness and moral truth, and the potential self-defeat problem if the debunker also relies on evolved cognition. Be frank about which structural feature of the debunking argument the activity necessarily fails to reproduce—perhaps the distinction between debunking the causal origin of a belief and debunking its content—and explain exactly how I should supply that missing piece verbally without lecturing over the experience. Also predict what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it—likely confusion about whether status points represent moral value or strategic value, or emotional defensiveness when students realize their endorsed norms were betrayed by their own choices—and suggest how to manage those failures without undermining the intended insight.
