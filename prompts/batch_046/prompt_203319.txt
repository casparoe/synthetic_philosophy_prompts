Write a deadpan parody of the author guidelines for a fictional peer-reviewed journal, The Syādvāda Review: An Interdisciplinary Journal of Non-Absolutist Inquiry. The journal applies the Jain doctrine of anekāntavāda—many-sidedness, the refusal to grant unqualified, one-sided (ekānta) assertions—to every submission regardless of discipline, treating syādvāda (conditional, “perhaps”-qualified predication) as mandatory methodological protocol. Format the piece as numbered editorial sections (for example, 1. Scope and Mission; 2. Mandatory Seven-Fold Analysis; 3. Prohibited Forms of Certainty; 4. Reviewer Obligations; 5. Corrections, Retractions, and the Non-Absolutist Impact Statement). Aim for roughly 700 words.

The satire should exaggerate a real structural feature of anekāntavāda—its logical commitment to holding multiple, apparently contradictory perspectives simultaneously without collapsing them into a single verdict—by translating it into bureaucratic rigidity. Require every empirical claim, statistical finding, or normative recommendation to be articulated through all seven modes of predication derived from saptabhaṅgī: perhaps it is; perhaps it is not; perhaps it is and is not; perhaps it is inexpressible; and the three compound forms in which inexpressibility is conjoined with the other modes. Render these in plain English within the text itself, glossing them briefly so the piece remains readable to someone with no background in Jain philosophy; do not rely on footnotes or Sanskrit glossaries.

The voice should be that of an earnest, overworked editorial board genuinely convinced that epistemic humility requires procedural paralysis. The humor must arise from the content rather than from invented absurdities: for instance, a climate-modeling submission is rejected not for error but for an abstract that asserts “the temperature is rising” without the requisite seven-fold qualification; a medical trial must conclude that “perhaps the drug works, perhaps it does not work, perhaps it works and does not work”; and the “Non-Absolutist Impact Statement” forces authors to explain how their own conclusions might be false from at least three incompatible standpoints.

Include one invented internal dispute—say, between the “Hard Non-Absolutists” and the “Moderate Conditionalists”—that mirrors genuine interpretive tensions within Jain logic (for example, whether compound modes involving inexpressibility may appear in acknowledgments or only in the results section) but frames them as petty editorial-board feuds over manuscript formatting.

Keep the irony sustained and deadpan: the board should never wink. The target is the over-institutionalization of multi-perspectival or relativist frameworks—treat the underlying doctrine fairly enough that a specialist recognizes you understand its actual logical architecture, but let the bureaucratic exaggeration expose the practical costs of treating every claim as permanently suspended. Do not ask for bibliographies, reading lists, or citations. Do not quote verbatim from any historical text; if you reference traditional formulations, paraphrase them in your own words and without quotation marks. Avoid leaning on specific dates, named classical texts, or obscure biographical details that would require verification; keep the philosophical machinery described in general, structurally accurate terms.
