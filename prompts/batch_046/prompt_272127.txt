Okay so I've been trying to understand Bradley's regress and the unity of the proposition problem, but I keep feeling like they're the exact same issue with different names. Bradley argues that if a relation R connects term a to term b, then you need some further relation R' to connect R to a, and then R'' to connect R' to R, etc., so relations can't actualy do any real work. Now for propositions — say, the claim that Socrates is wise — you've got Socrates, wisdom, and this relating factor tying them together. Isn't that just Bradley's regress applied to language? Like, the relating factor needs a linker to attach it to Socrates, and then that linker needs another one?

Then I read that Russell tried to solve this by saying judgment isn't a binary relation between a mind and one big proposition, but rather the mind stands in one direct relation to Socrates, wisdom, and the relating factor all at once. So if Russell is right, doesn't that mean there's literally no such thing as a proposition with internal unity? The unity is just whatever the mind does, and outside the mind you've just got an unbound list of items. So did Russell actualy solve Bradley's regress, or did he just relocate it into psychology and destroy propositional unity in the process?

Can someone straighten out whether these are genuinely distinct problems, and whether Russell's external-relation fix leaves propositions as mere heaps? Please seperate what's well-established in the literature from what's still genuinely disputed — I don't want to treat one camp's reading as if it's the obvious consensus.
