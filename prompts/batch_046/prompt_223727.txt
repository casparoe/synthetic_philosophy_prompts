George Eliot, born Mary Ann Evans in 1819, rejected the evangelical Christianity of her youth after encountering the biblical criticism of David Friedrich Strauss and the anthropological theology of Ludwig Feuerbach, whose work The Essence of Christianity she translated into English. Rather than adopting atheism, she developed what she termed a religion of humanity, in which moral duty and sympathetic feeling replaced divine command. In Middlemarch, Dorothea Brooke's charitable ambitions reflect this secularized Christian charity: Eliot preserves the ethical structure of Christianity—self-sacrifice for others—while removing its supernatural framework. Eliot's concept of sympathy was essentially Adam Smith's moral sentiment refined for Victorian social science; she believed that recognizing shared human needs creates moral obligation. Finally, Eliot maintained that belief in personal immortality was necessary to justify long-term moral progress, a view that surfaces in The Mill on the Floss, where Maggie Tulliver's death implies that individual souls endure beyond physical death to complete their moral development.
