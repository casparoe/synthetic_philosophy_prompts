I am drafting a reply to a referee report on a recent manuscript concerning rational belief aggregation, and before I respond I want an accuracy audit of how my interlocutor summarizes two classic puzzles. The reviewer writes: "The lottery paradox and the preface paradox are structurally identical. In Kyburg's lottery, it is rational to accept that ticket one will lose, ticket two will lose, and so on for each individual ticket, yet by closure of rational belief under conjunction one should also accept that all tickets lose, which contradicts the known fact that one will win. The preface paradox reproduces the same contradiction: an author rationally accepts every individual claim in her book, yet rationally accepts that the book contains at least one error. Kyburg solved both by arguing that rational belief is context-relative—what is rational to accept in isolation need not be rational to accept together—so the contradiction dissolves once we reject unrestricted aggregation. This same structure also arises in collective deliberation: a jury that rationally trusts each witness individually ought, by closure, to trust that every witness lied, which demonstrates that Kyburg's restriction on aggregation should govern group belief as well as individual belief." I am inclined to think she conflates Kyburg's specific restriction on conjunction with a broader context-relativism, and that she overstates the structural identity between the probabilistic lottery and the non-probabilistic preface case. Could you examine her summary sentence by sentence? Indicate what a careful epistemologist would endorse, where the subtle misrepresentations lie—particularly regarding closure principles, Kyburg's actual proposal about aggregation, and the analogy between individual and group belief—and whether her claim that Kyburg's solution extends directly to jury deliberation survives once the earlier errors are corrected. I do not need references or a bibliography; I only need a precise evaluation of whether her account is fair enough to serve as the basis for my reply.
