I have recently been asked to contribute to a public-history exhibition assessing the legacy of British administration in South Asia, and I keep returning to the same deadlock. On one side stand defenders of what might be called the counterfactual welfare thesis: that railways, codified law and parliamentary institutions left a positive net balance once actual outcomes are weighed against plausible non-colonial trajectories. On the other stand advocates of what might be called the sovereignty ledger: that cataloguing deindustrialisation, famine and extraction misses the deeper point—that treating self-rule and dignity as fungible with GDP renders the accounting itself morally illegitimate. The debate has run for decades through monographs, reviews and televised exchanges, yet neither camp concedes ground. Is the stalemate substantive—do they genuinely disagree about empirical counterfactuals, baseline selection, or causal attribution—or is it that the two sides are answering different questions under a single heading? Might one be measuring material welfare against a hypothetical trajectory while the other is measuring the non-commensurable cost of unconsented domination? I want a diagnosis: is this a verbal disagreement, a hidden empirical dispute, or two incommensurable frameworks? And what, if anything, would count as progress—a formal result in counterfactual economic history, a conceptual distinction separating welfare from legitimacy, or an admission that no single balance sheet can accommodate both?
