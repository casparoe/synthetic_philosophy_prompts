Can you explain the tension between pure time preference and what we might call "moral patienthood sliding" in longtermist ethics? The classic argument is that discounting welfare just cause it's futher in time is like caring less about someone cause they live farther north — pure spatial bias applied to time. But there's a less-discussed wrinkle: future people aren't just distant in time, they're ontologicaly thinner — less determinate, closer to possible people than actual ones. So if you reject pure time preference entirely, you might not just be saying "treat future welfare equally"; you might also be commiting yourself to giving full moral weight to merely possible people, which seems like its own mess. How do these two issues actually interact? Does dropping the pure rate collaspse the line between actual and potential moral patients, or can you defend temporal equality without sliding into full modal realism about generations that don't exist yet?
