Consider the conceptual problem of mechanistic anomaly detection: identifying when an internal causal process in a system has deviated or been replaced, rather than merely observing distributional shift in outputs. Before assessing specific frameworks, I want a specification of what a theory of mechanistic identity—the criteria for saying that two processes, possibly in different architectures or contexts, are instances of the same mechanism—must actually deliver. Lay out the full desiderata: the classes of cases it must correctly classify (e.g., parameter drift versus structural substitution versus adaptive fragmentation of a mechanism into distributed subcomponents); the distinctions it must preserve (such as between a mechanism being corrupted and a genuinely different mechanism being activated); the counterfactual or intervention-based conditions it must satisfy; and the consistency constraints with empirical findings about how mechanisms restructure under distribution shift. For each requirement, give the motivating consideration and mark whether it is non-negotiable or a preference of a particular camp (for example, literal graph-isomorphism versus invariant intervention profiles). Identify any pairs of desiderata that appear jointly unsatisfiable, and explain the tension. Finally, sketch briefly—without developing a full scorecard—how a causal-graph view, a representation-learning view, and an interventionist-process view each stand with respect to the list. The goal is a defended conceptual specification, not a survey or bibliography.
