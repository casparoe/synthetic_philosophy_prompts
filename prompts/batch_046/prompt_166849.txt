Exam question: Using the course framework of causal contribution, agential moral culpability, and institutional answerability, evaluate the objection that saying Germany is responsible for causing WWI requires attributing moral guilt to every German citizen alive in 1914, and explain what this implies for reading Fischer's argument about Imperial Germany's war aims.

Student answer: The Fischer thesis holds that Imperial Germany deliberately sought a continental war in 1914, especially through the annexationist aims embedded in the September Program, which establishes strong causal responsibility at the state level. Critics like Gerhard Ritter rejected this by insisting that alliance structures and mobilization timelines made all powers equally causal contributors, which means Germany cannot be singled out even causally. The objection that blaming Germany requires blaming every German citizen confuses distributive guilt with collective institutional answerability; Fischer clearly targeted the decision-making elite, not the population. Nevertheless, collective moral culpability requires a single unified intentional agent, and because a nation-state lacks a singular consciousness, it is conceptually impossible for it to bear moral blame in the agential sense. Consequently, Fischer's work is best interpreted as mapping causal mechanisms and institutional answerability without attributing true moral culpability to an abstract collective.
