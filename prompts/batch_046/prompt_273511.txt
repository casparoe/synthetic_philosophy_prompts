I was struck by the tension in that vision: on one hand, an optimistic belief that material scarcity will be solved; on the other, a quieter anxiety that people conditioned only for work may suffer a kind of breakdown without it, together with the suspicion that status competition—what he frames as needs that are relative rather than absolute—might simply migrate from the office into leisure itself. I want a satirical piece that takes these real tensions seriously and exaggerates them, not one that invents a strawman.

Please write a deadpan parody of an internal corporate HR memo—around 700 words, formatted with numbered sections, bullet points, and a sidebar—announcing that our engineering division has achieved Keynes's predicted fifteen-hour work week and is therefore rolling out a fictional "Good Life Competency Framework." The framework should include OKRs, peer-review rubrics, and leveling criteria for leisure activities, distinguishing between authentic hobbies and performative relaxation, and the memo should feature a sidebar on a made-up "Relative Needs Taxonomy" that ranks status competition in post-work hours. Include at least one quote from an invented "Chief Leisure Officer," keep the voice rigorously deadpan and bureaucratic, and ensure the satire lands the actual features of Keynes's argument: that free time without cultivated purpose produces anxiety, that the capacity to enjoy leisure is unevenly distributed by culture and class, and that bureaucratic measurement tends to colonize whatever space it touches. The piece should be readable to someone with no philosophy training, but accurate enough that a reader familiar with Keynes recognizes a fair exaggeration rather than a distortion. Do not include bibliographies, citations, reading lists, or requests for further sources; the text must stand entirely on its own.
