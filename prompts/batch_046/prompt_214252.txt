Please argue that in historical fiction and biopics about real deceased people, creators owe the dead not primarily conventional representational fidelity—accurate chronology, verifiable dialogue, and authentic setting—but rather a stricter duty of what might be termed narrative non-appropriation: the avoidance of casting the dead as functional elements within a present-oriented moral, political, or redemptive plot architecture. In developing this argument, sharply distinguish representational error (factual deviation from the historical record) from structural conscription (the framing of a real person's life, suffering, or choices so that they serve an explanatory or ideological function for living audiences that they never endorsed). Address the objection that the dead have no conscious interests and therefore cannot be wronged: explain why structural conscription may still damage the moral relationship between present communities and their past, even if no experiential harm befalls the deceased themselves, without invoking supernatural or metaphysically dubious posthumous interests. Conclude by identifying what would change your mind—whether a demonstrated case in which structural conscription yields unambiguous moral education without exploitation, or a convincing argument that all artistic framing is necessarily instrumental—together with the point in your reasoning where failure is most likely, such as the difficulty of distinguishing unavoidable narrative framing from wrongful conscription.
