I am drafting discussion material for an advanced seminar on philosophy of mind, and I keep returning to Daniel Dennett's notion of the self as a centre of narrative gravity—a fictional but functionally indispensable point around which autobiographical stories orbit. In our last session, a colleague from clinical neuropsychology described a particular pattern she had observed: patients with severe retrograde amnesia who, lacking any episodic access to their past, systematically construct coherent life-narratives from family testimony and photographs, yet report that these stories feel "borrowed" rather than owned, lacking the gravitational pull of personal memory. This struck me as a potential test case, but I am unsure whether it undermines Dennett's view—by showing that narrative coherence without ownership is hollow—or supports it—by showing that when narrative is externally sourced, the self dissolves precisely because the centre of gravity has not been internally generated.

Please write a dialogue between two equally competent philosophers, Dr. Rowan and Dr. Soren, debating this case. Rowan should defend the narrative gravity thesis, arguing that the amnesic patient's "borrowed" autobiography demonstrates exactly what Dennett predicts: without internal narrative generation, there is no self, only a distributed story without a centre. Soren should resist this, contending that the patient's persistent report of "mineness"—the feeling that something is missing, that the story is not hers—reveals a pre-narrative phenomenal anchor that narrative gravity cannot explain away. They must respond to each other's actual arguments rather than talk past one another: Rowan should address Soren's appeal to this residual sense of ownership, and Soren should address Rowan's claim that the absence of gravitational pull is precisely the absence of self.

Present your response in British English, with a formal but natural register. Organise it into two sections: first, "The Dialogue" (approximately 900 to 1100 words); second, "Points of Vulnerability" (approximately 200 words). In the second section, identify the specific argument or piece of evidence that, if strengthened, would lead you to favour one interlocutor over the other, and indicate precisely where your reconstruction—whether in Rowan's functionalist account of fictional selfhood or in Soren's phenomenological appeal to pre-narrative mineness—is most likely to be mistaken or incomplete.
