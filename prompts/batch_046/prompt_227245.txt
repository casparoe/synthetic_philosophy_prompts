I have spent the last couple of years trying to understand how scientists and philosophers talk about "causes" in medicine and engineering, without any university training in logic or statistics. From what I have read, there seem to be two camps that rarely meet on the same ground. One camp—let me call them the counterfactualists—treats causation as fundamentally a relation of counterfactual dependence across nearby possible situations. The other—the interventionists—treats it as fundamentally defined by invariant relationships under actual or hypothetical manipulations, represented with structural causal models.

Write the joint statement that a committed counterfactualist and a committed interventionist should produce after a good-faith adversarial collaboration focused specifically on this problem: in continuous physical systems, can either framework genuinely distinguish redundant causation—where two mechanisms are both present and either one could produce the outcome, but only one is actually operating, while the other is present but inert—from true overdetermination, in which both mechanisms actually contribute, without importing hidden normative judgments about which interventions count as "relevant" or which possible worlds count as "nearby"?

The document should contain: a numbered section of claims both sides can sign, including about what each framework formally requires and about the specific redundant-causation case; then a disagreements table giving the precise formulations of their remaining differences, each worded so that the side it represents would accept the wording; then the cruxes—the smaller questions such that settling them would move at least one side; and, for each crux, a concrete case or piece of evidence both sides agree bears on it, with each side's prediction written down in advance.

Please separate, throughout, what is settled among people who actually work on these theories from what remains genuinely contested, and do not present any disputed interpretation—such as the claim that interventionist analysis secretly collapses into counterfactual reasoning, or that counterfactual reasoning requires an untenable metaphysics of possible worlds—as though it were the consensus view.
