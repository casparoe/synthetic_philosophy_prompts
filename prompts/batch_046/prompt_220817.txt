I'm organizing the second half of a graduate seminar in philosophy of mind and want to devote roughly four weeks to the language of thought hypothesis and its conceptual rivals, treating it not as a historical artifact but as a live framework for explaining cognition. Rather than surveying the literature, I need a defended agenda: among the open conceptual questions and downstream implications, which ones actually deserve sustained attention for a philosopher without lab access, and in what order should they be tackled?

Specifically, I am weighing four interdependent lines of work. First, whether the classic arguments from systematicity and productivity genuinely require a discrete combinatorial syntax in mental representations, or whether distributed "vector-compositional" architectures can achieve the same functional results without symbolic structure. Second, whether LoT must be anchored in natural-language competence, or whether a non-linguistic mentalese can carry the full explanatory load — and if so, whether this severs the speech-thought analogy that motivates much of the view. Third, whether accepting LoT commits one to a particular theory of concept possession, and whether that commitment generates independent problems for explaining conceptual error or change. Fourth, what the implications are for non-human animal cognition and for artificial systems, particularly whether denying LoT to large language models is consistent with attributing it to humans when both appear to process information without an obvious internal symbolic code. Please rank these in terms of conceptual priority: for each, state whether it is upstream or downstream of the others, which criteria — tractability within pure philosophy, dependence of other debates on its resolution, or relevance to cognitive science — justify its position, and what a negative answer would actually change about our understanding of mind. I am not asking for a bibliography, citation list, or specific papers; I want an ordering defended by conceptual dependency and practical significance, together with an honest note on which of these questions might turn out to be overrated if their answers do not shift the broader landscape.
