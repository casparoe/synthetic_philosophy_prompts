After a long argument with a friend about whether algorithmic content-moderation flags “do things with words,” I want an accuracy audit of his summary of Austin and Searle. He put it like this: a performative utterance is essentially any statement that directly produces a real-world effect rather than describing one, so an automated takedown flag is a paradigmatic performative—it does not report a violation but enacts one—while ordinary human speech is derivative because it needs institutional backing. From this he concluded that Austin’s framework collapses for algorithmic speech, since algorithms lack the intentions required for felicitous performance, so algorithmic “misfires” should be treated as mere technical errors rather than infelicities. He also claimed that because performatives are neither true nor false but only “happy” or “unhappy,” algorithmic errors can never be false statements, only unauthorized procedures analogous to a wedding conducted by someone without legal standing. I suspect he is conflating illocutionary force with perlocutionary effect, and perhaps treating Austin’s exclusion of etiolated or parasitic uses as a universal rule about all non-literal speech, but I cannot locate every slip. Is this summary fair? Please separate what a careful Austinian or Searlean would accept, which parts are subtle misrepresentations or overstatements—especially regarding intentionality, institutional authority, and the true/false distinction—and whether the conclusion that algorithmic speech breaks the framework actually follows from the accurate parts.
