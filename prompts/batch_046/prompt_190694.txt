Here is an argument that seems to show that epistemic modesty, pursued consistently, undermines the very distinctions it is meant to preserve.

(1) If I am rational, I ought to correct my confidence in any belief B by the outside-view base rate for people with my profile believing B-type propositions.
(2) Deciding which reference class applies—say, “philosophers arguing for this conclusion”—and judging whether my case is exceptional are themselves beliefs formed through reasoning.
(3) By (1), those meta-beliefs must also be corrected by their own outside-view base rates.
(4) This generates an infinite regress: every correction requires a further correction, with no non-arbitrary stopping point.
(5) Without termination, confidence in B collapses not to a calibrated intermediate value but to the base rate of the widest available class—roughly that people are wrong about abstract claims about half the time.
(6) So consistent modesty requires me to hold every philosophical belief, whether meticulously argued or casually formed, at approximately that same flat rate.
(7) Yet that erases the distinction between careful reasoning and guesswork that modesty was supposed to preserve, and if applied to practical reasoning it paralyses action altogether.
(8) Therefore either the outside view cannot be applied to itself, or epistemic modesty is self-defeating.

Something feels wrong between (4) and (5)—perhaps the regress converges rather than flattening, or perhaps methodological beliefs belong to a different reference class—but I cannot name the slip. Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? I should prefer to keep (1) and (6), since abandoning either seems to forfeit modesty or to accept scepticism.
