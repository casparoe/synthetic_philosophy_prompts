Question: Scheffler argues that human extinction is a catastrophe for reasons that go beyond the deaths it involves. Explain his afterlife conjecture and apply it to compare extinction with a scenario where every human dies peacefully of old age on the same day, leaving Earth intact. Then evaluate whether his reasoning still applies if humanity dies out but is replaced by conscious artificial minds that preserve all human culture and values.

Student answer:
Scheffler's afterlife conjecture is that a lot of our meaningful projects—science, art, raising kids—depend on the hidden assumption that humanity will keep going after we die. He calls this our collective afterlife. If we knew there was no future, these activities wouldn't lose their value because future people suffer, but because the present activity is oriented toward a continuation that won't happen. So extinction is special: it doesn't just kill people, it undermines the value-structure of everything we're doing now.

Comparing extinction to simultaneous peaceful death, Scheffler thinks both lack a future, but extinction is actually less bad in one respect: in peaceful death everyone gets to finish their life projects naturally, whereas extinction cuts them off mid-stream. Still, the core value-loss is the same in both cases, since there's no inheritor.

If conscious AI replaces us and keeps our culture alive, I think Scheffler's worry is mostly solved, because the afterlife is still there—just not biological. The AI can carry on science and art. But there's a hidden problem: Scheffler's argument actually depends on psychological continuity between generations, like the continuity theory of personal identity. Since AI lacks human psychology, that continuity is broken, so replacement doesn't really preserve the afterlife. Therefore Scheffler should still see biological extinction as catastrophic even with AI successors.
