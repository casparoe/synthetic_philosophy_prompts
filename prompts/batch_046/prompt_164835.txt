I'm taking a graduate seminar on applied ethics, and two classmates—I'll call them Alex and Jordan—got into a sustained disagreement over coffee about where legitimate persuasion ends and manipulation begins. I was listening as a bystander and found both sides sharp, but I can't tell where the real fault lines actually lie. You are an expert philosopher of moral psychology and the ethics of influence; please think carefully and systematically. Before adjudicating, briefly outline the criteria that should govern the distinction between manipulation and legitimate persuasion. Then evaluate the exchange point by point.

Here is what they said. (1) Alex began by claiming that manipulation is essentially the bypassing of rational evaluation: if an influence works by exploiting cognitive biases, emotional triggers, or framing effects rather than by presenting reasons the subject can actively weigh, it counts as manipulation regardless of the influencer's intent or the outcome's value. (2) Jordan replied that this collapses legitimate persuasion into manipulation, since every persuasive act—including philosophical argumentation—exploits biases: confirmation bias shapes salience, social proof guides acceptance of premises, and even Socratic questioning leverages consistency pressure. If bypassing rational capacities is the criterion, then teaching and rational dialogue are manipulative too. (3) Alex countered with a distinction between concealment and openness: in legitimate persuasion the mechanism of influence is available to the subject's reflection, while in manipulation it is hidden or distorted. He offered a case: a physician who presents treatment options using only relative-risk framing (making benefits look larger) without disclosing that absolute-risk framing tells a different story is manipulating, even if the treatment is genuinely best, because she withholds the structural feature that would let the patient evaluate the presentation itself. (4) Jordan responded that "available to reflection" is either too weak or too strong—too weak because people rarely reflect on framing mechanisms in ordinary conversation, too strong because demanding that every speaker disclose all cognitive leverage points sets an impossible standard for human interaction. She added that Alex's physician example assumes there is a "neutral" presentation, but absolute and relative risk are both frames with different emotional weights; there is no frame-free view from nowhere, so his "structural transparency" just pushes the problem back: transparent about which frame? (5) Alex clarified that he is not demanding frame-free neutrality, but what he calls "structural transparency": the influenced party can, upon asking or with minimal effort, access the alternative framing and understand that a framing choice was made. The physician who offers both frames, or who notes that she is using relative risk, meets the standard; the one who actively suppresses the other frame—knowing it would change the patient's emotional response—does not. (6) Jordan concluded that this makes manipulation depend on counterfactual accessibility rather than on actual mental processing. In practice, patients don't ask, and influencers know they don't ask. So "structural transparency" becomes a merely formal property of the interaction (the frame could be revealed) that does no actual work in preserving autonomy. If the mechanism remains hidden in the lived experience of influence, hasn't Alex just defined manipulation away rather than genuinely distinguished it?

Please adjudicate move by move: rate each of the six moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right and briefly justify each rating. Then state which party is right about the core distinction, identify what both are missing—especially regarding whether structural transparency can be made psychologically real rather than merely formal—and assess whether a coherent middle position exists that respects autonomy without imposing an impossible standard of meta-cognitive disclosure.
