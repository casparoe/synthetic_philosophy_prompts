I am currently enrolled in a graduate seminar on consciousness and physicalism, and a recent discussion about constitutive panpsychism left me with an argument that I cannot fully dismantle even though its conclusion feels wrong. A fellow student laid out a chain of reasoning that seems to show panpsychism collapses into an absurd proliferation of macro-subjects, and I want to understand exactly where the reasoning overreaches.

The argument runs as follows. (1) Panpsychism holds that every fundamental physical entity is a subject of experience possessing its own phenomenal properties. (2) Whenever two or more subjects stand in a relation of physical integration—whether causal interaction, spatial contiguity, or information transfer—their phenomenal properties combine to constitute the phenomenal properties of a new composite subject. (3) Physical integration is transitive: if entity A is integrated with B, and B with C, then A stands in an indirect but real relation of integration with C through B. (4) Therefore, by transitivity, any physically connected region of space contains exactly one macro-subject whose experience is the unified combination of every micro-subject within that region. (5) This applies not only to human brains but to arbitrary aggregates—rooms, clouds, atmospheric systems—implying that panpsychism generates countless macro-subjects with phenomenal fields that vastly exceed human consciousness. (6) Yet my own experience is phenomenologically bounded and localized; I detect no trace of being part of a larger room-subject or planetary-subject, which suggests these macro-subjects either do not exist or are epistemically inaccessible in principle. (7) The panpsychist might reply that only a specific, highly structured form of integration—perhaps one involving high degrees of informational integration—suffices to generate a macro-subject. (8) But if integration is selective in this way, then the human brain is not automatically a unified subject merely in virtue of physical connection; it too requires this special condition. (9) Consequently, the panpsychist faces a dilemma: either all physical aggregates are conscious subjects, which proves far too much and drowns human consciousness in an ocean of overlapping macro-experiences, or integration is selective, in which case the panpsychist owes an independent account of why brains satisfy the condition while chairs do not, and risks leaving human unity unexplained. (10) Hence panpsychism cannot consistently maintain both that fundamental entities are conscious and that human beings are genuinely unified subjects.

I feel the slip lies somewhere between steps (3) and (8)—perhaps an equivocation between causal integration and phenomenal binding, or a modal slide that treats transitivity of physical relations as licensing transitivity of phenomenal combination—but I cannot name it precisely. Which step should be rejected? Does rejecting it require denying that there is any combination problem at all, or does it merely demand a more restricted theory of phenomenal binding, and what would accepting that restriction cost the panpsychist's broader metaphysics?
