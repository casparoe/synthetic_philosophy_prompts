The epistemicist maintains that vague predicates actually have precise, sharp extensions that we are constitutionally ignorant of, while the supervaluationist denies there is any unique correct sharpening, treating vagueness as semantic indeterminacy resolved only across admissible precisifications. Consider a calibrated digital gradient composed of exactly one thousand discrete pixels running smoothly from pure red to pure orange, where every adjacent pair is perceptually indistinguishable and precise measurement reveals no hidden discontinuity; ask not merely which pixel is the first orange one, but whether there is a metaphysically privileged cutoff at all, and whether the claim that there is no sharp boundary can itself be sharp or is subject to the same indeterminacy. Write an essay that weighs epistemicism against supervaluationism specifically on this calibrated case. Your essay should reach and justify a specific conclusion about which framework better handles the intuition that vagueness is irremediable at every order—that is, that there is no hidden fact waiting to be discovered even in principle, and that any attempt to fix a boundary merely relocates the problem. In defending your conclusion, examine whether epistemicism's appeal to ignorance can survive the charge that the ignorance is structurally irremediable rather than merely practical, and whether supervaluationism avoids the difficulty or only displaces it into the metalanguage that governs which precisifications count as admissible.
