You are a professional philosopher specializing in epistemic injustice and institutional ethics, advising a university governance board. Before drafting, briefly identify the core philosophical tension and the practical constraints the board must navigate.

The board—the University Archives Ethics Review Committee—must decide on the digitization and online release of the Riverside Oral History Archive, 120 interviews conducted between 1968 and 1974 with members of a linguistic minority group. The original cataloging imposed an external taxonomy—classifying testimonies by assimilation status and cognitive reliability—that community representatives say misrepresents their epistemic authority and applies hermeneutical categories they do not recognize. A coalition has proposed a restorative epistemic recataloging protocol: the metadata and abstracts would be rewritten using the community’s own conceptual framework, such as relational knowing and intergenerational testimony, and the digital presentation would use a dual-layer format—original scan plus community-annotated interpretation—rather than replacing the files.

The board must weigh this against the strongest counter-argument: altering descriptive metadata violates archival integrity by retroactively substituting one interpretive authority for another, and preserving the original cataloging as a historical artifact, with critical commentary appended separately, avoids a different form of hermeneutical imposition. There is also a precedent concern—if retrospective reinterpretation is permitted here, other holdings may face similar demands.

Draft a formal policy memo (approximately 500–700 words) addressed to the committee recommending a concrete, actionable decision—approve with modifications, reject, or approve conditionally. The memo should: (1) state the recommendation unambiguously at the outset; (2) explain precisely how this case instantiates testimonial and hermeneutical dimensions of epistemic injustice, treating the concepts as working tools rather than labels; (3) honestly address the archival-integrity objection and show why it does not defeat the recommendation; (4) propose specific, votable conditions governing the dual-layer presentation, transparency documentation, and scope limitations; and (5) include one paragraph conceding the strongest point on the losing side and explaining why it still falls short. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations. Write as a document the board can act on directly.
