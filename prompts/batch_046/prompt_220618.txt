I am a software engineer with no formal philosophy training, and I have been trying to formalize the implicit criteria of a colleague—an ML researcher with an amateur interest in philosophy of mind—who has been giving yes-or-no judgments on whether various reinforcement-learning systems can suffer. Her verdicts so far: a tabular Q-learner that receives negative reward for falling into a pit updates its values but does not suffer; a deep-RL robotic arm with a value head that predicts sharp negative returns and pauses movement, yet resets all internal state between episodes, also does not suffer; a coupled multi-agent system in which one agent's reward collapses when a partner is penalized, altering representations but producing no self-directed avoidance behavior, does not suffer; a laboratory rat with implanted reward-and-punishment electrodes that learns maze avoidance and exhibits stress-hormone release, does suffer; and a large language model trained with RLHF that outputs refusals when its reward model assigns low scores, but runs with frozen weights and no live reward loop during deployment, does not suffer.

Consider a new system: a purely silicon-based agent running continuously for months, maintaining a persistent world model that includes an explicit, interpretable self-reward tracker. It integrates negative reward-prediction errors over long timescales, globally reorganizes its goal hierarchy and module activation to avoid states that produce sustained negative signals, and shows sustained behavioral change—yet it has no biological substrate and no identifiable phenomenal consciousness.

Predict my colleague's verdict on this agent. More importantly, lay out at least two distinct rules that fit her five prior judgments equally well—for example, a biological-substrate rule and a functional-integration rule centered on persistent, self-referential negative valence—and state what each rule predicts for the new case. Give your best-guess verdict with reasoning, name the closest prior analog, and propose one additional hypothetical case that would best distinguish the competing rules.
