I am a high school student writing a philosophy paper on family policy, and I have noticed a specific asymmetry: in most wealthy democracies, the state provides paid parental leave and heavily subsidized childcare almost exclusively as instruments to keep both parents in the formal labor market, while offering little comparable public support for families that organize care through a stay-at-home parent, extended kin networks, or cooperative neighborhood arrangements. What philosophical commitments about the value of wage labor, the meaning of gender equality, and the proper boundary between family and market does this institutional design presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory of the good life and of state neutrality that makes such a policy seem fair rather than coercive; distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what merely accompanies it; and say whether the underlying view is coherent—especially since these same societies often celebrate individual "choice" in other domains of family life. If we replaced that implicit view with one that treated unpaid care work as equally worthy of public support regardless of its effect on labor-force participation, what concrete features of current leave and childcare policy would have to change?
