Design a 90-minute in-person simulation for a graduate philosophy and economics seminar of 12 students that makes participants bodily experience the tension between monotonicity and independence of irrelevant alternatives—specifically in the Kalai–Smorodinsky bargaining framework—before anyone names the axioms or the solution. Structure it as paired rounds over a divisible communal resource (time slots in a shared studio), using small real stakes (tokens redeemable for coffee vouchers). The hidden rule: each round one player silently receives a “shadow option” that expands only their feasible set without changing the disagreement point, so they should, under monotonicity, do no worse, yet under IIA they could be forced downward. Include precise rules for hidden-information revelation, a payoff scheme that rewards Pareto-optimal agreements but penalizes breakdown, a debrief that maps each round’s emotional arc onto the formal divergence between Kalai–Smorodinsky and Nash, and an honest forecast of first-run failures (collusion, misunderstanding of hidden sets). End with a frank note on which feature—the axiomatic uniqueness proof—this embodied play cannot reproduce and must be supplied conceptually. Separate technically settled properties (the axioms, the divergence from Nash) from genuinely contested interpretations (whether monotonicity captures a defensible notion of fairness).
