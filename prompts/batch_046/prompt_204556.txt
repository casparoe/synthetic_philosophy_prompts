As a philosophy professor serving on the Municipal Archive and Public Memory Commission, I request a formal decision memo addressing a petition that frames the conflict between collective memory and historical record through a specific conceptual claim rather than through generic appeals to representation. The petition comes from a community of descendants of displaced agricultural workers regarding the old mill site. Their sustained oral tradition holds that the 1932 eviction was executed not by county sheriffs, as the court filings and property deeds state, but by a private security force acting with tacit municipal approval; they further maintain that the eviction destroyed a system of mutual-aid governance that the documentary record never registered because that record was produced by the very institutions that organized the displacement. Crucially, the petitioners do not merely ask for an additional narrative. They invoke what they term epistemic restitution—the principle that when an archive is structurally incomplete in ways that systematically benefit the institutions that created it, the harm is not corrected by adding a footnote but by granting co-equal status to the testimonial practices of the affected community. They ask that the digital archive be amended to present their account as co-equal with the documentary record, and that the physical interpretive plaque be rewritten to state that the official record is incomplete and possibly suppressive. Our commission must recommend approval, rejection, or approval with conditions, and the recommendation must specify precisely what changes staff should make to both the digital metadata and the physical text. Please draft the memo in continuous prose only—no headings, no bullet points, no boldface, and no tables—moving as a single formal letter from framing to an explicit bottom line to reasoning. Treat the core philosophical issue as one of epistemic authority: under what conditions should a living community's mnemonic practices supplement or override a documentary record produced by institutions implicated in the original harm? Be explicit about the strongest consideration favoring the opposite conclusion—whether that is the erosion of evidentiary standards that allow archives to function as public goods, or the risk of collapsing the distinction between historical inquiry and political advocacy. Do not invoke specific scholars, named theoretical movements, or obscure conceptual frameworks that would not be familiar from general philosophical training; you may, however, refine or critique the petitioners' notion of epistemic restitution if it aids clarity. Do not provide bibliographies, citations, or reading lists. The memo should conclude with conditions precise enough to guide implementation—specifying, for example, whether co-equal presentation requires parallel columns, integrated narrative with explicit sourcing tags, or a separate but linked testimonial archive—and should explain what principle distinguishes permissible mnemonic supplementation from impermissible archival revisionism.
