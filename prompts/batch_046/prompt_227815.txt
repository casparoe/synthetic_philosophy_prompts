I've been arguing with my friends about whether our little advocacy collective should call every atrocity genocide in our reports, and I think we need a real procedure instead of just vibes. I read something about Lemkin's original concept being way broader than the UN legal box — including cultural and social destruction, not just mass killing — and I want to turn that into a practical checklist for our field director, who doesn't have a law degree and sometimes has to decide in like twenty minutes whether to issue a public statement. You're a senior advisor in international humanitarian law and advocacy strategy; please think carefully and start by brainstorming what the three main stages should be before you write anything.

Design a reusable decision procedure the director can run on any new situation. Stage one should check the legal category: protected group, specific intent, and one of the prohibited acts — say exactly what counts as sufficient evidence for each, and where this stage stops with a hard no. Stage two should check the broader moral category drawn from Lemkin's original framework: systematic destruction of language, religion, economic life, or social institutions — again, what evidence is enough, and where this picks up cases the legal stage misses. Stage three should check the politics of naming: who benefits, who faces retaliation, whether the label opens intervention doors or gets local partners killed. At each stage mark the spots where judgment is unavoidable and can't be automated, especially around proving intent from outside. The procedure should stop with seperate outcomes: legal yes plus moral yes plus strategic yes means issue the statement; legal yes but strategic harm means hand it to a human values call; legal no but moral yes means maybe frame it differently but flag the legal risk. Then run it on three hypothetical cases: (1) a government banning a minority language, destroying religious sites, and forcing assimilation in schools, with almost no direct killings; (2) a militia executing members of one ethnic group with clear intent, but where an international genocide label might trigger a state crackdown that wipes out survivors; and (3) a case where the procedure producse an answer our collective will definately hate — maybe legal genocide paired with strategic silence, or legal ambiguity paired with moral urgency to speak. Also tell me where the procedure will misfire, especially on intent, and don't include any citations or reading lists since our director won't have web access in the field.
