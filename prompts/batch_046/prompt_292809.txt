I am grading final papers for a graduate seminar on early twentieth-century value theory and need an independent evaluation of one response. The exam asked the following: In Principia Ethica, G. E. Moore identifies both personal affection—friendship, love, and intimate companionship—and the conscious appreciation of beautiful objects as among the greatest goods that exist in themselves. He also defends a principle of organic unity, according to which the value of a complex state of affairs is not necessarily equal to the sum of the values of its constituent parts. First, explain Moore's view of the relationship between these two goods: does he treat friendship as an intrinsic good independent of aesthetic enjoyment, or does he reduce one to the other? Second, consider two hypothetical lives: Life A is rich in deep personal affection but almost entirely lacking in aesthetic experience, while Life B offers abundant aesthetic appreciation but is largely devoid of intimate friendship. Given Moore's framework, can one consistently judge that Life A is closer to the ideal than Life B, or does the principle of organic unity block such comparative assessments altogether? Defend your answer with reference to how value attaches to mental states and to combinations of goods.

One student wrote the following. Moore clearly regards friendship and aesthetic appreciation as central parts of the ideal, yet he actually treats friendship as valuable primarily because it produces a distinctive mental enjoyment that is structurally continuous with the pleasure we take in beautiful objects; in other words, personal affection is not a fully independent intrinsic good but rather a particular source of the same kind of value that art supplies. Regarding organic unity, Moore holds that whenever goods are combined their joint value always exceeds the sum of the separate values, which implies that only a life containing both friendship and beauty can approach the ideal. Consequently, comparing Life A and Life B is impossible within Moore's framework, because any attempt to rank them would require treating the value of friendship and the value of beauty as separately quantifiable and then adding them up, precisely the kind of calculation that organic unity forbids. The student also notes correctly that Moore places intrinsic value in the conscious appreciation of the observer rather than in the physical properties of the object itself, so that Life B's possession of artifacts without companionship or perception would contribute little.
