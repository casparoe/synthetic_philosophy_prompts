I'm preparing a ninety-minute advanced undergraduate seminar on hinge epistemology and Wittgenstein's On Certainty with about fourteen students who have read selections from the text but haven't yet been given any technical vocabulary. I'd like you to design an in-class simulation—call it the Hinge Debt Game—that makes them feel, through structured action rather than lecture, why certain beliefs must stand fast if the practice of giving and asking for reasons is to remain possible. The students should discover that there are framework commitments that can't be doubted without collapsing the very activity of doubting, and they should experience this as a practical pressure before they learn the name for it.

Divide the class into four groups of three or four and give each group a concrete practical case—such as certifying a bridge load, diagnosing a patient from a chart, or authenticating an archival record. Each group must build a public justification chain of at least six linked reasons on cards. Once the chains are displayed, assign roles within each group: one Practitioner who must defend the judgment, one Radical Skeptic who may challenge any card in the chain, one Hinge Monitor who must identify when a challenged card is functioning as a framework condition rather than an empirical premise, and one Observer who tracks whether each doubt is active—actually stopping the practitioner—or idle. The Skeptic attacks upward through the chain and scores points by breaking empirical links; however, the Monitor may freeze a card by declaring it a local hinge, at which point the Skeptic may no longer demand further evidence for it but may only ask what specific practice collapses if it is false. After about twenty minutes, introduce a framework crisis: each group swaps case files and must apply its own deepest frozen hinge to the new case. They will discover that every chain carries an unacknowledged hinge debt—silent framework commitments that accumulate across practices and are not unique to any one justification.

Please provide a precise timeline with minute marks, the materials needed (cards, role sheets, a simple point scheme that doesn't require real money), rules for the freeze mechanism, and a debrief that maps specific moments in the game onto Wittgenstein's distinction between empirical propositions and hinge propositions. Be frank about what the simulation cannot reproduce—specifically that Wittgenstein's hinges have a grammatical rather than strategic status, and that the Monitor's decision to freeze is only a pragmatic stand-in for a logical necessity that doesn't depend on anyone's choice. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong on the first run—likely that Skeptics will treat hinges as ordinary premises and Monitors will over-freeze to protect their chain—and how to correct it without destroying the experiential point. Write everything in American English, do not ask students to cite specific page numbers, and do not include a bibliography, reading list, or any request for citations.
