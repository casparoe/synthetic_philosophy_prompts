During a discussion in my philosophy of social science course, two students—Maya and Leo—debated whether Sen's liberal paradox survives a twist they added: instead of two private individuals choosing books, they imagined two co-authors of a shared dataset, each retaining sovereign authority over whether to publish their own portion, but each also holding intense normative preferences about the other's publication choice. Maya insisted that Sen's standard domain-restriction reply still works, because principle P applies separately within each researcher's sovereign domain, and the Pareto conflict only appears if one wrongly extends P across domains; she added that Leo's concern about shared scientific norms is external to Sen's formal framework. Leo responded that this assumes preferences are exogenously given and domains are pre-social, which fails in collaborative inquiry where norms are co-constituted; he further argued that Sen's retreat to minimal liberalism is especially unstable here, since granting one researcher veto power over the other—based on nothing more than a strongly held moral preference—would undermine scientific transparency in a way that feels normatively wrong, not just formally paradoxical. Maya answered that Leo is smuggling in substantive commitments about scientific openness that Sen deliberately brackets, and that endogenous preference formation complicates boundary-drawing without dissolving it. Leo then claimed that if boundaries are genuinely indeterminate in practice, minimal liberalism collapses: either every researcher gets full liberal rights that paralyze collaboration, or we abandon liberal protection altogether for majoritarian or hierarchical decision rules, leaving no coherent middle position. They asked me to referee, and I want an adjudication rather than general commentary. Please evaluate their exchange step by step—addressing whether domain restriction holds in collaborative contexts, whether endogenous preferences erase boundaries, whether Leo illicitly imports scientific norms, whether minimal liberalism collapses without clear domains, and what both miss overall—delivering exactly five points in a numbered list, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion.
