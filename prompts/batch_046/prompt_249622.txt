I'm in a moral philosophy seminar this term and we've been reading Bernard Williams on integrity and that case where saving your wife involves one thought too many. I was arguing with my flatmate about where the line actually sits, and I realised my verdicts dont line up neatly. First, if a father pulls his own child out of a river without stopping to weigh numbers, that feels exactly right — he shouldn't be doing moral arithmetic. Second, if he pulls the child out but later admits he ran a quick mental check that saving her maximised expected wellbeing (his grief plus her years left), that feels corrupted, like the reasoning contaminated the act. Third, an artist who refuses to sell her life's work to fund a charity saving hundreds of lives — she says it's part of her soul and she can't betray it — strikes me as a legitimate integrity claim, not selfishness. But fourth, the same artist refuses the sale yet explains it by saying the emotional cost to her outweighs the social benefit and the charity might be inefficient; that feels wrong in a different way, shes treating her ground project as just another entry in a utilitarian ledger rather than owning it.

Then there's a fifth case thats bugging me: a doctor who is the only one able to perform a life-saving operation on a stranger refuses because she's in the middle of writing a novel central to her identity. Here my intuition splits — part of me says integrity can't excuse letting someone die, but another part says demanding she sacrifice her ground project is exactly the alienation Williams warns about.

So is there a coherent principle behind all this? Maybe impartial reasoning corrupts an act when it supplies the primary motive inside a ground project, but governs choices between projects and outside obligations? But then case four and case five clash — in four she's using impartial reasoning defensively, in five she's ignoring it defensively — and I can't tell whether my sympathy for case three depends on the magnitude of sacrifice or on the kind of project. Can you lay out a precise principle, or competing ones, stating which case motivates each clause, how it handles cases I haven't mentioned (like the artist's work being trivial but the charity saving thousands), and being honest about which of my five verdicts must go if I want simplicity?
