I am not an academic, but I have been thinking for some time about a distinction that keeps arising when people around me try to justify hard choices: the difference between a reason that actually moves someone and a reason that is supposed to be valid regardless of whether the person feels moved at all. I serve on a small volunteer panel that meets with people facing consequential life decisions—whether to accept a promotion that requires relocation, whether to continue long-term care for a relative, or whether to speak up about a problem at work. We do not tell people what to do; we help them examine whether the reasons they cite are genuinely rooted in what they care about, or whether they are borrowing authority from outside expectations that leave them cold.

Could you convert whatever philosophy genuinely knows about internal versus external reasons into a reusable procedural framework I can apply in these sessions? I need something like an ordered checklist or flowchart in plain language, with explicit decision points, that I can run in about twenty minutes without philosophical training. The framework must preserve the distinction that actually does the conceptual work—between a reason connected to the person's existing cares and one that is not—while dropping technical vocabulary that would confuse the room.

Please include: (1) a sequence of tests or questions I should ask in order, with the hard cases each test is designed to catch; (2) clear markers for the points where the procedure cannot eliminate judgment and I must make a human call; (3) three worked applications—one in which the procedure supports a course of action the person actively dislikes, one in which it reveals the person has mistaken an external social expectation for an internal reason, and one in which the framework genuinely stalls and requires my own judgment; and (4) an honest account of what this procedure cannot do, including the specific kinds of cases where it will likely misfire. Do not include bibliographies, citations, or reading lists, and avoid invoking niche theories or named debates I would not have encountered on my own; build only on ideas central enough to survive in general training. Write it so that someone with my background can apply it repeatedly without needing to look anything up.
