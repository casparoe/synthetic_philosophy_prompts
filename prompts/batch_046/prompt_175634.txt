Read the film Hotel Rwanda through the lens of Raphael Lemkin’s concept of genocide and the politics of naming atrocities. Rather than treating the narrative as a straightforward illustration of legal taxonomy, examine how it complicates the gap between moral recognition and official classification: for instance, how the identity-card system and hate-radio propaganda depicted in the film relate to Lemkin’s broader concern with the destruction of group culture, not only physical killing, and whether the Western refusal to apply the word genocide supports or undermines the idea that correct naming generates a binding duty to intervene. Which scenes best dramatise the tension between what is witnessed and what is officially named? Where does the film resist being reduced to an argument about categorisation, and what does its focus on individual moral choice amid bureaucratic denial contribute that philosophical analysis of genocide law alone tends to overlook?
