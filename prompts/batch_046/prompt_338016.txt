I've been working through the philosophy of biology literature for a graduate seminar, and I keep returning to the 2012 ENCODE Project Consortium announcement that roughly 80 percent of the human genome possesses biochemical 'function,' together with the immediate and forceful response from evolutionary biologists—Dan Graur being the most visible—who argued that biochemical activity is not equivalent to evolutionary function. I would like you to treat this controversy as a case study in the dispute between etiological (selected-effect) and causal-role accounts of biological function.

Specifically, map both sides onto the theoretical frameworks: what commitments about evolutionary history, current causal contribution, and the normative weight of functional attribution belong to the ENCODE researchers, and what belongs to critics like Graur? Then say what each account predicts about how the disagreement ought to have been settled—whether the absence of deep phylogenetic conservation should have ended the debate, or whether systematic biochemical assays should have prevailed. Beyond that, examine whether the episode vindicates the etiological view or exposes a structural limitation: in particular, if evolutionary history is genuinely silent about large stretches of non-coding DNA, does that entail those stretches have no function, or does it instead show that a causal-role analysis must precede any evolutionary attribution? Finally, assess whether these two accounts are genuine competitors or address different explanatory questions, and identify one specific feature of the debate—such as the functional status of transposable elements, or the relative authority of biochemical assays versus comparative genomics—that one framework illuminates clearly and the other leaves largely unaddressed.
