I run a philosophy discussion group focused on aesthetics and philosophy of mind, and we are preparing to examine Michael Ende's novel The Neverending Story through the lens of the paradox of fiction — specifically, the problem of how we can have genuine emotional responses to characters and events we know are invented. Rather than stay with the standard formulation that pits belief in non-existence against felt grief or hope, I want to use the novel's own structure as a philosophical experiment. Bastian Balthazar Bux is not merely a reader who sympathizes with Atreyu from a distance; he becomes an ontological participant in Fantastica, and the narrative explicitly warns that every story he invents reshapes the world he is trying to save. Meanwhile, the book Bastian reads is the same book we are reading, creating a recursive frame.

Please read the novel through this specific angle: does The Neverending Story resolve the paradox of fiction by dissolving the boundary between audience and fiction, or does it deepen the paradox by making Bastian's emotional responses to Atreyu's suffering simultaneously real within the diegesis and fictional from our external view? Focus on three scenes: Artax's death in the Swamp of Sadness, Bastian's refusal to speak the Childlike Empress's new name and thus allow Fantastica to be rebuilt, and the moment he finally breaks that paralysis by naming her. For each scene, ask whether Bastian's grief, reluctance, or hope is best understood as a standard fictional emotion, as a real emotion directed at an object that has become real-to-him, or as a third category — and whether the novel's meta-narrative framing forces us to treat our own emotional engagement as structurally identical to his. Where does the work illuminate the philosophical problem, and where does it resist being read as a mere illustration? Conclude by saying what this particular recursive framing adds to the debate that abstract argument about belief and imagination alone cannot capture: does it show that the paradox was never really about belief in the first place, or does it reveal a deeper tension between emotional investment and ontological commitment?
