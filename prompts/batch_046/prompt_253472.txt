I've agreed to run a session for our uni's philosophy society — mostly first-years but a few people from other departments who just come along — and I want to plan how to teach the guilt/shame distinction, but not in the boring way where you just list 'guilt is internal, shame is social' and call it done. The thing that's actually bothering me is whether a culture can deliberately choose to be a 'guilt culture' or a 'shame culture', and if that choice makes any sense given that shame can be internalised (like Bernard Williams's idea about the internalised other) while guilt can be totally performative and social. I was arguing with my flatmate about this last week and realised I don't actually know what order to put things in so that people don't just leave thinking shame is always the worse option.

So I'm not asking you to explain guilt and shame to me — I need a teaching plan. What's the dependency order: what concept has to land before you can even ask which one is the 'better' moral emotion? What's the one carrying example or scenario that should run through the whole session — and I don't want the classic 'cheating on exam vs being caught stealing' comparison because they've all heard that. Which misconceptions will they form at each stage — I'm guessing they'll think shame is just 'feeling bad in front of others' and that cultures pick one like a menu option — and exactly where in the sequence should I head each one off? I also need a check question at the end that shows whether they've actually grasped the idea that the choice between them isn't just cultural preference but connects to what kind of moral self you're trying to construct. Plus tell me what to leave out — maybe the anthropological literature on fear-cultures? — and say so explicitly.

The group is about twelve people, one hour, in a seminar room with a whiteboard. I'd like them to come away seeing that the 'which is better' question depends on whether you think morality is about internal consistency or about maintaining relationships, but I want them to build that tension themselves rather than me just stating it — they shouldn't just recieve the conclusion ready-made. Oh, and please don't suggest any readings or bibliographies — I don't want them hunting for papers they can't access. Sorry if this is a bit rambling, I'm writing this between lectures.
