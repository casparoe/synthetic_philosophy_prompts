Write a short story, in American English, about a municipal zoning inspector who has spent years constructing an exhaustive personal taxonomy of building-code violations, treating each classification as if it objectively determines his moral obligations rather than serving as an excuse. Center the plot on a single ambiguous case—an unpermitted garden shed erected by a neighbor he quietly resents—that forces him to recognize the taxonomy as a project of bad faith designed to conceal his radical freedom to approve or condemn. Show, rather than state, how he clings to the facticity of regulations to deny his transcendence, and include a moment in which he experiences Sartre's concept of the Look mediated not by another person but by the silent presence of the shed itself. Take a definite position on whether he ultimately embraces his freedom or retreats further into bad faith, and defend that ending with explicit reasoning. After the story, provide a brief author's note identifying the liberties you take with Sartre's framework or the contested interpretations you rely on—such as whether bad faith can be directed toward inanimate structures or whether the Look can genuinely be mediated by objects.
