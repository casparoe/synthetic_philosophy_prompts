I'm writing my final paper for a seminar on philosophy of history and environmental causation. We have examined how pathogens, climate anomalies, and volcanic events are treated as historical actors without reducing history to environmental determinism. Rather than rehearse familiar narratives about geographic destiny or the Black Death's labor-market effects, I want to examine a conceptual tension I keep encountering but have not found named precisely: what I might call retrospective causal inflation. In cases such as the Tambora eruption and the subsequent global cooling and harvest failures, historians often treat the volcanic event as an inevitable structural constraint, yet they treat the contemporaneous political and economic responses as contingent choices. The further an event recedes, the more environmental causes seem to expand in explanatory weight, while human agency contracts to a reactive role.

This raises three interrelated conceptual questions, and I am unsure which are genuinely upstream.

First, is there a defensible distinction between attributing narrative agency to a non-human force—treating an eruption or an epidemic as an actor with initiative—and treating it as a boundary condition that exposes institutional fragility?

Second, does the historiographic tendency to credit human ingenuity for successful adaptation to stress but to blame environmental shock for collapse constitute a systematic asymmetry that artificially strengthens determinist readings?

Third, can we formalize a middle category—something like structural vulnerability amplification—where an environmental shock is neither a sufficient cause nor a mere background condition, but a catalyst that activates pre-existing systemic failures?

If I must prioritize two for a philosophically rigorous but historically concrete paper of about fifteen pages, and I lack access to primary archival material, which ordering makes the most sense? Specifically, I want to know: which question is most tractable given existing secondary literature; which one, if resolved, would most change how we evaluate other cases; and which should be deprioritized because its answer depends on settling one of the others first? Please defend the ranking by reference to criteria such as conceptual tractability, downstream dependency, and relevance to avoiding determinism in historical explanation.
