I am a novelist working on a narrative in which a scanning technology reconstructs a person's complete neural architecture in a distant location without destroying the original body. In my scenario, the reconstruction retains every explicit memory of the original life, but the emotional coloring attached to those memories has been filtered out, so the duplicate recognizes the past without feeling the original's present attachments to it. Meanwhile, the original continues to live, gradually aware that another being shares her biography yet experiences none of her current affective bonds. I want to understand the conceptual structure behind Derek Parfit's teletransporter argument—specifically, whether his claim that psychological continuity is "all that matters" in survival can survive a case in which continuity is partial, bifurcated, and coexisting. If relation R connects the original and the duplicate only for cognitive states but not for emotional or evaluative ones, and if both individuals persist with equal claim to the past, does Parfit's framework require us to conclude that neither fully survives, that both survive with diminished weight, or that the very idea of "what matters" fractures once it is no longer anchored to a single continuing subject? Please explain how psychological continuity is supposed to ground personal identity, what happens to that grounding when continuity is distributed across two persisting beings with divergent inner lives, and whether the "all that matters" thesis implicitly requires uniqueness of the continuing subject in order to remain coherent.
