I am writing from the perspective of a student currently enrolled in an upper-level seminar on personal identity and the philosophy of mind, where we have recently examined Dennett's brain-transplant scenario—often discussed under the heading Where Am I?—in which Hamlet's brain is removed and kept alive in a vat while his body is sent on a dangerous mission, producing the puzzle of whether Hamlet is located with the brain, with the body, or nowhere in particular. If I understand Dennett's conclusion correctly, he resists treating the self as a Cartesian point and instead proposes that personal identity is anchored to a center of narrative gravity: a fictional but functionally real locus defined by the continuity of an autobiographical story rather than by the spatio-temporal location of any particular biological substrate. With this in mind, I want to press the framework on a hypothetical extension that my instructor mentioned only in passing—what she called a bifurcated narrative stream, in which Hamlet's cognitive architecture is duplicated across two physically separate bodies, each maintaining an identical, uninterrupted first-person narrative. My confusion is this: if identity genuinely tracks this narrative center, and the center is now instantiated in two non-interacting substrates, does Dennett's view entail that Hamlet is literally one and the same person present in two locations simultaneously? Or does the account implicitly carry a uniqueness constraint—perhaps that a center of narrative gravity must be anchored to a single spatio-temporal trajectory—so that duplication does not yield two Hamlets but rather a single Hamlet whose location has become metaphysically ambiguous? I am especially interested in whether the problem of other minds takes on a different form here: if I encounter both bodies, am I confronting one other mind whose location is uncertain, or two other minds that happen to share every mental property?
