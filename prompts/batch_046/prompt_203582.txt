You are a senior examiner in philosophy of history designing a final exam for an upper-level undergraduate seminar. Before drafting, think carefully about the precise crux that separates students who genuinely understand the covering-law versus narrative explanation debate from those who have only memorized standard labels such as Hempel, Gallie, or Mink. Then write one essay question (specify 90 minutes, 1,200 words), a model answer of about 600 words, and a marking scheme that distinguishes an A answer from a B answer by naming the specific analytical moves each must contain and the specific mistakes that should cost marks.

The question must not be answerable by reciting summaries of the DN model, narrative form, or configurational comprehension. Instead, inject the following niche conceptual twist: ask students to evaluate whether narrative explanation is genuinely epistemically autonomous from covering laws or merely a heuristic packaging of unstated generalizations, using a concrete constructed scenario—an institutional labor-arbitration board in 1927 that collapses despite structural economic and legal conditions that should have guaranteed its survival, with the collapse traceable to a specific sequence of misread internal documents and contingent personal commitments rather than to determinative macro-forces. Force students to identify exactly where the covering-law model fails in this case beyond generic appeals to "context" or "meaning," to show where narrative explanation risks unfalsifiable storytelling, and to adjudicate the tension with a clear conclusion.

The model answer must make four specific moves: (1) reconstruct how a covering-law explanation would have to proceed via generalizations about institutional behavior and show why those generalizations are either false or vacuous here; (2) trace the unique causal-interpretive narrative sequence that explains the collapse; (3) examine whether that narrative implicitly relies on psychological or sociological generalizations, and if so whether that undermines its claimed autonomy; (4) deliver an explicit, reasoned verdict on whether autonomy is sustained.

In the marking scheme, specify that an A answer executes all four moves, especially the critical adjudication in (3)–(4), while a B answer describes both sides and applies them descriptively to the scenario without resolving whether narrative explanation is reducible. List two predictable misconceptions—e.g., conflating narrative explanation with "subjective feeling" or treating the failure of the covering-law model as proof that no explanation exists at all—and indicate exactly where marks should be deducted for each. Do not ask for bibliographies, citations, reading lists, or references to specific page numbers. Write in formal exam-register language.
