I am drafting a novel whose protagonist, a neuroethicist, summarizes for a student what she terms the modular distribution thesis. She says—citing an unnamed review—that consciousness across animals should not be treated as a single binary property but as a cluster of separable capacities: affective valence, spatial integration, temporal binding, and narrative self-reference. Because these capacities dissociate—octopuses displaying rich spatial integration but weak temporal binding, corvids the reverse, and many invertebrates perhaps only the first—there is no coherent threshold dividing conscious from non-conscious beings. From this she draws two conclusions: first, that the problem of other minds for invertebrates dissolves, since we need only establish the presence of one module rather than full consciousness; second, that moral obligations ought to be disaggregated, meaning we can owe one form of consideration to an animal for its sentience and another, or none, for its lack of self-reference, so that moral patienthood becomes capacity-specific rather than creature-specific.

The framing strikes me as plausible for a character, but I fear it may smuggle in misrepresentations. Does the functional dissociation of cognitive capacities genuinely support a disaggregated ethics of this kind, or does it conflate empirical distribution with normative assignment? Is the claim that other minds dissolve into modular detection accurate, or does it misrepresent the epistemic challenge? And are there established positions in animal ethics that genuinely defend this kind of modular, non-binary moral patienthood, or is the view a distortion of more familiar gradualism? I would value an audit of which parts are sound, which are subtle conflations, and whether the conclusions survive correction.
