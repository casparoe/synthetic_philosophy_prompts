I'm drafting a chapter on literary form and historical theory and want to read García Márquez's One Hundred Years of Solitude through Braudel's concept of the longue durée—specifically his claim that geography and climate operate as nearly immobile structures that constrain, without fully determining, the event-level actions of individuals and groups. Rather than treating Macondo's endless rain, droughts, and banana-company heat as mere atmospheric symbolism, I want to ask whether they function in the novel as genuine structural actors in Braudel's sense: do they delimit the possible futures of the Buendía family in the way Braudel argues the Mediterranean's physical environment delimited ancient economies?

But I also want to push this into a formal question that is not usually raised in standard literary readings. The novel's cyclical repetition of names, fates, and weather patterns looks like a deterministic long-duration structure, yet its magical-realist ruptures—Remedios's ascension, the yellow butterflies, Melquíades's manuscript being deciphered in a single instant—seem to collapse generations into instantaneous events. Does the novel ultimately dramatize Braudel's hierarchy of temporalities (geographical time over social time over event time), or does magical realism collapse that hierarchy so that the "slow" structures are revealed to have always been narrative effects—stories told in a certain rhythm rather than material constraints?

Could you help me work through this? Specifically:

1. Which scenes treat climate or geography as an active constraint on human choice rather than background?
2. Where does the cyclical structure feel like an iron long-duration law, and where does a singular or magical event genuinely break it?
3. If we take Braudel seriously, does the novel's ending—when Macondo is erased by wind and the family line ends—count as an event-level catastrophe, or as the inevitable working-out of a geographical-social structure?
4. Does the novel add anything to Braudel's philosophy of history that Braudel's own historiography misses—perhaps about how people experience the invisibility of the structures they live inside?
