Read Hamlet's graveyard scene in Act V, scene i through Zhuangzi's philosophy of death as transformation (hua) and the dissolution of fixed self-boundaries, but frame the comparison around a specific conceptual tension: Hamlet's revenge obligation depends on the persistence of his father's specific identity and moral claim across death, an assumption that appears incompatible with Zhuangzi's metaphysics of universal flux and the equalization of things (qi wu). Traditionally, Hamlet's encounter with Yorick's skull is read as a memento mori that underscores the vanity of earthly distinction and the inevitability of decay. A Zhuangzian counter-reading might treat the skull not as the annihilated remainder of a unique individual but as one continuous transformation of qi among others, where the jester's laughter and the prince's grief are equally transient configurations lacking an enduring substrate. Yet if personal identity is fully dissolved into such flux, it becomes unclear how Hamlet can be bound by a duty that presupposes his father remains a distinct moral claimant rather than a dispersed element of universal nature. I want a reading that takes both the dramatic logic and Zhuangzi's metaphysics seriously: does Hamlet's horror at the skull's anonymity reflect a failure to achieve the sage's detachment, or does it correctly recognize that revenge, like grief, requires preserving particular identities that a philosophy of universal transformation cannot sustain? Does Hamlet's eventual acceptance of mortality in the play's final moments approximate the equanimity Zhuangzi displays upon his wife's death, or does the play's closure—where Fortinbras inherits a narrative of state continuity—demonstrate that Shakespeare ultimately privileges historical and moral persistence over metaphysical flux?
