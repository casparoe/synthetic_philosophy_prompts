I'm delivering the opening remarks next Thursday at a workshop I've convened called "When Peers Part Ways," held in a seminar room that will hold about twenty people: half are philosophers of science who have spent years with the conciliationism and steadfastness literature, and half are practicing epidemiologists and climate modelers who have lived through recent forecasting disputes but have never read a word of the epistemological debate. The occasion is functional rather than ceremonial—I need these remarks to establish that peer disagreement is being treated as a genuine epistemic problem, not a social inconvenience, and I also need them to quietly signal that I am personally caught in exactly such a dispute right now. A senior colleague and I co-authored an analysis of a shared dataset, and we have arrived at contradictory interpretations; she believes I should conciliate and lower my confidence in our joint conclusion, while I believe the disagreement is largely manufactured by different background priors rather than by any failure of reasoning on either side. I want the speech to turn on the conciliationism-versus-steadfastness tension in a way that makes both philosophical camps feel heard but also unsettled, particularly by pressing a niche question that feels live for me: whether the demand to split the difference weakens when the conflict is rooted in priors rather than in shared evidence, and whether steadfastness is rational only if one can identify the specific source of the peer's error, or if that identification is always available only in hindsight. The remarks should be polished and formal, continuous prose without headings or bullet points or bold text or tables, roughly five to seven minutes aloud, suitable for a mixed audience that includes family members of one of the invited scientists who will be sitting in the back row. They should not summarize the literature, cite texts, or offer a reading list; they should move like a letter, shaping what can be said in the workshop by framing the central tension personally and philosophically at once, and they should end by posing a single framing question for the sessions that follow: is there any principled boundary between a disagreement that demands conciliation and one that permits steadfastness, or does the boundary itself collapse once we admit that every peer dispute ultimately traces back to priors we never fully share?
