I've been arguing with my uncle about whether to keep an old civic monument, and it made me think about something I can't name. He started by saying we have to preserve the statue exactly because it's part of our continuous heritage — like history is this fragile chain we can't break. Then five minutes later he was comparing reformers to founding fathers who tore everything up and started fresh, using them as heroic models. Then when I brought up that the monument celebrates a pretty ugly period, he said well we have to learn from those mistakes and move past them, almost like history is dead weight we need to clear away. He did all three in one conversation without seeming to notice they pull in different directions.

I just finished reading Nietzsche's essay on the uses and disadvantages of history for life — the one with monumental, antiquarian, and critical history — and I recognize the three moves he lays out. But he seems to treat them as separate temperaments or needs, not as something one person switches between oppertunistically in a single debate. Is there an established term for this specific failure mode where someone cycles through all three? Or is it just ordinary hypocrisy? I want to know exactly how it relates to Nietzsche's framework — does he anticipate this kind of mixed mode — and if there's no name, what should we call it? Also, how is it different from just being selective with evidence, since each move is individually reasonable?
