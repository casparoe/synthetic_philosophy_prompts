Read Jean-Pierre Jeunet’s film *Amélie* (2001) through the framework of Hume’s “Of the Standard of Taste,” but treat Amélie’s orchestrated interventions in strangers’ lives—the tactile guided tour she gives a blind man through Montmartre, the revenge she engineers for the abused grocer, the reunion she stages for the photo-booth couple—not as mere whimsy, but as a case of aesthetic paternalism that sharpens Hume’s calibration problem. Hume claims that true judges possess delicacy of imagination, freedom from prejudice, and good sense, yet he seems to identify them partly through their convergence on correct judgments, leaving an apparent circularity: we know the standard by the judges, and the judges by the standard. Amélie acts as a self-appointed true judge, selecting which small sensory details others ought to notice and feel. Does the film present her curation as a resolution to that circularity—taste refined through guided, shared practice—or as its corruption, producing sentimentality rather than genuinely autonomous sentiment? Focus on two or three specific scenes, and ask whether the work complicates Hume’s account by showing that the social transmission of taste may require consent to be legitimate, and that beauty engineered for another person might be experienced by them without ever being truly perceived as their own. End by saying what the film adds to the philosophical debate about aesthetic testimony and the autonomy of judgment that argument alone does not capture.
