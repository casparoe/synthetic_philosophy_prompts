question: Rousseau's Letter to d'Alembert is usually summarised as 'theatre corrupts morals,' but he actually builds a more specifc argument about the kind of civic life Geneva needs. Explain his distinction between passive spectacle and active particpation, how it connects to his worry about amour-propre, and whether he can consistently condemn Parisian theatre while having written Narcisse and Le Devin du Village himself.

student answer:
Rousseau's main point is that theatre turns citizens into passive spectators rather than active participants in public life. In Geneva people already have public festivals where everyone joins in, but a theatre would replace that with people just sitting and watching actors pretend. He thinks this is dangerous because watching representation makes you confuse fiction with reality, and then you start performing your own life for others instead of being authentic. That's the amour-propre part — theatre feeds vanity.

He also says Geneva is too small and virtuous to survive the luxury and expense of a theatre, which is really just an aristocratic institution. So he wants to copy Sparta's ban on all arts, not just theatre, because Sparta had the right kind of civic religion to keep people honest.

The tension with his own plays is only apparent. Narcisse was written before he developed his mature view, and Le Devin du Village is different because it's a pastoral opera about simple villagers, so it doesn't involve the kind of representational illusion he's attacking. In fact, he allows music and dance if they are participatory, but he draws a hard line at representational art. So his position is consistent: the form matters, not just the content.

Also, in the Social Contract he says republics need a civil religion to stop corruption, and Geneva lacks one, so that's another reason theatre would be especially harmful there.
