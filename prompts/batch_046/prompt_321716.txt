hey, drafting a short essay for a seminar and want feedback before i commit to the full version. my thesis is that pascal's fragment about justice and force — the bit where justice without force is powerless, and force without justice is dangerous — is usually taught like proto-weber sociology, just describing state power. but i think that's off. reading it alongside his notes on custom, the fall, and true vs human justice, it looks more theological: human justice is always corrupt because of original sin, so coercion isn't an external add-on, it's the only thing keeping society from collapsing. the 'justice' part is mostly imaginary, people just call custom by that name.

the weedy part: i want to apply this to what i'm calling bureaucratic invisibility. modern states hide the sword inside procedures, algorithms, forms — nobody sees the coercion, so people think they're governed by reason. pascal would probably see this as worse than open tyranny, because now you've got force with no honest admission of it, and people confuse procedural fairness with actual justice. i link this to his idea that custom becomes second nature and we mistake it for eternal law.

the tension i'm wrestling with: if all human justice is corrupt and backed by force, why does pascal defend obedience to existing authority? my answer is that rebellion just swaps one corrupt system for another, plus it adds the sin of pride. so the right move is internal detachment — obey outwardly, know inwardly that it's all custom and force.

does this stretch pascal too far? is the bureaucratic invisibility thing too anachronistic? am i right that he distinguishes true justice (christian charity) from human justice (custom + coersive power)? i want accuracy without just repeating the standard intro-course reading. also sorry for the typo's, i wrote this on my phone.
