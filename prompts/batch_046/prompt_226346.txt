I'm the chair of a clinical ethics consultation service at a mid-size teaching hospital, and my board is repeatedly asked whether a clinical team may ethically proceed with a protocol or treatment plan that depends on a mechanistic or predictive claim for which the available evidence is genuinely ambiguous — not clearly false, but not supported to ordinary clinical standards. I need a reusable decision procedure, grounded in William James's defense of belief in certain forced, live, and momentous cases and W. K. Clifford's universal prohibition against belief on insufficient evidence, that my service can apply in about twenty minutes per case. The deliverable should be an ordered checklist in plain language: the conditions to test in sequence, what counts as satisfying or failing each, where the procedure stops with a clear prohibition on proceeding, where it stops with a conditional permission contingent on institutional safeguards, and where it explicitly defers to human judgment because the issue is one of values rather than epistemic rules. Be explicit about which steps are genuine judgments disguised as procedural checks, flag the conditions under which the procedure is likely to misfire — for instance, when a team frames an option as forced that is actually optional — and state what the procedure cannot resolve, such as whether hope can outweigh the duty to avoid error or whether an institution can hold a belief in the same way an individual can.

Apply the procedure to three cases:
1. A Phase II immunotherapy trial that excludes patients using a biomarker whose predictive value rests only on contradictory preclinical data and one small retrospective cohort.
2. A compassionate-use request for an unapproved gene-editing therapy for a single pediatric patient with a rapidly progressive disorder, where the mechanism is plausible but animal studies show both efficacy and lethal toxicity, and the family presents the choice as proceeding or watching death occur.
3. A decision whether to continue enrolling in an adaptive platform trial after an unexpected cluster of adverse events that might indicate a true risk or a statistical fluke, where stopping abandons current participants but continuing requires assuming the risk is manageable.

In each case, run the checklist, identify the exact point where judgment cannot be eliminated, and report the procedure's output. Include at least one case in which the procedure permits proceeding but you anticipate I will find that result unwelcome, and explain why the procedure does not block it.

Finally, say how confident you are in the reconstruction of James's and Clifford's positions underlying the checklist, and single out the specific point about which you are least certain.
