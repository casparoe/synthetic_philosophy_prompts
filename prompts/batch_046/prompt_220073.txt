I was chatting with my editor and some friends over coffee about a feature I'm drafting on populatoin axiology — specifically whether adding more happy people is always a win, or if there's a hidden catch that makes big populations weird. I have some gut reactions to hypothetical worlds, and I'm trying to figure out what principle, if any, holds them together, or if I'm just being inconsistent.

Here's what I think. Take World A: a billion people living lives at happiness 100, full of art and love and all that. Now take World B: a hundred billion people living lives barely worth living — happiness 0.5, just enough that they'd preferr existing to not. My intuition is that A is way better; I don't buy the repugnant conclusion. But then, if I start from A and ask whether I should create one extra person at happiness 0.5, my gut says yes, that's a tiny improvement. So adding a barely-positive life is good in isolation, but scaling it up to massive numbers suddenly feels wrong.

Then there's the neutral case. World C is A plus ten billion people at exactly 0 — not suffering, not enjoying, just in a kind of permanent dreamless sleep, if you can call that a life. I think C is neither better nor worse than A. So neutral lives don't move the needle.

But here's the weird one I made up for my article, the 'duplicate shelf' scenario. World D has a thousand unique people at happiness 80, with rich social lives. World E has ten thousand people, but they're perfect psychological copies of those same thousand, arranged so they never interact and don't know about each other — each copy is at 80. Even though E has ten times the total happiness, I feel like D and E are roughly on par, or maybe D is even a bit better. There's something about duplication that doesn't stack the way raw numbers suggest.

So my verdicts are: reject B over A (reject huge barely-positive populations), accept adding one barely-positive life to A, treat neutral additions as irrelevant, and apply some kind of discount or ceiling to duplicated lives. Is there one account that delivers all four? If not, which verdict should I overturn? And whatever principle you land on, what does it say about a case I haven't listed — say, a world where happiness is distributed unevenly but everyone is above some critical threshold?
