I have been thinking about the institutional architecture of post-surgical neuropsychological care, specifically the protocols used with patients who have undergone total corpus callosotomy for intractable epilepsy. In the assessment phase, clinicians routinely employ a bilateral testing paradigm: visual stimuli are presented tachistoscopically to the left or right visual field, motor responses are collected separately through verbal report (left hemisphere) and contralateral manual pointing (right hemisphere), and the results are recorded as two distinct hemispheric profiles. The same clinic, however, operates under a unitary-patient protocol for all legal, ethical, and therapeutic purposes. A single informed-consent document is executed—almost always via verbal agreement and right-hand signature, channels dominated by the left hemisphere—one medical record is maintained, and if the hemispheres generate conflicting responses to the same clinical question—say, the left hemisphere verbally endorses a proposed intervention while the right hemisphere points to its rejection—the conflict is resolved by deferring to the verbal response and treating the organism as having spoken with one voice. This arrangement strikes me as philosophically loaded. What conception of personal identity, agency, and the unity of consciousness must be in place for it to be rational to collect data as though two cognitive systems are present but to act as though only one will exists? Reconstruct the implicit theory: does the practice strictly presuppose that linguistic narration marks the authoritative boundary of the self, or is it compatible with several competing accounts—perhaps a functionalist view that privileges reportable integration, or even a dual-subject view that simply treats administrative unity as a practical fiction? Furthermore, distinguish what the protocol genuinely requires from what merely fits it well. If we were to take the hemispheric conflict not as measurement noise but as evidence of genuinely separate centers of preference and experience, what concrete revisions would be necessary in consent law, clinical record architecture, and the assignment of rights, and which of those revisions would be administratively intolerable versus philosophically unavoidable?
