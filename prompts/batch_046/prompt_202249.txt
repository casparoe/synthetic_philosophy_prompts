I am writing a proposal for a one-semester independent-study fellowship—approximately 5,000 words—to examine whether Simone Weil’s critique of political parties can be reconstructed as a critique of epistemic partisanship in modern deliberative institutions. The question has become personally urgent since I served on a citizens’ assembly in my own municipality and observed how quickly collective deliberation generated narrative loyalty even in the absence of formal party structures. Precisely: does Weil’s argument that parties manufacture collective passions incompatible with justice apply with equal force to non-party deliberative bodies—such as sortition-based assemblies—when they exhibit analogous dynamics, and can her remedy of judgment unbound by collective affiliation be scaled institutionally without collapsing into either atomism or expert authoritarianism? Existing treatments of Weil’s call for the abolition of parties tend to read it either as a historical anti-party manifesto or as an extension of her religious mysticism; they rarely extract a portable model of judgment-corruption, and they almost never test that model against contemporary deliberative-democratic design. My method will be conceptual reconstruction followed by institutional stress-testing: first, derive from Weil’s published writings on justice, obligation, and attention a formal account of how collective affiliation distorts epistemic openness; second, apply that account to documented dynamics in citizens’ assemblies, using publicly available evaluation reports rather than original empirical fieldwork; third, evaluate whether any institutional architecture—such as rotating micro-juries with explicit narrative-refusal protocols—can preserve Weil’s conditions for truthful judgment at democratic scale. The obvious alternative would be a purely historical exegesis or a purely empirical study of assembly dynamics; I reject the first because it cannot address institutional design, and the second because without a philosophical model of judgment-corruption the empirical findings remain descriptive. The project succeeds if it yields a clear taxonomy: conditions under which Weil’s critique extends beyond formal parties, conditions under which it collapses without them, and one institutional design that survives the stress-test. It fails if Weil’s notion of attention proves inseparable from her Catholic theology, rendering secular reconstruction arbitrary, or if the heterogeneity of assembly reports blocks any philosophical generalization. Given the semester limit, I will restrict theological interpretation to her published essays rather than her notebooks, and I will confine empirical comparison to three well-documented national cases.
