I am drafting an essay that combines disjunctivism in the philosophy of perception with predictive-processing accounts of illusion. The core claim is that the argument from illusion presupposes a shared mental factor not only in experience but in precision-weighted prediction-error dynamics: veridical seeing and hallucinating share a sensory input profile but diverge in whether hierarchical models are revised. I want to extend this to artificial vision systems, arguing that an adversarial misclassification is not an illusory perceptual state with shared content-structure, but rather a case where precision-estimation fails and the system updates its model in a way that has no analog in correct classification. I also plan to address the objection that disjunctivism cannot explain why illusions feel like veridical perceptions, by suggesting that the resemblance is not a shared phenomenal quality but a post-hoc confabulation driven by metacognitive monitoring. Please comment on whether this accurately captures standard disjunctivist commitments, whether predictive processing genuinely supports the denial of a common factor, and whether extending the view to machine vision is conceptually coherent. I am particularly interested in which premise is weakest and what specific counterexample would most seriously threaten the argument.
