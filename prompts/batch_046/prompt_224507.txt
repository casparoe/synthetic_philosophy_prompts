I'm working on a novel where two philosophers—let's call them Mara and Leo—are stuck in a bar argument about whether guilt or shame is the better moral emotion, and whether a culture can actually choose between them. I need them to sound like real people who know thier stuff, not strawmen. The usual 'guilt is internal, shame is social' framing is too shallow for this scene, so I'm injecting a slightly weirder idea: that guilt works like a private ledger where you're auditing yourself, while shame depends on what I keep calling a 'visibility architecure'—a structure of being-seen that can be internalized so thoroughly it feels private even when it's relational.

Mara should defend shame. She should argue that a culture can redesign that visibility architecure—making moral failure a communal spectacle rather than an internal audit—without destroying autonomy, because internalized visibility isn't social contingency but a form of relational identity. Leo should defend guilt. He should say that once morality depends on being seen, you've outsourced conscience to social contingency, and no culture can 'choose' shame without giving up the idea of a private moral law. But they can't talk past each other: Mara needs to address Leo's outsourcing objection head-on, and Leo needs to respond to her claim that a private ledger is really just internalized visibility wearing a mask. I want real back-and-forth, not just trading slogans.

Can you write that dialogue? Keep it sharp and evenly matched—both should land punches. Write it in American English, please. And don't worry about citing anyone; this is just texture for my book.
