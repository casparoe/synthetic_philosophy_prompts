So there's this thing in both animal welfare law and AI governance now where regulators sort entities into four cognitive tiers — Darwinian (just hardwired reflex), Skinnerian (learns by reward/punishment), Popperian (can model counterfactuals), and Gregorian (uses language/tools of culture) — and they assign rights or oversight levels based on which tier you land in. Like, a Gregorian creature gets full moral standing, a Popperian gets partial, Skinnerian gets welfare-only, Darwinian gets nothing. But here's the weird part: in practice they apply it inconsistantly. A highly trained LLM gets classified as Gregorian because it processes symbols, so it gets ethical review boards, but a chimpanzee that uses sign language and teaches its kids gets stuck at Popperian because it "lacks cultural transmission of abstract tools." Meanwhile a simple reflex-based robot is Darwinian and gets no protections even if it suffers in some functionalist sense.

What view of mind and moral status does that sorting practice actually presuppose? Does it require that only Gregorian-level cognition grounds full moral rights, or could a sentience-based view still justify the tiers? Is the practice assuming the four-tier ladder is also a moral heirarchy, or is it just using it as a tractability heuristic? And what's with the inconsistency — does the legal system actually need the four-tier model, or is it compatible with a flat view where suffering alone matters? If we replaced the tier system with something like "moral standing tracks capacity for suffering regardless of cognitive architecture," what parts of current regulation would have to change?
