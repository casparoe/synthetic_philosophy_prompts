Design a teaching plan for a single ninety-minute philosophy-of-language graduate seminar. The audience is eight MA students who have just completed Quine's Word and Object and have never read Davidson; your task is not to explain Davidson's radical interpretation to me, but to plan exactly how you will lead them through it.

Your plan must specify:

1. The dependency-ordered sequence in which you will introduce the conceptual components—radical translation, the principle of charity, the holism of belief and meaning, and the conditions for attributing intentional content—together with the justification for each ordering constraint (for instance, why belief-attribution and meaning-fixation must be presented as interdependent rather than sequential).

2. One concrete carrying example that will structure the entire session. This must not be Quine's "gavagai" case. Describe a specific scenario in which an interpreter encounters a speaker who applies an ordinary noun—say, "glass"—in a context that also suggests an idiosyncratic belief about the physical properties of windows, so that Quinean indeterminacy permits resolution either by meaning-shift or by localized belief-error. State precisely what the example must contain (an ambiguity that charity resolves globally rather than locally) and what it must lack (no exotic physics, no direct mental-state access, no predictive device stronger than human judgment). Explain why this structure is necessary to make Davidson's point visible.

3. A misconception map identifying at least four predictable errors, including at least one carried over from their Quine background—treating charity as a merely pragmatic heuristic for translation rather than a constitutive condition for having beliefs and meanings at all—and at least one modern misconception, such as the claim that radical interpretation cannot apply to artificial language systems because they share no world and possess no consciousness. For each misconception, specify the exact point in the sequence where you will defuse it and the move you will use.

4. A closing check for understanding: one targeted question or brief exercise that demonstrates whether the students have grasped the interdependence of belief and meaning under charity, rather than having simply memorized the principle of charity itself.

5. One substantial Davidsonian theme—such as triangulation, the rejection of the scheme/content distinction, or the Tarskian truth-theoretic apparatus—that you will explicitly name but deliberately leave undeveloped, with a brief justification for deferring it.

Constraints: Provide no reading list, bibliography, or citations. Do not include verbatim quotations from Davidson's texts. The plan should be fully executable without web access.
