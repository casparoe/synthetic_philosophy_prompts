I am a doctoral student in moral psychology and philosophy of medicine. At my university hospital's clinical ethics consultation service, consultants routinely classify family objections to proposed treatment plans into two categories: 'authentic value commitments,' which merit accommodation even when they foreseeably cause harm, and 'distorting moral biases,' which the service treats as correctable through counselling or substituted judgement. The classification relies on an institutional checklist that evaluates temporal stability, coherence with the patient's prior life narrative, and the intensity of emotional investment—criteria adapted from preference-satisfaction models but applied directly to moral conviction. What puzzles me is that the service simultaneously maintains an official stance of broad moral pluralism, framing most ethical disagreement as a clash between incommensurable personal preferences rather than as correctable error.

What philosophical commitments does this practice presuppose? Must the distinction between an accommodated value and an overridden bias presuppose that some moral judgements track objective facts—about welfare, about the boundaries of the self, or about the conditions for autonomous authority—or can it be reconstructed within a consistently subjectivist framework, perhaps by appealing to higher-order preference coherence? Does the reliance on narrative continuity and diachronic stability as sorting criteria implicitly commit the institution to a particular theory of personal identity, one in which temporal unity is a precondition for legitimate moral authority? And is the practice genuinely consistent with its declared pluralism, or does it quietly depend on a form of moral realism about harm and autonomy that its official policy denies?

Please reconstruct the implicit theory with precision, distinguishing what the practice strictly requires from what merely accompanies it. Identify points where the practice is philosophically underdetermined—whether rival accounts of moral judgement and personal identity could license the same institutional rules. Finally, please be explicit about what would change your mind: which argument or empirical finding—say, evidence that narrative coherence metrics are culturally variable in ways that undermine their authority, or a compelling subjectivist explanation for why instability undermines moral legitimacy—would lead you to a different assessment of the practice's commitments, and where your reasoning is most vulnerable to failure.
