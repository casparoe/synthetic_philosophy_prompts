Bertrand's paradox for a random chord in a circle—where uniform randomness over endpoints, over radii with perpendicular orientation, and over interior midpoints yields probabilities of 1/3, 1/2, and 1/4 respectively for the chord exceeding the side of an inscribed equilateral triangle—has been debated since the late nineteenth century, yet the literature repeatedly returns to the same repertoire: symmetry appeals, demands for a specified physical generating mechanism, and accusations that the principle of indifference is either incoherent over continuous spaces or perfectly rehabilitated by transformation-group invariance. I want a diagnosis of why this debate has stalled rather than a survey of the answers.

My working hypothesis is that the disagreement is partly verbal—turning on whether "equally possible" denotes an epistemic symmetry in belief or a structural invariance in parameter space—and partly a hidden divergence over the question being asked, with one camp treating the problem as normative epistemology (how should a rational agent assign credences given total ignorance?) and another treating it as applied probability (what measure does a specified physical randomization process induce?). But I also suspect a deeper, concealed formal issue: that the sides implicitly accept different constraints on admissible parameterizations, analogous to gauge-fixing conditions in physics, and that the paradox is merely a symptom of an unresolved question about whether a homogeneous space admits a canonical measure independent of representation.

Diagnose the stall using what the main positions actually say—those who defend a refined principle of indifference via symmetry groups and invariant measures, those who reject uniform priors over continuous spaces altogether, and those who insist the problem is ill-posed without an explicit physical mechanism—to determine whether the impasse is verbal ambiguity, different questions pursued under a single heading, incompatible starting intuitions that neither side can argue for, or a disguised empirical or formal disagreement about canonical parameterization. Is there a hidden dependency on a formal result about uniqueness of invariant measures that, if settled, would dissolve the dispute? Or does the pluralism of answers indicate that the underlying question is genuinely ill-posed?

Would an explicit distinction between epistemic and physical interpretations of randomness restructure the debate?
