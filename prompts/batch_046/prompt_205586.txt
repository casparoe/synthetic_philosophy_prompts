I have recently become involved in revising a family-trust instrument that contains contingent beneficiary clauses—specifically, provisions referring to "the first child born to my sister after the grantor's death" and, in a digital-assets section, "the certified governance algorithm that will administer this portfolio under whatever regulatory framework is in force in 2040." The document treats both descriptions as fully operative: rights can vest in them, conditions can be evaluated against them, and the trust remains enforceable even if neither referent ever materializes. This strikes me as philosophically puzzling. What commitments about reference, existence, and linguistic meaning does this legal drafting practice presuppose? Must it assume a Russellian analysis, according to which definite descriptions are quantificational devices that carry no existential commitment? Or does it implicitly rely on a Meinongian view that non-existent objects can genuinely bear properties and enter into legal relations? Could a deflationary or speech-act account of legal language explain the same practice without any metaphysical commitments about reference at all? Please reconstruct the implicit theory—or theories—of reference embedded in this convention, clearly distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what merely happens to be compatible with it, and indicate where the institutional design is philosophically underdetermined. In your response, separate what is settled from what remains genuinely contested among philosophers of language and legal theorists, and do not present any contested interpretation—such as the claim that Russell's theory alone licenses these clauses—as the consensus view.
