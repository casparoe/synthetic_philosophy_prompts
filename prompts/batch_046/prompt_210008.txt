Is there an established term for the following pattern? In debates about welfare reform or speech regulation, I often see an interlocutor defend a position by appealing to “liberal” principles, but the meaning of the word silently shifts with the audience. To a European listener, the speaker invokes classical liberalism—limited state, negative liberty, market openness—to suggest that the policy protects individual freedom; to an American listener, the same speaker invokes social liberalism—equality of opportunity, redistributive justice, progressive reform—to suggest that the policy advances collective fairness. The two versions are not a defensible core and an indefensible extension; they are genuinely incompatible political philosophies that happen to share a label. The speaker does not appear to be deliberately equivocating—often they seem unaware of the slide—but the result is that the claim is shielded from criticism in either context by borrowing authority from the other. It is not quite motte-and-bailey, because there is no strategic retreat to a weaker position; nor is it plain equivocation, since there is no single hidden meaning being substituted. Is there a recognised concept in rhetoric, political philosophy, or linguistics that captures this specific cross-Atlantic lexical oscillation used as an argumentative shelter? If not, could you propose a precise name and definition, with clear boundary conditions that distinguish it from both motte-and-bailey and from standard polysemy?
