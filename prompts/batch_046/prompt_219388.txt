I am trying to work out my own view on presentism in historiography: can historians ever fully escape the standards of their own time, and when does using those standards become a distortion rather than an illumination? To organize my intuitions I have started distinguishing three species in my own mind—evaluative presentism (applying our moral norms to past agents), explanatory presentism (using our causal or theoretical categories to account for past events), and hermeneutic presentism (interpreting past concepts or practices through our own conceptual schemes). But I am not sure whether these are genuinely separate or just faces of one deeper problem. Here are my verdicts on concrete cases.

Case 1: A historian writes that eighteenth-century slaveholders were “morally depraved individuals” without noting that slaveholding was legally protected and widely accepted in their society. My verdict: this is evaluative presentism and should be avoided; we can condemn the institution, but attributing personal depravity without contextual qualification erases the difference between violating a shared norm and violating one that did not yet exist for them.

Case 2: A historian explains the failure of the 1848 revolutions by saying the proletariat suffered from “the absence of modern class consciousness.” Verdict: this is explanatory presentism and is distorting. It imposes a Marxist theoretical category that was not operative for the actors themselves and therefore mischaracterizes their motivations.

Case 3: A historian describes a twelfth-century Islamic jurist’s reasoning about legal analogy (qiyas) as “an early form of analogical reasoning in decision theory.” Verdict: this is hermeneutic presentism, but I think it is acceptable. It is a conceptual translation for modern readers, not a claim about what the jurist believed he was doing.

Case 4: A historian uses modern epidemiological models to estimate mortality during the Black Death and compares the outcomes to modern pandemic responses, without blaming medieval doctors for not knowing germ theory. Verdict: this is acceptable and not presentist. The comparison highlights structural differences in knowledge and resources rather than imposing anachronistic obligations.

I keep going back and forth on this one.
