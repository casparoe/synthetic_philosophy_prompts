I am preparing a module for my advanced philosophy of language seminar on nested interpretation, and we have been examining the tripartite distinction—associated with Douglas Hofstadter's Gödel, Escher, Bach—between the frame message, the outer message, and the inner message, asking precisely where meaning resides when these layers fail to align. Rather than revisit the standard illustrations every student has already encountered, I want you to construct an original thought experiment, confined to a single paragraph and set in an ordinary institutional context such as a layered protocol within a clinical decision-support system or a nested corporate directive, in which one directive is embedded within both a framing instruction and an outer contextual message. Engineer the case so that locating meaning in the frame requires action A, locating it in the outer message requires action B, and locating it in the inner message requires action C, with none of the three outcomes being patently absurd. To block the usual resolution through speaker intention or conversational implicature, inject a specific structural twist—call it frame inversion—wherein the framing layer and the embedded core exchange functional roles in a circular manner that prevents any non-arbitrary determination of which layer is genuinely framing the other. After presenting the scenario, demonstrate the verdict that each of the three location-theories delivers and explain why, then isolate precisely which feature of the case performs the isolating work, and describe what happens to the divergence if that frame-inversion feature is removed. Please separate what is settled about multi-layered interpretation from what remains genuinely contested among philosophers of language and cognitive scientists, and do not present a contested view as the consensus. Present everything in continuous prose only, without headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, as one might write in a detailed letter to a colleague.
