I'm working through Plato's treatment of drunkenness for a seminar on ancient ethics and have constructed an argument that seems to prove the philosopher-king can never exist. (1) In the Laws, the Athenian Stranger institutes supervised symposia in which citizens drink wine under a sober magistrate, claiming that alcohol loosens the soul and thereby reveals whether one's nature is gold, silver, bronze, or iron; this revelation, he says, is indispensable to correct civic education. (2) For this test to work, wine must actually alter the cognitive and affective structure of the soul: if one's rational control remains fully intact, no hidden disposition is exposed, and the magistrate learns nothing. (3) In the Symposium, Alcibiades reports that Socrates once consumed more wine than anyone present yet was never seen in a state of drunkenness, and the dialogue treats this immunity as proof of an almost superhuman philosophical self-mastery. (4) If Socrates is Plato's paradigmatic philosopher, then perfect philosophical virtue includes precisely this immunity to alcohol's soul-loosening effects. (5) So the ideal philosopher is the one soul that cannot be tested by the symposion, since his rational architecture is never loosened. (6) Yet according to the Laws, undergoing this revelatory exposure is a necessary condition for the education that produces virtuous citizens and, by extension, the philosophical rulers who design the institutions. (7) Therefore the philosopher-king must both be immune to the test (to possess perfect virtue) and subject to it (to have been educated). (8) This is contradictory, which suggests Plato's account of philosophical development is incoherent.

I'd rather not surrender (2) or (4), since they seem central to the texts, but I'm open to rejecting (6) if that doesn't collapse the whole framework. Where is the weak link? Is there an equivocation between the civic test for ordinary citizens and the formation of the philosopher? Or does Socrates' case actually undermine (4) in a way I haven't seen? What does giving up each alternative cost?
