I run a seminar on moral psychology and political violence for upper-level undergraduates—about 16 students, 90 minutes, meeting once. I want an in-class simulation that makes participants feel the explanatory tension between situational, cultural, and dispositional accounts of perpetrator behavior, specifically the debate between Browning’s study of Reserve Police Battalion 101 and Goldhagen’s argument about eliminationist antisemitism and voluntarism. The activity should not be a lecture or case study; participants should experience the pull themselves through structured roles, hidden incentives, and staged choices.

Design a runnable activity with rules, roles, and small stakes—no real money is needed, but there should be social or procedural costs and rewards. The scenario should be fictional and abstracted—not a direct reenactment of the historical unit—but structurally analogous: a unit receives a sequence of escalating directives, and members must decide whether to comply, delay, delegate, or resist, with consequences distributed through peer observation and role-based obligations. Crucially, inject a mechanism that forces participants to generate their own post-hoc justifications in writing at two decision points; these should be collected anonymously and then revealed during the debrief. Also build in a hidden split: half the group operates under a bureaucratic-efficiency framing and half under an ideological-purification framing, with the framing revealed only after the second round.

The debrief should map specific moments in the simulation onto Browning’s situational factors—peer pressure, division of labor, career concerns, gradual escalation—and Goldhagen’s cultural-ideological factors—pre-existing eliminationist belief, voluntarism, cultural normalization. It should also address what happens to justifications that fit neither taxonomy cleanly—the residue—and ask whether that residue reveals a gap in both frameworks. Be explicit about what the simulation cannot reproduce, such as actual ideological indoctrination over years or real lethal consequences, and how the facilitator must supply that missing element verbally. Finally, predict what will go wrong the first time—for example, participants guessing the hidden framing split too early, or moral grandstanding derailing the escalation—and tell me how to handle it without breaking the illusion.

No lecture up front; the scholars and positions should not be named until the debrief. The participants should come away seeing that the same behavioral sequence can be generated by very different internal architectures, and that distinguishing them requires more than observing compliance.
