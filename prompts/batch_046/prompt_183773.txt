Having recently retired from experimental physics and begun reading in political philosophy, I have tried to work out my own stance on distributive justice and would welcome a consistency audit before I present it in a seminar. Here is the package I have assembled. First, I accept Nozick’s entitlement theory—justice in acquisition, justice in transfer, and justice in rectification—as the correct framework, and I find the Wilt Chamberlain argument convincing: if people freely pay Chamberlain twenty-five cents per viewing, the resulting distribution is just regardless of its inequality, and enforcing any patterned end-state requires perpetual coercive interference. Second, drawing on my scientific background, I treat natural talents as products of evolutionary and genetic contingency—much as physical constants are contingent outcomes rather than earned achievements—so that Chamberlain’s height, reflexes, and coordination confer no deep moral desert, and the portion of income attributable purely to these unchosen advantages should be subject to some collective claim rather than full private entitlement. Third, I hold that justice in initial acquisition requires a strong Lockean proviso: appropriation is legitimate only if it leaves enough and as good for others, which in a world of scarce land and climate stability is genuinely constraining. Fourth, I believe widespread historical injustice means most present holdings are tainted by broken acquisition chains, yet I also maintain that broad redistribution today is unwarranted because we lack the epistemic precision to match specific victims with specific perpetrators or heirs. Fifth, I think the entitlement framework should be amended for non-rival digital goods: copying information violates no one’s rights, so intellectual property is not a natural entitlement. Sixth, I reject any principle that requires continuous adjustment of holdings to match a desired pattern, even across generations.

These commitments feel individually defensible, but I worry they pull in incompatible directions. Does my acceptance of the Wilt Chamberlain argument survive my denial that Chamberlain morally deserves his talents? Can a strict Nozickian framework coexist with the strong Lockean proviso that Nozick himself rejected? Does my skepticism about present redistribution genuinely conflict with my belief that most holdings are tainted, or is that tension merely apparent? And does my amendment for digital goods undermine the coherence of the transfer principle rather than extending it consistently? Please audit this set: rank the genuine tensions by severity, identify which pairs only appear incompatible but dissolve on closer inspection, and specify the least costly revision—without assuming I am willing to abandon either the Wilt Chamberlain argument or the rectification principle. Finally, please say what would change your mind: indicate whether a refined argument about moral desert, a different interpretation of the Lockean proviso, or an empirical claim about the traceability of historical wrongs would lead you to reject one of these commitments, and flag the point in your reasoning that is most vulnerable to failure.
