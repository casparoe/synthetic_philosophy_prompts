Contemporary consciousness science operates with a striking institutional split. In the laboratory, researchers deploy no-report paradigms—training subjects to withhold verbal or behavioral responses during binocular rivalry or masking tasks—in order to isolate neural correlates of consciousness without the confounding activity of reporting mechanisms. The underlying assumption is that consciousness can be present, and its neural signature detected, even in the absence of any observable behavior or subjective testimony. Yet in clinical neurology, the assessment of disorders of consciousness relies almost entirely on behavioral scales that grade recovery through observable responses to command, generally withholding the attribution of consciousness from patients who show no such behavior regardless of what neuroimaging might suggest. Alongside these practices runs a philosophical framework that distinguishes a hard problem—explaining why physical processes give rise to subjective experience—from the easy problems of cognitive function, information access, and behavioral integration.

This tripartite pattern raises a puzzle. The laboratory paradigm seems to presuppose that consciousness is an objective neural state decoupled from both first-person access and behavioral expression. The clinical practice seems to presuppose that consciousness, at least in its medically relevant form, is essentially tied to observable functional capacities, closer to access or global integration than to raw phenomenal feel. The philosophical framing, meanwhile, treats subjective experience as an irreducible datum that any adequate theory must accommodate, yet neither the lab nor the clinic treats it as epistemically primary.

What implicit philosophical theory of consciousness—of its ontology, its epistemic accessibility, and its relation to cognitive function—does this institutional configuration actually presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit view, distinguishing what the practices strictly require from what they merely accommodate. Is the pattern coherent, or do the laboratory's objectivism, the clinic's behaviorism, and philosophy's phenomenal realism pull in incompatible directions? If one replaced the underlying view with a consistently first-personal, anti-functionalist account—one on which consciousness is essentially tied to subjective report and cannot be inferred from neural activity alone—what would have to change in both experimental design and clinical diagnosis? Conversely, if one adopted a fully reductive functionalism that dissolved the hard problem into an account of global information integration, could the philosophical framing survive, or would it collapse into a category error?
