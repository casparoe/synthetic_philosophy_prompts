As my own work on scientific taxonomy has turned toward the stability of category boundaries, I have found myself returning to Douglas Hofstadter's account of fluid concepts and the Copycat architecture, according to which concepts slip analogically within microdomains rather than obeying fixed definitions. I want to examine the International Astronomical Union's 2006 vote redefining "planet" and excluding Pluto through exactly this lens. Please analyze the episode—especially the analogical pressure exerted by Eris, Charon, and other Kuiper Belt objects, and the tension between orbital-clearing and geophysical-roundness criteria—as a case of conceptual slippage: what did the actors face, and does the framework explain why empirical clarity did not resolve the dispute? Identify which dynamics the theory captures well (for instance, the historical stretching and contraction of "planet" to include then exclude Ceres and Pluto) and which it misdescribes or overlooks (such as the institutional demand for a binary classification despite graded differences). Finally, assess what the case shows about the theory itself: does the IAU's eventual crystallization of a crisp boundary support the claim that scientific concepts are genuinely fluid, or does it reveal that institutional authority can artificially freeze concepts that remain analogically slippery?
