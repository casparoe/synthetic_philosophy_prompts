ok so my friend who's into philosophy of religion tried to explain molinism to me over coffee and said something like this: God has three logical moments of knowledge. First natural knowledge — every necessary truth and every possible world. Then middle knowledge — all the counterfactuals of creaturely freedom, like if Peter were in situation S he would deny Christ three times. Then free knowledge — what actually happens in the world God decides to actualize. The middle part is "between" the others because it's logically prior to God's creative will but still dependant on what free creatures would do, not on God himself. So God doesn't cause anyone's choices; he just surveys the feasable worlds, reads the counterfactual lookup table, and picks the one where free choices align best with his plan. That's how he gets complete foreknowledge without determinism. My friend also said Plantinga used this framework in the 80s to defend free will, especially with the transworld depravity idea — that in every world God could actualize with free beings, some evil occurs, so God picks the least bad option. He said the grounding objection, that these counterfactuals have no basis in reality, was basically dissolved in the 90s once philosophers showed they're just primitive modal facts about worlds. He concluded molinism is now the default view in analitic philosophy of religion, and that open theists reject it only because they deny God knows any future contingents. I'm suspicious. Does middle knowledge really avoid determinism if God is still selecting which world gets actualized? And is the grounding objection actually settled, or is my friend treating an open problem like it's solved? Also, doesn't the fact that God picks the world affect which counterfactuals are true? Can you audit this: what's accurate, what's a standard simplification, what's misrepresented — especially about the relationship between world-selection and freedom — and whether the conclusion that molinism is the "default" survives?
