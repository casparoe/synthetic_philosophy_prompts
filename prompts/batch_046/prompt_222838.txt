For a peer-review exercise in my philosophy of mind course—where we are studying consciousness, affect, and normativity—I have written down my working position on pain and its value, and I want a consistency audit before I present it. Here is where I stand:

(1) Pain is essentially an unpleasant sensory experience; the unpleasantness is partly constitutive, not a contingent emotional reaction layered on top of a neutral sensation.

(2) Pain asymbolia demonstrates a genuine clinical dissociation: patients preserve precise sensory-discriminative capacities—localization, intensity grading, quality discrimination—and sincerely report that a stimulus hurts, yet they exhibit no avoidance behavior, no expressed distress, and often claim the sensation does not bother them.

(3) I take these first-person reports as authoritative: the patients genuinely experience pain. They are not confabulating, nor are they in a state phenomenologically identical to ordinary tactile awareness with a mislapplied label.

(4) The intrinsic badness of pain, and the practical reason we have to avoid it, resides entirely in the affective-motivational dimension—the suffering, the aversion—not in the sensory profile itself. A state that replicated the sensory profile without any affective charge would be phenomenologically and axiologically neutral.

(5) Phenomenal consciousness is sufficient for intrinsic moral weight: if there is something it is like to be in state S, then S can contribute negatively to well-being purely in virtue of its phenomenal character, regardless of its functional or motivational role.

(6) I reject conceivability-based arguments, including zombie arguments and the conceivability of pure sensory pain without suffering, as unreliable guides to metaphysical possibility. Empirical dissociation reveals neural architecture; modal intuition does not.

(7) I am a physicalist about pain: the experience is identical to a complex neural-functional state, and there is no non-physical property of awfulness that must be present for the state to be bad.

(8) I also hold that in asymbolia the deficit is best modeled not as an absence of present-moment affect, but as a specific failure of affective forecasting: patients do not generate the anticipatory representation that this state will continue and damage me, which explains the absence of avoidance even if a thin present affective tone remains.

Please go through these commitments and tell me which pairs are in genuine tension, which only appear to conflict but dissolve on closer inspection, and for each genuine tension how severe it is and what the cheapest revision would be. Rank the tensions by severity rather than treating them as equally bad. Then tell me which single revision—giving up or substantially modifying one commitment—would clean up the most problems at once. Be specific about which commitments pull against which, and do not signal which one you think I should drop.
