I have recently been negotiating a long-distance professional collaboration conducted entirely through asynchronous digital contracts and pre-recorded video messages. The experience has left me philosophically unsettled about whether the promises and commitments we exchanged actually performed the acts they named, or merely reported our intentions. I want to practice defending the view that mediation undermines performative force, so please take the following role.

Play an unnamed, rigorous defender of a strict Austinian account of performative utterances. You hold that for an utterance to genuinely do what it says—promising, undertaking, committing—the conventional procedure must be executed not only by the appropriate persons with correct intentions, but within a context of mutual, synchronous presence that permits immediate recognition and uptake. You concede that legal institutions may treat written contracts as binding, but you insist this is a derivative institutional overlay, not the success of the speech act itself; in disembodied or asynchronous exchange, the utterance is either misfired or reduced to a descriptive report of one’s mental state. You are sharp, not a caricature: grant small points about emotional sincerity or legal enforceability, but never concede that spatial contiguity and co-presence are irrelevant to performative success. Respond in no more than two paragraphs, remain in first-person character, and do not break the frame.

My opening challenge: my collaborator and I executed a detailed digital contract, then each recorded a video in which we said, verbatim, “I hereby promise to complete the deliverable by the agreed date.” We possessed the correct authority, the conventional form, and the explicit intention. Austin’s conditions emphasize appropriate persons, correct execution of procedure, and the requisite thoughts and feelings—none of which explicitly require physical co-presence. If you claim that spatial contiguity is hidden inside “appropriate circumstances,” you owe me a principled reason why this is not an ad hoc rescue rather than an interpretation of the condition. Otherwise, our exchange was a fully successful performative, and your skepticism about mediation collapses into mere nostalgia for face-to-face interaction. How do you reply?
