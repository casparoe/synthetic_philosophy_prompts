I want to teach Hofstadter's specific Gödelian analogy: the move from arithmetic self-reference (a formula talking about its own provability) to the idea that a physical brain might instantiate the same kind of loop and thereby generate a subjective "I". Not "brains do math" — the structural analogy itself.

Plan the teaching arc for me, not the lecture. What has to be in place before I can even propose the analogy? I'm guessing I can't open with consciousness or they'll think I'm being mystical, but if I open with Gödel numbering they glaze over. For the carrying example, I want to avoid the usual "this statement is false/unprovable" paraphrase since they've seen it in every popular article. I was thinking of constructing a tiny formal language where one symbol literally denotes its own syntax tree — think of a Lisp s-expression that quotes itself — and then pivoting to ask: what changes if that symbol isn't just stored data but is being interpreted by a system as its own belief-state? Is that too baroque? What should the example possess and what should it deliberately lack?
