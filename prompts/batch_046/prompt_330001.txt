I've been working out my own view on psychiatric diagnosis—I'm not in the field, but I've read enough popular writing to form some opinions, and I want to check whether they actually fit together. Here is where I stand:

(1) Conditions like ADHD or major depression are real patterns of suffering, but they are "human kinds": the label loops back and changes the people it describes, so the category itself shifts as people learn about it.

(2) I think this looping effect is stronger in adults than in children, because adults have more capacity to reshape their habits, self-image, and daily behavior around a diagnosis.

(3) The steep rise in adult ADHD diagnoses since the early 2000s is mostly overdiagnosis—clinicians pathologizing ordinary distractibility—rather than proof that looping has actually manufactured new disorders.

(4) Even so, looping is powerful enough to generate genuinely novel symptom clusters over time, which is why diagnostic manuals should be revised every five to ten years to keep up.

(5) To protect people from harmful looping, doctors should reserve formal diagnoses for severe, biologically obvious cases and avoid labeling mild or ambiguous traits.

(6) Once someone receives a diagnosis, it should be treated as a stable, enduring fact about them, not something that might shift just because the manual is updated.
