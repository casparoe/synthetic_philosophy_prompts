As a novelist constructing a protagonist who lives with pain asymbolia—she registers tissue damage with precise sensory discrimination yet feels none of the usual aversive urgency—I find myself needing the underlying philosophical architecture rather than clinical case notes. Specifically, I want the full desiderata that any theory of pain's intrinsic badness must satisfy if it is to accommodate her condition without reducing her experience to mere cognitive absence or conflating it with ordinary suffering.

Please provide the specification: a rigorous list of what such a theory must deliver. For each desideratum, include the phenomenon it must cover—ordinary nociceptive aversion, asymbolic registration lacking affective-motivational force, phantom analogues, and the neuroscience of dissociated insular and cingulate processing; the distinction it must preserve—for instance, between badness as an intrinsic phenomenal quality versus as a derivative motivational consequence, or between absence of aversion and absence of awareness; and whether it is non-negotiable or belongs to the preferences of a specific camp, such as representationalist, imperativist, evaluativist, or affective-motivational accounts.

I also want one explicit criterion addressing what I will call narrative-integrative resilience: whether the badness of a pain depends on the sufferer's capacity to embed it within an autobiographical arc of anticipated relief and retrospective meaning, since my character's temporal self-understanding is precisely what the condition seems to fracture. After laying out the list, identify which single requirement does the most damage to the major existing accounts, and flag any pair of desiderata that current neuroscience suggests may be jointly unsatisfiable. Do not provide a reading list or citations; I need only the criteria, sharply delineated, with the reasoning behind each.
