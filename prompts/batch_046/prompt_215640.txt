I'm writing the final exam for my philosphy of mind course — third-year undergrads, not grad school, but they've read Dennett and maybe a little Baars. I want one essay question, a model answer of about 700 words, and a marking scheme that splits an A from a B by naming the exact philosophical moves each has to make.

Don't just ask them to recite the Cartesian theater vs multiple drafts distinction; I want it in the weeds. So frame it around this niche hypothetical view I'm calling "staged revisionism" — there's one canonical narrative stream, but it's assembled retrospectively by competing subsystems and then "published" into working memory by a global workspace mechanism that never witnesses it, only locks it in. The claim is this keeps unity without a Cartesian audience while avoiding Dennett's pandemonium where every draft is equally real.

The exam prompt should ask students to do three things: first, reconstruct Dennett's argument that the Cartesian theater is empirically and conceptually bankrupt, using either change blindness or split-brain confabulation (their pick); second, explain exactly why the multiple drafts model denies there's a canonical version of experience, and how that is meant to dissolve the theater problem; third, evaluate whether staged revisionism actually improves on Dennett — specifically, does the publication mechanism smuggle a spectator back in, and does Dennett's own view allow enough narrative coherence or does it collapse into something like eliminativism about the self?

Make it a 45-minute write-up, 40 marks total. The rubric needs to say that A answers must distinguish epistemic access from ontological multiplicity and must show why locking a draft isn't the same as observing it; B answers confuse Dennett with eliminativism, or treat the workspace mechanism as obviously Cartesian. Include the specific mistakes that should cost marks — for instance, saying Dennett thinks there's no self at all, or saying the publication step is just "the brain watching itself."

No bibliography, no citation demands — I don't want them guessing page numbers. Keep it meaningful for a model that can't search, but don't lean on obscure experiments they won't have memorized. One or two typos in the prompt are totally fine.
