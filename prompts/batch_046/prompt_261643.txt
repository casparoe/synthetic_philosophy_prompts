I am preparing a graduate seminar on moral luck and narrative ethics, and I would like you to read Ian McEwan's Atonement through the philosophical frameworks of Thomas Nagel and Bernard Williams, but with a twist that avoids the usual illustrative reading. Rather than treating Briony's false accusation of Robbie Turner as a straightforward case of resultant moral luck — harm that exceeds what she could have intended or foreseen — I want the analysis to center on what we might call narrative constitutive luck: the possibility that the kind of moral agent Briony is, and the kind of judgment we can render about her, depend not only on her psychological traits but on her authorial control over the very events that constitute her culpability.

Please examine the novel's structure, especially the metafictional revelation in the final section that much of the preceding narrative — including the redemptive reunion between Robbie and Cecilia — has been composed, revised, and selectively arranged by Briony herself as an aesthetic act of atonement. Ask whether this frame undermines Williams's notion of agent-regret, since the "facts" to which Briony responds are partly of her own narrative making, or whether it reveals a deeper form of constitutive luck: that she is the kind of person for whom moral repair can only take the form of fictional control over reality. I want you to move carefully between the philosophy — Nagel's taxonomy of resultant, circumstantial, and constitutive luck, and Williams's claim that rational regret need not imply full causal control — and the specific scenes of the accusation near the fountain, the wartime separation, and the closing authorial confession. Where does the text resist being mapped onto the philosophical categories? Does McEwan preserve a stable fact of the matter about Briony's moral standing despite her manipulation of the record, or does the novel dissolve the very conditions under which moral luck can be assessed? Do not offer a bibliography, reading list, or citations to secondary literature; I am interested solely in whether the work, read closely, can illuminate the internal limits of moral-luck theory.
