You are a senior philosopher of medicine and conceptual analyst with deep familiarity with philosophy of biology, clinical taxonomy, and normative theory. Think carefully: begin with a brief, unstructured brainstorm of the conceptual landscape, then move to a structured, defended agenda. A philanthropic funder will support about eight interdisciplinary researchers—philosophers, clinicians, and conceptual analysts—for three years on one tightly focused program in the philosophy of health and disease. The program must sit at the intersection of the naturalism versus normativism debate (whether disease is definable in purely biological-descriptive terms, as in biostatistical or evolutionary-dysfunction accounts, or necessarily involves normative judgments about harm, suffering, or undesirability) and a newer, under-theorized conceptual challenge: the clinical proliferation of "pre-disease" and risk-state categories—such as pre-diabetes, mild cognitive impairment, pre-hypertension, and analogous risk-labeling practices in psychiatry—that describe states which are statistically abnormal relative to population norms yet often functionally adaptive, or at least not clearly dysfunctional, in modern environments. Call this the adaptive-dysregulation boundary problem: it forces the question of whether classification should turn on evolutionary dysfunction, on present harm, on statistical deviation, or on some hybrid, and it has direct implications for how institutions communicate risk, justify intervention, and avoid over-medicalization.

The funder wants a dependency-aware prioritization of open questions and lines of work at exactly this intersection. Which questions are upstream of the others—such that settling them would reshape how the downstream ones are asked? Which are tractable for philosophers and conceptual analysts working without large-scale empirical labs, and which depend on clinical data that this group cannot generate? Which should be deprioritized because resolving them would not substantially change clinical practice or philosophical taxonomy? For each priority, specify what kind of argument or evidence over the next two to three years would cause you to reorder the list. Your deliverable must be a defended agenda for resource allocation, not a survey of the field, not a bibliography, and not a reading list. Do not provide citations, ask for references, invent specific paper titles, or quote texts verbatim. You may refer to well-known theoretical positions (for example, biostatistical naturalism, harmful dysfunction analysis, or holistic normativism) by name without citation, but do not depend on precise dates or page numbers. The answer should be written in American English and should focus on conceptual work that matters for institutional classification, patient communication, and intervention policy—not merely on abstract taxonomy for its own sake.
