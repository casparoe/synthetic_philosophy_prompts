Consider the view that phenomenal consciousness is not identical to access consciousness, and that global workspace theory explains only the latter. More specifically, hold the variant claim that phenomenal experience arises precisely when a representational state achieves global broadcast but is simultaneously prevented—by competitive inhibition at the working-memory interface—from being recruited as input for further reasoning or report. On this account, qualitative character is not an extra property but the structural signature of a blocked broadcast: globally available yet cognitively inert. I want an honest assessment of how exposed this position is.

What would have to be true for it to be wrong? Which empirical findings, conceptual results, or formal arguments would count against it, and which of these are live rather than merely logically possible?

First, address the empirical exposure. If neuroimaging revealed that phenomenal experience correlates not with broadcast suppression but with enhanced integration across prefrontal-parietal networks, or if patients with disrupted working-memory consolidation still reported full phenomenal experience without any corresponding sensory-cortical residue, how damaging would these be? Would a committed defender concede them as decisive, or could they be accommodated by adjusting the inhibition mechanism?

Second, examine the conceptual and formal risks. If the distinction between broadcast and blocked broadcast collapses into a mere difference in attentional weighting—something global workspace theory already handles—does the view reduce to standard access consciousness with an unnecessary appendage? Or if formal models of information integration show that any globally available state necessarily carries a functional profile matching access, does the cognitive inertness condition become incoherent?

Third, map the rival explanations that become more plausible if this view fails. Would we be pushed toward a pure access-consciousness account, toward a higher-order theory, or toward a non-functional property dualism? And does the view risk insulating itself by redefining blockage post hoc—interpreting every case of apparent phenomenal experience without access as hidden blockage, and every case of access without experience as hidden failure of broadcast—such that no observation could refute it? If so, is that insulation a theoretical virtue or a fatal flaw for a position of this kind?

Please rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, specify what defenders of the blocked-broadcast view would themselves concede as counting against them, and assess whether the position has been qualified into unfalsifiability—and if it has, whether that is a problem for a view that aims to be empirically grounded. I am particularly interested in whether any result—in neuroscience, in formal theories of consciousness, or in the philosophy of representation—would genuinely force abandonment rather than mere refinement.
