The answer reads:

"The heterogeneity problem is that pleasures seem too different—eating, solving equations, and feeling warmth—to share any single quality. Bentham addressed this by saying pleasure is just intensity and duration, so differences are quantitative. This avoids the problem because we can compare them on one scale. Mill, however, said some pleasures are qualitatively higher, like intellectual ones. This makes heterogeneity worse, since now we have two kinds with nothing in common at all.

Some philosophers in Ryle's tradition avoid the problem by rejecting inner feelings altogether: pleasure is just a disposition to seek repetition. That unifies chocolate-pleasure and proof-pleasure behaviorally. But a more precise response is attitudinal hedonism, where every pleasure is an attitude of being pleased about some state of affairs. What unifies them is not feeling but intentional content and direction of fit, so the heterogeneity problem dissolves. But if we apply the tonic/phasic distinction, tonic pleasures—like a general mood of well-being—lack the sharp peak that Bentham's scale measures. So tonic states are not actually pleasures; they are moods. Therefore hedonism should only apply to phasic pleasures. This means the heterogeneity problem is limited to peak experiences and is not a serious objection."
