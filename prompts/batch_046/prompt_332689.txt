I'm constructing a decision-tree instrument for a philosophy-of-mind workshop that classifies responses to the paradox of phenomenal judgment—the tension between causal inertness of qualia and our apparent ability to form true beliefs about them. Build the tree from logical commitments and concrete cases rather than from theory-labels, so a respondent can navigate it without knowing the literature. Use this root scenario: a subject reports, "this red experience is vivid"; the report and all its neural causes are fully specified; the phenomenal redness plays zero causal role in producing the report, in any correction of it, or in subsequent behavior. From there, structure splits by (1) whether the belief is true, false, or undefined; (2) if true, whether its accuracy is grounded in representation of physical correlates, in direct non-causal acquaintance, or in brute coincidence with no explanatory bridge; (3) whether evolutionary persistence of a phenomenal vocabulary can be explained by correlates alone or requires causal efficacy. Include a zombie-twin comparison—identical physical reports with and without experience—to test whether the respondent thinks any epistemic or behavioral difference must exist, and an error-correction case in which neural feedback revises a judgment without the experience contributing. Required leaves: physicalist denial of inertness (experience is a causal-role property); interactionist dualism (experience causes judgment despite closure worries); error-theoretic epiphenomenalism (all phenomenal judgments false or empty); representational-proxy epiphenomenalism (judgments track neural correlates and are true of them, with qualia as inert accompaniments); direct-acquaintance epiphenomenalism (non-causal access justifies true belief); and brute-correlation epiphenomenalism (true belief with no explanation). Flag branches that merge distinct named views—e.g., some neutral-monist and acquaintance-based dualist positions may land together—and note any empty combinations, such as accepting direct acquaintance while denying that acquaintance bears any structural relation to the physical state causing the judgment, diagnosing whether the gap is incoherent, unattractive, or unexplored. Every node must be phrased as a claim or case judgment, never as "are you an X-ist?" Limit the tree to six decision nodes; annotate each leaf with the view(s) hosted, the problem it solves, and the problem it inherits. Deliver this as a structured flowchart outline—branch conditions, split logic, leaf annotations—within about 450 words. Do not include bibliographies, citations, reading lists, or attributions to specific texts, since the instrument is meant for offline use.
