As a novelist constructing a historical narrative set among the mathematicians of Göttingen and Halle in the years before the First World War, I have been studying the conceptual rupture that occurred when Georg Cantor defended the reality of actual infinite collections against the older view that infinity exists only as an unending potentiality. In my notes I have reconstructed a passage from Cantor's writings of the 1880s, where he argues that treating an infinite set as a completed totality is not self-contradictory provided one accepts transfinite cardinal numbers, yet he cautions that without such numbers one falls into antinomies that confuse the potential for endless addition with the actuality of a finished whole. Meanwhile, my fictional antagonist holds a rigorously finitist position, maintaining that every mathematical object must be constructible in finitely many steps and that talk of a completed infinite collection is merely a façon de parler.

This tension becomes acute in my plot when the characters discuss an analogy later popularized by David Hilbert: a hotel possessing a countable infinity of rooms, every one occupied, which can nevertheless accommodate a new guest by shifting each current occupant one room upward. From my reading, finitists reject this not as false arithmetic but as an ontological misdescription: they claim the procedure describes only a rule for potential reassignment, never a simultaneous state in which every room is filled and yet an empty room exists. I am trying to understand precisely what Cantor's defense of the actual infinite contributes to this dispute—whether Hilbert's hotel genuinely demonstrates that a completed infinite set can have the same cardinality as a proper subset of itself, or whether the example merely formalizes an iterative process that finitists can accept without conceding the existence of actual infinity.

Could you help me understand, in terms suitable for shaping philosophically informed dialogue between my characters, the most important conceptual implications of this clash? Specifically, I want to grasp whether Cantor's acceptance of actual infinities resolves the paradox of the hotel as a property of being, or whether it only relocates the mystery into the axioms of set theory, leaving the finitist objection intact as a critique of metaphysical commitment rather than of logical consistency. I need conceptual clarity for narrative construction rather than a scholarly apparatus, so please explain the core distinction without recourse to modern bibliographies or specialized literature lists.
