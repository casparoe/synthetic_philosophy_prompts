As a novelist who often embeds conceptual puzzles in narrative form, I have been struggling with a knot at the center of my current manuscript: characters keep invoking "the alignment problem" to describe entirely different structural failures, and I am unsure whether they are disputing one phenomenon or merely speaking past one another with a shared phrase. To make the confusion precise, I have found myself distinguishing three facets that are routinely collapsed together. The first is a problem of specification—how contested, context-sensitive human values can be translated into objectives that an artificial system can actually pursue without misinterpretation. The second is a problem of stability—whether whatever objectives are specified will survive the system's own scaling, self-modification, or interaction with environments that fall outside the original design parameters. The third is a problem of delegation—who holds legitimate authority to decide which values deserve specification and protection in the first place, especially when those values conflict across cultures, generations, or institutional contexts. These three seem to pull in different directions: specification feels hermeneutic and philosophical, stability feels dynamical and technical, and delegation feels political and constitutional.

Write an essay on whether the alignment problem is best understood as a single, unified challenge with interdependent parts, or as a family of conceptually distinct problems that happen to share a name. Weigh the arguments for unity—for instance, that stability is meaningless without prior specification, or that delegation is itself just another value to be specified—against the arguments for pluralism, such as the claim that stability can be addressed through engineering safeguards largely independent of value content, or that delegation raises questions of democratic legitimacy that do not reduce to technical design. Reach a definite conclusion and defend it rigorously: should these facets be studied and governed as one problem, or separated into several? You need not cite specific authors or texts; develop the reasoning from the conceptual architecture itself, and do not end on an agnostic or non-committal note.
