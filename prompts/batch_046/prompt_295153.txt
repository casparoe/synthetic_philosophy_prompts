After 30 years in systems biology I retired and started reading philosophy of mind, and I've gotten stuck on pain asymbolia—those cases where people report pain but don't mind it, don't pull away, don't suffer at all. I'm now convinced this shows the unpleasantness isn't part of the raw sensation but a seperate evaluative module that can disconnect. So my view is that pain is just an interoceptive alarm with no built-in badness; the normative weight is all in the tagging, not the signal. That'd mean ethical arguments that treat causing pain as specially wrong because of its intrinsic phenomology are mixing up the detector with the danger—like saying a smoke alarm's beep is itself harmful. What would actually count against this? I want the serious defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward: which empirical results, which conceptual distinctions (sensory vs affective dimensions, maybe predictive-processing accounts of interoception), and which rival interpretations of the asymbolia data. Also say what my view predicts that rivals don't, whether it risks anything real or has been qualified into unfalsifiability, and if so whether that's actually a problem. Tell me which objections defenders of this view would concede versus which they'd brush off. And please seperate what's genuinely settled in the literature from what's still genuinely contested among people who know the topic—don't present a contested take as consensus.
