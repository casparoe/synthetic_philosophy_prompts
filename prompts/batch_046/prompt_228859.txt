I work as a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy, though I have been following an online course on philosophy of science and recently argued with friends about how mental-health classification actually works. I want to state a position I have been gravitating toward and ask precisely where it is vulnerable.

The view is this: psychiatric diagnostic categories — the kind clustered in manuals such as the DSM — operate as interactive kinds in the sense developed by Ian Hacking. Once a person receives such a classification, the label does not merely record a pre-existing condition; it enters the person's self-conception, reshapes behavior, alters the responses of clinicians and family, and thereby feeds back into the very phenomena being classified. I take this looping not as an epistemic distortion to be corrected, but as partly constitutive of the disorders in question. From this I draw a stronger conclusion: efforts to replace psychiatric taxonomy with purely biological or neurobiological schemes — ones that aim to classify mental conditions solely by underlying neural mechanisms — are conceptually misguided, because they treat mental disorders as if they were indifferent kinds like chemical elements, ignoring the fact that clinical and social meaning is woven into the conditions themselves. On this account, the looping effect is not a bug that undermines the reality or utility of these categories; it is a feature that explains why they remain indispensable despite heterogeneous biological correlates.

What I want is an honest assessment of what would genuinely count against this position. Which empirical results — for example, about the cross-cultural stability of symptom profiles when labeling practices differ, or about the clinical success of dimensional research frameworks that deliberately avoid categorical diagnosis — would be decisive rather than merely awkward? Which philosophical arguments show that being an interactive kind undermines, rather than supports, the authority of a clinical taxonomy? And what do defenders of this looping-constitutive view themselves concede as a real weakness — perhaps cases where looping is absent, or where harmful feedback renders a category clinically useless? I also want to know whether the position risks being qualified into unfalsifiability: if every failure of biological reduction is read as further proof that looping is unavoidable, has the view insulated itself in a way that is problematic for a framework claim of this sort? Please distinguish the evidence that should actually move me from the evidence that defenders could absorb without serious damage, and indicate whether that insulation, if it exists, is a fatal flaw or merely the cost of holding a view about constitutive social processes.
