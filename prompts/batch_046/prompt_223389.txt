As a retired biologist recently turned to philosophy, I am preparing a single session for a non-specialist community seminar on simultaneous invention—Newton and Leibniz, Darwin and Wallace—specifically whether discoveries are inevitable and whether attribution matters. Rather than opening with the calculus dispute, I intend to carry the session with an analogy to convergent evolution: the independent emergence of the camera eye in vertebrates and cephalopods from a common ancestor that lacked one. Do not explain the philosophy itself; plan how I should teach it. First, state the conceptual dependency: what must be secured—distinction between inevitability and contingency, between discovery and construction—before the evolutionary analogy can illuminate rather than mislead? Next, specify the carrying example precisely, and explain why the historical case should come second, not first. Then map the order in which the four components—historical inevitability, the contingency of formulation, the normative weight of credit, and the ontological status of the discovered object—must be introduced, noting which depends on which. Anticipate the misconception that convergence proves nature holds a single correct blueprint, and identify exactly where in the sequence I should defuse it. Anticipate also the scientist’s misconception that a discovery is like finding a pre-existing object in nature, and state the precise point in the arc where I should head it off. Give me one focused question for the end that reveals whether a listener has grasped the philosophical problem rather than merely retained the biological anecdote. Finally, state explicitly what to leave out—detailed biographical chronology, mathematical formalism, and any bibliographic references—and say so.
