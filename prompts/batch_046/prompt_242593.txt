I'm on the advisory council for the Midtown Historical Trust and this petition just landed in my lap—it's personal because my grandmother's letters are in the same correspondence file. The trust holds the papers of Elias Vorn, the 1970s planner behind the Riverside demolition that displaced hundreds of families. The collection has dry policy memos plus personal letters between Vorn and residents, including families pleading with him to stop. Now Vorn's descendants want a "right to be forgotten" applied: redacted from the searchable index, the correspondence pulled from public access, but the policy docs stay. They lean on that Rieff-style idea that cultures need therapeutic forgetting so wounds can actually close, not stay open in institutional memory. Other council members cite Margalit's mnemonic solidarity—if we erase the interpersonal record, we break the shared memory chain that binds the displaced community to what really happened.

Here's the twist that makes this not just another archive debate. The trust just launched an AI reconstruction exibit that synthesizes voices from the papers into an immersive narrative, so visitors can "hear" Vorn and the residents. The family wants Vorn's synthesized voice and the residents' voices pulled from that algorithmic layer, even if the raw papers stay in cold storage. So we're deciding whether forgetting can be layered: physical preservation but algorithmic suppression.

Please draft the short memo I'll circulate before Thursday's meeting. Start with the memo text itself—no preface, no "here is the memo"—then follow with the reasoning. Recommend a concrete course: approve, reject, or approve with conditions and a clear timeline. Take thier therapeutic-forgetting argument seriously, don't just restate archive law, and deal with the solidarity concern plus the weird fact that we're now governing what an algorithm is allowed to reconstruct.
