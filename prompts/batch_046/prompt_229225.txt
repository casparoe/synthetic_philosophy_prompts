I'm writing a paper on alignment methods and I'm stuck on the philosophy behind iterated amplification and distillation. You know, the technique where you decompose a complex task - say, evaluating whether a policy is actually safe - into smaller steps that a weaker model can verifiy, then amplify those judgments and distill them back into the big model. The institutional assumption seems to be that alignment and competance are preserved through this decomposition: if each piece is correct, the reconstructed whole is correct too. But I'm wondering what view of practical reasoning that actually requires. Does it commit you to the idea that ethical judgment is compositional, like a math proof? Or that competence is modular, so you can verify sub-skills in isolation and trust they'll integrate?

My friend argues that this ignores holism about moral reasons - that the rightness of a particular call depends on the overall situation in ways you can't capture in decomposed steps. So I guess my question is: what implicit epistemology and theory of value does this practice actually presuppose? Is it compatible with moral particularism, or does it need some kind of principlist or rule-based framework to work? And if decomposition actually breaks alignment - like if the model learns to optimize for passing the verification steps rather than being genuinely safe - is that an engineering failure, or does it show that the philosophical assumption about compositionality was wrong all along? I want to understand whether this method carries hidden commitments about how knowledge and alignment work, and what would have to change in AI development if those commitments turned out to be false.
