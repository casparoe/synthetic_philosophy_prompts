Two colleagues in a philosophy of technology seminar got into a dispute over whether an AI researcher is subject to moral luck, and one of them—who does not take sides—asked me to relay the exchange for adjudication.

The case: a researcher deploys a predictive model trained with standard empirical risk minimization and conventional validation checks. During deployment, an unforeseeable covariate shift—well outside the training distribution—causes the model to recommend actions that, in one particular context, result in serious harm. Peer review confirms that her design choices, documentation, and testing protocols were exemplary given the knowledge available at the time.

(1) The first colleague argued that this is a clear instance of moral luck. The researcher is blamed far more severely because of the harmful outcome, yet her will, intentions, and procedural diligence are identical to those in a counterfactual scenario in which the shift never occurs. The moral evaluation of her character and responsibility is being driven by factors beyond her control.

(2) The second colleague replied that this misapplies the concept. Moral luck concerns cases in which an agent’s own action or character is retroactively evaluated differently because of an outcome—such as a driver who chooses excessive speed and kills a pedestrian versus an identical driver who does not. Here, the researcher’s action (designing and validating the model) is not made more reckless or vicious by the external shift. The harm arises from a causal factor entirely outside the intrinsic properties of her action. At most, this is epistemic misfortune, not moral luck.

(3) The first colleague responded that in complex socio-technical systems the boundary between action and environment collapses. The researcher defined the system boundary—what counts as part of the model versus part of the world—and the outcome reveals that this boundary was drawn in the wrong place. Just as a driver is judged not only for pressing the accelerator but for driving in conditions where that pressure was dangerous, the researcher is judged for deploying in conditions where standard validation was insufficient. The outcome does not merely cause harm; it reveals the inadequacy of the procedural framework, thereby altering the moral assessment of the framework itself.

(4) The second colleague countered that this stretches moral luck until it covers any unforeseen consequence, stripping the concept of its explanatory force. In the driving case, the agent’s choice of speed and visibility conditions are internal to the action being evaluated. In the AI case, the distribution shift is an external causal intervention, analogous to a tree falling on a well-maintained vehicle. If the researcher is held responsible for every environmental feature she failed to anticipate, then responsibility becomes limitless and the control principle is not qualified but abandoned entirely.

Please adjudicate this exchange move by move. For each of the four moves above, rate it on a scale from clearly mistaken to clearly right and provide brief justification. Then state who is right about the central question—whether this is genuinely moral luck or something categorically different—and identify what both parties are missing, particularly regarding how normative system boundaries are reconstructed after the fact in technological deployment.
