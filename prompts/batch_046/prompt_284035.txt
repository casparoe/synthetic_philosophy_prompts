In my sociology of knowledge seminar, we have been working through a debate that seems to have stalled despite decades of scholarship: whether Weber's study of Protestant asceticism and the emergence of capitalist rationality can be understood as a claim that culture functions as a genuine causal force, or whether it is better read as an interpretive correlation that offers understanding without explanatory power. The pattern of disagreement is remarkably stable. On one side, empirical historians present regional counterexamples—flourishing Catholic merchant networks in early modern Italy, agrarian Calvinist communities in Scotland—and treat these as straightforward falsifications of any culture-driven thesis. On the other side, defenders insist that Weber never advanced a universal statistical law but traced what he called an elective affinity between an ethical orientation and an economic form, meaning that singular historical counterexamples miss the point of an argument about subjective motivation and meaningful connection rather than about constant conjunction.

My working hypothesis is that the impasse is not primarily about the archives but about the ontology of social explanation: one camp implicitly requires causation to take the form of covering-law regularity or mechanism, while the other operates with a neo-Kantian conception of adequate causation that permits singular meaningful links without universal correlation. But I am unsure whether this is the right diagnosis. Is the disagreement actually verbal—a confusion over whether "cause" and "explanation" apply to values in the same way they apply to physical events? Or are the two sides answering genuinely different questions, one about the psychological genesis of economic dispositions and the other about the macro-institutional conditions for capital accumulation, while using the same vocabulary? I want an honest structural diagnosis rather than a historical verdict: tell me whether this is a substantive clash about evidence and ontology, a verbal dispute that should be dissolved by distinguishing causal from constitutive explanation, or an ill-posed question that assumes culture must either cause capitalism or be epiphenomenal. And if you think progress is possible, specify what form it should take—a conceptual distinction, a formal model of cultural transmission, a change in the framing of the question, or an argument that no resolution should be expected and why.
