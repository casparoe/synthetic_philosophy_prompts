I sit on an institutional ethics review board that advises on food-system research and policy recommendations. Our published precedents are these. First, we endorsed shifting consumption away from conventional broiler chicken, citing both the direct suffering of the birds and the high wild-mammal and wild-bird mortality associated with feed-crop cultivation. Second, we opposed conventional feedlot beef on analogous grounds—direct slaughter plus crop-input and pasture-conversion harms—but we permitted limited rotational grass-fed beef on degraded, non-arable pasture, noting that it avoids feed-crop mortality and can restore soil carbon, despite the direct killing of cattle. Third, we endorsed bivalve farming (oysters, mussels) and well-managed plant-based diets as lowest-impact options, on the grounds that bivalves likely lack integrated sentience and require no crop or pasture inputs. Fourth, we rejected a blanket prohibition on all animal-derived foods based solely on indirect wild-animal suffering from agricultural land use, insisting that indirect ecological harms must be weighed against direct suffering, nutritional yield, and land-use substitution effects, rather than treated as absolute veto conditions. Fifth, we rejected a uniform tax or ban on all meat regardless of production system, ruling that moral evaluation must distinguish high crop-input systems from those with minimal or negative crop-input footprints.

We now face a new proposal for evaluation: large-scale commercial harvest of wild freshwater crayfish from restored riparian wetlands. The system involves no cultivation, no feed inputs, and no direct slaughter of farmed sentient animals; the crayfish are captured by passive trapping and killed by rapid chilling, and their sentience is uncertain but not clearly established. However, maintaining the wetland buffers for harvest requires seasonal mechanical clearing of aquatic vegetation, which displaces wild amphibian breeding populations (frogs, salamanders) that previously occupied these zones. Lifecycle assessments suggest that the indirect wild-animal mortality—displaced amphibians, plus energy-related climate impacts on aquatic invertebrates—per kilogram of protein is comparable to that of conventional chicken feed production, though distributed across different taxa and life stages. The direct suffering footprint is near zero, but the ecological footprint is substantial.
