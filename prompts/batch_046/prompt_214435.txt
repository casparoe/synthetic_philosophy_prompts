I am a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy, and I have been trying to work through Berkeley's master argument—the claim that one cannot conceive of an object existing without being perceived—by analogizing it to state verification in distributed systems. It seems to me that if you attempt to describe a server log entry describing a crash that occurred while no observer was present, any such description necessarily embeds an implicit observer (the reader of the log, or the logging daemon), just as Berkeley says any conception of an unperceived tree embeds the conceiver. However, I keep encountering the objection that Berkeley's point is restricted to direct sensory presence and does not extend to mediated or stored representations. This strikes me as an artificial boundary: if the master argument is genuinely about the conditions of conceivability rather than about physical proximity, then archived logs and database records should be ontologically on par with trees in an empty garden—both require an actual perceiver to have being, not merely for epistemic access. Is my inference mistaken? Specifically, does Berkeley's reasoning actually distinguish between direct perception and mediated storage in a principled way, or does the master argument, if taken seriously, collapse into a form of verificationism about all persistent information structures, including those in computer systems?
