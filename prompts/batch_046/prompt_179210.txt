As a journalist covering philosophy of mind and cognitive science, I have followed the long stalemate between those who treat consciousness as a "user illusion"—an evolutionary interface or representational fiction that hides neural computation behind a seamless phenomenal display—and those who treat such language as either self-refuting or as a category error that mistakes epistemic limitation for ontological absence. Both camps accept the same neuroscience, the same evolutionary framework, the same distinction between access and phenomenal consciousness, and the same failure to derive qualitative feel from functional architecture alone. Decades of argument have not produced convergence, yet neither has anyone identified a clear empirical crux—no brain scan, no computational model, no formal proof—that would compel either side to concede.

My working theory is that the disagreement is partly verbal and partly about hidden standards: the illusionist uses "illusion" to mean that phenomenal properties are structurally transparent representations which misrepresent computation as intrinsic quality, while the anti-illusionist uses "real" to require direct acquaintance rather than representational fidelity, so they talk past one another about transparency and ontological commitment. But I want a diagnosis rather than a survey. Is this stall caused by a semantic divergence around "illusion" and "representation," by a substantive disagreement over whether first-person reports are data or confabulation, by different criteria for what counts as an adequate explanation, or by an ill-posed question that treats "user illusion" as a single category rather than a family of representational failures?

Please diagnose the structure of the disagreement with reference to what each side actually says about representational transparency, the ontological status of phenomenal properties, and the conditions under which an experience could be said to misrepresent its own nature. I also want you to consider a specific tension that is rarely made explicit: if consciousness is a user interface in the strict evolutionary sense, it should exhibit representational closure—no leakage of underlying computational structure into the content of experience—yet first-person reports routinely reference attentional binding, cognitive effort, and temporal dynamics that seem to mirror processing architecture. Is this "leakage problem" a genuine empirical challenge to the interface view, a confusion about levels of description, or a red herring?

Tell me what kind of disagreement this really is—verbal, substantive, about standards of evidence, or about starting intuitions that neither side can argue for—and whether anything, such as a formal result on representational systems, a conceptual distinction between epistemic and ontological illusion, an experimental finding about metacognitive access, or a reframing of the question, could actually end the impasse or should not be expected to. Finally, say explicitly what argument or consideration would change your mind about this diagnosis, and identify where your reasoning is most likely to fail.
