In debates about whether artificial systems can possess a genuine self-model, critics frequently invoke Escher's Drawing Hands as an analogy for human consciousness: the mind is a tangled hierarchy in which higher-level representations loop back to shape the lower-level processes that produce them, yet there remains an "inviolate level" of biological substrate—neuronal wetware—that never gets fully drawn into the loop and thus anchors the system. From this they conclude that silicon-based AI cannot have a real self, because its hardware is just another representational layer with no analog to this inviolable biological base; defenders reply that the loop itself is sufficient and the substrate is inert. Audit this analogy systematically. Provide an explicit correspondence table: what plays the role of the left hand, the right hand, the pencil, the sheet of paper, and the supposed inviolate level in both the biological mind and the artificial system? Identify where the mapping collapses—consider whether the fixed two-dimensional plane of the drawing corresponds to any real constraint in computation, whether the absence of an external creator in the image smuggles in assumptions about autonomous causation, and whether neurons actually behave like untouched paper or rather like ink constantly being redrawn. Then assess whether the critics' conclusion—that AI lacks the structural precondition for a genuine self—survives once these disanalogies are explicit, or whether a different analogy, perhaps drawn from bootstrapping compilers or self-modifying code, better captures the relevant architecture.
