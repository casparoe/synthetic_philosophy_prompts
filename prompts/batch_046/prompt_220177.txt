As a software engineer without formal philosophy training, I recently heard Thomas Szasz's slogan that mental illness is a myth invoked in a debate about AI-driven psychiatric screening. The speaker used it to argue that building predictive models for depression or anxiety is conceptually confused—comparable to debugging software by measuring hardware temperature—because the conditions being predicted are not genuine diseases. I want to understand exactly what that slogan could be claiming, since "myth," "illness," and "mental" each seem to admit multiple precise readings. Could you lay out the distinct theses hidden in the claim that mental illness is a myth—for example, an ontological denial that psychological suffering exists, a biological denial that it has organic causes, a taxonomic claim that psychiatric categories fail to carve nature at its joints, a normative claim that diagnosis mainly serves social control, or a conceptual claim that "illness" is being applied as a category mistake? For each reading, please indicate whether it is trivial, substantive and defensible, substantive and false, or incoherent; and say which of these interpretations, if any, actually support the conclusion that algorithmic psychiatric diagnosis is misguided. I am not seeking bibliographies, citations, or a reading list—I need a disambiguation menu with verdicts that I can evaluate without searching the literature. One framing I keep returning to is whether a defensible version of the slogan treats psychiatric suffering as analogous to a software specification error rather than a hardware failure: does that analogy capture any legitimate reading, or does it smuggle in assumptions that collapse under scrutiny?
