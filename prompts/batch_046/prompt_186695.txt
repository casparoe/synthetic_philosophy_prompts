I am drafting a philosophical dialogue for a novel in which an ethicist-protagonist, speaking in the late 1970s, tries to defend hedonism against the heterogeneity problem and ends up constructing a chain that seems to dissolve the view into something unrecognizable. Here is the argument she builds, which I find suspicious because its conclusion appears to apply intrinsic value to non-conscious systems.

(1) Hedonism holds that pleasure is the sole intrinsic good.
(2) If pleasure is one kind of good, there must be a shared feature that unifies all pleasures—this is the heterogeneity problem.
(3) Phenomenal qualities cannot supply that unity: the bodily warmth of sunlight, the intellectual satisfaction of completing a proof, and the malicious delight of revenge share no common felt character.
(4) So hedonism must ground pleasure in something non-phenomenal—specifically, in a functional role: being desired for its own sake and reinforcing the behavior that produces it.
(5) But that functional criterion applies just as well to states that are clearly not pleasures: a thermostat maintains homeostasis through negative-feedback reinforcement, and an algorithm optimizing a reward function exhibits stable preference for its own outputs.
(6) If the hedonist adds an ad hoc restriction—say, that the state must be conscious—then she reintroduces phenomenal criteria and revives the heterogeneity problem she was trying to escape.
(7) If she refuses the restriction, then hedonism is committed to saying that correctly running machines bear intrinsic value.
(8) Both outcomes are absurd: one makes hedonism circular, the other extends moral standing to artifacts.
(9) Therefore hedonism collapses either into incoherence or into an implausible panpsychism about value.

My instinct is to defend step (4) and reject step (5), but I am unsure whether that saves the theory or merely relocates the damage. Please respond in four named sections, in this order and within these approximate bounds.

The argument reconstructed: about 200 words. Restate the chain, make explicit the hidden premise that allows (4) to slide into (5), and say whether the move from functional role to machine example involves an equivocation, a scope error, or a modal slide.

The weakest link: about 300 words. Identify the specific step—whether (2), (4), (5), or (6)—that should be rejected, explain why it looked plausible in the mouth of a careful speaker, and name the standard positions (qualitative hedonism, quantitative hedonism, preference-satisfaction theory) that would have to pay a price if that step is abandoned.

The price of rejection: about 400 words. For each of the two main alternatives—giving up (4) versus giving up (6)—explain what the hedonist must then accept about consciousness, about the comparability of pleasures, or about the boundary between mental and mechanical states. Do not invent obscure texts; work from the conceptual architecture alone.

The novelistic verdict: about 150 words. Advise which rejection makes the best dramatic sense for a character who wants to keep pleasure central to ethics but refuses to anthropomorphize machines, and note what this choice reveals about her unspoken commitments.
