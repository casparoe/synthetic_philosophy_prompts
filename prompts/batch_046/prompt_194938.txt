I'm more interested in a stricter version: what if anekāntavāda isn't just "everyone's partly right" but a structural rule that forces you to tag every claim with the specific aspect (like dravya vs. paryaya) it addresses, plus an explicit list of the standpoints from which it fails? I'm taking a course on comparative philosophy and this came up when we talked about scientific pluralism — my professor said pluralism usually collapses into either monism or full-blown relativism, but maybe this framework avoids both.

So I'm wondering: what are the most important implications of taking that stricter, tagging-version of anekāntavāda seriously for contemporary debates? Specifically, does it change how we should think about deep disagreement (say over consciousness or climate ethics), does it make the idea of a "best overall theory" incoherent or just more precise, and does it actually help resolve disputes or just make them more verbose?
