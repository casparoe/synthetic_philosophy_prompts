I'm advising a long-running interdisciplinary team that has spent over a decade refining a predictive framework for how urban bird communities respond to green-infrastructure changes. The framework's central commitment is that continuous canopy connectivity is the primary driver of species richness, with all other variables treated as secondary modifiers. Over the past two years, three independent field studies have shown that in high-density districts, small, disconnected shrub clusters with particular moisture-retention properties consistently support greater avian diversity than equivalent-area continuous cover. The senior researcher proposes to preserve the core framework by introducing an auxiliary hypothesis about localized humidity dynamics that activates only above a certain building-height threshold, arguing that this refinement extends rather than protects the model. A younger colleague contends that this move is ad hoc, that the framework has become degenerative, and that the team should abandon connectivity-first modeling in favor of a mosaic-patch paradigm. Several members feel the dispute is not merely empirical but concerns what legitimately counts as saving a theory versus abandoning it. They have asked me how to think about whether to revise the auxiliary assumptions or replace the core model. What criteria should govern their decision, and how can they distinguish a productive theoretical adjustment from a retreat?
