I am writing a historical novel set among Scandinavian intellectuals in the 1840s, and I have been consulting a critical essay that summarizes Søren Kierkegaard's attack on the press and the public in what is now usually called The Present Age. The essay puts it this way: Kierkegaard treats the public not as a body of citizens but as an abstraction literally invented by the press, a phantom that acquires moral authority precisely because no particular person belongs to it or can be held responsible for its opinions. The mechanism is leveling, which the essay describes as the reduction of all qualitative distinctions—between genius and mediocrity, between religious passion and idle curiosity—to mere quantitative differences in circulation and popularity. As a result, the age becomes reflective rather than active, substituting talk for commitment. The essay further claims that for Kierkegaard leveling is essentially identical to democratic equality, that he holds the press solely responsible for dissolving individual responsibility, and that his remedy is an aristocratic withdrawal in which the single individual abandons public discourse entirely, cultivating private religious inwardness in the expectation that the press and the public will eventually collapse without his participation.

Something in this sounds too tidy, and I worry the essay has turned Kierkegaard's religious psychology into a political program. Before I build a character's dialogue around this account, I need an accuracy audit. Which parts are faithful to Kierkegaard's actual argument? Where does the essay conflate leveling with democratic politics in a way he would reject? Is it accurate to say he blamed the press alone, or that he expected the public sphere to wither? And does his notion of the single individual really entail a withdrawal that leaves the press behind, or is the relationship between inwardness and publicity more complicated? Please separate what is accurate, what is a standard simplification, and what the essay has subtly misrepresented, especially regarding whether Kierkegaard ever proposed that private inwardness could cause the collapse of public opinion.
