In my advanced philosophy seminar on value pluralism, our instructor has evaluated five hypothetical "inter-value treaties"—negotiated compromises between groups with incompatible moral frameworks that yield practical gains neither could secure alone. Her rulings so far have been: (1) a secular research institute and a religious order share a medical complex, with abortion services excluded on-site but stem-cell research permitted in an independent wing—approved, on the ground that institutional separation preserves each framework's integrity; (2) a libertarian commune and a municipal welfare state agree to a flat tax funding private defense cooperatives—rejected, with the comment that fundamental rights had been treated as fungible commodities; (3) a pacifist society and a defensive alliance agree that pacifists will not obstruct purely defensive recruitment provided the alliance permanently abandons offensive operations—approved with conditions, described as a boundary adjustment rather than a concession of principle; (4) an environmental preservation collective and a development corporation agree to protect 85% of old-growth forest and permit limited, regulated logging in the remaining 15% to fund a regional hospital—approved with conditions, emphasizing that the protected portion retains non-instrumental status; (5) an anti-surveillance collective and a security agency agree that the collective will tolerate thirty-day metadata retention in exchange for the permanent destruction of all biometric archives—rejected, on the ground that privacy and security are not exchangeable currencies.

We now have a sixth proposal for evaluation: a community that regards unconditional honesty as constitutive of its identity agrees to allow its members to practice strategic deception in international diplomatic negotiations, in exchange for the negotiating partners permanently eliminating all tariffs on the community's cultural exports and endowing an independent museum and educational curriculum controlled entirely by the community.

Predict whether she would approve or reject this treaty, and identify the implicit rule or rules that fit her first five verdicts equally well—perhaps a distinction between instrumental adjustments and the commodification of core values, or between structurally coherent institutional firewalls and arrangements that generate internal contradiction, or something else entirely. For each competing rule, say precisely what it predicts about this sixth case. Then tell me which further hypothetical treaty would best distinguish those rules from one another—for instance, one in which no constitutive value is traded but the institutional structure is deeply incoherent, or one in which a core value is compartmentalized within a perfectly stable firewall. I am less interested in a single answer than in the map of competing interpretations and in knowing which analog among her precedents is closest.
