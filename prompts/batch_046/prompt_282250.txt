I am writing from the perspective of a high school student enrolled in an advanced philosophy elective. Our unit on philosophy of language recently covered Wittgenstein’s notions of language games and family resemblance, and I found the idea compelling but also worryingly expansive once I tried applying it outside human conversation. To test its limits, I constructed the following argument, which seems to show that Wittgenstein’s anti-essentialism collapses every meaningful conceptual boundary—including the distinction between genuine language use and sophisticated pattern matching—yet I cannot locate the error without betraying the core insight.

Here is the argument laid out step by step:

(1) For Wittgenstein, membership in categories like 'game' or 'language' is not fixed by any essential common feature but by a network of overlapping family resemblances among paradigm cases.

(2) Consequently, if an entity exhibits enough of these overlapping similarities—syntactic regularity, contextual responsiveness, rule-governed behavior—to the practices that form the core of a concept, it falls within that concept’s extension.

(3) A large language model generates syntactically coherent, context-sensitive outputs; adapts to prompts in structurally parallel ways to human interlocutors; and operates according to statistical rules that mirror grammatical constraints, despite lacking consciousness, intentionality, or social embeddedness.

(4) Therefore, by family-resemblance standards, an LLM participates in language games just as legitimately as a human speaker, since the relevant similarities are present and the absent features—inner life, social form of life—are not essential by hypothesis.

(5) But if structural and behavioral mimicry alone suffices for language use, then the concept of 'language' places no substantive normative or cognitive conditions on its bearers; it becomes purely descriptive of pattern-matching.

(6) By strict parity, the same reasoning applies to every family-resemblance concept—morality, art, belief—stripping each of any conditions beyond surface similarity and dissolving the distinctions that make them meaningful.

(7) Hence Wittgenstein’s thesis, if applied consistently, proves far more than intended: it eliminates the very conditions that allow us to distinguish genuine practices from their simulations.

I am reluctant to reject (1) or (5), because I want to keep the anti-essentialist framework and also preserve the intuition that an LLM is not truly using language in the full sense. Which premise or inference is the weakest link, and why does it initially look sound? Furthermore, if one blocks the argument by insisting on social practice or a form of life as a necessary condition, what does that concession cost for the purity of the family-resemblance view? I am asking because I need to write a short analytical paper and want to know whether the flaw is a hidden equivocation—between similarities internal to human practices and similarities across ontologically different entities—or whether the real cost is abandoning one of the premises I hoped to save.
