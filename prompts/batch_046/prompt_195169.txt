Consider the following exam question and a student’s written response.

Question: In moral philosophy, the distinction between doing harm and allowing harm is often invoked to defend the view that actively killing is morally worse than letting die. Analyze this distinction in the following institutional case: a hospital maintains one dialysis machine that currently sustains Patient A, who has depended on it for years. A physician may reallocate the machine to Patients B through F, saving all five but causing A to die within hours; if she does nothing, B through F will die. Some argue that reallocation is “killing A,” whereas inaction is “letting B–F die,” and therefore she should not switch. Evaluate whether the doing/allowing distinction applies cleanly here, and whether the physician’s role within a collective decision-making body alters the moral assessment.

Student answer: The reasoning depends on the doing/allowing distinction that Philippa Foot articulated in her analysis of directed harm, where she maintained that negative duties are absolute and cannot be overridden by aggregate welfare considerations. Applying this, the physician who switches the machine actively kills Patient A, violating that absolute duty, whereas leaving the machine in place merely allows B through F to die of natural causes, which is permissible. Yet this analysis overlooks that the physician functions here as a component within a collective distributive institution, which dissolves individual causal authorship; she therefore performs neither killing nor letting-die, but only enacts a pre-authorized allocation protocol. Moreover, because the outcome—death—is phenomenologically identical for A regardless of its causal origin, and because saving five lives clearly outweighs preserving one, the doing/allowing distinction carries no moral weight in this institutional context. Hence she ought to reallocate the machine, though not on the ground that killing is acceptable, but on the ground that collective agency removes the act from the moral category of doing altogether.
