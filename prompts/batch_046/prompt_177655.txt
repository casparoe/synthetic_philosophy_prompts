I have spent the last six months collaborating with a specialist whose descriptions of our shared work seem, on their face, to commit her to obvious falsehoods about inanimate things. She says that our central database "desires" regular maintenance, that an overdue deliverable "suffers" from neglect, and that an obsolete security layer "disbelieves" in modern protocols. Initially I treated this as colorful metaphor, yet she acts on these descriptions—she postponed a critical update because, as she put it, "the architecture is not yet prepared to welcome it," and she anticipated a system failure that did not occur under any literal interpretation of her warning.

If I take her language at face value, she attributes mental states and suffering to software, which makes her predictions unreliable and her practical reasoning appear deeply irrational. If instead I treat "desires," "suffers," and "disbelieves" as domain-specific technical terms with precise functional meanings, her conduct becomes entirely coherent and her track record is strong. The difficulty is that she also produces utterances that seem contradictory even under the technical reading: in one session she claimed the database both "rejects" and "eagerly receives" the same data feed, yet her subsequent actions showed no confusion whatsoever.

I am trying to determine whether there is a disciplined method for deciding when to reinterpret someone's words rather than attributing false or contradictory beliefs to them—particularly when the reinterpretation requires me to treat common psychological vocabulary as specialized jargon. Does the consistency of her behavior provide a reason to prefer the reinterpretation, or am I simply avoiding an uncomfortable conclusion about her rationality? And how should I weigh a single contradiction in her speech against an otherwise unified pattern of successful action?
