As a software engineer without formal philosophy training, I am preparing to moderate a podcast interview with an unnamed philosopher who defends a position I will describe below. The position combines the scale argument for insect sentience with a claim drawn from distributed-systems thinking: there may be on the order of 10^19 insects alive at any moment, so even if the prior that any single insect is sentient is very low—say, one in a thousand—the aggregate expected moral weight could exceed that of all mammals combined. The defender argues that our resistance to this conclusion stems from a kind of unit bias—treating moral consideration as attached to discrete, observable subjects rather than distributed across a massive population, much as one might wrongly evaluate a distributed system by inspecting only one node.

Please adopt the role of an expert philosophical interviewer. Before drafting questions, take a moment to think carefully and briefly outline the main tension points: whether expected-value aggregation is appropriate when individual sentience is uncertain, whether the unit-bias analogy genuinely explains our intuition or merely redescribes it, and what practical ethical obligations follow if the aggregate view is correct but we cannot identify which individuals are sentient.

Then draft eight to ten focused interview questions that press beyond a restatement of the arithmetic. I want at least one question on the moral framework underlying aggregation, one on the unit-bias diagnosis, and one on practical decision-making under opacity. For each question, include a follow-up keyed to the most likely reply (for instance, if the guest appeals to risk ethics, ask how that framework applies when the risk is not borne by a known individual; if the guest says the analogy is only heuristic, ask what independent argument remains). Keep the tone rigorous but accessible. Do not ask for biography, citations, or reading lists.
