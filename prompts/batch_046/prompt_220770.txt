hey, i cover science policy and philosophy of science for a magazine, and there's this rhetorical move i keep seeing in arguments about what counts as real science. someone attacks a fringe claim—say, a diet cure that explains away every failure with a new excuse—by saying it's unfalsifiable, that the predictions are always protected by some after-the-fact rule. then, when a critic applies the same standard to a mainstream theory with lots of auxilliary assumptions—maybe evolutionary psych or parts of cosmology—the defenders don't defend its falsifiability; they switch tracks and talk about predictive success, coherence, or how all science needs protective adjustments. but then five minutes later they're back to saying the fringe thing isn't science because you can't falsify it. it's not just that popper's criterion is hard to apply consistently; i understand that. it's the pattern of treating falsifiability as the decisive line only when you're attacking something, and treating it as irrelevant when you're defending something. does this selective deployment have a name in the philosophy of science? is it just motivated reasoning with a philosophical vocabulary, or is there a sharper concept—maybe related to how demarcation criteria get used as weapons rather than rules? and if there's no established term, what should we call it, and how exactly is it different from plain hypocrisy or from Lakatos's point about protective belts?
