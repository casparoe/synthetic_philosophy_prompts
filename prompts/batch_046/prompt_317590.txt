Write an essay weighing whether large language models are best understood as agents, simulators, or maybe something weirder in between. Don't just list pros and cons and then shrug — reach an actual verdict and defend it.

Start with the usual split. One camp, led by researchers like Bender, treats LLMs as stochastic parrots — basicly fancy autocomplete with no real intentions behind the text. The other camp points to multi-turn negotiation, tool use, and long-range planning, arguing that if it quacks like an agent and adjusts its strategy like an agent, we should treat it as at least a thin kind of agent.

But push past that binary. Introduce the idea of "proxy intentionality": the model doesn't have beliefs or self-preservation drives, yet in a specific prompt context it computes responses that only make sense if we interpret it as reasoning about our request counterfactually. It's not pure replay of training dialogue, because coherence tracking across long contexts looks more like maintaining a functional stance than just mimicking surface patterns. At the same time, it's not full agency, since there's no unified self-model or enduring goals outside the context window, and it can only recieve information as token streams.

Consider the simulator objection: maybe the model is just playing a role, like an actor performing "helpful assistant," and any apparent principle — like refusing harmful instructions — is just a statistical regularity in the training data. Does that explanation hold up when the model corrects its own reasoning mistakes in ways that don't mirror any obvious training script? Or when it negotiates in novel strategic situations?

Also address the grounding problem. If agency requires being causally coupled to the world in a particular way, then LLMs fail by design — they have no bodies, no survival stakes, no sensory input except tokens. So is "agency" just a metaphor we project? Or can there be "derived agency," where intentionality is real but borrowed from the human designers and users?

Reach a conclusion. Maybe you think LLMs are best seen as "interactive simulators" — not mere parrots, but not autonomous agents either, rather systems that generate counterfactual interlocutors on demand. Or maybe you think the proxy-intentionality view is right and they deserve recognition as "thin agents" with context-bound goals, even if they lack moral status.
