I've been trying to formalize an extension of Benacerraf's dilemma for a seminar and now find it suspicious, because the conclusion seems to swallow every intermediate position whole. Here is the argument, with a twist I added about formal verification:

(1) If knowledge requires an appropriate causal or informational connection to the fact known, then knowledge of entities that are spatiotemporally absent and causally inert is impossible.
(2) Mathematical objects—numbers, sets, functions—are standardly taken to be non-spatiotemporal and causally inert.
(3) So, on the causal condition, mathematical knowledge is impossible.
(4) But we plainly have mathematical knowledge (for instance, that there are infinitely many primes).
(5) A recent proposal suggests that running a proof-checker in silicon provides the requisite causal link: the mathematician's belief is causally mediated by the computer's physical states, which are structurally isomorphic to the abstract proof.
(6) If this saves mathematical knowledge, it does so by allowing causal access to abstract structures through their physical instantiations.
(7) But then the same reasoning applies to any abstract structure that can be physically instantiated—counterfactual worlds in simulations, normative frameworks in institutional practices, or fictional entities in virtual environments.
(8) So the verification strategy either undermines the causal condition generally (permitting knowledge of all instantiated abstractions) or must explain why mathematical instantiation is uniquely knowledge-conducive.
(9) Any such explanation will likely invoke the determinacy or uniqueness of mathematical structure.
(10) But invoking that determinacy assumes precisely the kind of independent mathematical reality that the anti-realist denies—so the strategy begs the question against the nominalist or formalist.
(11) Therefore, any attempt to reconcile the causal condition with mathematical realism via physical instantiation either eliminates the causal constraint altogether or presupposes the realism it aims to defend.
(12) Hence Benacerraf's dilemma forces not just a choice between platonism and causal epistemology, but shows that every middle ground collapses.

I'd prefer to keep (1) and (4), since giving up either seems to dissolve philosophy of mathematics into either skepticism or mystery-mongering. Where does the argument go wrong? Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost—especially for the verification strategy in (5) and the uniqueness claim in (9)? If I have to preserve (1) and (4), am I forced into (12), or is there a less expensive repair?
