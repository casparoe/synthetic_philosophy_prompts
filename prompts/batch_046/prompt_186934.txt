I'm a graduate student in a philosophy of logic seminar, and I'm drafting a hypothetical adversarial-collaboration document between two camps that reject classical explosion but have been arguing past each other in the literature. The first camp are ontological dialetheists: they hold that some contradictions are literally true—not only in semantic paradoxes but in ordinary change, vagueness, and institutional reasoning—and they treat paraconsistent logic as tracking real structures of the world. The second camp are paraconsistent instrumentalists: they agree that classical logic fails in inconsistent contexts and that we must reason with paraconsistent frameworks, but they deny that any contradiction is ever genuinely true; they regard every inconsistency as a representational artifact, an epistemic conflict, or a linguistic imprecision that a more precise framework could in principle dissolve.

Rather than rehashing the standard Liar-paradox framing, I want the collaboration to center on an injected test case. Consider a regulatory framework that assigns an entity to category C if and only if it possesses property P, and excludes it otherwise. The framework's own operational criteria determinately apply to entity E in both directions: E is classified as possessing P and as lacking P, with no available meta-criterion that breaks the tie. The dialetheists treat this as a true institutional contradiction—an actual state of affairs in which the framework genuinely entails both A and not-A. The instrumentalists treat it as a resolvable representational conflict: the contradiction belongs to the model or the language, not to reality, and a sufficiently refined regimentation could eliminate it without loss.

Please draft the joint statement that both sides could sign. First, provide a numbered section of agreed claims: the formal properties they both accept about paraconsistent logics such as LP and relevant entailment; the shared diagnosis of why classical logic is inadequate for inconsistent contexts; the precise formal structure of the regulatory-boundary case; and any theorems or results both camps acknowledge without interpretive gloss. Second, present a disagreements table with two columns—one phrased in the dialetheist's own voice (endorsing true contradictions in reality, including institutional ones), one in the instrumentalist's voice (endorsing only tolerated representational inconsistencies that are never affirmed as true)—with each entry worded so its own side would accept it. Third, identify the cruxes: sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one party. These should include whether a contradiction can be true yet permanently epistemically inaccessible; whether every apparent contradiction can in principle be dissolved by finer distinctions without changing the subject matter; and whether paraconsistency without dialetheism secretly restores consistency at a meta-level. For each crux, record the specific argument, case, or observation both sides agree bears on it, and write down each side's explicit prediction of the outcome in advance. Finally, diagnose whether any portion of the dispute is merely verbal—particularly around terms like "genuine contradiction," "real inconsistency," or "truth in the world versus truth in the model"—and if so, propose a disambiguated vocabulary both can adopt for the residual disagreement. The document should be formal, precise, and free of appeals to authority, citation lists, or reading recommendations; treat the formal logic as common ground and the regulatory case as the shared test bed.
