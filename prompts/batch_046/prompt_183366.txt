I am a doctoral researcher working at the intersection of philosophy of mind and AI governance. I have come to defend a particular version of the intentional stance applied to large language models, which I shall call the pragmatic corrigibility stance. I treat this as a normative claim about interpretive practice rather than an ontological claim about artificial minds, and I hold that the stance is reflexively stable: successful error-correction within the framework generates further predictive utility, producing a pragmatic bootstrapping loop that binds designers and regulators without requiring metaphysical realism.

What would genuinely count against this position? I am not asking for the familiar eliminativist charge that LLMs lack consciousness, nor for the standard realist demand for biological embodiment. - Which normative or modal features of human belief-attribution — for example, commitment to consistency norms, susceptibility to rational persuasion, or robustness across counterfactual variations in training data — could be shown to be absent in LLMs in a way that breaks the bootstrapping loop rather than merely weakening it?
- Could a purely statistical or causal-mechanistic monitoring framework achieve the same regulatory enforceability, and what precisely would it have to demonstrate?
- Has the view already been qualified into unfalsifiability — say, by defining corrigibility circularly in terms of the stance itself — and if so, is that insulation fatal for a position that claims normative force, or is it an acceptable feature of a pragmatic framework?
