In post-mortems of large-scale failures — engineering disasters, institutional collapses, policy rollouts that went wrong — I keep noticing a specific compound error that I don't have a word for, and I'm not sure whether it's just hindsight bias stacked on the narrative fallacy or a distinct configuration with its own structure.

The pattern runs like this. After a failure, analysts identify specific decision-points — a design choice, a warning ignored, a budget cut — and present them as obviously wrong in retrospect, as if any competent actor should have seen the risk given the information then available. At the same time, they construct a macro-narrative about organizational culture, incentive structures, or systemic pressure that made those exact decisions inevitable for the people inside the system. The two explanations are offered together, often in the same report, without anyone flagging that they pull in opposite directions: if the organizational dynamics genuinely made the choice rational or unavoidable for the actors, then the micro-level claim that it was "obviously" a mistake depends on smuggling in information the actors did not have; conversely, if the mistake was genuinely obvious even with the information available at the time, then the systemic narrative is redundant and the failure reduces to individual incompetence rather than a structural story.

I have seen this in aerospace failure retrospectives and in financial crisis analyses, and it feels different from plain hindsight bias (which is just overestimating ex-ante predictability) and from Taleb's narrative fallacy (which is imposing a coherent causal story on what may be a random or overdetermined sequence), because the specific damage is the concealed mutual inconsistency between a counterfactual judgment about what individuals could have done and a deterministic claim about what the system made them do. Is there an established term for this exact compound — the simultaneous deployment of micro-level hindsight judgment and macro-level deterministic narrative in a way that masks their tension? If not, can you carve out the phenomenon precisely: define it, distinguish it from ordinary outcome bias and from a legitimate multilevel explanation that respects both levels without collapse, and provide boundary cases that show when a retrospective account has fallen into it versus when it has genuinely integrated individual and systemic causation?
