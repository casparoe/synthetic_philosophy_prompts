I'm trying to figure out if there's a coherent line between therapy and enhancement, going by my gut on specific cases. Correcting a newborn's cleft palate so they can speak: therapy, obviously. Giving a healthy teenager a nootropic that boosts working memory by 15%: enhancement, no doubt. But then there's the musician who's loosing her high-frequency hearing from normal aging, not disease; she gets an implant that restores her to her 25-year-old hearing, but it also lets her perceive ultrasonic ranges. I keep going back and forth on whether that's therapy, enhancement, or both at once, since it's one device with one setting. Genetic editing feels clearer at first: fixing a mutation that gives you 90% Alzheimer's risk by 60 is therapy. But what about editing out a variant that raises depression risk by 30% but is also linked to higher creative output? Removing it feels like therapy if you have the depression, but if you don't, is it still therapy because you're preventing a potential disorder, or enhancement because you're altering a trait that's part of your cognitive profile? Then there's the brain-computer interface: for a paralyzed person to walk, therapy; for a healthy person to type with thoughts, enhancement. Except if they have severe RSI and can't use their hands - then it feels therapeutic, but they're still gaining a capability they never had naturally. The case that really breaks my intuition is this hypothetical "baseline reset" pill that restores your immune system to its age-20 state. For a 60-year-old with autoimmune issues, therapy. For a healthy 60-year-old, enhancement. But for a 30-year-old whose immune system was permanently weakened by an industrial toxin at 25 - not to "diseased" levels, just bottom 10% - taking it to reach age-20 levels feels like therapy to me, even though they'd be exceeding the average healthy 30-year-old. So maybe the principle is about returning to one's own prior healthy trajectory rather than a species norm? But then the musician's aging is also a natural trajectory, so why doesn't that count? Is there one principle that saves all these verdicts, or do I have to drop one? And what would it say about a healthy teenager using a sleep-reduction implant that lets them function on three hours with no long-term damage - therapy for modern life demands, or pure enhancement?
