I am drafting my position for a philosophy-of-knowledge seminar and want a consistency audit before I present. Here is the package I hold on epistemic injustice:

(1) Epistemic injustice is fundamentally a structural failure in the distribution of credibility and hermeneutical resources, not an aggregate of individual moral wrongs.
(2) Testimonial injustice can occur through purely statistical, identity-blind reliability heuristics—what I call structural statistical discrimination—when those heuristics reproduce historically produced disparities.
(3) The notion of a purely epistemic normative domain, separable from ethics and politics, is conceptually confused; evidential standards are themselves normatively constructed.
(4) Individual testimonial injustices are conceptually derivative: an individual act is unjust only by participating in an unjust structural pattern, with no purely interpersonal testimonial wrong.
(5) Corrective practice requires credibility rebalancing: sometimes deliberately assigning greater weight to testimony from structurally marginalized sources than local evidential norms dictate, treating credibility as a positional good to be redistributed within local epistemic communities.
(6) Credibility excess granted to dominant groups is also unjust, but its wrongness is purely relational—there is no direct harm to the over-credited party; the wrong consists entirely in the correlating deficit imposed on others.

I suspect hidden tensions—especially between (2) and (4) on whether individual heuristic reasoning can be unjust without explicit structural awareness, and between (3) and (5) on whether we can identify a baseline of local evidential norms to exceed if all norms are value-laden. Meanwhile, (1) and (6) may only appear to conflict.

Please audit this package: rank the tensions by severity, identify which pairs are only apparently in conflict, specify what must give in each genuine case, and tell me which single revision would clean up the most problems. Be committed—do not lay out options neutrally; take definite positions on where the fault lines lie, what the cheapest fixes cost, and where my defense against an imagined critic is weakest.
