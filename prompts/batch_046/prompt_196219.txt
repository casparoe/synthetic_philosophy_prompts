I'm a software engineer without any formal philosophy background, but I co-host a podcast that explores reasoning frameworks used in AI and engineering. Next week we're interviewing Dr. K, an unnamed philosopher who has recently proposed treating the presumption of independence not as a statistical prior but as a normative heuristic with an explicit axiomatic structure. In their framework, agents facing compound uncertainty apply an independence-by-default rule, which is then defeated only when one of three conditions is met: direct structural correlation, shared causal ancestry, or observed covariance above a specified threshold. Dr. K argues that this avoids both the overconfidence of naive Bayesian updating and the computational paralysis of full joint-distribution modeling. They have also introduced a specific thought experiment—the dual-algorithm allocation problem—in which two apparently independent subroutines share a hidden resource bottleneck, to illustrate how the defeat conditions are supposed to catch failures without collapsing the heuristic retroactively.

You are an expert interviewer in philosophy of reasoning and decision theory. Before drafting anything, please take a moment to think carefully and briefly brainstorm the standard objections to formalizing heuristic independence—for instance, that it may smuggle in unexamined priors, that defeat conditions might require exactly the dependency-tracking the heuristic is meant to shortcut, and that relocating the difficulty to meta-level rules could leave the core reasoning unchanged. Then, working from that analysis, draft a sequence of eight to ten interview questions that go well beyond asking Dr. K to restate the view. The questions should turn on the actual mechanics of the framework and should include:

1. At least one question pressing how the defeat conditions avoid circularity, since identifying shared causal ancestry seems to demand the very joint knowledge the heuristic is designed to bypass.
2. At least one question on the dual-algorithm allocation problem, asking precisely how the framework handles the hidden bottleneck without retrospectively undermining the independence claim.
3. At least one question on whether the framework genuinely distinguishes independence as a working assumption from independence as a normative commitment, and what practical difference that distinction makes for an engineer implementing the rule in code.
4. At least one question addressing the charge that formalizing the heuristic merely relocates the hard problem into the meta-level defeat rules.

For each main question, include a follow-up keyed to the reply Dr. K is most likely to give—if they appeal to empirical learning of defeat conditions, ask about reliability under small samples; if they invoke resource constraints, ask why that justifies independence rather than agnosticism; if they distinguish structural from evidential independence, ask how an agent is meant to recognize the boundary in practice. Keep the tone rigorous but accessible to a technically-minded audience with no philosophy training. Do not ask for bibliographies, citations, or reading lists. Do not invoke obscure literature that a well-read model would not recognize; focus entirely on the conceptual architecture. Present your brief brainstorming first, then list the questions in order.
