I've been sketching a short essay on a tension I think is underexplored in two-dimensional semantics, and I'd like your comments on whether the line of argument holds together conceptually and where it might be going wrong.

The core idea is this: standard 2D accounts treat the primary intension of a term as anchored to an individual's epistemic situation—what they can rule out a priori—while the secondary intension fixes the reference across possible worlds. I want to apply this framework to a socially fragmented concept: imagine a linguistic community that uses 'jade' uniformly for both nephrite and jadeite, but where different subgroups hold incompatible theoretical commitments about which substance is the 'true' jade, without anyone knowing the chemical distinction. My essay argues that in such cases there is no single primary intension shared by the community; instead, the epistemic modal profile is distributed across social roles. This suggests either that 2D semantics needs a 'social primary intension' layer—tracking what the collective can rule out given distributed knowledge—or that we should abandon the idea that primary intensions are psychologically realized at all and treat them as purely normative constructs tied to communal entitlement.

I'm particularly interested in whether this misrepresents how specialists in two-dimensional semantics actually conceive of the primary intension—whether they already allow for social distribution—or whether I'm forcing an individualistic framework onto a concept that was never meant to bear that weight. I also want to know if the move from conceptual fragmentation to distributed modal profiles is coherent, or if it merely confuses epistemic possibility with sociological disagreement, and whether the argument is philosophically interesting or just a redescription of familiar social-epistemological problems in modal clothing. Please separate what is settled in the literature on two-dimensional semantics from what is genuinely contested among people who know the topic, and do not present a contested interpretation—such as whether primary intensions must be individually realized—as if it were the consensus view.
