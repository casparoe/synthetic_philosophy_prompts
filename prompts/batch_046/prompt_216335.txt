I am a high school junior observing a student-wellness framework that our district has adopted under the name of an “Agency-First Protocol,” though I am asking about its conceptual structure rather than about that particular title. The policy requires any student seeking counseling to complete a structured self-assessment that divides their distress into two categories: concerns that lie within their direct control—such as the effort they invest in studying, the interpretations they place on feedback, or the habits they maintain—and concerns that lie outside it, including a teacher’s grading curve, college admissions outcomes, peer behavior, or family financial stress. The counseling office offers clinical support only for the first category. For the second, students are referred not to therapy but to mandatory philosophy workshops that treat their emotional response as a cognitive error to be dissolved: the curriculum teaches them to apply a version of the Stoic distinction between what is in our power and what is not, with the explicit aim of reducing attachment to outcomes rather than addressing the external conditions that produce them.

What puzzles me is an apparent tension within the same institution. When a student is harmed by another person’s actions—say, through sustained bullying—the school recognizes that student as genuinely wronged and may intervene against the perpetrator. Yet if that same student reports anxiety about the harm they have suffered, or about the structural conditions that make such harm likely, the framework classifies that anxiety as belonging to the second column and redirects it toward philosophical correction rather than emotional validation or institutional redress. So I want to ask: what implicit theory of emotional legitimacy, moral psychology, or the nature of suffering does this administrative division presuppose? Must it commit to the view that all distress over uncontrollable outcomes is reducible to a failure of judgment, or can it be made consistent with rival views that treat some emotional responses to external events as rational and worthy of acknowledgment? Please reconstruct the implicit philosophy, distinguishing carefully what the practice strictly requires from what merely fits it well, and indicate whether the framework is philosophically underdetermined—whether several competing theories of welfare, agency, or responsibility could license the same referral structure. Finally, if we replaced the underlying view with one that grants full moral standing to emotional responses to structural and uncontrollable conditions, what concrete changes would have to be made to the counseling referral system and to the design of those philosophy workshops?
