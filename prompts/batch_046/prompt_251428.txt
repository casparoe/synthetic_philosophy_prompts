I've been thinking on my own about what actually makes something corruption, and I've settled on a view I haven't seen laid out quite this way: the key distinction isn't whether gifts or favors create social obligation—since every society runs on obligations—but whether those obligations are what I'd call reversible. If both parties can walk away from the exchange without facing ruinous loss of livelihood, standing, or safety, then it's reciprocity or clientelism, not corruption, even if money changes hands and even if Western anti-bribery codes would label it bribery. But if one party is locked in—if refusing the favor or leaving the relationship would mean destitution, exile, or violence—then the exchange is corrupt, regardless of how ordinary or culturally expected it looks. I apply this to patronage networks: a boss giving jobs to relatives isn't corrupt if those relatives could refuse and survive elsewhere, but it becomes corrupt the moment refusal is effectively impossible. I also suspect Western standards misclassify practices because they treat all obligation-creating gifts as suspicious without asking whether the obligation is reversible. What would count against this? I want the arguments and observations that should actually move me, sorted by how decisive they'd be: conceptual objections that show reversibility collapses into power imbalance without explaining corruption; empirical cases where my test clearly misclassifies something; moral objections that say I'm excusing harm by calling it culture; and an honest assessment of whether I've qualified the view so heavily—say, by letting ruinous be interpreted loosely—that nothing could falsify it, and whether that insulation is a problem for a claim like this.
