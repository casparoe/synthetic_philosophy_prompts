My thesis: I defend a specific normative reading of Keynes's interwar essay on abundance and the fifteen-hour workweek. I argue that Keynes's dismissal of the love of money as a somewhat disgusting morbidity is not rhetorical color but expresses a structural critique of instrumental rationality inherited from his Bloomsbury association with G.E. Moore. On this reading, Keynes distinguishes absolute needs—those tied to intrinsic goods such as contemplation, friendship, and aesthetic experience—from relative needs, which are positional and competitive. Economic growth is justified only as a transitional mechanism for satisfying absolute needs; once those are secured, further growth becomes pathological because it extends the domain of instrumental reasoning into areas that should be governed by intrinsic valuation. I apply this to contemporary debates: longtermist frameworks that treat GDP growth and utility maximization as the primary metrics for a post-scarcity future risk value lock-in precisely because they fail to preserve protected non-market spaces—analogous to the Bloomsbury domestic economy of artistic exchange and gift—where non-instrumental capacities can be cultivated. I claim that proposals such as universal basic income miss the institutional point unless they include decommodification of leisure, not merely its monetization.
