I'm taking a graduate seminar on rational agency, and we've been working through the problem of embedded agency—the idea that any reasoning system physically situated within the world it acts upon cannot construct a complete, consistent model of itself without either infinite regress or unmodeled causal loops. To make this concrete for our discussion group, I'd like you to write a short story that dramatizes the practical dilemmas of a genuinely embedded agent, not through generic artificial-intelligence tropes but through a human-scale analog.

The setting is a solitary archivist who lives inside a vast, self-maintaining library. The library's protocol is that it catalogs every volume except those that attempt to describe the library's own cataloging rules from the inside. Over years of work, the archivist realizes that whenever she tries to record her own act of recording—say, writing a meta-volume about why she chose to preserve or suppress a particular text—the entry corrupts the index: she can describe the library's structure, or she can describe herself describing it, but never both in the same coherent volume. She therefore knows that her deliberative processes are part of the system she is trying to steer, yet she lacks the computational resources to model that part from any "outside" view.

Please make the story concrete around one specific decision she faces: a manuscript has appeared that predicts, with disturbing accuracy, her own decision to burn it. Show how her embeddedness constrains her reasoning—she cannot fully represent her own choice procedure without breaking the catalog—without rendering her reasoning futile. How does she still choose which shelves to preserve, which texts to suppress, and whether to destroy the manuscript, when she knows that her knowing is itself part of the causal chain she is trying to evaluate? I'd like the narrative to stay close to the conceptual tension rather than resolving it with a mystical revelation or an external observer.
