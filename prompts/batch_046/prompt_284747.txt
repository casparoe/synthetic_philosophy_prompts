I don't have any formal training in philosophy, but after a long evening talking with friends about what it really means to be honest when you speak, I found myself stuck on a problem that seems to sit right between common sense and something more precise. We were debating whether it is wrong to say something with real conviction when you do not personally possess that special kind of certainty people call knowledge—say, repeating a climate finding from a report you trust but cannot verify yourself, or endorsing a historical claim you learned from a well-made documentary. One friend insisted that unless you are absolutely sure, you ought to stay silent or hedge so heavily that your words become useless; another argued that genuine belief is enough, and that demanding more is unrealistic in a world where nobody can check everything firsthand. I think both positions miss something important, and I want you to construct a careful argument for a middle view I have started calling accountable transmission.

Please argue that the correct norm governing ordinary assertion is neither the strict knowledge norm—which requires that every speaker personally track the truth of what they say—nor the bare belief norm—which permits speaking on conviction alone without further responsibility. Instead, defend the claim that assertion should be governed by accountable transmission: you may, and sometimes should, assert a claim you do not personally know, provided that you genuinely believe it, that you can explicitly identify the epistemic pipeline—the specific expert body, study, or chain of reasoning—through which it reaches you, and that you accept a standing obligation to revise or withdraw your claim if that pipeline proves unreliable. You should explain why this is not merely belief in disguise, emphasizing that the requirement to name and stand behind one's pipeline introduces a distinctive form of vicarious accountability that protects listeners from reckless speculation. At the same time, show why this standard avoids the paralysis that a pure knowledge norm would create in a society built on distributed expertise, where insisting that every speaker be an expert would silence most valuable conversation. I am not asking for reading lists, citations, or references to specific philosophers by name; I want a direct, reasoned defense that takes seriously both the social purpose of sharing information and the moral risk of speaking without proper grounding.
