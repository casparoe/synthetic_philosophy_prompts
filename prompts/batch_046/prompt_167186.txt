I'm a retired theoretical physicist who has spent the past several years reading philosophy of mathematics and science, and I am now preparing a proposal for a six-month independent research fellowship at an interdisciplinary institute that supports work spanning science and philosophy. The project concerns the Continuum Hypothesis and its classical independence from standard ZFC set theory. As I understand the existing philosophical discussion, it is largely structured by a dichotomy: platonist realism holds that CH possesses a definite truth value that we have not yet discovered, while axiomatic pluralism holds that there is no unique answer and that different set-theoretic universes are equally legitimate. I want to investigate whether this framing overlooks a third interpretive possibility drawn from the philosophy of science—namely, that CH independence represents a form of permanent mathematical underdetermination structurally analogous to the empirical equivalence of competing physical theories, such as different interpretations of quantum mechanics. On this view, the central issue is not whether a single true universe of sets exists, but whether available extensions of ZFC can be evaluated by the same non-empirical virtues—explanatory coherence, fruitfulness, simplicity, and integrative power—that scientists invoke when empirical data cannot adjudicate between theories. The precise research question is therefore: Does interpreting CH independence through the lens of scientific underdetermination dissolve, sharpen, or merely relocate the standard realism-versus-pluralism debate, and what normative criteria for axiom choice emerge if the analogy holds? To pursue this, I would spend the first two months clarifying the logical structure of the independence results and mapping the landscape of proposed resolutions—including large-cardinal axioms and forcing axioms—without committing to any particular formal development. The next two months would be devoted to constructing a comparative framework: identifying the dimensions along which physically equivalent theories are assessed, then testing whether those dimensions apply meaningfully to competing set-theoretic extensions. The final two months would evaluate whether the analogy actually changes the normative status of axiom selection—specifically, whether it licenses treating certain axioms as conventionally adopted frameworks rather than as discoveries or arbitrary choices—and would produce a written analysis. A successful result would be a clearly articulated taxonomy showing where the scientific analogy holds, where it breaks down, and whether it offers a genuinely distinct philosophical interpretation of CH independence. The project fails if the analogy collapses entirely—for instance, if set-theoretic ontology proves so different from physical ontology that underdetermination in science provides no illuminating parallel—thereby leaving the original dichotomy untouched. Given that the work is conceptual analysis rather than technical set-theoretic proof, I believe six months is realistic, though I recognize the risk that constructing a rigorous comparative framework may prove broader than the available time. Please write this as a polished, formal proposal: state the question with precision, explain why current positions leave it open, describe the approach and approximate timeline, define honest conditions for success and failure, and assess whether the scope is appropriate for the fellowship period. Do not include a bibliography, citation list, or reading list.
