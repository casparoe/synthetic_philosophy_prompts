I serve on my university's Committee on International Research Partnerships, and we must decide by next month's meeting whether to renew our memorandum of understanding with the Institute for Comparative Governance in K., a partner institution in a Central European state that has, over the past eight years, replaced independent university boards with ministerially appointed administrators, restricted public funding for gender and migration studies, and centralized peer-review processes for research grants—while retaining multiparty elections and formal judicial review.

Our existing policy, adopted three years ago, permits suspension only in cases of "systematic democratic backsliding that materially compromises academic freedom." The committee is divided. One faction holds that the partner country's changes fall within the range of conservative democratic governance, and that labeling them "backsliding" imports a partisan framework that treats any departure from liberal-progressive norms as democratic decay. The other faction holds that the structural replacement of university autonomy with political appointment is precisely the institutional erosion that comparative scholars identify, regardless of whether elections continue.

To break the deadlock, I have proposed that we assess the case through a dual-axis framework rather than relying solely on electoral metrics. The first axis measures procedural erosion: alterations to the rules that select and constrain governing power. The second measures what I am calling epistemic closure: the systematic removal of conditions necessary for research communities to correct errors, pursue unpopular lines of inquiry, and maintain autonomy in agenda-setting. The strongest objection is that "epistemic closure" is itself a normatively loaded concept that smuggles liberal assumptions about knowledge production into our assessment, and that our committee risks imposing a philosophical conception of academic freedom onto a partner that operates within a different democratic tradition.

Please draft the short policy memo I will circulate before the meeting. It should recommend a concrete course of action—renewal with conditions, suspension, or termination—with a clear time frame and specific conditions if applicable. The memo should apply the dual-axis framework honestly, map the partner's situation onto both axes, treat the "partisan label" objection as the strongest consideration on the other side rather than dismissing it, and conclude with an actionable bottom line the committee could vote on. It should also identify which single further development, either in the partner country or in our own framework, would most clearly settle whether our decision was correct.
