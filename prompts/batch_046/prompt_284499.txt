At a study-group meeting last week, two members—call them Mara and Jules—got into a long exchange about Berkeley's master argument and whether it survives a particular modern framing. I found both sides sharper than usual and couldn't tell where the reasoning actually breaks down. I'd like a point-by-point adjudication.

Mara started with the classic formulation: for any sensible object, its being is its being perceived. She argued that trying to conceive an unperceived tree is self-defeating, because every quality we ascribe to the tree—color, shape, solidity—is perceptible by definition; strip away the perceiver and we are left not with a hidden tree but with the concept of a tree emptied of content. Jules replied that this mistakes a psychological limitation for a metaphysical one: we routinely conceive of things we are not currently observing, such as a laptop in a closed drawer, without contradiction. The contradiction, he said, only arises if we try simultaneously to perceive the absence of perception, which no one is proposing.

Mara countered that Berkeley's point is semantic, not psychological. To call something a sensible object just is to place it in the domain of possible perception; so asserting its existence outside that domain violates the conditions under which the term has meaning. Jules said this looks circular—defining physical objects into mental dependence—unless Mara can show independently that the concept of matter contains no residual content after perceptible qualities are removed.

Mara then proposed a more precise version: imagine a distributed sensor network that logs every physical interaction with a tree in an isolated forest, storing the data in an encrypted ledger that no human ever reads. She claimed that even here, the state of the tree is nothing but a structured relation among actual and possible measurements; without a mind to interpret the ledger entries as measurements of something, there is no tree-state, only uninterpreted symbols. Jules objected that this concedes too much to structural realism: the ledger describes relations that obtain whether or not anyone interprets them, and those relations are precisely what physics takes as real. If Mara allows structural descriptions to exist unperceived, she has abandoned Berkeley's core claim that existence is mental.

Jules pushed further with a niche twist: he asked whether Mara's position can distinguish between an unperceived tree and an unperceived quantum superposition that has not decohered. In both cases, he said, we have a mathematical structure with no conscious observer. If Mara answers that both depend on being conceived in God's mind, then her argument has collapsed into a generic theological claim and lost its specific force against materialism. If she answers that the quantum case is different because superposition lacks definite properties, then she has implicitly admitted that there are mind-independent states—just ones with indefinite properties—which undermines her universal thesis.

Mara's final move was to deny the analogy: quantum mechanics, on her view, describes not unobserved entities but the statistical patterns among observed outcomes; the superposition is a calculational device, not a hidden state waiting to be perceived. Therefore, Jules's example does not establish an unperceived structure but only an unobserved predictive tool. Jules replied that treating successful predictive structures as mere tools is itself a form of anti-realism that needs independent defense, and that Mara is now defending instrumentalism rather than idealism.

Please adjudicate this exchange move by move. For each of Mara's and Jules's contributions, rate whether it is clearly mistaken, mostly right, ambiguous, or mostly wrong, and briefly justify the rating. Then say who is more right overall, and identify what—if anything—both are missing that would actually resolve the dispute, without asking for bibliographies or external sources.
