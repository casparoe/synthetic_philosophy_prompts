As a retired biologist who has only recently begun reading seriously in philosophy and social theory, I am assembling a dataset of conceptual prompts, and I would like one designed for an interdisciplinary workshop setting. Specifically: for a reading group of about ten to twelve adult learners—retired scientists, graduate students, and interested non-specialists—with ninety minutes available, design a structured deliberation or simulation that makes the functional logic of post-defeat lost-cause national myths feel unavoidable rather than merely irrational.

The domain should cover the structural patterns visible in cases such as the Confederacy, Masada, Kosovo, and Dunkirk, but I want the activity centered on a specific mechanism I will call defeat inversion: the tendency of collective memory after catastrophic failure to reverse the causal arrow of defeat, attributing loss not to strategic error or internal division but to external betrayal, overwhelming force, or the moral purity of the defeated party, thereby converting historical shame into a renewable resource for group identity and intergenerational loyalty.

The deliverable should be a fully specified, runnable activity with explicit roles—for example, an archivist subgroup holding verified historical event-cards drawn from anonymized defeat narratives, and a mnemonic council tasked with drafting a commemorative charter for a successor generation—plus rules, a hidden payoff matrix that reveals an inverse correlation between historical fidelity and social cohesion precisely for events involving internal failure, a timeline fitting the ninety-minute window, and a debrief protocol. The idea must emerge from what participants actually do—negotiating omissions, strategically reframing causes, and voting on commemorative symbols—before it is named by the facilitator.

In the debrief, map specific negotiation moments onto the mechanics of defeat inversion; state what will predictably go wrong when the activity is run, such as participants conflating strategic accommodation with genuine belief or resisting the hidden payoff revelation, and explain how the facilitator should handle each failure mode; and be frank about which feature of the phenomenon the simulation cannot reproduce—specifically, the multi-generational emotional sedimentation and slow institutional repetition that makes a constructed myth feel like inherited common sense rather than a committee decision—and note that the facilitator must supply this dimension explicitly.

Finally, in any theoretical framing you provide, separate what is broadly settled in the relevant literature—that collective memory is selective, serves present social functions, and that post-defeat societies frequently generate redemptive narratives—from what remains genuinely contested among historians, sociologists, and philosophers of collective memory, such as whether defeat inversion is best explained as adaptive cultural evolution, a rational response to status threat, or an irreducible feature of narrative identity formation, and do not present any one of these explanatory frameworks as the consensus view.
