I've been reading about corruption—clientelism, gift-giving, and whether Western anti-corruption norms are just cultural imperialism. The debate seems stuck: anthropologists show that in many societies exchanging favors is just how social life works, while philosophers and policy folks call it corruption and want it stamped out. Both sides agree on the ethnograpy, they just talk past each other. Is this a verbal dispute about the word 'corruption', a real moral disagreement about impartiality, or is there a hidden factual claim—maybe about whether these practices actually erode state capacity—that neither side is testing?

My hunch, coming from a physics background, is that people treat corruption like a binary switch when it's really a phase transition: there's a critical point where reciprical obligations stop being social glue and start being institutional decay, and the disagreement is mostly about where that critical point sits for different governance structures. But maybe I'm forcing a science metaphor onto a normative mess.

Can you diagnose what's actually stalling this? Is it bedrock intuitions about fairness, different standards of evidence, or are they answering different questions—one about local morality, one about global governance? And if your diagnosis is right, what would actually move the needle: a clearer conceptual distinction, an empirical study, or should we just accept the stall?
