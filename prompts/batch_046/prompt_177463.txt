I've been arguing with a mate from my Dostoevsky seminar about that Grand Inquisitor passage in Brothers Karamazov, and he walked me into this chain that I can't find the exit from. Roughly:

(1) The Inquisitor claims people don't actually want freedom — they want miracle, mystery, and authority — because freedom makes them anxious and unhappy. Call this the Preference Claim.

(2) If a benevolent authority knows that a subject will be happier without a particular choice, and that choice has no independent value, then removing the choice is the right thing to do.

(3) The Preference Claim isn't confined to religion; it applies wherever autonomy risks suffering — politics, work, relationships, even small daily decisions. So it's a general welfare thesis.

(4) Now, if people form adaptive preferences (the Sen / Nussbaum literature), they'll come to prefer whatever is available to them. So asking people whether they want freedom is circular — they'll just endorse the secure option they've been given.

(5) Therefore freedom is at best instrumentally valuable, and at worst a kind of false consciousness. Any system that maximises happiness by removing choice is justified, regardless of whether the subject recognises the loss.

(6) But that means the Inquisitor's church is correct, and so is total algorithmic governance, overprotective parenting, and never letting anyone learn from mistakes — basically autonomy should be engineered out of every domain.

(7) So the Inquisitor's reasoning, taken seriously, proves that freedom has zero value and ought to be eliminated everywhere.

I feel like there's a slide between (3) and (5), or maybe (4) is doing dodgy work by conflating revealed preference with normative preference, but I can't name the slip. Which step should I reject, and what does giving it up cost? I'd really like to hang onto (1) and (2) if possible, because they feel psychologically real. Could you analyse this in British English, and then after the main answer stick a brief structured summary in a JSON object with these fields: "rejected_step", "why_it_looked_fine", "cost_of_rejection", "what_the_slip_was", "bottom_line"?
