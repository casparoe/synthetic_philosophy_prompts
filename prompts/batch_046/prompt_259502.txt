I work as a backend software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, and recently, in an informal discussion with colleagues, I became interested in whether reasons can exist independently of anyone who actually holds them. The specific case that sparked the debate is a deprecated authentication microservice in our legacy codebase. Its internal documentation contains a detailed design rationale explaining why token-expiry windows should be fifteen minutes rather than sixty, referencing trade-offs between replay-attack surface and user friction. The service has been decommissioned for years; no running process consults these comments, and no current team member has actively reasoned through them. Some colleagues argued that these notes are merely fossilized descriptions of past human reasoning—like finding an old calculator with an answer etched into it—carrying no normative force now that no agent acts on them. Others contended that the justification structure remains intact as an abstract relation among security properties, so that if we rebuilt the module tomorrow the reason would apply immediately, suggesting it was never genuinely tied to a particular mind.

Please write an essay that takes a definite position on whether such "free-floating rationales"—reasons without reasoners—are genuinely possible, using this kind of software-artifact case as the central analogy rather than standard philosophical examples. Weigh the main arguments carefully: the view that reasons are essentially normative relations requiring an agent's perspective to have any direction, versus the view that reasons are abstract structures (comparable to protocol invariants or unexecuted assertions in formal verification) that can be instantiated in text, code, or physical configurations without being "held" by anyone. Do not conclude with ambiguity—defend either that free-floating rationales exist (perhaps treating the deprecated module's comments as genuinely normative claims waiting only for implementation) or that they are always parasitic on actual or potential reasoners (perhaps treating them as syntactically intact but semantically inert until parsed by a mind or process). Justify your verdict with careful development of the analogy, and address the objection that if reasons can float free, we ought to be able to identify concrete, non-mental instances—like the legacy comments—without implicitly smuggling in a hidden interpreter.
