I am in my final year of sixth form, reading philosophy of language and logic, and I have become preoccupied with Donald Davidson's account of radical interpretation. Rather than seeking a summary, I wish to understand where the genuinely live intellectual work lies. In particular, I am trying to work out how a researcher with limited time — perhaps three years of focused study — ought to distribute their attention among the unresolved tensions that surround the principle of charity, the holism of belief, and the boundaries of interpretive constraint.

In my own reflection I keep returning to a configuration that seems insufficiently examined in the standard treatments: situations in which an interpreter applies charity so rigorously that the attributed belief-system becomes internally coherent yet conceptually incommensurable with the speaker's own self-understanding. This seems especially acute in contexts involving symbolic practices that lack direct causal interaction with a shared physical environment — for example, the interpretation of highly formalised legal codes or ritual notation systems in which the 'world' being referred to is largely institutionally constructed. Does this kind of 'radical misinterpretation' mark a genuine limit to Davidson's framework, or is it merely an extreme, and therefore unproblematic, application of it?

With that in mind, I should like you to prioritise the following lines of inquiry and defend the ordering. First, the tension between radical interpretation and semantic externalism: can meaning be fixed by interpretation alone when causal-environmental constraints are weak or absent? Second, whether the principle of charity can be calibrated without collapsing into a form of conceptual imperialism when applied across radically divergent conceptual schemes. Third, the possibility of extending radical interpretation to non-propositional or non-linguistic symbolic systems, and whether such an extension requires abandoning the belief-desire psychology that Davidson treats as foundational. Please explain which of these questions are upstream of the others, which are tractable with current conceptual tools, and which should be deprioritised because resolving them would not substantially alter the broader landscape. I am not asking for a bibliography or a reading list; I want a defended agenda — an account of where the philosophical stakes genuinely lie and what kind of argument or evidence over the coming years would most alter your ranking. If you think my framing of 'radical misinterpretation' is itself confused, say so and explain why.
