I'm taking this course on history of medicine and honestly I'm stuck on a writing problem. There's this pattern I keep seeing in books about 19th-century asylums and colonial health campaigns - the author spends chapters explaining how doctors back then genuinely believed in humoral theory or racial science, how institutions worked differently, all that careful contextual work. But then the ending always pivots to "and obviously by modern standards this was unethical," and the context just becomes a long explanation for why they couldn't be like us yet. It's not plain presentism because they aren't just calling people monsters; they do the historicist work. But it's also not real historicism because modern medical ethics is still the hidden endpoint measuring everything.

I saw it in a book on French psychiatric reform, another on census practices in British India, and one on Scandinavian eugenics debates. In each case the "historical context" is like a one-way bridge pointing to our present values, so the historian gets to seem generous and rigorous while still delivering the same moral verdict. Does this specific hybrid have a name? I know Whig history and presentism, but neither captures the double move where contextualization becomes an excuse-structure for failing standards they never actually held. If there's no term, can someone coin one with clear boundaries - like how it differs from plain bad history or from moral relativism where you just say "it was fine for them"? And is escaping your own era's standards even possible once you write conclusions, or is that just a fantasy we tell ourselves?
