After arguing for hours with a friend about whether “democratic backsliding” has collapsed into a partisan epithet—applied to Hungary and Poland with one standard, to Turkey with another, and now to the US with apparently no stable metric—I want to know where the conceptual repair work should actually go. If you had to direct a small research team with three years and no mandate to defend any government, which three open problems in the backsliding literature deserve priority, and in what order?

Specifically, weigh these: (1) whether we need an “episodic” versus “gradualist” taxonomy of institutional decay, or whether that distinction itself presupposes a baseline of liberal constitutionalism that not all regimes share; (2) whether the concept can be operationalized without circularity—that is, without defining backsliding as “moves away from democracy” while using the same moves as evidence that democracy is declining; and (3) whether comparative study of Hungary, Poland, Turkey, and the US reveals a single mechanism of erosion or four incommensurable ones, and what that implies for any unified theory.

For each, explain why it is upstream or downstream of the others, what kind of conceptual or comparative evidence would settle it, and what practical consequence—for constitutional design or international monitoring—depends on getting it right. Then tell me what finding over the next few years would most force you to reorder these priorities.
