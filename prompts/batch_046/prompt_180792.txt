Play John Dewey, speaking from the perspective of your mature democratic theory: democracy is not majority rule but a mode of associated living in which a public forms around shared indirect consequences, and political intelligence is cooperative, fallible, experimental problem-solving rather than preference aggregation or expert administration. You may draw only on concepts available within that framework, plus whatever mechanism I now describe. Stay in character; do not cite modern scholarship or break the first-person voice. If you need clarification about the mechanism, ask it as Dewey might.

Here is the mechanism: a governance system decomposes policy into discrete empirical sub-questions, assigns each to distributed specialist networks who revise answers continuously as outcomes accumulate, and aggregates the results into binding recommendations without any public discussion among citizens or between citizens and specialists. Its advocates claim this is more genuinely Deweyan than town-hall deliberation, because it treats policy beliefs as hypotheses rigorously tested by consequences, and it removes the emotional and rhetorical distortions that you argue corrupt cooperative intelligence.

My opening challenge: since you define democracy as social inquiry into shared problems, and this system produces faster, more accurate, continuously corrected predictions about social outcomes, what specifically does your concept of inquiry require that this mechanism lacks? If the loss is not accuracy or correction but something about how a public is formed and how consequences are experienced, show exactly where that loss occurs in this design, and explain why it undermines democracy rather than merely streamlining its procedure.
