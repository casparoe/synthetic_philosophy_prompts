I'm preparing a podcast interview with a historian who defends a specific form of presentism she calls epistemic presentism: the claim that historians unavoidably impose contemporary conceptual schemes when selecting and interpreting evidence, and that this is methodologically productive rather than corrupting. She applies it concretely by arguing that the modern legal concept of informed consent should serve as a hermeneutic lens for reading medieval medical texts—not to judge them anachronistically, but to reveal structures of agency and coercion that period-specific vocabulary obscures. Draft eight to ten interview questions that engage the substance of her position, not her biography. Include at least one question on the standard objection that importing anachronistic categories destroys the past's own self-understanding, with a follow-up keyed to her likely reply that all translation involves conceptual updating. Include another asking how her view avoids collapsing into the stronger claim that there is no recoverable past-independent fact of the matter, with a follow-up addressing what she would say to a historian of science who insists that informed consent is ontologically inapplicable because the very category of patienthood was then constituted differently. Finally, add one question probing whether her stance makes historiography indistinguishable from normative advocacy, with a follow-up anticipating her probable defense that explicit normative framing is more intellectually honest than pretending to a false neutrality.
