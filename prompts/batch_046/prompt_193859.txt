I am studying Kant's essay On a Supposed Right to Lie from Philanthropy, in which he answers Benjamin Constant's claim that one may lie to a murderer at the door in order to protect an innocent life. The following passage summarizes Kant's central reasoning.

Even when a false statement succeeds in saving a life, it violates an unconditional duty of truthfulness. The liar treats rational beings not as ends in themselves but as instruments to be manipulated, and in doing so he corrupts the very source of all legal and social obligation. If the host answers falsely about the victim's whereabouts, the murderer might be misdirected and, by leaving, accidentally encounter the victim elsewhere; yet this risk is not the real ground of the prohibition. The essential wrong is that the speaker damages humanity in his own person and undermines the universal credibility upon which all rights and contracts depend. Truthfulness in declaration admits no exception based on benevolent motives, because any permitted exception would destroy the possibility of reliable testimony altogether.

I am trying to understand how these different strands of the argument relate to one another. Is the primary wrong a direct duty owed to the murderer himself—who, despite his murderous intent, remains a rational being entitled not to be deceived—or is it an indirect, systemic harm done to the institution of human communication and trust? And what precise work is the observation about the lie accidentally causing the opposite outcome meant to do? Does Kant introduce it only to show that consequential reasoning is unreliable, or does it serve a deeper purpose in explaining why the speaker cannot claim moral ownership over the results of his words? Could you help me clarify whether the direct duty to each rational agent and the indirect preservation of social trust are meant to reinforce each other, and if so, which one carries the foundational weight in Kant's rejection of the supposed right?
