I am preparing a lecture on Marx’s account of exploitation and alienation for a course that pairs philosophical texts with classic cinema. We are screening Fritz Lang’s Metropolis (1927), and I want to use it not merely as an illustration of worker exploitation but as a site where Marx’s conceptual distinctions become visually ambiguous. The film famously depicts the underground workers as synchronized extensions of the Moloch-like machines, and it frames Freder’s mediation between head and hands as a resolution. I am particularly interested in whether the film captures what Marx, especially in the Grundrisse and Capital, describes as the transition from formal to real subsumption: the point at which capital no longer merely directs labor but reshapes the labor process itself through machinery, so that the worker’s physical movements are dictated by the technical requirements of fixed capital rather than by human skill or pace.

Read Metropolis through this lens. Does the machinery sequence—where workers collapse from exhaustion and are replaced like interchangeable parts—depict alienation primarily as subjective suffering, or does it visualize the structural domination of dead labor over living labor that Marx associates with real subsumption? Where does the film’s resolution, with Freder acting as the mediator between brain and hands, fall short of Marx’s critique? In particular, I want you to consider whether the film’s humanist ending inadvertently reinstates the very separation it depicts: by making reconciliation depend on a singular charismatic figure rather than on the transformation of the labor process itself, does Metropolis turn a structural critique into a moral drama about individual virtue? Please treat both the film’s visual logic and Marx’s theoretical architecture seriously, and say where the film resists being reduced to an allegory—whether there are scenes or images that complicate the idea that alienation can be resolved by goodwill alone. I do not need citations or a reading list; I am looking for an interpretive argument I can present to students who have read excerpts from the 1844 manuscripts and Capital, but not the entire corpus.
