I've been working through Graham Priest's arguments on dialetheism and paraconsistent logic—particularly his claim that certain contradictions arising at the boundary of cognition are not merely tolerable but genuinely true. I understand that formal systems like LP block the principle of explosion, so that accepting a contradiction does not license one to derive every arbitrary proposition. But I'm struggling with how this applies outside pure logic. Priest seems to suggest that contradictions emerge specifically at the limits of thought—cases where we attempt to conceptualize totalities that include themselves—so dialetheism is not just a technical fix for semantic paradoxes like the liar, but a claim about the structure of rational inquiry itself. Here is where I get confused: if contradictions can be true at these cognitive boundaries, and paraconsistent logic is specifically designed to prevent them from spreading across the system, doesn't that still leave us with an arbitration problem in practical domains? When two researchers or policymakers hold flatly contradictory beliefs about, say, climate risk or distributive justice—neither belief involving a self-referential totality—what prevents a dialetheist from treating that ordinary disagreement as just another true contradiction rather than as a mistake that demands resolution? In other words, does dialetheism secretly depend on a separate, non-logical theory of error to do any work in science or ethics, and if so, hasn't the view already conceded that consistency carries normative weight after all?
