I have been thinking about Douglas Hofstadter's idea that an "I" is not a fixed soul inside one skull but rather a kind of self-referential pattern—a strange loop—that might persist, in fragments, inside other people who knew the original well. Because I have no formal philosophical training, I tried to make this concrete with a scenario I invented and then tested it on a friend who reads casually in the area. Imagine two people, M and N, who for twelve years kept one shared handwritten journal, writing in strict alternation sentence by sentence without ever signing an entry, until neither could reliably say which lines were hers. After N dies suddenly, M continues writing alone, yet she reports that the prose still feels like "us" rather than "me," and she finds phrases emerging that she is certain she would never have composed in isolation. I asked my friend whether this genuinely shows that an "I" can be distributed across brains in Hofstadter's sense, or whether the case is being misread.

My friend's written response was this: yes, it does show distribution, because Hofstadter says the self is essentially a software-like pattern that does not depend on any specific physical matter, so once M and N built a shared loop through the journal, N's self-pattern was literally running inside M's brain all along; when N died, that loop did not vanish but kept operating in M, which is exactly why M feels "us" and produces uncharacteristic sentences. The friend added that Hofstadter made the same point after his own wife died, saying parts of her lived on in him and in friends, except here the sharing happened while both were alive, which only strengthens the case. The only weakness the friend acknowledged is emotional—M might feel grief or loneliness—but insisted that is not a metaphysical problem, so the conclusion stands.

Please treat that response as an exam answer and grade it on a scale from 0 to 100, where 100 is fully correct. Give me a point-by-point justification: which parts correctly grasp Hofstadter's view about patterns and persistence, where the reasoning makes subtle slips (for instance, about substrate independence, about what it means for a pattern to live in another mind, or about applying post-death persistence to a living co-author), and whether the conclusion actually follows from the premises. Also tell me what objection my friend is probably hoping nobody raises, and how serious that hidden objection is. I am not looking for a bibliography or further reading—just an assessment of the reasoning itself.
