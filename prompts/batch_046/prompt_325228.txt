Compare the intentionalist and structural-agency frameworks for attributing moral responsibility to a nation-state for the outbreak of the First World War, using the Fischer controversy as the concrete reference point. The intentionalist framework treats collective blame as analogous to individual blame: it requires identifiable intentions, decisive choice-points, and a coherent strategic plan—such as the claim that German leadership deliberately pursued continental hegemony and accepted the risk of general war. The structural-agency framework, by contrast, holds that an institution can be blameworthy when its organizational architecture systematically rewards escalation, suppresses dissenting information, and distributes causal roles such that no single actor holds the full intention, yet the collective outcome is not accidental. Apply both to the following hypothetical: declassified records reveal that a senior German official in July 1914 explicitly warned against full mobilization, yet was overruled not by malicious design but by bureaucratic protocol that automatically elevated military railway timetables over diplomatic objections. Can structural-agency treat this as confirming institutional blameworthiness without attributing a uniform intention to every relevant actor? Conversely, can intentionalism accommodate this evidence without collapsing into the revisionist claim that alliance rigidities and mobilization protocols fully excuse the collective? Conclude by assessing which framework better explains what it means, in a genuinely moral rather than causal sense, to say that "Germany" bears responsibility for 1914.
