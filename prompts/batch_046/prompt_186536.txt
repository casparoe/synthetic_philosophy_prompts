Two people in my book club got stuck in an arguement over Pascal — specifically that bit about justice being powerless without force, and force being tyranny without justice. One said this clearly means every workable legal system needs some actual coercive backup, otherwise it's just moralising; she used our local housing co-op's no-fine mediation scheme as an example, saying it fails exactly because there's no teeth. The other said she was misreading Pascal: justice for him is just conventional rules people recognise, so if the co-op agrees on outcomes through talk and social shunning, that's justice with its own kind of force — peer pressure counts. She then said this means Pascal's distinction isn't between violence and talk, but between legitimate and illegitimate power. The first one came back: if peer pressure counts as force, then Pascal's claim collapses into justice always being backed by pressure, which makes the idea of justice without force meaningless. The second said that's not a collapse, it's the point — you can't seperate them. They wouldn't budge. I was just listening. Can you go through who's right on which point — especially whether social pressure counts as Pascal's force — and say what both are missing?
