I am writing a novel about a family confronting an advance directive left by a patriarch now lost to dementia, and I want the ethical architecture of the story to rest on something I actually believe rather than on received opinion. To that end, I have been testing my intuitions against particular cases and find that they cluster, but not cleanly.

Consider these verdicts. A man who directed that he not receive artificial nutrition should he develop severe Alzheimer's with total loss of recognition and constant agitation is now in precisely that condition; I believe the document binds and the tube should be withdrawn, though he no longer understands what a document is. A woman in an identically severe state, but placid, responsive to music, and apparently content, left the same instruction; here I hesitate, feeling that withdrawing care would harm a being who can still suffer or be comforted, as if the self that wrote the directive has been replaced by another who deserves protection. A composer directed that he be allowed to die if he lost all capacity for music; he is now deaf but mentally acute, composing through vibration and teaching with unmistakable joy, and I think the condition has not been triggered despite the literal deprivation. A patient wrote that she should not be kept alive if she became "a burden to my children"; she now requires extensive care, expresses gratitude for it, and her children wish to continue, though she occasionally voices regret for still living; I think the directive should not govern, because the relational meaning of burden has transformed. The case that divides me is this: a woman with frontotemporal dementia retains almost no autobiographical memory, has become exuberantly affectionate in ways her family finds beautiful, and left a directive stating that if she is "not the person I was," life should not be prolonged; on some days I think a new self now inhabits the body and the old authority is void, on others I think biological continuity preserves the original claim, and I cannot stabilize my judgment. Is there a principle—perhaps one that treats the authority of an earlier self as a kind of social convention in Lewis's sense, sustained only where coordination between temporal stages remains possible—that can organize these judgments? State the principle with precision, assign each clause to the case it explains, predict its verdict for a scenario in which a patient verbally revokes his directive after losing short-term memory but retaining emotional coherence, and tell me honestly which of my intuitions it must reject in order to avoid ad hoc complexity. I ask this without any need for citations or reading lists; I want only the reasoning itself, rendered in continuous prose without headings, bullet points, bold type, or tables, in American English.
