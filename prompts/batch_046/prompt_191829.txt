After a long and somewhat exhausting argument with a close friend about whether shifting away from beef and chicken toward plant-based proteins genuinely reduces total sentient suffering, I have been trying to work out where Jeremy Bentham would stand on the specific comparative problem that has only become empirically tractable in the last few decades. The dispute centered on three categories: pasture-raised beef, which involves direct, intentional killing of large mammals but displaces relatively few wild animals per unit of protein; industrial chicken production, which inflicts prolonged suffering on enormous numbers of birds with comparatively high neural complexity; and crop-based agriculture, which avoids direct institutional harm to domesticated animals yet causes diffuse, structural mortality among wild rodents, insects, and birds through mechanical harvesting, pesticide application, and habitat conversion. My friend argued that Bentham, applying his criterion of the capacity to suffer without species boundary, would be forced to conclude that plant agriculture is the worst option because the absolute number of wild sentient deaths is highest, whereas I countered that Bentham's utilitarian calculus would weight the intensity and duration of suffering more heavily than mere mortality counts, thereby favoring crops over chickens and perhaps even over beef.

I want you to predict, with reasoning reconstructed from Bentham's broader moral and political philosophy rather than from a single well-known passage, exactly how he would adjudicate this triad if he were presented with modern agronomic data—specifically, mortality curves for wild vertebrates in monoculture fields, neuroethological evidence that avian pain processing differs structurally from mammalian nociception, and supply-chain analyses that distinguish direct institutional harm from diffuse structural harm. Would he treat all suffering as strictly fungible across species and contexts, or would he allow that the temporal profile of distress, the neural architecture of the sufferer, and the moral distinction between intentional and foreseeable-but-unintended harm modify the calculation? Furthermore, identify the weakest point in your reconstruction—the place where attributing this specific framework to Bentham relies most heavily on projection rather than on his actual commitments.
