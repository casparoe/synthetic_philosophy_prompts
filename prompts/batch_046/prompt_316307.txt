The debate between the Self-Sampling Assumption and the Self-Indication Assumption in anthropic reasoning—centered on Bostrom’s presumptuous-philosopher objection—has persisted since the early 2000s with almost no convergence: SSA advocates continue to argue that SIA lets one infer an enormous universe from mere existence, while SIA defenders maintain that ignoring observer-selection effects is the deeper error. The formal derivations and toy-universe setups are identical across the literature, yet neither side revises its position. Is this stall actually about probability theory, or is it a disagreement about what counts as evidence when the sample space is constituted by one’s own presence? Specifically, are the sides disputing the correct reference class for observers, or are they answering different questions—one about predictive betting, one about rational credence—under a single Bayesian vocabulary? Given your diagnosis, could progress come from a formal result that separates SSA from SIA outside contrived two-universe models, from a conceptual dissolution of the reference-class problem (for instance, via an indexical-lottery framing applied symmetrically to both sides), or is the impasse a sign that anthropic reasoning lacks the canonical prior required for the dispute to be well-formed? I want a diagnosis grounded in what each side actually commits to, not a survey of interpretations, together with an honest answer about whether anything—experiment being impossible here—could genuinely move it.
