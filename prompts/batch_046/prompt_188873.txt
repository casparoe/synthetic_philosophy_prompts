I have constructed an argument that seems to show moral learning from biography is structurally impossible, yet this conclusion is hard to accept given Plutarch's explicit aims and the persistent practice of reading lives for virtue. I want a committed diagnosis: which step fails, why it looked plausible, and what giving it up costs.

Here is the argument.

(1) If biography teaches moral virtue, it must provide either direct exemplars for imitation or material for independent critical evaluation of character.
(2) Direct imitation is unavailable: the subjects of biography—statesmen, generals, saints—acted in institutional and temporal contexts radically unlike the reader's, so replicating their actions is neither possible nor appropriate.
(3) Independent critical evaluation is also unavailable, because hagiography and even classical biography construct character through selective narrative framing—omitting vices, magnifying virtues, and compressing moral development into static typological moments—so the reader has no access to the subject except through the biographer's prior moral taxonomy.
(4) Therefore any normative insight the reader draws from a life is circular: she uses the biography to calibrate her judgment, but the biography is already calibrated by the biographer's judgments.
(5) Non-circular moral learning requires unmediated access to moral reality; without it, biography teaches only the biographer's prejudices.
(6) Hence biography cannot teach moral virtue, and Plutarch's claim that his parallel lives cultivate the reader's soul rests on a systematic illusion.
(7) But we do learn from biography, or at least many report genuine moral insight; so (6) is false, and the argument must fail somewhere.

The argument feels suspicious because the conclusion undermines an entire tradition without explaining away the apparent success of biographical moral education. I am inclined to keep (1) and (3)—that biography teaches through framing and that framing is unavoidable—but I want to know exactly where the slide happens. Is the mistake in (4), treating calibration as vicious rather than virtuous? In (5), demanding unmediated access that moral epistemology never requires? Or in (2), conflating imitation of actions with emulation of dispositions? Give me a definite answer: identify the weakest step, explain why it seemed necessary, and tell me what I must pay—conceptually and practically—to reject it. I am not asking for a survey of positions; I want a committed verdict with reasons.
