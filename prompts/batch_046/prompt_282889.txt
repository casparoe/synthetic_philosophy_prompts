Design a 90-minute seminar activity for 16 upper-level philosophy students centered on the Epicurean argument that death is not harmful, arranged so that the group experiences a structural pressure before anyone names the philosophical source. Divide the session into three timed phases with assigned roles. Phase 1 (25 minutes): split students into four Councils—Living, Not-Yet-Born, Dead, and Never-Conceived—each receiving three case cards describing institutional allocation dilemmas (e.g., a budget divided between life-saving treatment and posthumous memorials; a reproductive policy weighing the prevention of painful births against the promotion of happy ones; inheritance rights versus prenatal investment funds). Each Council must distribute 100 “concern units” across the four temporal bins under a single constraint: a unit may be placed only where a subject exists to bear harm or benefit. After allocation, Councils reveal their distributions publicly. Phase 2 (30 minutes): impose the Symmetry Lock—the facilitator states that the Not-Yet-Born and Dead bins are structurally identical, both lacking a present subject—and requires every Council to revise allocations so that identical bins receive identical concern, or to write a public justification for asymmetry. Then present a hidden Stake Card forcing a collective vote: if the group withdraws all concern from the Dead bin to preserve consistency, it must also withdraw concern from the Not-Yet-Born bin, which undermines common reproductive and future-oriented ethics; if it retains concern for the Not-Yet-Born, it must restore equivalent concern for the Dead, dissolving the Epicurean consolation. Phase 3 (20 minutes): each Council drafts a one-paragraph Boundary Charter stating whether it has smuggled in a metaphysics of potential subjects, adopted a purely experiential criterion of harm, or abandoned consistency, and reads it aloud. Debrief (15 minutes): map Phase 1 onto the “no subject, no harm” premise; the Symmetry Lock onto the comparison of pre-vital and post-mortem non-existence; and the Stake Card onto the practical instability that arises when the argument is extended from individual psychology to institutional moral bookkeeping. Note what will predictably go wrong: students typically rescue asymmetry by appealing to relationships, narrative identity, or institutional legacy, which the activity permits but cannot adjudicate, so the facilitator should be ready to distinguish experiential harm from relational harm without resolving the dispute. Be frank about what the activity cannot reproduce: the argument’s intended first-person effect—the dissolution of death-anxiety—requires subjective acceptance that a third-person allocation game can only model structurally, not emotionally; the facilitator must supply that framing explicitly. Frame the four bins not as metaphysical categories but as actual moral-bookkeeping entries maintained by estates, clinics, and memorial budgets, so the paradox appears in accounting logic rather than abstract ontology. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations, and ensure all rules and payoffs are arranged so participants feel the conflict between consistency and intuition before the idea is named.
