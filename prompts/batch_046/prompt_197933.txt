In preparing next term's graduate seminar on modal epistemology, I have been sharpening a particular worry about the conceivability-possibility link. I want to distinguish first-order phenomenal conceivability—the direct imaginative construction of a scenario—from second-order structural conceivability, which asks whether that constructed model could be instantiated without undermining the cognitive conditions that made the construction available in the first place. When I apply this distinction to the familiar case of water not being H2O, the first-order level permits me to imagine a clear, thirst-quenching liquid that lacks hydrogen bonding; but at the second-order level, it seems the very neural and biochemical processes that enable my modeling depend on aqueous chemistry, so the scenario appears to violate its own conditions of conceivability. From this, I am tempted to conclude that conceivability does not guide metaphysical possibility at all, but rather traces the biological boundaries of the conceiver—what one might call a cognitive-essentialist collapse. Yet I suspect there is a category mistake here, either in treating the vehicle of conception as part of the content of what is conceived, or in assuming that second-order conditions generate modal constraints rather than epistemic ones. Could you help me locate precisely where the confusion lies, and whether this two-level framework can be rehabilitated without falling into subject-dependent modal relativism?
