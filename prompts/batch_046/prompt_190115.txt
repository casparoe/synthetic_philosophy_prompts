In my philosophy of ethics reading group we have spent the better part of eighteen months circling what is usually called the paradox of deontology — the tension between the prohibition on violating individual rights and the apparent obligation to prevent a greater number of such violations — without either side conceding a millimetre. The stalemate is peculiar because the disagreement is not over the descriptive facts: both camps accept that standard deontological frameworks forbid harming one innocent person even to save five, and both accept that this produces counter-intuitive results in aggregation scenarios. One side, which I will call the Incommensurability Camp, treats this result as a feature: moral reasons of the constraint type are not fungible, they argue, and any framework that permits trade-offs among them has already abandoned deontology for a disguised form of consequentialism. The other side, the Lexical-Structure Camp, insists that the paradox is an artefact of representing deontic reasons as scalar weights rather than as lexically ordered priorities; once the formal structure is corrected, they claim, the contradiction dissolves without sacrificing the non-instrumental status of persons.

What makes the impasse frustrating is that both sides have come to accept the same battery of test cases — not only the familiar trolley variants but also a more niche scenario I want to inject, which we have been calling the cascade case: an agent can violate a single constraint now to prevent a chain of five subsequent violations, but only if each link in the chain requires a distinct agent, so that no single later agent faces the aggregated choice. Even here, agreement on the case structure is total, yet the Incommensurability Camp sees it as confirming that constraints are agent-relative and non-aggregative, whereas the Lexical-Structure Camp sees it as showing that lexical ordering must be applied at the level of institutional design rather than individual choice.

My hypothesis is that this is not a substantive disagreement about moral facts, but a disagreement about what counts as a successful dissolution of a paradox — one side demanding a phenomenological account of why agents experience the constraint as absolute, the other demanding a formal consistency proof within deontic logic. But I am unsure whether there is also a hidden empirical disagreement about how moral reasoning actually operates, or whether the two camps are simply answering different questions under the shared label "the paradox of deontology": one asking whether deontology is coherent, the other asking whether it is psychologically sustainable.

Could you diagnose the structure of this disagreement? Is it verbal, substantive, or a mixture; do the sides differ about facts, about standards of formal adequacy, about which cases count as data, or about bedrock intuitions that neither can argue for? Is there a hidden empirical or formal question — perhaps about the representability of lexical priorities in multi-agent deontic frameworks — that is actually doing the work? Are the camps answering different questions under one name, or is the question itself ill-posed? And given your diagnosis, what would count as genuine progress: a distinction that separates the two problems, an experiment or formal result that both sides would accept as bearing on the crux, a change of framing, or an argument that no progress should be expected and why?
