While revisiting Nietzsche’s 1874 essay on the uses and disadvantages of history for life—specifically his tripartite distinction between monumental, antiquarian, and critical history—I have been attempting to apply the scheme to what might be called institutionalised mnemonic culture: a social arrangement in which heritage archiving, heroic national narratives, and perpetual deconstructive critique all operate simultaneously, often reinforced by algorithmic amplification and curatorial automation. From this application I have built an argument that seems to show Nietzsche’s taxonomy cannot diagnose any modern pathology of memory, yet I cannot identify the misstep.

(1) For Nietzsche, each mode—monumental, antiquarian, and critical—is healthy for life only when it serves present vitality and is kept in proper proportion to the others.
(2) If a society institutionalises all three modes at once, then any particular engagement with the past can be subsumed under at least one mode that, in principle, serves life.
(3) Therefore, in such a society, no historical practice can be genuinely harmful or excessive, since it is always redeemable as an instance of one legitimate, life-serving category.
(4) Yet Nietzsche explicitly warns that an overabundance of any single mode—antiquarian accumulation without critical dissolution, for instance—poisons life and produces a kind of historical indigestion.
(5) So if (3) holds, Nietzsche’s warnings about excess become incoherent: excess cannot occur if every instance is automatically justified by its membership in a life-serving mode.
(6) To preserve the coherence of these warnings, we must reject (3) and concede that some practices—mere archival hoarding, or monumental spectacle detached from present action—serve none of the three modes genuinely.
(7) But this concession requires an independent criterion for “genuine service to present life” that is not itself derived from the three modes.
(8) Nietzsche offers no such external standard; for him, the modes are defined precisely by their functional relation to life.
(9) Hence the framework either renders historical excess impossible (contradicting Nietzsche’s own diagnoses) or silently depends on a normative measure that it explicitly denies.

Where does this argument go wrong? Is the flaw an equivocation between being classifiable under a mode and actually enhancing present vitality, or is there a hidden scope shift between “a society employs all three modes” and “every individual practice is correctly regulated by one”? If I am forced to choose, I should prefer to retain (1) and (4)—the claims that the modes require proportion and that excess harms life—rather than (3). But I want to understand the price of that choice: does preserving them commit me to (7) and thereby import a non-Nietzschean standard, or can I reject a different premise with less damage to the taxonomy? Please answer in British English, with careful attention to whether the relation between the modes is meant to be mutually regulatory or merely coexistent, and rank the available options by how much each costs.
