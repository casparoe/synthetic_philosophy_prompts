Write the opening remarks—approximately seven minutes—for an interdisciplinary workshop on university archives and automated data-retention policy. The audience is divided among archivists and special-collections librarians, digital-systems engineers, student-privacy advocates, and historians of science. The central policy tension being debated is whether institutions should deploy algorithmic “right-to-be-forgotten” protocols that automatically purge certain administrative and alumni records after fixed intervals.

Organize the address around Nietzsche’s three modes of relating to the past—monumental, antiquarian, and critical—not as rhetorical decoration but as the governing architecture of the argument. Each mode should map onto a concrete institutional temptation: the monumental impulse to curate heroic founding narratives and celebrated alumni legacies; the antiquarian drive to preserve every digital trace regardless of storage cost or present burden; and the critical necessity of deliberate, selective forgetting so that living institutional purpose is not suffocated by accumulated record.

Inject the following conceptual twist: because digital storage is now cheap and retention is the technical default, forgetting is no longer a passive decay but an active, labor-intensive, and resource-consuming achievement. Consequently, the “critical” mode of history requires explicit political will and engineering effort rather than mere neglect. At the same time, navigate the room’s division carefully—several archivists will hear “critical forgetting” as an assault on marginalized histories that were barely preserved in the first place—so the remarks must distinguish life-serving, judgmental release from indiscriminate erasure.

The speech should be fully intelligible to the engineers without condescending to the archivists, and it should avoid bibliographies, citations, references to secondary scholarship, or appeals to texts the audience cannot be expected to have at hand. Let the philosophical framework determine not only the content but also the emotional register and the boundaries of what can and cannot be said on this occasion.
