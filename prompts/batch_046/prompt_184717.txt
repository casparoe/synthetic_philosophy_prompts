I have been discussing Zhuangzi’s butterfly dream with a small philosophy reading group, and I keep noticing a recurring move that I do not have a precise word for. In the familiar modern versions of dream scepticism—whether Cartesian or the brain-in-a-vat variant—people tend to imagine discovering that their present experience is illusory by means of a kind of awakening: one “wakes up” in the real world and sees the previous state as a mere dream or simulation. The transition is treated as epistemically authoritative in one direction only; leaving the illusion is taken to reveal the true ontology, whereas the symmetrical possibility—that one might “fall asleep” from reality into an equally real butterfly-state—is treated as just another layer of doubt rather than a parallel transformation. Even in commentaries on Zhuangzi, I find that readers often ask “which state is the real one?”, yet they still implicitly frame the answer around the human waking-state, as though the butterfly’s awakening into human form were necessarily the corrective moment.

What I am trying to isolate is not dream scepticism itself, nor the problem of other minds, but specifically this asymmetrical privileging of the “exit” transition—the assumption that moving out of one experiential framework into another is, by that very movement, gaining access to a more fundamental reality, even when the narrative structure offers no internal criterion for that privilege. I can see it operating not only in the butterfly story but also in simulation hypotheses, in certain readings of Plato’s Cave where escaping the cave is treated as an ontological promotion rather than a change of perspective, and in everyday talk of “waking up” to social or political realities. Does this pattern have an established name in philosophy of mind or epistemology—perhaps within literature on hierarchical realities, ontological parity, or phenomenological framing? If there is no precise term, how should one distinguish it from ordinary sceptical ascent, and where are its edges: for instance, does it apply only when the transition is phenomenologically symmetrical in both directions, or whenever an argument treats one experiential frame as the default?

Please answer in British English, and do not be satisfied with a bare label: I want to know exactly what the phenomenon is, what it is not, which existing concepts come closest, how each differs from the thing I have described, and whether any of them fit well or only approximately.
