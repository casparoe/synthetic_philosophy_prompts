Here are my judgments about when welfare conditionality crosses into unjust paternalism or undeserved punishment, and I want to know whether they hang together.

Case 1: A recipient is caught deliberately concealing cash income, and benefits are suspended for six months. This seems clearly justified.

Case 2: A recipient declines a job offer that pays below the legal minimum wage through a training-exemption loophole, and her benefits are reduced. This seems wrong—the condition is being used to coerce acceptance of exploitation.

Case 3: A recipient misses two mandatory job-training sessions because she is caring for a dying parent, and her payments are automatically suspended with no appeal window. This seems wrong.

Case 4: The state introduces a pilot program that sends behavioral letters comparing each recipient's job-search activity to anonymized peer benchmarks; those who do not log into the portal within ten days are flagged for review and face delayed payments. The framing is transparent, but the mechanism manufactures social pressure. I think this is permissible only if recipients can opt out of the comparison without penalty, yet I also feel uneasy about the state engineering stigma in this way.

Case 5: An algorithm predicts, from postal-code data and linguistic patterns in her application, that a recipient is likely to violate future tenancy conditions; she has never violated any condition, but her emergency housing aid is downgraded. This seems clearly wrong.

Case 6: A recipient spends six months caring full-time for her dying parent and makes no job-search effort; her benefits are suspended. I think this is wrong.

Case 7: Another recipient spends six months writing an unpublished novel, makes no job-search effort, and her benefits are suspended. Here I am inclined to say the suspension is justified, or at least not clearly wrong—though I recognize that Cases 6 and 7 are structurally identical in terms of labor-market withdrawal.

Case 8: The state requires recipients to sign a personal-responsibility contract containing moral language about earning one's citizenship before receiving aid that is legally unconditional. I think this is permissible but distasteful, provided signing is not coerced through hidden penalties.

My central difficulty is that Cases 6 and 7 pull in opposite directions—both involve voluntary withdrawal from the labor market for a personal project—yet I treat them differently. Meanwhile, Cases 2 and 3 both involve non-compliance with conditions, but I reject the penalties for different reasons (exploitation versus absence of procedural safeguard).

Is there a coherent principle—perhaps involving retrospective accountability, predictive reliability, or the distinction between direct coercion and architectural pressure—that captures Cases 1 through 5 and 8? What does it predict for Cases 6 and 7: must I overturn one verdict, or can a more complex principle save both at the cost of simplicity?

Please also apply your principle to a case I have not listed: a recipient refuses Saturday work on conscientious religious grounds, offers an alternative weekday schedule, and is denied both the accommodation and the benefit.

Finally, please say what would change your mind—specifically, which argument about the moral status of predictive profiling or about the value of unpaid caregiving relative to other personal projects would lead you to reverse your judgment about Case 5 or about the tension between Cases 6 and 7, and identify the point in your reasoning where you are most likely to be wrong.
