I have been working through some literature on moral psychology and find myself caught on a conceptual knot that I hope you can help untangle. The standard framing of the situationist critique against virtue ethics holds that if virtues are robust global traits—dispositions to behave consistently across diverse contexts—then the empirical evidence for high situational variability in moral behavior undermines the very possibility of virtue. In response, some defenders have proposed what we might call a modular virtue model: rather than treating courage or honesty as unitary global dispositions, virtues are reconceived as clusters of domain-specific capacities that are activated only within particular situational trigger profiles. On this view, an agent might possess the virtue of honesty in professional contexts but not in intimate ones, not because of a failure of global character, but because the virtue itself is structurally localized.

My question concerns the conceptual coherence of this modular move. Specifically, I want to understand whether modularizing virtues genuinely preserves the normative and explanatory ambitions of virtue ethics, or whether it collapses the view into a form of situationism by another name. To approach this, please explain the conceptual distinction between trait stability—the idea that a virtue persists across time for a given agent—and trait specificity—the idea that a virtue is defined relative to a restricted class of situations. In particular, address whether virtue ethics requires global stability for its normative force, or whether localized stability is sufficient to ground judgments of moral character, practical wisdom, and the cultivation of virtue through habituation. I am especially interested in how this distinction bears on Aristotle’s idea that virtue is a hexis, a stable state of character, and whether a modular reinterpretation can remain faithful to that conceptual core without simply conceding the situationist’s central claim.
