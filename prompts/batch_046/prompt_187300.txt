I'm preparing a presentation for a graduate seminar on legal philosophy, and I want to test whether layered meta-rules genuinely resolve the classic paradox of constitutional self-reference or merely relocate it. To keep the analysis precise, I have constructed an idealized model charter—call it the Lexicon—organized into three tiers. Tier I contains substantive provisions amendable by a two-thirds legislative vote. Tier II governs the rules for amending Tier I, and can only be altered by unanimous consent. Tier III consists of a single clause stating that Tier II may be amended by simple majority, but only if the amending text explicitly cites Tier III itself. This produces a reflexive loop: the authority to change the conditions of change depends on satisfying a condition that is itself part of the conditions being changed.

Please argue for the position that this three-tier architecture does not dissolve the self-reference paradox but transforms it into a problem of interpretive authority. Specifically, argue that every attempt to invoke Tier III to amend Tier II requires a prior determination—by a court or legislative body—of whether the citation requirement has been satisfied, and that this determination is either itself subject to Tier II's unanimous-consent rule, thereby recreating the original loop, or governed by an unamendable external norm, thereby introducing an arbitrary extra-constitutional foundation. In your argument, address whether the distinction between changing a rule and applying a rule can be maintained in this setting, and explain why the explicit self-reference in Tier III makes the paradox more acute than it would be in a system where self-amendment is only implicit. Treat the Lexicon as a purely theoretical construct; you need not appeal to any actual historical constitution.
