Please describe the most important implications of Strawson's reactive attitudes framework for evaluating moral responsibility in cases of "displaced reactive attitudes" — situations in which the appropriate target of resentment or moral indignation is absent, replaced, or structurally unavailable, and the emotional response is genuinely directed at a proxy (for instance, an institutional successor, an artificial system designed to absorb blame, or a collective entity that lacks the psychological capacities for reciprocal engagement). Specifically, address the following: first, whether Strawson's distinction between the participant reactive attitude and the objective attitude can be sustained when the target is not a moral agent capable of entering into reciprocal interpersonal relations; second, whether practices of moral repair — apology, forgiveness, and reconciliation — retain their internal point if the recipient of these practices cannot genuinely participate in them; and third, what this reveals about the boundaries of reactive-attitude-based moral responsibility beyond direct interpersonal dyads. Your answer should not depend on citations or bibliographic references, and should assume only familiarity with the core structure of Strawson's argument rather than with specialised secondary literature.
