In philosophy of action and comparative cognition, the stereotyped burrow-checking routine of the sphex wasp is routinely invoked as a paradigm of sphexishness—mechanical rigidity that seems incompatible with genuine agency. Yet a long-standing disagreement remains deadlocked over whether such rigidity undermines free will specifically because it takes the form of perturbation-invariant sub-routines—behavioral loops that persist unchanged regardless of contextual alteration—or whether determinism in any form, even deeply entrenched but context-sensitive habit, is equally threatening. Those who defend the rigidity-threat view hold that free will requires counterfactual plasticity at the architectural level: an agent must be capable of suspending and revising its own goal-pursuit mechanisms in response to novel evidence, not merely adapting its outputs within fixed parameters. Their opponents—the integrationists—accept the same ethological descriptions but reject plasticity as the relevant criterion; they argue that human free action itself depends on rigid, reliability-preserving modules (syntactic structures, moral heuristics, procedural memory) and that what distinguishes agency is not local flexibility but whether the agent’s overall cognitive economy can represent and endorse its own reasons for acting. For years these sides have remained immobile: they share every empirical claim about wasp neurology and human habit formation, they jointly deny that determinism per se is the enemy, and neither has revised its core commitment. My working hypothesis is that the stalemate is not a substantive dispute about free will but a clash over competing standards of mechanistic explanation—one side demands that free behavior be inexplicable by isolated sub-routines, the other demands only that it be explicable by an integrated rational architecture—though I am unsure whether a hidden empirical question about actual neural architectures of flexibility, or an unrecognized conceptual ambiguity in “mechanical,” is doing the real work. Diagnose the disagreement: is it verbal, substantive, or a case of two questions—one metaphysical, one explanatory—being pursued under a single heading? Then tell me whether anything—an empirical finding about insect or mammalian cognition, a formal model of behavioral invariance, a conceptual distinction between modular and integrated rigidity—could actually move the debate, or whether the impasse indicates that the framing question is ill-posed.
