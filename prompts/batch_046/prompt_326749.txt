I am a journalist who covers science, medicine, and the history of ideas, and I am preparing to deliver a talk at an interdisciplinary symposium on bioethics and health policy. I intend to defend the following thesis: that the clinical and legal adoption of whole-brain death in the 1980s was not an ontological discovery about the cessation of human life, but rather a pragmatic reconceptualization shaped primarily by the logistical demands of transplantation medicine; therefore, I argue that circulatory-respiratory death should be reinstated as the sole legally sufficient criterion for declaring death, while bodies that meet brain-death criteria but retain cardiac function should be regarded as severely disabled living persons from whom organs may be removed only under explicit prior consent, not under a presumed death certificate. The audience will include neurologists and transplant surgeons who depend on brain-death protocols for organ procurement, bioethicists committed to the dead-donor rule, and philosophers of medicine familiar with both the integrative-function and the higher-brain formulations of death. Please prepare me for the objections I will actually face: list them in the order they are likely to arise, phrase each as the corresponding specialist group would phrase it, indicate for each whether I should concede a point or hold firm, note the weakest part of my reply, and identify the objection I am most hoping no one raises—specifically, the practical argument from the surgical contingent that widespread adoption of my view would collapse viable donation programs. I need this structured as a speaker's prep sheet rather than as a general literature review.
