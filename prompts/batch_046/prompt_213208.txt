I'm taking this philosophy of mind seminar and we got into a huge debate about self-deception—like, is it even possible or is it just a confused concept people use when they mean something else. My friend walked me into this argument and now I can't find the exit. It starts off reasonable but ends up saying nobody can ever be wrong about their own motives, which feels wrong but I can't name the exact slip.

(1) If I'm genuinely self-deceived about p, then I genuinely believe p and genuinely believe not-p at the same time, in the same mental system.
(2) But belief is transparent to itself: if I believe p, then I have immedate first-person access to the fact that I believe p.
(3) So if I believed p and not-p together, I'd immedately recognize I hold contradictory beliefs.
(4) Recognizing that you're holding contradictory beliefs is a state of clear irrationality, and anyone in that state is compelled to resolve it.
(5) So no rational agent can maintain contradictory beliefs in a single mental system for long.
(6) But self-deception is usually described as happening in rational people who aren't in obvious breakdown.
(7) Therefore self-deception can't involve literally believing both p and not-p in the same system.
(8) The only other option is that one belief is unconscious or doesn't belong to me, but then it's not self-deception—it's just being deceived by a part of me I don't identify with.
(9) So self-deception is impossible, and in fact any claim that I don't know my own motives is false.

I want to keep the idea that self-deception involves some kind of contradiction, but I don't want to end up saying implicit bias or unconscious motivation is impossible. Where does this go wrong? Is it that transparency only applies to occurrent beliefs? Or is there an equivocation between believing p and being disposed to act on p? What's the cheapest fix that lets me keep (1) and (7) mostly intact?
