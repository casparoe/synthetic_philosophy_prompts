Write an essay in American English addressing whether everyday social conventions—specifically tipping norms, queuing practices, and rules against jaywalking—constitute genuine moral obligations rather than mere customs, and whether temporary visitors have a moral duty to comply with the local versions of these conventions.

Structure your argument around three distinct possible grounds for such a duty: (1) explicit or implicit consent upon entering a community; (2) a general anti-free-riding obligation not to exploit cooperative schemes from which one benefits; and (3) a direct harm-avoidance principle that applies independently of local custom. For each ground, evaluate whether it can justify visitor compliance in hard cases—such as queuing where line-cutting is locally tolerated, tipping where service wages are legally guaranteed but the custom persists, or jaywalking where pedestrian signals are ignored by residents—without collapsing into the claim that any arbitrary local practice generates moral bindingness.

Weigh the principal objections: that conventions stabilize legitimate expectations and that outsiders disrupt them without bearing the social costs of non-compliance, versus the claim that moral obligations cannot be generated by contingent practice alone and that visitors lack the social embeddedness or reciprocal relationships that make conventions binding for members.

Reach a definite conclusion. Defend the view that visitors have a defeasible, context-sensitive obligation to follow local conventions only when non-compliance would directly burden specific cooperators and the convention is not grossly unjust—rejecting both the view that all local customs automatically bind visitors and the view that visitors are morally exempt from all conventional norms. Justify your verdict by identifying which of the three grounds survives critical scrutiny and which fails, and explain why your position avoids treating mere etiquette as morality while still recognizing that some conventional norms protect genuine cooperative goods.
