I have recently become the primary caregiver for my father, whose progressive dementia has made the continuity of personal identity feel less like an abstract philosophical puzzle and more like a practical crisis I navigate each day. Watching his decision-making patterns, emotional reflexes, and narrative habits gradually detach from his own awareness and become reconstructed—often without my intending it—within my own cognitive routines has led me to reconsider Douglas Hofstadter’s strange-loop account of the self. On his view, an “I” is not bound to a single brain but is a pattern that can persist, in fragments, within the minds of those who knew the original substrate well. I want to develop an essay extending this idea into what I will call asymmetrical inter-brain persistence, but I am unsure whether the extension holds together conceptually.

Here are my draft notes for the essay.

The core claim is that Hofstadter’s model of distributed persistence is usually imagined as roughly symmetrical: two brains host overlapping patterns, and the death or decline of one substrate leaves the other as a living archive. In progressive dementia care, however, the replication is sharply one-directional. The patient’s substrate gradually loses the very patterns—preferences for risk, tonal habits of conversation, ethical heuristics—that the caregiver absorbs in order to navigate daily life. The caregiver therefore comes to host fragments of the patient’s “I” that the patient himself no longer maintains. I want to argue that this creates a distinct ontological situation: not shared identity, but parasitic persistence, in which Pattern P exists in Brain B1 (the caregiver) precisely because it has become unstable in Brain B2 (the original substrate).

This raises three difficulties I have not resolved. First, within Hofstadter’s framework, a strange loop requires a kind of self-referential stability; if the original loop is broken, is the copy in the caregiver still the same loop, or merely a derivative simulation? Second, the caregiver’s own “I” is modified by hosting these fragments, producing a hybrid loop whose boundaries are unclear. If I now make decisions using my father’s reconstructed risk preferences, is that an expression of his persisting self, an expression of my altered self, or an emergent third structure? Third, there is an ethical asymmetry: the caregiver hosts patterns that may conflict with the patient’s present expressed wishes, yet those patterns feel, to the caregiver, like authentic continuations rather than impositions.

I am writing to ask for your comments and suggestions on these notes. Specifically: does the notion of asymmetrical persistence remain coherent within a loop-theoretic framework, or does it collapse into a metaphor for psychological influence? Are there conceptual resources—perhaps from theories of extended cognition or shared agency—that could help distinguish genuinely hosting another’s self-fragment from merely simulating it for practical ends? And where is the argument most vulnerable: is there an empirical or conceptual counterexample that would show the caregiver is never hosting fragments of another “I,” but only building predictive models? I am particularly interested in whether you think the hybrid-loop problem undermines the entire proposal, and what kind of consideration—phenomenological, neuropsychological, or logical—would most move you on that point.
