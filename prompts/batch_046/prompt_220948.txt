My friend and I just finished a long, heated argument about intellectual property and whether ideas can genuinely be owned. Rather than continuing to talk past each other, I want us to draft the adversarial-collaboration statement we should have produced from the outset. The two camps are these. Camp A holds that ideas are non-rival social-relational artifacts lacking any intrinsic proprietor; that intellectual property is purely an instrumental legal fiction manufacturing artificial scarcity in goods that are naturally abundant; and that the only coherent evaluative metric is access velocity, not incentive maximization. Camp B holds that idea-patterns are direct causal products of individual cognitive labor; that Lockean labor-mixing applies structurally to mental products just as it does to physical ones; and that patent or copyright is therefore a recognition of pre-legal ownership, not a state-granted monopoly privilege. Draft the joint statement both camps could sign. Structure it as follows: first, a numbered section of agreed claims—covering at minimum the non-rivalrous ontology of ideas, the idea-expression distinction, the empirical opacity of innovation-causation studies, and the social costs of over-enclosure—phrased neutrally enough that neither camp objects. Second, a disagreements table stating the precise residual differences in wording each camp accepts for its own position: specifically, whether mental labor-mixing generates a natural right in the abstract pattern itself; whether artificial scarcity in non-rival goods can ever be justified by access-velocity trade-offs; and whether the state’s role is constitutive or merely recognitive. Third, identify the cruxes—sub-questions such that resolving them would move at least one camp—including whether an idea-pattern can be individuated with sufficient precision to bear ownership attributions, and whether the comparative history of open-source scientific progress versus patent-protected pharmaceutical development actually distinguishes our predictive commitments. Fourth, for each crux, specify one concrete case, argument, or empirical comparison both camps agree bears on it, and record in advance what each camp predicts that evidence will show. Do not ask for bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; do not invoke obscure theorists or doctrines that require external verification; and inject enough conceptual specificity—particularly around the ontology of mental labor-products and the metric of access velocity—that the answer must be constructed from reasoning rather than recalled from standard debate scripts.
