Having spent my career in experimental physics, I have lately found myself drawn to metaphysical questions about causation that my scientific training long took for granted. I would like you to examine the Great Fire of London in September 1666 through the lens of Al-Ghazālī's occasionalism — specifically, the denial of secondary causation and the thesis that God is the direct and sole efficient cause of every particular event, with observed regularities constituting only a customary course, al-ʿāda, rather than necessary connections between causes and effects.

The episode is well known: the fire began in a bakery on Pudding Lane, spread rapidly through timber-framed buildings aided by an easterly wind, destroyed much of the medieval city, and prompted subsequent rebuilding regulations mandating brick construction, wider streets, and regulated party walls. Analyze this sequence through occasionalism. First, map the episode precisely: identify the putative causes that natural philosophers and modern fire engineers cite — the properties of wood, the direction of wind, the proximity of structures — and say exactly what status Al-Ghazālī's metaphysics assigns to each. Does occasionalism treat these as mere occasions for divine action, as epistemic heuristics without ontological weight, or as something else?

Next, apply the following conceptual framing drawn from philosophy of science: distinguish between an invariant generalization — a stable correlation, such as that fire tends to spread through dry timber — and a causal mechanism — the physical process by which heat transfers and combustion propagates. Apply this distinction to Al-Ghazālī's account. Does occasionalism reject invariant generalizations, reject mechanisms, or reconstrue both as descriptions of divine custom? Can the occasionalist acknowledge the predictive success of the brick-building mandates without implicitly conceding that brick possesses a genuine causal power to resist fire?

Then assess the bidirectional illumination. What does occasionalism explain about the episode that a mechanistic account does not — for instance, the contingency of the regularity itself, or the absence of metaphysical necessity in fire's behavior? Conversely, what does the historical regulatory response — the practical assumption that altering material conditions reliably alters outcomes — reveal about the commitments embedded in occasionalism? Does the episode function as a confirmation, an anomaly, or a case the theory never anticipated?
