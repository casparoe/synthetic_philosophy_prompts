As a retired physicist who has only recently turned to philosophy, I have become preoccupied with sphexishness—the rigid, invariant behavioral sequence of the digger wasp that seems incapable of adapting when its environment is altered, and which is often invoked to ask whether determinism leaves room for any meaningful flexibility or freedom. Suppose Gottfried Leibniz were alive today and confronted with modern neuroscience and artificial intelligence, which distinguish sharply between hard-coded, sphexish architectures—where every transition is fixed in advance—and adaptive, learning-based systems that remain fully deterministic at the physical level yet exhibit open-ended, context-sensitive behavior. Given his commitments to the principle of sufficient reason, pre-established harmony, and the idea that freedom consists in rational self-determination rather than mere absence of constraint, how do you think he would have interpreted this distinction?

Specifically, would he have treated adaptive flexibility as a genuine form of spontaneity that his account of monadic appetition could accommodate, or only as a more complex species of mechanical determinism? Would he have felt compelled to revise his claim that contingency and freedom are grounded in the internal rational structure of the agent rather than in observable behavioral plasticity? And facing evidence that even highly flexible behavior is determined by prior physical states, which elements of his metaphysics—his doctrine of monads, his distinction between necessary and contingent truths, or his theodicy—would be most strained, and which could absorb the finding with minimal adjustment? I am not asking for a bibliography, only a conceptual reconstruction that ranks the pressures on his system from the most damaging to the easily accommodated.
