I'm working through an argument about the Principal Principle that seems to prove far too much, and I can't find the exit. Here is the reasoning as I have constructed it:

(1) The Principal Principle requires that a rational agent's credence in an event E match the known objective chance of E, except where she holds admissible evidence.
(2) Admissible evidence is conventionally restricted to information about the chance-generating mechanism; direct observation of outcomes—including first-person memory and perceptual tracking—is inadmissible.
(3) Now consider a diachronic self-location case. At time t the agent knows that a chancy branching process will place her in either state S1 or S2 at t+1, each with objective chance 0.5. At t+1 she retains a vivid first-person memory of having been in S1.
(4) By (1) and (2), her rational credence at t+1 that she is in S1 should remain 0.5, since her memory is direct outcome-observation and therefore inadmissible.
(5) Generalizing, whenever an agent knows she is embedded in a symmetric stochastic process, she must discount her own empirical tracking—where she is, what she sees, which path she occupies—to match the prior chancy distribution.
(6) But this implies that first-person empirical knowledge is systematically undermined by deference to objective chance, which is absurd: it would require her to treat her own perceptions as no more reliable than a coin flip regardless of their coherence and detail.
(7) So either the Principal Principle is false, or the admissible/inadmissible distinction collapses in first-person contexts.

The conclusion in (7) feels too destructive—if admissible evidence includes all first-person observation, then PP is empty; if it excludes all of it, then PP destroys ordinary empirical reasoning. I want to preserve both the idea that rational belief should sometimes defer to objective chance and the idea that first-person empirical tracking is generally reliable, but I can't see how to keep both without contradiction. Where does the argument slip? Is there an equivocation in (2) between statistical outcome-data and self-locating evidence? A scope error in (5) that illicitly extends third-person deference to de se beliefs? Or is (4) actually valid, with the real cost being that we must accept some form of skeptical self-location? If I reject (2), what does that do to the standard motivation for PP associated with Lewis? Please diagnose the weakest step, explain why it looked plausible on its face, and price the alternatives—what each standard position (the strict Lewisian deference view, the evidentialist restriction that broadens admissibility, and the de se-constraint view that exempts self-locating belief) must give up to block the argument. Write your answer in American English.
