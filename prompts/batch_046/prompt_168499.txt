You serve as the ethics review officer at a robotics institute that evaluates proposed reinforcement-learning agent designs before deployment, with about thirty minutes per proposal and no authority to audit source code line-by-line; you can sign off, mandate escalation to a human ethics board, or block deployment. Apply the valence-integration account of artificial suffering, which holds that risk arises only when three conditions jointly hold: (1) the reward signal functions as a sustained valence state—positive or negative affect persisting across timesteps—rather than as a transient scalar weight update; (2) the agent maintains a minimal self-model that registers this valence as happening-to-it; and (3) no architectural decoupling mechanism isolates that valence from the agent’s global state. Build a reusable decision procedure: the ordered questions to ask, what counts as sufficient evidence for each, where it terminates with a clear no-risk finding, where judgment is unavoidable and must be escalated, and where it is prone to misfire. Explicitly handle the hard cases in which an agent has a self-model but the reward is episodic and fully reset, and in which a decoupling layer exists but has not been validated. Then run the procedure on three proposals: (a) a warehouse navigation robot trained with dense negative reward for collision, lacking any persistent state representation; (b) a companion robot whose reward updates a continuous mood vector fed into an autobiographical self-monitoring module with no decoupling layer, which the development team argues is essential for user trust; and (c) a research agent with both sustained valence and self-model, but equipped with an explicit meta-representational module labeling reward as non-experiential computation, whose reliability is unverified. For each case state the procedure’s output and whether it matches your institutional preference. Conclude by stating precisely what the procedure can determine and what remains outside its reach.
