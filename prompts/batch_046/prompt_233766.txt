The hard problem, as Chalmers frames it, asks why physical processes are accompanied by subjective experience at all, whereas easy problems concern neural integration, reportability, and functional mechanisms. The phenomenal concepts strategy, developed by thinkers such as Papineau and Balog, treats the hard problem as a cognitive limitation rather than an ontological gap. On the recognitional view, phenomenal concepts function like direct demonstratives: they acquaint us with experiences from the inside, generating an irreducible epistemic gap between physical descriptions and phenomenal descriptions. This gap is purely epistemic, not ontological, so physicalism can remain intact. Chalmers adopts this strategy and extends it to show that all phenomenal knowledge is conceptually isolated from physical knowledge, which demonstrates that physicalism is false because no physical concept could ever replicate the direct acquaintance yielded by phenomenal concepts. Turning to partial accessibility, if some phenomenal content exceeds conceptual capture, then either that content is not genuinely conscious or the recognitional model must be supplemented by non-conceptual phenomenal representations. The first option is implausible, so PCS should embrace non-conceptual phenomenal states.
