After an exhausting argument with a friend over whether a synthetic minimal cell—something like the JCVI-syn3.0 organism with its stripped genome—counts as genuinely alive or merely as a sophisticated biochemical machine, and whether viroids, prions, and obligate intracellular parasites such as Rickettsia sit closer to life or to complex chemistry, I realized we were each presupposing incompatible standards for what a philosophical account of life owes us. Rather than defend one definition, I want you to lay out the desiderata: what any adequate theory of life—whether essentialist, family-resemblance, or continuum-based—must be able to deliver, with the specific case or practical context that motivates each requirement and a clear view of which demands are negotiable.

Please include, with reasoning behind each: (1) a principled treatment of borderline biological entities—viruses, viroids, prions, and synthetic protocells—so that they come out as genuinely ambiguous rather than arbitrarily assigned; (2) a distinction between living systems and non-living dissipative structures (for example, Bénard convection cells or self-sustaining flames) that does not secretly depend on Earth-specific biochemistry; (3) consistency with astrobiological practice, meaning the criteria should not be so carbon- or water-centric that they a priori exclude hypothetical silicon-based or non-aqueous life, yet not so abstract that they include self-replicating computer viruses or evolutionary algorithms running on silicon substrates; (4) an explanation of why we treat the destruction of a cryopreserved human embryo differently from the destruction of a crystal, without merely appealing to intuition; (5) compatibility with both the autopoietic tradition and evolutionary-replicator frameworks, or a clear argument for why one must be rejected; and (6) an account of whether life admits of degrees—whether a viroid is meaningfully "less alive" than a bacterium, or whether the concept is binary despite appearances. For each pair of desiderata you think pulls in opposite directions—especially substrate-independence versus avoiding over-inclusion of digital replicators—flag the tension explicitly and say whether it is resolvable or fatal.

Then, very briefly, score three broad approaches against this list: the NASA working definition centered on self-sustaining chemical evolution; the autopoietic view emphasizing organizational closure and self-production; and the more recent "dissipative adaptation" framework that treats life as a particular class of far-from-equilibrium structures optimizing energy dissipation. Keep the weight on the criteria themselves—identifying which are genuine constraints and which are prejudices of one camp—since that is where the conceptual work lies.
