You are a senior clinical ethicist and philosopher of mind advising a university hospital’s Institutional Review Board for Emerging Neurotechnologies. The board must reach a concrete decision on Protocol 7B: a proposed 18-month trial for patients with progressive cortical degeneration in which biological neurons are replaced one-by-one with synthetic analogues in a strictly interleaved pattern—no brain region is fully replaced before replacement begins elsewhere. The familiar philosophical issue is whether personal identity survives gradual substrate replacement, but the protocol introduces a specific wrinkle: at months 6, 12, and 18, the patient must complete an “identity-continuity affidavit,” evaluated separately by the remaining biological architecture and the integrated synthetic architecture, which may diverge in their narrative self-assessments. The sponsor argues this is ethically continuous with organ transplantation and that divergence is merely a correctable engineering artifact; critics argue interleaved replacement creates a distributed self with no clear identity boundary, and that a single informed-consent signature at enrollment fails to respect autonomy because the subject who completes the trial may not be the subject who consented.

Please draft a concise formal memo (about 600–800 words) recommending whether the board should approve the protocol outright, approve with specific conditions, or reject it, and include a clear implementation timeline or revision deadline. Before writing the memo, briefly outline: (1) the strongest philosophical argument favoring approval, (2) the strongest philosophical argument against it, and (3) one concrete institutional safeguard or condition that directly addresses the core concern of the losing side. The memo should treat the divergence problem as a genuine autonomy issue rather than dismissing it as noise, and it should offer an actionable bottom line—such as a staged-consent framework with divergence thresholds—rather than recommending further philosophical study. Write in a formal, polished tone appropriate for circulation to clinicians, legal counsel, and patient advocates. Think carefully and step through the reasoning before presenting the recommendation.
