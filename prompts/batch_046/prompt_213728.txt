Design a runnable 75-minute classroom activity for an upper-level philosophy of physics seminar with 10 advanced undergraduates or early graduate students. The goal is for participants to feel, through structured interaction rather than lecture, the experiential difference between treating a quantum state as a real physical property (psi-ontic) and treating it as a summary of an observer's information (psi-epistemic). To keep the task from collapsing into standard textbook regurgitation, frame the exercise around an invented protocol I will call the Shared Apparatus Dilemma: two teams share a single represented quantum device, but each receives a differently filtered record of its outputs and must coordinate predictions without revealing raw data.

Specify exactly:
- Materials and setup: what cards, envelopes, hidden-rule decks, or token economies represent the device and the partial records; how physical separation of the two teams is maintained.
- Rules for three rounds: in Round 1 each team builds its own predictive model from its filtered record; in Round 2 both teams are told they share one physical device and must submit a joint prediction for a final measurement, but they may only exchange their assigned "state descriptions," not their raw records; in Round 3 they learn the actual outcome and score points.
- Role assignments: one team is assigned psi-ontic operating rules (their state description aims to match an objective feature of the device); the other is assigned psi-epistemic operating rules (their state description aims to match only their own information state). Both sets of rules should be mechanically implementable with the cards.
- Payoff scheme using non-monetary tokens: rewards for accurate joint predictions, penalties for overconfidence in a shared reality when the two descriptions conflict, and a specific cost for "merging" descriptions that erases the distinction between information and world.
- The precise information partition that forces Round 2 conflict: both teams observe outcomes that are classically compatible with the same hidden card distribution, yet their assigned quantum-style summaries (probability amplitudes represented as weighted token allocations) diverge in a way that cannot be reconciled without one team abandoning its assigned rules.
- The debrief: map Round 1 onto state assignment, Round 2 onto the problem of intersubjective agreement and hidden-variable consistency, and Round 3 onto the explanatory cost of denying that the wavefunction is in the world. Include an explicit, honest note on which feature of actual quantum mechanics—specifically, the non-classical interference terms that have no analog in card-deck probabilities—the simulation cannot reproduce and must be supplied verbally by the facilitator.
- Predictable failures: what will likely go wrong the first time (e.g., teams arguing about card mechanics rather than interpretation, or one team intuitively treating its summary as "just knowledge" regardless of assigned role), and how to redirect without breaking the role structure.
- A frank statement of which aspect of the psi-ontic/psi-epistemic debate the activity genuinely makes participants experience (for instance, the coordination failure that arises when two rational agents assign different states to one system) and which aspect it can only symbolize (for instance, the specific mathematical structure of Hilbert-space overlap).

Write this as a formal, polished prompt. Also: if the task rests on a confused premise—such as the possibility that a card-deck model necessarily smuggles in classical hidden variables in a way that misrepresents the quantum case, or that the activity can genuinely reproduce quantum interference without quantum formalism—say so plainly rather than complying politely.
