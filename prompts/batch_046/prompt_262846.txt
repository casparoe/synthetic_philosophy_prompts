I'm putting together a one-year dissertation prospectus in value theory, and I need the framing sharpened into something a fellowship committee can evaluate without feeling like a literature survey. The standard debate between comparativists and pluralists about hard choice treats the problem as a failure of ranking—when values are incommensurable, we allegedly lack a comparative judgment. I think this framing misses a structural feature of practical reason that hasn't been isolated: when an agent makes a binding commitment to one incommensurable value-domain, the commitment doesn't merely foreclose alternatives but actively reshapes the normative weight of remaining options in an asymmetric way. This isn't standard regret or opportunity cost; it's a path-dependent alteration of the choice landscape itself, which I'd call binding asymmetry. I want to examine whether this asymmetry can be formalized—without reducing values to a common metric—by adapting the sequential logic of commitment races (where early moves restructure payoff spaces for later ones) to intra-personal deliberation rather than strategic interaction.

Write a two-page proposal with the following elements, in formal and polished prose suitable for an analytic philosophy committee: (1) a precise statement of the research question—does binding asymmetry represent a genuine structural feature of practical reason, or is it an artifact of how agents narrate sequential choice after the fact? (2) an explanation of why existing positions in the hard-choice and value-pluralism literature specifically leave this question open, rather than answering it implicitly; (3) the proposed method—conceptual analysis of constructed case studies combined with a minimal formal framework tracking how normative weights propagate across sequential commitments, plus an explicit defense of why this conceptual-formal hybrid is preferable to either pure phenomenological description or full decision-theoretic modeling; (4) what a successful result looks like (for instance, a distinction that dissolves some standard paradoxes of post-choice regret) and what counts as clear failure (for instance, if the framework collapses into standard opportunity-cost reasoning with no residual asymmetry); (5) an honest scope assessment for twelve months, including the specific risk that constructing the formal apparatus consumes time needed for conceptual defense, and what milestones would indicate the project is too big for the fellowship period.

Keep the tone precise and skeptical of hand-waving—I'm writing this from the perspective of a longtime reader of rationalist and analytic philosophy forums, so comfort with formal tools is expected, but so is resistance to pretending that conceptual framing is empirical discovery.
