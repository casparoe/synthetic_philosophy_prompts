I have noticed a recurring interpretive move in discussions of Libet-style readiness-potential studies, and I am unsure whether philosophers of neuroscience have given it a specific name. The move works like this. A commentator cites the fact that the readiness potential begins several hundred milliseconds before the subject reports the conscious decision to move, and concludes that the brain has already determined the action before consciousness enters. If one objects that the study only involves arbitrary finger flexions and therefore tells us nothing about serious deliberative choice, the commentator often replies that the same neural dynamics appear in more complex decisions, so the point generalizes. But if one then asks what exactly the brain determined — the particular finger, the timing, the mere fact of moving, or the prior intention to participate in the task — the commentator shifts ground and says the readiness potential only reflects a general motor preparation, not a specific choice, thereby protecting the claim from being falsified by the triviality of the movement. In other words, the neural signal is treated as specific enough to undermine agency when that serves the argument, and as generic enough to survive objections about triviality when that serves the argument. This feels like more than ordinary ambiguity because both readings are deployed within the same overall case against free will. Does this pattern of selectively assigning specificity to pre-conscious neural activity have an established label in the literature on volition, and if not, can you define it precisely, distinguishing it from simple equivocation and from the legitimate point that different levels of description apply to the same event? Please also say whether recognizing this pattern changes how one should evaluate Libet-style objections to agency, and do so in continuous prose only, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, as in a letter.
