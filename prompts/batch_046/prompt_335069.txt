I'm preparing a unit for a graduate philosophy-of-history seminar on the demarcation problem as it applies specifically to pseudohistory rather than to science. We have discussed standard Popperian and Kuhnian frames, but I want a classification instrument that forces students to locate themselves by answering concrete case-questions rather than by selecting familiar labels. Please construct a decision tree—exactly three binary branch nodes that generate eight logical combinations—with seven named positions placed as leaves and one combination deliberately left empty.

The tree must be built from the logical space of answers first, then the positions mapped onto it: state the three diagnostic questions, show how the yes/no paths combine, and only then assign the named views to their respective leaves. Each node should pose a concrete commitment about one of the following cases, phrased so a student can answer without knowing the philosophical labels in advance: (1) Fomenko's New Chronology, specifically its statistical identification of "phantom" dynastic duplicates that conflict with stratigraphic and radiocarbon evidence; (2) ancient-astronaut explanations of megalithic precision, assessed in light of both the absence of non-terrestrial artifacts and the presence of incremental technological precursors; and (3) Plato's Atlantis narrative taken together with scattered trans-Atlantic geological and cultural parallels. The three separating questions should concern, respectively, whether the decisive failure of such claims is empirical or formal/methodological; whether mainstream rejection is best explained as an epistemically warranted response or as a sociological mechanism of boundary maintenance; and whether anomalous parallels that resist conventional explanation should be held in epistemic suspension or absorbed into existing naturalistic frameworks.

The seven positions that must appear as leaves are: (i) a naïve falsificationist who treats pseudohistory as empirically refuted conjecture; (ii) a Kuhnian sociology-of-science boundary-policer who treats dismissal as paradigm-protective social practice; (iii) a craft-norm internalist who denies there is a deep demarcation problem and sees only violations of disciplinary craft rules; (iv) a radical hermeneutic incommensurabilist who holds that pseudohistorical and mainstream narratives are irreconcilable language games with no neutral arbitration; (v) an epistemic-humility or anomaly-tolerant revisionist who argues mainstream history dismisses anomalous data too quickly; (vi) a structuralist pattern-matcher—an under-discussed view—who grants that figures like Fomenko detect real statistical correlations in chronicle repetition but misattribute them to phantom dynasties rather than to textual transmission and scribal duplication mechanisms; and (vii) a methodological pluralist with evidentiary thresholds who maintains that multiple historiographical methods are legitimate but each must satisfy its own internal consistency and evidential floor, so Atlantis fails on internal coherence, Fomenko on evidential floor, and ancient-astronaut hypotheses on both.

For each occupied leaf, provide the position name, a one-sentence diagnosis of why that specific combination of answers lands there, and a brief flag noting whether any other required leaf shares the same answer-pattern and therefore cannot be separated by this tree. Then identify the single unoccupied combination of answers, briefly diagnose why it remains empty—whether incoherent, conceptually unattractive, or merely unexplored—and sketch what view might fill it if developed. The audience is graduate students in philosophy of history who need to classify their own commitments by reasoning about the three cases, not by memorizing a bibliography; accordingly, do not include citations, reading lists, or requests for specific scholar attributions, and ensure the instrument is answerable entirely from conceptual analysis of the cases above.
