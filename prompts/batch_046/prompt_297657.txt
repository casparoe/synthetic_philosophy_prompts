At a philosophy reading group, two regular members—Nina and Theo—debated whether a colleague of theirs, Dr. Park, has an internal reason to finish a long-abandoned monograph on modal realism that she never intended to write. Park had been assigned the task of completing her deceased mentor's unfinished book; she finds the subject matter remote from her own work in epistemology, has no desire to publish in metaphysics, and regards the assignment as an institutional burden.

Nina argued that Park clearly lacks an internal reason to continue. Drawing on the standard distinction between internal and external reasons, Nina maintained that an internal reason must connect to some element in the agent's own subjective motivational set—her actual desires, commitments, or projects. Park's own projects lie elsewhere; the assignment is purely external, sustained only by departmental expectation and a sense of professional obligation. Even if completing the book would be admirable, the reason to do so applies to Park regardless of her particular psychology, which is precisely what makes it external.

Theo objected that Nina applies the distinction too mechanically. Park's practical identity, Theo claimed, now includes being her mentor's intellectual successor: she alone possesses the technical grasp of the arguments, she was present during their development, and her refusal would effectively erase a distinctive philosophical voice. This is not generic social pressure but a reason tied to Park's specific standing and competence within that tradition. To abandon the work would be a form of self-betrayal, undermining her own role as a serious interlocutor in that domain. Therefore the reason connects to Park's practical identity and is internal, even if she never explicitly chose the role.

Nina replied that Theo is merely relabeling external reasons. Socially assigned roles—mentor's successor, departmental custodian—are not part of Park's subjective motivational set unless she has actually endorsed them. Park sees herself as an epistemologist, not an archivist for modal realism. The reason Theo identifies applies to anyone in Park's institutional position, regardless of their personal commitments; that is the hallmark of an external reason. The fact that Park is uniquely competent makes the obligation stronger, but competence does not convert an imposed duty into an internal motivation.

Theo answered that Nina demands an implausibly explicit form of endorsement. Many of our strongest internal reasons come from commitments we discover through engagement rather than prior choice—parenthood, deep friendship, vocational responsibility. Park might find, upon working through the manuscript, that she cares about seeing her mentor's arguments properly represented. Moreover, Nina's view risks collapsing the distinction: if only pre-existing desires generate internal reasons, then nearly all moral and professional obligations become external, leaving the category of internal reasons too sparse to do the work philosophers want it to do.

Nina countered that discovery is different from imposition. If Park genuinely comes to care through engagement, she acquires a new internal reason—but that is a contingent psychological fact, not proof that she already possessed one. Until such a change occurs, Theo is wrong to claim that the assigned role itself supplies an internal reason.

They asked me to referee and I could not. Please adjudicate point by point: who is right about the conditions for an internal reason, about whether assigned practical identities can supply them without explicit endorsement, and about whether discovery-through-engagement resolves the dispute. Then say who is more right overall, and identify any important consideration both Nina and Theo overlook.
