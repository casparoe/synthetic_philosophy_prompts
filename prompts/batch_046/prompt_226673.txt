I recently became a parent, and climate change has shifted for me from an abstract policy debate into a concrete question about what obligations I hold toward my child and toward people who do not yet exist. I want to read Cormac McCarthy’s The Road through the lens of intergenerational justice, but I am less interested in treating the father-son relationship as a simple allegory of environmental stewardship than in testing whether the novel undermines the very framework of climate ethics. Most philosophical accounts of climate justice assume that present actions harm identifiable future people who can be said to have been wronged; McCarthy’s world, however, is one in which the biosphere has collapsed and the boy’s existence seems inseparable from the catastrophe that made his life so precarious. Does the novel dramatize the non-identity problem—if the conditions of his birth depend on the harm, can he coherently be said to have been wronged by it—or does it instead show that intergenerational obligation is not about distributing goods to specific successors but about maintaining a world in which the concept of inheritance still makes sense? I want to examine particular moments: the father’s refusal to narrate the lost past, the boy’s strange moral purity that seems almost post-human, and the fragile continuity offered by the ending. How do these scenes illuminate, complicate, or resist philosophical accounts of climate debt and future-person harm? And where the work resists being read as an argument—where its imagery of scavenging, silence, and dead nature exceeds what distributive justice can capture—what does that resistance tell us about the limits of applying formal intergenerational frameworks to climate grief? I am preparing this for a discussion group of parents who are trying to think clearly about climate anxiety without reducing it to either despair or technocratic optimism, so I want a reading that takes both the novel and the climate-justice literature seriously, without collapsing either into an illustration of the other.
