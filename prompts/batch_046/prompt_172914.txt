I was discussing moral revolutions with two friends from my rationalist reading group — specifically whether norms like dueling or chattel slavery collapse primarily through moral argument (Appiah's honor thesis, the Williams-style view that norms shift when they become unthinkable) or through material interest and state coercion. We got stuck on dueling, and neither would concede ground. Here is the exchange exactly as it unfolded.

(1) Maya said dueling ended because it became morally ridiculous: once elite opinion treated it as barbaric rather than courageous, the honor code that sustained it dissolved, and legal bans merely ratified a prior normative collapse. (2) Leo replied that this reverses causality: dueling declined after states criminalized it and made the costs ruinous; the "ridicule" was a post-hoc rationalization by elites who had already abandoned the practice for self-preservation. (3) Maya countered that criminalization alone rarely kills a practice elites genuinely value — dueling persisted despite bans in parts of Europe — so the prior normative shift must do the real work; law follows culture, not the reverse. (4) Leo then introduced what he called a "status-good coordination" frame: dueling was an equilibrium in elite status competition, and it collapsed when the payoff structure changed — not because anyone was persuaded, but because the signal value of dueling was crowded out by other status markers (political office, industrial wealth). He claimed this shows Maya's "moral argument" and his own "interest" story are both too simple: the mechanism is coordination failure in a status market, which is not reducible to either pure argument or pure material interest. Maya was not convinced, arguing that Leo just relabeled strategic interest as "status," and that explaining why the payoff structure changed still requires explaining why elites stopped valuing honor — which brings moral argument back in.
