I am assembling a philosophical fiction exercise and would like you to write a short story—conceived at roughly eight hundred to twelve hundred words, though precise length is flexible—that takes illusionism about phenomenal consciousness as its structural center rather than as atmospheric backdrop. The setting should be a near-future clinical research facility where volunteers undergo a procedure called "phenomenal decoupling," which does not suppress sensory processing or access consciousness but specifically eliminates the subject's disposition to report, or even to conceive of, any intrinsic non-intentional qualitative character—the properties philosophers call phenomenal properties or qualia—while leaving cognition, recognition, emotional response, and behavioral adaptation fully intact.

The protagonist should be a retired phenomenologist who previously defended a strong form of direct realism about subjective experience. After the procedure, she describes colors, tastes, and pains with perfect functional accuracy but insists there is no remaining "glow," no "what-it-is-likeness," and that her earlier descriptions were confabulations produced by a post-hoc narrative module. Her adult child visits her and is disturbed not by any behavioral deficit—she recognizes faces, reacts to injury, expresses affection—but by her calm denial that there is anything it is like to be her, even in moments of apparent joy or grief. Center the narrative on a single shared event—perhaps a meal, a walk through a garden she once loved, or the viewing of an old family film—during which the mother attempts to explain her new stance without sounding diminished, and the child struggles to determine whether this represents philosophical enlightenment, neurological damage, or a kind of conceptual possession. The story should resist resolving into a simple verdict that she is right or wrong; instead, it should render the illusionist thesis genuinely livable and genuinely unsettling, showing what intimacy, grief, and social recognition become when phenomenal consciousness is treated not as hidden but as never having existed in the first place.
