I am a doctoral student in philosophy of literature working on moral relativism and narrative form. I am preparing a seminar paper that reads Ursula K. Le Guin's short story 'The Ones Who Walk Away from Omelas' not as a straightforward parable about utilitarianism, but through a conceptual lens I am trying to sharpen: the distinction between what I call 'frame-bound normative relativism', in which moral claims are valid only relative to an institutional framework that itself remains unchallenged, and 'trans-frame moral refusal', in which an agent rejects not merely the norms but the framing conditions that make those norms coherent. In the story, the citizens of Omelas appear to inhabit a closed normative architecture: they know of the child in the cellar, they accept that the child's suffering sustains their happiness, and they treat this knowledge as compatible with moral life rather than as a moral catastrophe. The ones who walk away, by contrast, seem to step outside the frame entirely.

Could you provide a philosophical reading that takes both Le Guin's text and the meta-ethical debate seriously? Specifically: does the story present the citizens as moral relativists about suffering—treating the child's pain as morally irrelevant within the Omelas frame—or as absolutists who have made a tragic but necessary exception? Where does the narrative resist reducing the walkers to mere 'moral tourists' who prefer a cleaner conscience? Which scenes—perhaps the description of the citizens' happiness, the child's condition, or the departure of the walkers—best illuminate whether the story is dramatising a form of institutional relativism that philosophical accounts of relativism rarely address? And finally, what does the fiction add to the conceptual debate that a direct argument about relativism and institutional complicity cannot capture? I would be grateful for an answer written in British English, and I do not need bibliographic references or reading lists—only the critical argument.
