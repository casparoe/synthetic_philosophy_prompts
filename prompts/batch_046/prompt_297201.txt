I serve on my secondary school's Student Judicial Policy Committee, which must revise our disciplinary code following a contested case. The case concerns a junior who altered laboratory data for a regional science competition; she clearly possessed the rational and volitional capacities to understand the rules, yet she acted under extreme economic pressure because the scholarship tied to the award was the only means to prevent her family's eviction. Our existing code prescribes automatic disqualification and suspension calibrated to match the moral wrongness of the act—what proponents call giving the student her just deserts. A faculty member has proposed a functional-accountability framework drawn from contemporary free-will debates: rather than asking whether the student metaphysically deserves suffering, we ask only whether she exercised the capacities for rational self-control that make holding her accountable meaningful, and then we design a rehabilitative sanction aimed at restoring epistemic trust within the school community rather than inflicting proportional harm. Some committee members argue that this framework respects the student's agency without invoking dubious retributive metaphysics; others object that it eliminates genuine moral responsibility by treating punishment as mere social engineering, thereby failing to honor the intuition that a wrongdoer who could have done otherwise genuinely merits blame. Please draft a formal memo recommending whether the committee should adopt the functional-accountability framework for cases of academic dishonesty, stating a clear bottom line—approve, reject, or approve with conditions—and providing reasoning that honestly addresses the strongest consideration advanced by the losing side.
