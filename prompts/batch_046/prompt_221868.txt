Construct a thought experiment set in an ordinary urban transit hub that uses a “silent-queue” norm: locals coordinate boarding solely by maintaining an unspoken, body-based spacing of roughly one arm’s length, with no physical markers, verbal acknowledgment, or explicit turn-taking. A visitor from a culture with rigid, visually marked lines enters the hub and must position themselves. Engineer the scenario—realistic, confined to one paragraph, and free of deception, exotic ritual, or ignorance of physical harm—so that three positions yield precisely these verdicts: (1) a conventionalist-contractualist view holds the visitor acquires a binding moral obligation to adopt the local silent-queue practice upon entry; (2) a universalist-Kantian view holds there is no such obligation because the arm’s-length rule depends on tacit embodied knowledge that an outsider cannot adopt without causing disruptive collision, so universalizing outsider adoption fails; and (3) a contextualist-particularist view denies any general answer, holding that whether the visitor should conform is fixed entirely by the immediate relational dynamics of the crowd at that moment rather than by convention or principle. After presenting the case, demonstrate that each theory indeed delivers its assigned verdict by identifying the specific feature of the silent queue—its lack of explicit rules, its embodied tacit structure, or the visitor’s epistemic access—that activates that theory’s machinery. Then state honestly which feature is doing the real work, explain what happens to all three verdicts if that feature is removed, and include a brief note on how a defender of the universalist position would attack the case and how the conventionalist would reply. Do not ask for citations, bibliographies, or references to specific texts.
