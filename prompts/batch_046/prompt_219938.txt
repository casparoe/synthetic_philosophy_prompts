I'm a senior ethics compliance officer for a national clinical-trial oversight board, and I need a reusable decision procedure—structured as a checklist or flowchart in words—that I can apply repeatedly to determine whether a principal investigator bears sanction-worthy moral culpability when a trial participant suffers serious harm. I review roughly a dozen such adverse-event cases per quarter, each within a forty-eight-hour window, and my recommendation must survive review by a three-person administrative panel with no formal philosophy training; the stakes range from formal reprimand to license revocation, so the procedure must be defensible in an administrative hearing, yet it must also be transparent about where judgment calls are merely dressed up as procedural steps. I have only the case file, a two-hour peer-expert consultation I can commission, and no ability to search literature or compute novel statistics.

The theoretical framework I want translated is not the standard Nagel-Williams taxonomy used in introductory surveys, but a rebuilt three-filter model I am developing: first, "agent control nodes," which isolate the specific decisions, omissions, or protocol deviations the PI actually made; second, "epistemic exposure nodes," which assess what a reasonable peer with identical training and access could have foreseen at the moment of action, using only base-rate judgments available in standard clinical references rather than retrospective knowledge; and third—this is the injected niche distinction—"systemic exposure nodes," which ask whether the harm was structurally inevitable given the trial's design parameters (sample size, interaction space of permitted co-medications, exclusion-criteria gaps) regardless of the PI's individual vigilance. I need these translated into ordered tests I can run without philosophical jargon, with explicit instructions for what to do when numerical base rates are missing, which distinctions must survive simplification, and which can be dropped for operational speed.

Please deliver the protocol itself, then apply it to three worked cases: (A) a PI who followed every protocol step precisely, but an unlisted drug-drug interaction—absent from all reference databases at the time—caused fatal organ failure; (B) a PI with a documented, peer-reviewed history of risk-seeking methodological choices who made an identical procedural decision to case A and produced the same harm; and (C) a case where the procedure ultimately exonerates the PI but yields an answer I will find intuitively unacceptable, with an explanation of why the filters point that way. For each case, trace the path through the three nodes, state the recommended sanction level (none, reprimand, or revocation), and flag any point where the officer must sign off personally despite the procedural output. Finally, specify explicitly what the procedure cannot do—whether it resolves institutional versus individual responsibility, whether it eliminates moral judgment entirely, or whether it misfires in predictable ways—and identify the hard-case boundary conditions where I should suspend the checklist and escalate to the full panel.
