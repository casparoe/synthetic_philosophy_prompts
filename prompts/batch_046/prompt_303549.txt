For an upper-level undergraduate seminar on well-being (14 students, 90 minutes), design a runnable simulation called the Modular Life Architecture that makes the conflict between subjective satisfaction and objective conditions visceral before anyone names it. Give each student a hidden flourishing-profile card—hedonist, adaptive-desire-satisfaction, objective-list with achievement and knowledge conditions, or narrative-coherence with an authorship constraint—that assigns point values to three independently toggled axes: phenomenological quality (simulated versus embodied), social recognition (genuine versus fabricated status), and causal efficacy (real-world effects versus null-output simulation). In three 10-minute negotiation rounds with rotating partners, pairs must draft a joint life contract specifying which axes are real and which are machine-generated, then score it against both hidden profiles; rules should forbid explicit mention of Nozick or the standard binary plug-in decision. At minute 45, run a 15-minute plenary auction in which each student reveals one axis preference and defends it using only the vocabulary of their hidden theory, without naming it. Provide a minute-by-minute timeline from 0 to 90, specify how to handle students who try to collapse the three axes into a single intuition, and include a debrief that maps each breakdown in negotiation onto a specific well-being theory. End with an honest note on which feature of the classic experience-machine scenario—irreversibility, the absence of risk, or the all-or-nothing structure—this modular format necessarily fails to reproduce and that the facilitator must supply verbally.
