I'm on the programme committee for the Riverside Cultural Centre—mostly because I'm a novelist and they wanted someone who thinks in stories, not because I can tell my Weber from my elbow. We're meant to decide by Friday whether to approve the gallery design for an exhibition called 'The Spirit That Built the Market', which tells the story that Calvinist religious values were a genuine causal driver of modern capitalism. The designers want visitors to walk through rooms that treat theological anxiety like an engine: it produces rationalised labour, which produces capital accumulation. There's even an interactive display where you can toggle Calvinism 'off' and watch the market fail to emerge, which feels like treating culture as a switch you can flip rather than a background condition.

The curators say this is just Weber's classic thesis, presented vividly. The sociologists on the board—plus one philosopher who keeps muttering about category mistakes—say we're smuggling in the idea that values are efficient causes in the same way coal or machinery are, and that an institution like ours shouldn't endorse that framing as settled fact. Our chair wants a written opinion from me to circulate before Thursday's meeting: it needs a clear recommendation—approve, reject, or approve with conditions—plus actual criteria the design team could respond to, and an honest account of the strongest argument on the other side. Not just restating policy; there isn't one, it's our call.

Could you draft that memo? Write it in British English, please—I'm tired of American spell-check correcting my behaviour. And say up front what would actually change your mind: which finding or argument would push you to reject the exhibition outright, and where your own reasoning is most likely to go wobbly. Don't give me a bibliography or further reading list; I can't chase sources, and neither can half our committee. I also don't want direct quotations from anyone—just paraphrase in your own words. One thing: I keep spelling recieve wrong, so don't be surprised if there's a typo or two floating around. I need this to read like a real document we could vote on, not an essay for a journal.
