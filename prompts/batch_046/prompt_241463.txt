For some time, program equilibrium has occupied an uneasy position between game theory and theoretical computer science. One camp presents it as a substantive extension of strategic reasoning: agents are modelled not merely as payoff-maximisers with beliefs, but as algorithms whose source code may be available to other agents, thereby permitting equilibria that violate the independence assumptions underpinning standard Nash reasoning. The opposing camp regards it as an explanatory redundancy—mathematically coherent, certainly, but reproducible within a sufficiently enriched Bayesian framework of bounded rationality, so that the computational framing adds no genuine predictive or explanatory content. The debate has persisted without clear resolution, and my working hypothesis is that the stall is not about formal results—both sides largely accept the theorems—nor about hidden empirical predictions, but about unarticulated criteria for what counts as an explanatory reduction in strategic theory. One side seems to require that an explanation display how agents could compute an outcome; the other requires only that it display why the outcome is stable given preferences and information sets. Is this diagnosis correct? Or is there a concealed empirical disagreement—perhaps about whether human reasoners actually represent one another's algorithms—masquerading as a philosophical dispute about explanation? I want a structural diagnosis rather than a survey: is the disagreement verbal, is it about standards of explanation, or does it rest on a substantive but unacknowledged psychological premise? If it concerns explanation, could a conceptual distinction—say, between computational accessibility (whether an agent can in principle execute another's code) and strategic accessibility (whether an agent can treat another's behaviour as a predictable function of payoffs alone)—dissolve the impasse, or merely rename it? And if there is a hidden empirical core, what kind of evidence—behavioural, formal, or conceptual—would actually be capable of resolving it?
