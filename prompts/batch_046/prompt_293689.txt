I've been chewing on lexical-threshold negative utilitarianism — the idea that once suffering crosses even a minimal threshold, pleasure can't compensate because they're incommensurable — and I built this argument that seems to blow it up, but I feel like I'm cheating somewhere.

(1) If pleasure and pain are genuinely incommensurable, there's no rational exchange rate between them.
(2) The lexical rule says suffering always dominates pleasure regardless of magnitude, which supplies an ordering: minimize suffering first, and only at literally zero suffering do we start weighing pleasure.
(3) But supplying an ordering where one factor always dominates is just putting them on a single scale with an infinite weight, not leaving them incommensurable.
(4) So the view is inconsistent: it claims incommensurability but its secretly relies on perfect commensurability with suffering weighted infinitely.
(5) If we drop the incommensurability claim and keep the infinite weight, we get strong negative utilitarianism, which implies we should eliminate all suffering even at the cost of all pleasure — e.g., a painless anhedonic world is better than a world with one barely-noticeable itch and universal bliss.
(6) If we keep incommensurability and drop the infinite weight, then we cant justify any trade-off that involves suffering, including a vaccine that causes a brief sting but saves thousands of lives.
(7) So either way the "threshold" idea collapses into either self-contradiction or an absurd extremism that makes every medical procedure wrong.

Where's the sleight of hand? I want to keep the intuition that suffering is worse than pleasure is good, but I dont want to bite the bullet on (5). Which step should I reject, and what does giving it up cost?
