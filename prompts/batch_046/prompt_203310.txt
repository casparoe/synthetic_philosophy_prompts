At a recent study-group meeting with colleagues, I listened to a debate about whether hyperbolic discounting actually resolves the ancient problem of weakness of will—akrasia—rather than merely redescribing it. My colleague Maya began by arguing that hyperbolic discounting dissolves akrasia altogether: if present and future selves weight rewards differently, then the judgment I call "better" is just the preference ordering of a distant temporal slice, and there is no single agent being overruled—only inter-temporal preference reversal. Leo responded that this avoids the core phenomenological problem by redefining the agent; if the near-future self and distant self are genuinely different preference-orderings, then what looks like weakness is merely ordinary change of mind, and nothing explains why I experience it as failure rather than simple revision. Maya then offered an analogy from distributed computing: when nodes in a system hold inconsistent state, we diagnose a consensus failure within one system, not the emergence of separate agents, so we should treat the hyperbolic discounter as one agent suffering a broken commitment mechanism rather than as multiple agents. Finally, Maya proposed that rational precommitment devices—such as blocking distracting websites in advance—function like write-ahead logging in databases: they are structures that enforce temporal consistency, which implies the agent implicitly endorses her distant preference, meaning hyperbolic discounting does not eliminate akrasia but reveals it as a failure to implement one's own endorsed protocol.
