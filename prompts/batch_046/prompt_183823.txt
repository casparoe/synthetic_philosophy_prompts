As a doctoral student in environmental ethics and philosophy of biology, I have been attempting to systematize my moral judgments about chemical and biological interventions directed at insect populations. I am drawn to a tentative principle I call functional-replaceability-weighted-by-trophic-dependency: the moral cost of killing invertebrates is inversely proportional to their reproductive replaceability within the local population and directly proportional to the irreplaceability of their ecological role in sustaining dependent species or ecosystem processes. I am unsure whether this framework actually coheres with my case-level intuitions, and I suspect that at least one pair of judgments pulls in opposite directions.

Here are my verdicts, presented concretely.

1. A homeowner uses an aerosol pyrethroid spray inside a kitchen to kill a single paper wasp that entered through an open window, despite the fact that the wasp could easily have been captured and released outdoors. Verdict: clearly wrong, grossly disproportionate.

2. During a documented West Nile virus outbreak, municipal authorities apply larvicide to urban storm drains, knowing that it will kill thousands of non-biting chironomid midges in addition to the target Culex mosquitoes. Verdict: permissible, and arguably obligatory.

3. An ornamental nursery applies systemic neonicotinoids to decorative, non-food shrubs solely to preserve aesthetic appearance, with foreseeable runoff that kills soil-dwelling springtails and predatory ground beetles in adjacent habitats. Verdict: wrong, despite the absence of any direct risk to human health.

4. A farmer deploys a species-specific RNA-interference biopesticide that disrupts digestion only in a target caterpillar pest, leaving pollinators, predators, and detritivores unharmed. Verdict: permissible, and methodologically preferable.

5. A conservation biologist authorizes aerial baiting with an insect-specific toxin to eradicate an invasive Argentine ant supercolony on an island, accepting that some native ant species will also be killed, in order to protect endemic ground-nesting birds whose reproductive success is collapsing. Verdict: permissible, perhaps required.

6. A beekeeper applies an essential-oil miticide inside hives to treat active Varroa mite infestations, knowing that a small number of worker bees will be killed as collateral damage. Verdict: permissible.

7. The same beekeeper applies the identical treatment prophylactically to every hive in a region with no detected infestation, purely as a precautionary measure. Verdict: not permissible.

The tension I cannot resolve lies between case 1 and case 5. In case 1, I judge the killing of one highly replaceable individual—an organism with enormous reproductive potential in the local population—to be seriously wrong, primarily because the human interest served is trivial and a non-lethal alternative is readily available. In case 5, I accept the killing of many individuals across multiple species, including some ecologically irreplaceable native ants, because the conservation objective is weighty and no feasible non-lethal alternative exists. This pattern seems partly driven by proportionality of means to ends and partly by the specificity of the intervention, but then case 2 also involves killing many replaceable midges in pursuit of a weighty public-health goal, which I also accept, while case 3 involves killing replaceable soil invertebrates for a trivial aesthetic goal, which I reject. So replaceability alone does not seem to do the work.

Is there a single, coherent principle—whether my functional-replaceability framework or an alternative—that delivers all seven verdicts without ad hoc adjustments? If not, which verdict should I overturn: my strong condemnation of the wasp spray, or my acceptance of collateral damage in the island conservation baiting? Furthermore, please state precisely what the best principle predicts for a case I have not listed: a community-garden collective applies broad-spectrum botanical insecticide to shared vegetable plots, knowing it will suppress local solitary bee populations that are not the agricultural pollinators for those specific crops but are embedded in the regional pollinator network.

Please present your analysis formally: identify the principle or small family of candidate principles, specify which clause or condition is motivated by which of the cases above, state the prediction for the unlisted garden case, and be explicit about which of my seven verdicts, if any, the principle cannot accommodate.
