You are a senior lecturer in philosophy of language with experience teaching metaphysics and the philosophy of science to advanced undergraduates, and you have led seminars on indeterminacy before. I am a university student currently enrolled in an upper-division seminar on vagueness and language; next week I must lead a forty-minute peer session, and I need a teaching plan rather than a delivered lecture. Please think carefully, begin with a brief brainstorming of the conceptual dependencies, and then present a structured plan.

The concept is the dispute between epistemicism—the view that vague predicates have sharp but epistemically inaccessible boundaries—and supervaluationism—the view that vague sentences lack a unique correct precisification and therefore sometimes lack a determinate truth-value. I want to avoid the standard pedagogical crutches of 'bald' and 'heap'; instead, the entire session should turn on one concrete, somewhat niche case: the boundary between a moribund language and an extinct language in documentary linguistics, specifically the transition point for a language with dwindling native speakers, fluent semi-speakers, heritage learners, and extensive archival records, but no institutional decree marking the cutoff.

Plan the session as follows:

1. Dependency structure. Specify the logical order in which the components must be introduced—what must be secured before epistemicism can be coherently presented, and what must be in place before supervaluationism can be contrasted with it—rather than merely a chronological order. Explain why reversing the sequence causes specific conceptual confusion.

2. The carrying example. Describe precisely how the moribund/extinct language boundary must be presented: what features it needs (gradual decline in speaker competence, institutional record-keeping, gradients of fluency) and what it must lack (a binary official designation) so that it genuinely tests both theories without begging the question in favor of either.

3. Misconception map. Identify the three most predictable misconceptions this audience will form. I anticipate: (a) treating epistemicism as merely an empirical claim that we lack sufficient census data about speakers; (b) interpreting supervaluationism as the claim that all borderline cases are false; and (c) conflating linguistic vagueness with ontic indeterminacy in the world itself. For each, specify the exact point in the sequence where it will arise and how to head it off before it solidifies.

4. Check for understanding. Design one precise end-of-session question that applies both frameworks to a novel borderline case within the same domain, one that would reveal whether a student has grasped the conceptual distinction rather than just memorized labels.

5. What to leave out. State explicitly which pieces of standard technical machinery—such as the margin-for-error principle central to epistemicism, the formal semantics of admissible precisifications, or objections drawn from degree-theoretic approaches—should be openly refused at this level, with a brief justification for each omission.

Please do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; the session is meant to stand on conceptual reasoning alone.
