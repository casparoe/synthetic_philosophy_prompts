I'm preparing a seminar contribution on inductive reasoning in risk regulation, and I'm trying to work through a concrete case that isn't just the standard philosophy-of-science classroom example. I'd like you to treat the 2011 Fukushima Daiichi nuclear disaster—specifically the pre-2011 risk assessments conducted by TEPCO and Japanese nuclear regulators—as a case study in Nelson Goodman's new riddle of induction. You can assume the basic chronology is familiar: the plant was designed for a tsunami of roughly 5.7 meters based on historical records in the region, and the March 2011 wave exceeded 14 meters, overwhelming defenses and causing meltdowns. The question is not about the engineering failure itself, but about the structure of the inductive reasoning that made the lower figure seem projectible.

Please analyze the episode through Goodman's framework of projectible versus non-projectible predicates, with particular attention to the role of entrenchment. Map the reasoning carefully: treat the regulatory predicate 'safe-at-design-basis-tsunami-height' as analogous to Goodman's 'green'—projectible within the entrenched dataset of observed events—while asking whether the actual geophysical dynamics made it functionally 'grue' (true for all observed pre-2011 cases but false thereafter, tied to an accidental feature of the observation window rather than to a law-like regularity). You should address what Goodman-style analysis says the regulators faced—was their error a failure to revise predicates in light of anomalies such as geological evidence for larger prehistoric tsunamis, or was it a failure of entrenchment itself?—and what they should have done differently, if anything, by the lights of the theory. Then turn the analysis around: which features of the Fukushima case does Goodman's linguistic and logical framework illuminate well (for instance, institutional inertia in predicate selection), and which does it misdescribe or leave silent (for instance, the physical causation of plate-boundary earthquakes, the political economy of regulatory capture, or the quantitative nature of probabilistic risk assessment)? Be disciplined about the mapping—identify who plays the role of the projector, what counts as the evidence, and what counts as the predicate revision—and be explicit about where the theory is silent rather than forcing it to speak.

In doing this, please separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who know the topic, and do not present a contested view—such as whether Goodman's entrenchment is best understood as psychological, sociological, or purely logical, or whether the Fukushima failure was primarily an inductive failure rather than a failure of data disclosure and institutional incentives—as if it were the consensus. I am not asking for a bibliography, reading list, or citations; I want an analysis that a knowledgeable interlocutor could follow without external lookup, but that does not pretend to settle debates that remain genuinely open.
