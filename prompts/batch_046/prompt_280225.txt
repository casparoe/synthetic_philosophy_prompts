I've been discussing research priorities with a colleague who directs a small philosophy group — three years of funding, two postdocs, and a shared background in Kantian epistemology, though with almost no access to empirical laboratories. We want our work on transcendental arguments to be genuinely constructive rather than purely exegetical, but we are unsure where the live philosophical leverage currently lies. I can identify at least three competing directions that seem open. The first concerns whether transcendental arguments can be reconstructed without presupposing synthetic a priori judgments, relying instead solely on premises about constitutive relations or conceptual necessity; if that reconstruction fails, a broad family of neo-Kantian projects loses its foundation, yet I am not convinced the technical work is tractable for philosophers without extensive training in formal logic. The second concerns whether the transcendental account of apperceptive self-consciousness can be analogically extended into normative domains — moral agency, aesthetic judgment, or intersubjective recognition — without collapsing into empirical psychology; this seems conceptually ambitious, but it is unclear how much downstream work in ethics or aesthetics genuinely depends on resolving the analogy correctly. The third concerns whether growing skepticism about the reliability of introspective reports should lead us to weaken, revise, or perhaps paradoxically strengthen the transcendental claim that self-consciousness requires a unified subject of experience; this feels timely, but I worry it risks devolving into an empirical dispute that our group is not equipped to adjudicate.

Considering these three directions — or any other open line you judge more pressing — which should we prioritize, and in what order? I want a defended agenda rather than a survey or a bibliography. For each proposed line, please specify: (a) why it is tractable given our profile, resources, and three-year horizon; (b) which other philosophical questions depend on its resolution; and (c) what kind of intermediate result over the next few years would either elevate it to the clear top priority or reveal it as a poor investment of effort. I am especially interested in whether one of these directions is upstream of the others — that is, whether settling it first would clarify, constrain, or dissolve parts of the remaining agenda. Please treat importance as relative to our specific situation: limited personnel, no lab access, and a goal of producing philosophically significant rather than historically descriptive work.
