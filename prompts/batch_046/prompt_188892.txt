As a novelist who has long been interested in the precise moment when a character stops being an instrument of the plot and begins to direct it, I am struck by the institutional taxonomy used in artificial intelligence research and policy that divides systems into "tool AI" and "agent AI." The practice classifies a system as a mere tool when it executes discrete tasks only upon direct human initiation and lacks the internal architecture to chain sub-tasks across extended time horizons without renewed authorization; once a system can autonomously initiate, plan, and pursue multi-step objectives over long durations, it is reclassified as an agent. What philosophical theory of agency does this boundary presuppose? Does it require that genuine agency involves self-initiated temporal extension and causal independence from the user, or does it quietly rely on a compatibilist framework in which autonomy is simply a property of internal decision architecture regardless of origin? Moreover, is the distinction consistent with how the same institutions treat human researchers—who are themselves embedded in funding cycles, institutional review loops, and hierarchical authorization structures—as fully autonomous moral agents? Reconstruct the implicit theory of action and responsibility that this taxonomy requires, distinguish what it strictly presupposes from what merely fits it comfortably, and examine whether the boundary would collapse if we adopted a relational or distributed view of agency, in which autonomy is not a binary property located inside the system but an emergent feature of the human-machine interaction loop.
