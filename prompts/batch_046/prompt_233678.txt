I'm drafting a feature on why large language models feel like they're constantly missing the point in conversation, and I've been trying to use Grice to frame it. From what I gather, speaker meaning isn't just the literal sentence — it's the literal content plus whatever you implicate by flouting or violating one of the conversational maxims, like saying "It's a bit chilly in here" to mean close the window. But I'm caught in a wierd loop about cancellability. Grice points out that implicatures can be cancelled without contradiction: I can say "Some of my students passed the exam — in fact, all of them did." If speaker meaning is literally built out of both the said part and the implicated part, then my speaker meaning initially contains both "some passed" and "not all passed." But then I cancel the second piece. Doesn't that mean my speaker meaning shifts after I finish speaking? Or worse, if I can cancel it so easily, was the implicature ever actually part of what I meant, as opposed to just a halucination the listener projected onto me?

I think I'm conflating speaker meaning with listener uptake, but I can't locate the boundary. Is the mistake assuming that speaker meaning is one flat package of propositions? Or is there something about the maxims being rational expectations rather than semantic rules that keeps the said and the implicated from colliding? I'd like the conceptual fix, not a literature review — I'm writing for general readers and just need to stop tying myself in knots.
