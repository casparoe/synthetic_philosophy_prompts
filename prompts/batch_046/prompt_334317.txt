I am organising a 90-minute workshop for twelve postgraduate philosophy students who have encountered introductory readings on Indian epistemology but are not specialists in Jain thought. I want them to experience, through structured interaction rather than lecture, why anekāntavāda is not a banal form of relativism but a rigorous ontology in which every object possesses an indefinite number of aspects, so that any unqualified predication is necessarily partial. Specifically, I want the session to turn on the tension between dravyāstika-naya, which regards an entity as an enduring substance, and paryāyāstika-naya, which regards the same entity as a continuous flux of instantaneous modes—both perspectives being metaphysically legitimate, yet jointly generating contradiction if asserted without the qualifying particle syāt.

Please design a runnable classroom game or role-play that makes this tension visceral. The participants should be divided into teams, each assigned one of five standpoints—substance-view, mode-view, spatial-partial, temporal-partial, and ethical-intentional—and presented with a single staged case: a merchant in a marketplace who performs an act that can be described as non-violent, economically destructive, and karmically binding, depending on the aspect selected. Each team must produce an unqualified written verdict—true, false, or inexpressible—before seeing the others’ judgments. Only then should they be instructed to synthesise all five verdicts into one collective statement, with rules that prevent them from simply voting or averaging. The incentives should involve small, real stakes, such as a shared pool of points that can only be retained if the synthesis avoids contradiction, but that is forfeited if any team withdraws its original claim. Include a clear timeline for the 90 minutes, explicit role assignments, any materials needed, and rules for the synthesis phase.

After describing the activity, provide a debrief plan that maps the moments of contradiction onto specific branches of syādvāda, explains what will predictably fail on the first run—such as one team dominating the synthesis, or participants collapsing the distinction into subjective opinion—and states frankly which feature of Jain metaphysics, namely the simultaneous permanence and change of the jīva, the exercise cannot reproduce without direct philosophical instruction. Be explicit about the limitation regarding the avaktavya branch, which a verbal classroom format struggles to instantiate genuinely. Present your response in British English, with a hard limit of no more than 650 words, and begin with a one-paragraph bottom line that summarises the core mechanism of the game and the central limitation you have identified.
