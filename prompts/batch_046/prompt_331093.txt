As a retired experimental physicist who has only recently begun reading philosophy of mind and medical ethics, I have been puzzling over how hospitals and courts treat advance directives for patients with progressive dementia. The institutional practice is this: a cognitively intact person writes a directive refusing life-extending interventions should they develop severe dementia; years later, when they are profoundly demented yet appear content and responsive to simple pleasures, clinicians and family members often hesitate to enforce that refusal, sometimes arguing that the present, comfortable person is not the same subject who authored the directive, while legal frameworks continue to treat the document as binding because it expresses the "true" or "precedent" self. Yet the same system does not treat a patient's ordinary religious or political conversion—say, a former atheist who now finds comfort in prayer—as invalidating prior commitments made in a different mental state, unless some clinical pathology is diagnosed. This asymmetry seems to presuppose a particular ontology of personal identity: not merely a biological continuity view, nor a purely presentist, experiential bundle theory, but something closer to Dennett's idea of the self as a center of narrative gravity—a stable, diachronic author of a life story whose authority can be undermined only by disruptions that break narrative coherence rather than by ordinary preference change. But I am unsure whether the practice strictly requires that commitment. Could it instead be sustained by a Lockean psychological-continuity account with a normative filter about which mental changes count as "authentic"? Or is it philosophically underdetermined, compatible with several rival theories as long as they include a distinction between pathological disruption and ordinary revision? Reconstruct the implicit theory of selfhood that this practice presupposes, distinguish what it strictly requires from what merely fits it well, and tell me what specific features of clinical ethics and advance-directive law would have to change if we replaced the narrative-center view with a purely biological-continuity account or with a Humean, momentary-self view.
