I am writing my dissertation on Schopenhauer's ethics, and the question has become personally urgent since my partner was recently diagnosed with a neurodegenerative condition characterized by relentless, unfulfillable craving that no conventional treatment has eased. Schopenhauer died in 1860, long before neuroscience distinguished the affective "wanting" system from the pleasure of "liking," and long before anyone could imagine what I will call tranquilist neuromodulation: a class of targeted interventions that selectively suppress the motivational salience of unmet needs without producing euphoria, sedation, or cognitive dulling, leaving personality, reasoning, and emotional responsiveness largely intact.

Given his metaphysics of the will and his conviction that suffering arises necessarily from unsatisfied craving, how do you think Schopenhauer would have evaluated a modern regimen that achieves sustained absence of craving and suffering not through ascetic discipline or compassionate insight, but through this kind of biological attenuation? Would he have regarded it as the technological fulfillment of his ethical ideal—the will's denial achieved without the agony of denial—or as a counterfeit tranquility that leaves the will structurally intact but chemically masked, analogous to the difference between genuine resignation and mere narcotic suppression? I am particularly interested in whether he would have drawn a principled distinction, within his own framework, between the moral worth of craving's absence achieved through internal transformation versus its absence achieved through external manipulation, and whether the latter could ever possess the redemptive value he associates with the true denial of the will.
