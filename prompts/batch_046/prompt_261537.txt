In my philosophy of science seminar, two classmates, Mara and Leo, reconstructed an argument they had over coffee about a hypothetical researcher, Dr. Reeves, a climate modeler who has developed a regional precipitation model and, on the basis of extensive first-order data, assigns 85% confidence to a 20% rainfall increase over the next decade. She then acquires higher-order information: the specific parameter-estimation technique she used has, across other scientific domains, yielded overconfident predictions in roughly 30% of expert applications. Leo argued that this is straightforward higher-order defeat—Reeves should lower her first-order credence, perhaps to around 60%, because she now possesses direct evidence that her reasoning process is unreliable in this class of cases. Mara agreed an update was required but insisted it should be calibrated differently: the 30% failure rate is an aggregate statistical fact about other domains, not a direct underminer of Reeves’s particular physical reasoning about precipitation dynamics, so she should apply it with a domain-relevance discount and only reduce her confidence modestly, perhaps to 75%.

Leo replied that Mara was treating higher-order evidence as if it were just another first-order data point about rainfall patterns, when its proper force concerns the agent’s epistemic position. Mara countered that Leo was sliding toward epistemic akrasia: if Reeves genuinely retains an 85% belief in the rainfall conclusion while simultaneously believing that her belief-forming process in this area is likely flawed, she holds a belief she regards as irrational—exactly the structure of akrasia. Leo said that was a misdiagnosis; the rational response is to reduce the first-order credence downward so that belief and meta-belief align, and akrasia only arises if she refuses to make that adjustment. Mara then introduced a distinction she had been working with—between procedural and substantive higher-order evidence. Procedural evidence, she said, bears on the reliability of one’s reasoning process, while substantive evidence bears directly on the world-state in question. On her view, Reeves possesses only procedural evidence, which should diminish her all-things-considered rational confidence in holding the belief at strength 85%, but need not alter the internal content or detached “object-level” assessment she makes of the model itself, thereby allowing her to avoid akrasia by separating process-confidence from conclusion-confidence.

Leo objected that the distinction collapses in practice: confidence in one’s reasoning about a specific conclusion is inseparable from confidence in that conclusion, so procedural defeat necessarily transmits downward. He then added a further twist. Suppose Reeves also knows that she is specifically poor at recognizing when her parameter method fails in climate contexts, though she is generally accurate in economics. This means her second-order assessment of her own unreliability is itself suspect in this domain. Leo argued this generates a damaging regress—if every attempt at self-correction requires a further meta-assessment of one’s competence to correct, rational calibration becomes impossible and Reeves is stuck either with unreflective overconfidence or with paralyzing infinite ascent. Mara responded that the regress is only apparent: it terminates in a practical, track-record-based judgment about her overall calibration history, not in an unending loop. Leo said that is precisely where the problem lies, because such practical judgments are themselves the locus of the bias she is trying to correct.

They asked me to referee and I could not. Please go through their exchange point by point and adjudicate who is right at each stage—not merely who wins overall. Specifically, address whether Mara’s procedural-substantive distinction actually succeeds in blocking akrasia, and whether Leo’s regress argument genuinely undermines rational self-correction or mislocates the problem. Say what, if anything, both are missing, and indicate whose position is more defensible in the end. Do not provide bibliographies or reading lists; adjudicate the arguments as they stand.
