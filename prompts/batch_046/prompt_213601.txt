I'm drafting a historical novel in which two scholars share an office in a provincial archive. In one scene they debate whether describing the past as progress toward the present is necessarily fallacious. The first, Elias, argues that Whig history is wrong when it judges earlier societies by values they never held; yet he allows an exception—if historical actors themselves explicitly intended outcomes resembling our present norms, then calling their actions progressive is accurate rather than anachronistic. The second, Mara, replies that this exception collapses, because intentions are always reconstructed from outcomes; one can always find some past statement that sounds modern if read charitably enough, and selecting which statements matter is already an outcome-dependent choice. Elias counters with a specific case: a revolutionary constitution drafted in Saint-Domingue that explicitly proclaimed universal citizenship as a deliberate rupture from colonial hierarchy, not as a later imposition. Mara responds that even here, elevating this document ignores the far greater number of contradictory intentions that failed and left no institutional trace; describing this moment as progress remains a matter of selecting survivors and calling them destiny. Elias then introduces what he terms an "archival asymmetry": document survival correlates with institutional power, so our access to progressive intentions is already filtered through success. This, he claims, makes Whig history not merely a narrative error but an epistemic one—any general claim that the past moved toward us rests on a sample systematically biased by survival. Mara grants the bias but insists it is a problem of evidence, not of evaluation; it may obscure alternative movements, but it does not falsify the claim that the documented ones genuinely advanced values they themselves endorsed. She asks whether Elias is denying that successful movements can be called progressive, or only warning that we cannot generalize from them. Elias admits he is unsure. They ask me, as the novelist constructing their exchange, to adjudicate. Please examine their dialogue move by move: rate each of Elias's and Mara's arguments from clearly mistaken to clearly right, briefly justify each rating, indicate who is more right overall, and say what both overlook—particularly whether archival asymmetry undermines historical evaluation itself or merely complicates the evidence available to it.
