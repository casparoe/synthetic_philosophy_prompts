I've come to think that habitat destruction—specifcally fragmentation—probably lowers total wild-animal suffering, and not just by cutting headcount. The reasoning is that intact ecosystems run on source-sink dynamics that keep pumping out surplus r-selected individuals (small rodents, larvae, passerines) into marginal habitats where they live short, stress-heavy lives. Fragmentation breaks the source populations, collapses that reproductive subsidy, and turns edges into ecological traps: high mortality, low replacement. So when conservationists push for connectivity, they might actually be reopening welfare factories rather than saving sentient lives. What would count against this? I want the real defeaters, not just "biodiversity is intrinsically good." Could edge effects raise per-capita suffering enough to swamp the population drop? Do K-selected species in fragments suffer more from social disruption, and does that matter if they're rare? Is my assumption that suffering scales with lifespan and stress just wrong? Rank them from fatal to awkward, say which ones someone committed to this view should honestly conceed, and check whether I've qualified the claim into unfalsifiability—like retreating to "it depends on taxonomic weights" whenever data looks bad. Also, is there any formal model showing source-sink collapse improves net welfare, or is that part still hand-wavy?
