In my philosophy of cognition course we have been examining what our instructor terms the Darwin-Turing inversion: the structural possibility that a system can exhibit high-level competence—whether in evolutionary adaptation or in computational problem-solving—without possessing anything analogous to human comprehension of the task it performs. I want to apply this framework to the well-known 2016 Go match between AlphaGo and Lee Sedol, specifically to Move 37 in Game 2, which commentators widely characterized as surprisingly creative and which Sedol himself interpreted as revealing an almost human-like intuition. Rather than treat the match as mere technological spectacle, please analyze it as a case study in competence without comprehension, using two distinctions I want to introduce for this purpose. First, distinguish teleonomic competence, meaning successful performance shaped by iterative optimization pressures that carry no representational goals, from heuristic opacity, meaning the agent's inability to articulate the reasoning behind its own success. Second, apply these to Move 37: in precisely what respects does AlphaGo's execution display teleonomic competence rather than strategic comprehension? Does Sedol's reaction—reading creativity and intuition into the move—represent a justified inference or an anthropocentric projection that collapses heuristic opacity into hidden understanding? Then address the limits of the Darwinian analogy: evolutionary selection operates across generations through differential survival, whereas AlphaGo's strength derives from gradient descent on a loss function over a static architecture. Is this disanalogy fatal to invoking Darwin in this context, or does it reveal a deeper formal parallel between selection and optimization that strengthens the inversion thesis? Finally, say what this episode shows about the theory itself: does AlphaGo's victory confirm that competence can float entirely free of comprehension, or does the human audience's persistent attribution of understanding to the machine indicate that the inversion is psychologically unstable—perhaps even that comprehension is an unavoidable interpretive frame we impose on any sufficiently complex competence? Be disciplined in mapping which elements of the match correspond to competence, which to its absence, and where the theory remains silent rather than forcing a verdict. I do not need citations or a bibliography; the analysis should stand on common knowledge of the match and the conceptual framework.
