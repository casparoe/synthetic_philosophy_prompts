I hold that an ant colony can be the bearer of a unified conscious mind—substantially the substrate-independent claim dramatized in the Ant Fugue—because mental properties depend on organizational integration rather than on neuronal tissue. Tell me what would genuinely count against this view, beyond the standard objections about missing central controllers or about individual ants lacking consciousness. Specifically, which empirical patterns in collective behavior—such as sustained adaptive task allocation under partial pheromone disruption versus rigid dysfunction when long-range signaling is replaced by deterministic mechanical coupling—would actually threaten the claim? Which philosophical commitments—about phenomenal unity, about whether a point of view requires a single physical locus, about the gap between functional coordination and experiential binding—does the position depend on, and which of these could be shown false? Rank the defeaters: which would be fatal, which merely awkward, and which defenders of the colony-mind view would concede but treat as irrelevant? Finally, assess whether the view has been insulated into unfalsifiability—if every counterexample can be absorbed by saying the colony was not integrated enough—and whether that insulation is a theoretical virtue or a vice.
