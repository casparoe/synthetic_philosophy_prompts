I serve on the Allocation and Ethics Committee of the Riverside Municipal Archive, and at next Thursday’s session we must render a binding decision on our sole annual digitization grant. Our vendor contract permits only one fully funded project; partial allocations are administratively impossible. Proposal Alpha requests support to digitize, OCR-process, and structure-metadata the complete administrative corpus of the State Factory Inspectorate (1923–1978) together with the board minutes and capital-investment ledgers of the three largest automated manufacturing firms in the region. The result would be a searchable, quantitatively legible database designed for macro-scale economic and regulatory history. Proposal Beta requests support to digitize a recently discovered, highly fragmentary cache recovered from a single small gear plant: roughly four hundred handwritten shift logs bearing worker annotations in the margins, a dozen personal letters composed on the reverse sides of rejected production schedules, and three reel-to-reel audio interviews conducted in 1981 with retired line operators. The Beta materials resist standard archival metadata schemas; they contain no continuous administrative narrative, but they preserve granular, first-person accounts of how workers encountered, interpreted, and sometimes subverted the introduction of numerical-control machinery on the shop floor.

This is not merely a curatorial dispute over storage priorities; it instantiates a philosophical disagreement about the nature and value of historical knowledge itself. Proponents of Alpha argue that history from below, when pursued through fragments alone, risks a kind of epistemic parochialism: without the structural context provided by state inspections, capital flows, and managerial decision-making, microhistorical voices become aesthetically compelling but analytically inert. They warn that funding Beta would privilege narrative recoverability over structural legibility, allowing us to celebrate individual agency while forfeiting the capacity to explain the institutional mechanisms—financing structures, regulatory exemptions, automation timelines—that actually determined workers’ conditions. Proponents of Beta reply that the Alpha records already survive in dispersed corporate and state repositories, so digitizing them here merely replicates a long-dominant history of elites and states. They contend that microhistorical fragments—especially those preserved accidentally, like correspondence written on industrial scrap—carry a distinctive moral and epistemic weight precisely because they resist aggregation into statistical series. Their resistance to quantification, they argue, is not an archival failure but a methodological feature: it forces historians to confront the limits of macro-narrative when accounting for lived experience, and it documents forms of practical knowledge and informal resistance that administrative archives systematically erase.

Please draft the committee’s formal decision memo, addressed to the Archive Board and suitable for circulation before Thursday’s vote. The memo should recommend full funding for one proposal, or—if you judge it defensible despite the contractual constraint—propose a principled framework for an unavoidable split with explicit trade-offs. Your recommendation must include concrete conditions, safeguards, or companion commitments that the losing side could recognize as genuinely responsive to their strongest concern, whether that concern is structural legibility or subaltern erasure. You must treat the best argument against your own position honestly and directly, not dismiss it with a procedural gesture. The memo should stand entirely on its conceptual reasoning; do not include bibliographies, reading lists, citation requests, or appeals to literature I should consult. It should read as a document the board could act upon immediately.
