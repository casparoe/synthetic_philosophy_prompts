Question: Explain Yudkowsky's Coherent Extrapolated Volition (CEV) and contrast it with the philosophical idea of idealized preferences in welfare economics and social choice theory (e.g., Harsanyi-style aggregation). Specifically address: (a) What problem does the extrapolation step solve that direct preference aggregation doesn't? (b) Does the coherence condition do real normative work, or is it basically averaging with extra steps? (c) Give one serious objection to treating extrapolated volition as normatively authoritative.

Student answer:
CEV says an AI should do what humanity collectively wants if we all knew more, thought faster, and were closer to the people we wished we were. Welfare economists like Harsanyi also discuss idealized preferences — rational, fully informed ones — and then aggregate them. (a) The extrapolation step matters because our actual preferences are contradictory, biased, and shaped by ignorance; if you just aggregate current revealed preferences you lock in those errors. (b) The coherence condition is doing real work. Once preferences are extrapolated, they should converge on a shared volition. If convergence isn't complete, CEV applies a coherence filter that weights each person's view by its internal consistency, giving more influence to the more rational agents. This is essentially Rawlsian reflective equilibrium moved from inside one mind to between people, not simple democratic averaging — it's averaging with a coherence filter borrowed from moral psychology. (c) A major objection is that whoever designs the extrapolation procedure decides what counts as knowing more or being a better person, so they can smuggle in hidden values. Bostrom raises this exact point in his discussion of indirect normativity, warning that the AI's interpretation will reflect programmer assumptions.
