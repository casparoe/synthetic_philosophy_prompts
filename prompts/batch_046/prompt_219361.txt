I've constructed an argument about Jain anekāntavāda that seems to dissolve every disagreement into perspectival harmony and then turns that result back on the doctrine itself, leaving it either self-refuting or practically empty, and I can't see the exit. (1) Anekāntavāda holds that every object possesses infinite attributes (ananta-dharma) and that any single judgment expresses only one partial standpoint (naya). (2) The seven-fold predicational scheme (saptabhaṅgī) captures this by qualifying assertions with syāt — in some respect — so that apparently contradictory predicates can both apply to one object without absolute contradiction. (3) If this applies generally, then most apparent disagreements between people are not genuine contradictions but descriptions of different aspects; for example, one observer registering a global temperature rise and another registering a local cold snap are not contradicting each other but occupying different nayas. (4) Now apply the doctrine to itself: the claim that all disagreement is merely perspectival is itself a judgment made from one particular naya. (5) From another naya, some disagreements are absolute contradictions that cannot be reconciled by shifting aspects. (6) So anekāntavāda entails both that there are no absolute contradictions and that there are some. (7) One might defend the doctrine by qualifying the meta-claim with syāt as well, but then the qualification applies to itself, generating an infinite regress of meta-standpoints with no stable fixed point. (8) The remaining options seem to be either accepting an arbitrary stopping point — which violates the refusal to grant any single view absolute authority — or conceding that the doctrine applies trivially to everything and therefore explains nothing. I would most like to preserve (1) and (3). Which step should be rejected, why did it look plausible, and what does abandoning it cost? If the argument is valid, which premise is the least expensive to give up?
