I am constructing a case study for a graduate seminar on institutional design and collective action, and I have reconstructed the decision record of a hypothetical regional commons tribunal—call it the Riparian and Atmospheric Commons Panel—that has issued five prior rulings on resource-sharing schemes without ever articulating its governing principle. In Case 1, the Panel permitted a voluntary rotational grazing agreement in which each herder self-capped stock below the sustainable yield, emphasizing that physical exclusion was impossible and that collective monitoring substituted for property boundaries. In Case 2, it rejected a private enclosure of a communal fishery even with full monetary compensation, holding that the resource was inherently non-excludable and that compensation could not restore communal access rights. In Case 3, it approved—over substantial privacy objections—a mandatory digital telemetry system for groundwater extraction that made every individual withdrawal visible to all users in real time, reasoning that transparency eliminated the information asymmetry driving overuse. In Case 4, it blocked a pilot program for temporary, auctioned extraction permits, arguing that price-based allocation collapsed the conceptual distinction between usage rights and ownership rights. In Case 5, it endorsed the conversion of local woodland into a community-managed carbon sink with diffuse regional benefits but localized ecological costs, but only on the strict condition that the affected local population held a binding veto over the initial conversion.

Now consider Case 6: a cooperative urban network of air-quality sensors that would generate a continuously updated, freely accessible public map of pollution hotspots. The network would be funded by a mandatory, non-optional micro-levy on all vehicle registrations, and while the data would be entirely open-access and non-rivalrous, the affected neighborhoods would have only advisory—not binding—input on sensor placement. Assuming the Panel wishes to remain consistent with its own track record, what should it decide about Case 6? Identify the closest analog among Cases 1 through 5 and justify the comparison in detail. Then articulate at least two distinct, jointly plausible principles that could rationalize the first five rulings but that diverge on Case 6, state the predicted verdict for each principle, and defend which principle you think the Panel is more likely to be implicitly running, with explicit reasons drawn from the pattern of rulings. Finally, specify one further hypothetical case—ideally one involving a purely informational commons with no physical extraction whatsoever—that would serve as a decisive test between the two principles you have identified, and explain exactly what outcome in that case would settle the matter.
