In contemporary philosophical treatments of Buddhist anattā and personal identity, one frequently encounters the analogy between the self and a corporation: just as a corporation possesses no substantial existence independent of its shareholders, assets, bylaws, and legal relations, so the person is said to be nothing over and above the five skandhas—rūpa, vedanā, saṃjñā, saṃskāra, and vijñāna—arranged through conventional designation (prajñapti). Proponents often deploy this comparison to license a specific conclusion: that denying a permanent ātman is not metaphysically nihilistic, but rather an ontological deflation compatible with moral agency, narrative continuity, and even rebirth (punarbhava) without a transmigrating substrate. I want to audit precisely how far this mapping holds, particularly in light of the Abhidharmic doctrine of radical momentariness (kṣaṇikatva)—the claim that each skandha exists only for an instant before being replaced.

Please begin by giving an explicit correspondence table: what in the corporate model plays the role of the charter, the board, the shareholders, the legal liability, and the dissolution? Then address the disanalogies that bear on the analogy's intended work. Does the corporation's dependence on a continuous legal charter and institutional memory across time smuggle in exactly the diachronic persistence condition that kṣaṇikatva denies? How does the Buddhist two-truth framework—conventional (saṃvṛti) versus ultimate (paramārtha)—function in this comparison: does it provide resources the corporate analogy lacks, or does it expose a structural weakness? Furthermore, examine whether the analogy can accommodate the phenomenology of first-person ownership and the specific mechanics of karmic continuity across lives without covertly reintroducing a substantive self.

Finally, assess whether the conclusion—that anattā is fully compatible with a robust, conventionally real personal identity—survives once these disanalogies are made explicit, or whether the corporate image was doing the argumentative labor all along. If the model collapses under this pressure, suggest whether an alternative analogy—such as the stream (srotas) or the flame—would fare better given the constraints of momentariness and two-truth ontology.
