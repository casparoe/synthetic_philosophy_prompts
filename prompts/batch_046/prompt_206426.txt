I run a 90-minute graduate seminar on just war theory with 12 students who have encountered the moral equality of combatants but not the contractualist challenge based on risk-assumption contracts. Design a structured in-class deliberation with assigned roles, hidden information envelopes, and a forced collective decision: half the group plays soldiers who have sworn protective oaths to local civilians; the other half plays soldiers from an opposing force with no such ties; both face an identical tactical choice about engaging an enemy position. Arrange the rules and symbolic payoff structure so that participants first experience from within the role why treating both sides equally feels unavoidable, then—through a revealed information card—discover that one side’s protective commitments generate obligations that are rendered morally invisible by that very equality. Include a precise timeline with decision points, a debrief that maps each moment onto the independence thesis and onto the contractualist critique, an honest prediction of what will fail the first time (participants will likely confuse legal rules of engagement with moral permissions), and a frank note on which feature of contractualism—the idea that liability to harm is generated by voluntary risk-assumption rather than by individual culpability—the simulation cannot reproduce and the facilitator must supply. No up-front lecture; do not name either theory until after the decision point.
