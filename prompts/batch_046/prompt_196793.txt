I'm preparing a seminar for philosophy-of-mind graduate students who have just worked through Kaplan's account of indexicals and are inclined to treat Hofstadter's strange-loop model of the self as either redundant with direct reference or as a confusion about language. I want to teach the idea that the symbol 'I' in a brain denotes not a speaker in context but the distributed, self-referential pattern that generates it. Design the session's explanatory arc: the prerequisite concept that must be secured before Hofstadter's claim sounds like a genuine competitor rather than a category mistake; one carrying example or analogy that exhibits genuine feedback without a central controller, with explicit conditions on what it must include and what it must lack; the misconceptions they will raise in sequence—starting from the indexical objection, then a homunculus worry, then the charge that self-reference is a feature of syntax rather than of neural dynamics—together with the precise point in the arc where each should be absorbed; a brief check for understanding that distinguishes grasping the loop structure from merely repeating its vocabulary; and an explicit statement of what technical material should be acknowledged as omitted at this level. The plan should assume no web access, should not ask for bibliographies or citations, and should be framed so that a student who understands the idea can apply it to a new case of symbolic self-reference without consulting outside sources.
