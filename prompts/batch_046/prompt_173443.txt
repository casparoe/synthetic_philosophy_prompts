You are a professional philosopher of history and an experienced examiner for upper-level undergraduate seminars. Before drafting, please take a few minutes to brainstorm the specific historiographical pitfalls that arise when a present-day writer frames past public-health reforms as inevitable moral enlightenment. Think carefully about how to embed a concrete narrative with hidden teleology, and about the philosophical distinction between explaining why an event occurred and justifying it as progress.

Your task is to write a 45-minute final-examination essay question worth 30 marks for an upper-level philosophy of history seminar. The prompt should feel personally relevant: imagine you have recently been commissioned to draft a museum panel on the history of sanitation in your own city, and you have noticed yourself describing 19th-century opponents of sewerage and chlorination as backward and modern universal acceptance as the inevitable endpoint of ethical maturation.

Please construct the exam as follows:

First, present students with a short, fictional but realistic historiographical narrative (approximately 150 words) describing the adoption of chlorinated water supplies in a 19th-century municipality. The narrative should subtly contain three Whig moves: a presentist moral taxonomy that judges past actors by current standards; a retrospective inevitability that treats contingent political choices as necessary stages; and the suppression of alternative outcomes that were genuinely available at the time.

Second, ask students to complete three tasks:
1. Diagnose precisely how each of the three Whig moves operates in the narrative, with explicit reference to specific sentences or claims in the text.
2. Assess whether a historian can legitimately claim that genuine moral progress occurred in this case without falling into the Whig fallacy. In doing so, they should draw on the philosophical distinction between diachronic causal explanation and synchronic normative evaluation, and explain why conflating the two produces the illusion of inevitable convergence.
3. Rewrite one paragraph of the narrative (approximately 80–100 words) so that it preserves full causal explanation—including economic interests, scientific debate, and political contingency—while entirely removing teleological or presentist language.

Third, provide a model answer of approximately 600 words that demonstrates the required analytical moves.

Fourth, provide a marking scheme that clearly separates an A answer from a B answer. For an A, the scheme should require: accurate identification of all three embedded moves with textual specificity; a rigorous application of the explanation/evaluation distinction; and a concrete structural revision that replaces moralised language with causal and contingent description. For a B, the scheme should identify typical failures: reciting a generic definition of Whig history without applying it to the narrative; listing bias or presentism as labels without showing their mechanism; proposing cosmetic additions (e.g., include more voices) rather than removing teleological structure; and failing to address whether progress-language can ever be philosophically salvageable.

Write in polished British English. Do not request bibliographies, reading lists, or citations. Do not include verbatim quotations from actual published texts unless you have fetched and verified the source; paraphrase all references without quotation marks. Ensure that the difficulty lies in selecting and applying the conceptual crux, not in recalling obscure terminology.
