In my graduate seminar on existentialist phenomenology last week, two doctoral students—Elena and Marcus—became locked in a disagreement over a hypothetical case they had constructed together. The case concerned a concert pianist recently diagnosed with focal dystonia, an involuntary muscular condition that makes precise digital control impossible; despite this, she continues to practice and perform, insisting that her suffering is not merely endured but actively chosen, because only through the discipline of performance does she maintain the artistic identity she has always projected for herself. Elena maintained that this is a clear instance of bad faith: the pianist treats her neurological condition as an unchangeable essence, being-in-itself, thereby using her facticity to flee from the anguish of recognizing that she could abandon music entirely and constitute herself through an entirely different fundamental project. Marcus objected that Elena was conflating authentic commitment with self-deception; he argued that Sartre’s radical freedom operates within facticity rather than annihilating it, so the pianist’s acknowledgment of her physical limits, combined with her refusal to abandon the project she values, demonstrates precisely the integration of transcendence and situation that Sartre describes as authentic existence, not its betrayal.

Elena responded by sharpening her diagnosis. She granted that sustained projects require stability, but she distinguished between a freely maintained commitment and what she called a facticity fetish, in which a physical limitation is invested with metaphysical weight precisely to avoid the responsibility of reinvention. In her view, the pianist does not merely choose music; she uses her broken hands as an alibi, treating her dystonia as destiny so that she never has to face the void of having no predetermined essence. Marcus countered that without a principled criterion for distinguishing healthy attachment from this alleged fetishism, Elena’s standard would condemn every enduring identity—teacher, parent, citizen—to perpetual suspicion, reducing Sartre’s ethics to an impossible demand for continuous self-annihilation. He further noted that if the pianist genuinely experienced her choice as anguished and deliberate, then by Sartre’s own account she was owning her freedom, not denying it; the presence of suffering, he insisted, is not sufficient proof of flight from freedom. Elena then pressed a more subtle point: she suggested that the pianist’s suffering was not a byproduct of authentic choice but its functional substitute, allowing her to occupy the socially valorized role of the tragic artist without ever undergoing the reflective crisis that true freedom demands. Marcus replied that this interpretation risks psychologizing Sartre’s ontology, substituting a clinical suspicion of hidden motives for the structural analysis of consciousness—particularly the distinction between pre-reflective and reflective awareness—that Sartre actually provides.

As the discussion grew more technical, touching on whether bad faith must always be conscious of itself and on how situation mediates between freedom and determinacy, I realized I could not easily adjudicate their exchange without imposing my own interpretive commitments. I would therefore ask you to evaluate their arguments point by point. Specifically, please assess Elena’s initial charge of bad faith, Marcus’s defense of authentic commitment within facticity, Elena’s introduction of the facticity fetish as a distinct mechanism, Marcus’s worry about perpetual self-annihilation, Elena’s claim that suffering functions as a substitute for reflective crisis, and Marcus’s charge of psychologization. For each move, indicate whether it is clearly mistaken, largely correct but incomplete, or largely correct and well-targeted, and then say who is more right overall and what both parties appear to be overlooking—particularly regarding how Sartre’s notion of situation might mediate between their positions.
