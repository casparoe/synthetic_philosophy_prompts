Look, I've been teaching a grad seminar on AI ethics and my students keep collapsing the alignment problem into a single technical puzzle, which I think misses something important. I want to push them — and myself — on whether we're actually dealing with several conceptually distinct issues that happen to share a name. There's the familiar specification problem: we ask for X and get X-maximized-in-some-deranged-way. There's the robustness problem: the system behaves well in training but diverges under distribution shift. But I'm increasingly convinced there's a third issue that doesn't reduce to either, and I don't think it has a stable name in the literature. Call it the problem of normative intelligibility: even if a system reliably does what we want and remains stable, does it operate with anything like our concept of harm, or is it merely optimizing over a correlation structure that happens to align with our values? The question isn't just about inner vs. outer alignment — that framing feels like it's treating the system like a black box with hidden goals. I'm asking whether the very category of 'alignment' presupposes that the system's reasoning is accessible to our practices of justification.

So here's what I need you to do: explain how these three problems actually differ at the conceptual level, not just technically. Does treating them as seperate problems change what we take a successful solution to look like? And is that third layer genuinely part of alignment, or is it just philosphy homework that engineers can safely ignore? Don't give me a bibliography; I want to see if the distinction holds up under pressure. I was discussing this with a colleague over coffee and she said I was overcomplicating it, but I think the overcomplication is in pretending it's simple.
