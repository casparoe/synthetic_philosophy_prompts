Please evaluate the following examination response, treating it as an instructor grading a philosophy exam on norms of assertion.

Exam question: A climate scientist, Dr. Chen, asserts in an official report that "the Atlantic Meridional Overturning Circulation will likely collapse by 2070." She assigns this claim a personal credence of 0.60, regards the available evidence as sufficient for belief but insufficient for knowledge—citing deep model disagreement about tipping-point dynamics and the assessment bodies' classification of such timelines as low-confidence, high-impact scenarios rather than probable predictions—and is fully aware that the claim may turn out false. Her colleague Dr. Patel, who defends the knowledge norm of assertion (that one should assert only what one knows), objects that Chen should not have made the claim. Chen replies: "Your norm defeats itself. Consider Dr. X, who asserts the same claim with credence 0.999, is correct, yet her belief is Gettiered—formed through a process that is generally reliable but happens to be environmentally lucky in this particular case—so she lacks knowledge. By your norm, X should remain silent, which is manifestly absurd; therefore the knowledge norm is false, and the correct norm is belief alone: assert what you genuinely believe, regardless of credence." Assess Chen's reply. In particular, examine whether it mischaracterizes the standard formulation of the knowledge norm, whether it mishandles the Gettier case, whether it conceals a hidden premise about what follows from the absurdity of silencing X, and whether any intermediate norm—such as reasonable belief or justified belief—might better accommodate both Chen and X.

Student answer (submitted for 20 points):

"Chen's reply is sound and Patel's objection fails. First, the knowledge norm, as Timothy Williamson defends it, requires justified true belief for permissible assertion. In Dr. X's case, she possesses overwhelming justification (credence 0.999) and her belief is true, so she meets the JTB conditions and therefore possesses knowledge. Hence there is no genuine Gettier counterexample to the knowledge norm; Chen's analogy collapses because X actually knows. Second, since the knowledge norm cannot accommodate high-credence beliefs that fall short of knowledge, we should reject it in favor of the belief norm articulated by Jennifer Lackey, according to which one may assert any proposition one genuinely believes, irrespective of confidence level. Under this norm, Chen's 0.60 credence clearly constitutes belief, so her original assertion was permissible. Third, Patel confuses epistemic norms with practical risk: even if Chen lacks knowledge, the truth norm—assert only what is actually true—would condemn her only if the claim is false, which she has no reason to know. Therefore Chen correctly rejects the knowledge norm, and Patel should concede that belief alone governs assertion. The argument contains no hidden premises and requires no intermediate positions. I award Chen's reasoning full marks and recommend that the exam accept her reply as correct."
