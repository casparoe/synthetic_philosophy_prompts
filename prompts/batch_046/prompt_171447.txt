I'm designing the framing diagram for a graduate seminar on intergenerational ethics. We've worked through Derek Parfit's original reproductive case and its climate-policy analog, but I want a diagnostic taxonomy that sorts students by the logical commitments they actually hold rather than by which author they can name. The instrument should function as a decision tree: every internal node must be a concrete case or logical claim on which the positions genuinely diverge, phrased so that someone can answer it without already knowing the literature's labels. The root commitment is this: a present choice determines which specific future individuals will exist, and in the actual world those individuals will have lives worth living but lower welfare than the alternatives who would exist under a different choice.

Please build the tree with four branching levels. First, ask whether the nonidentity problem blocks every person-affecting complaint—specifically, whether Child A (born now with a moderate disability) or Generation X (born into a high-emission climate path) can coherently be said to be wronged, given that they owe their very existence to the choice in question. For those who answer that the block succeeds, split on whether an impersonal reason nevertheless remains—whether we should wait or change policy because the world is simply better, or because a principle of procreative or climate beneficence applies regardless of identity. For those who answer that the block fails, split on whether the surviving moral reason is comparative (the actual people are worse off relative to the counterfactual Child B or Generation Y) or non-comparative; here inject a niche structural-relational option in which the wrong lies not in welfare comparison at all but in the choice's failure to maintain just conditions of intergenerational transmission, irrespective of who ends up living. Then apply a cross-cutting branch to every path except the pure actualist: does the evaluation shift when the choice is collective (a carbon policy affecting billions) rather than individual (a couple's reproductive timing)? This should yield named leaves—Parfit-style impersonalism, the Boonin-style person-affecting restriction, comparative-harm survivalism, and the structural-relational view—alongside unnamed combinations such as accepting comparative harm for reproduction while denying it applies collectively, or accepting structural wrongs only for climate and not for private reproductive choice.

For each leaf, include the position's name if it is defended in the literature, or label it 'unnamed' if not, followed by one sentence describing its most awkward commitment. For every empty cell in the logical space, add a brief diagnosis of whether the gap is incoherent, merely unattractive, or an unexplored research space. Keep every node phrased as a direct question using only the reproductive analog (Child A now versus Child B after a one-year delay) and the climate analog (high-emission path yielding Generation X versus low-emission path yielding Generation Y). Do not provide a bibliography, reading list, or citation; the goal is to map the logical space of the debate, not to assign texts. The intended audience is seminar students who understand the basic problem but have not yet memorized the secondary taxonomy.
