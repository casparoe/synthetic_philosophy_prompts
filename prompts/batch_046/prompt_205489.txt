I am a high school senior drafting materials for an advanced seminar on the philosophy of history and complex systems, and I need a single essay question—designed for about 75 minutes of writing—that addresses contingency and chaos in historical explanation through the traditional proverb about the lost nail, the lost shoe, the lost horse, the lost rider, the lost battle, and the lost kingdom. Rather than treating the proverb as a generic illustration of the butterfly effect, the question should use it as a conceptual test case that forces students to disentangle two mechanisms: deterministic chaos, in which infinitesimal differences in initial conditions are exponentially amplified by the dynamics of a complex system, and agent-level contingency, in which historical paths branch because of deliberate or accidental human choices that are not reducible to micro-variations in a deterministic model. Students should apply this distinction precisely to the 1914 Sarajevo assassination, particularly the wrong turn taken by the Archduke’s driver, and evaluate whether that episode is better explained as chaotic amplification or as counterfactual branching, and whether the proverb itself implicitly conflates these mechanisms in a misleading way.

Please provide three things. First, the essay question itself, phrased so that it cannot be answered by merely defining chaos theory and summarizing the proverb; it should require students to construct an argument about which mechanism actually explains the Sarajevo case and to criticize the proverb’s implicit ontology of causation. Second, a model answer of approximately 600 to 700 words that demonstrates the required disentanglement, applies it to the driver’s wrong turn with concrete historical reasoning, and reaches a nuanced conclusion about whether the proverb describes chaos, contingency, or an illegitimate mixture. Third, a marking scheme out of 20 marks that lists five specific analytical moves an excellent answer must make, assigns marks to each, and includes a one-line note on the typical mistake that costs marks for that component. The scheme should clearly separate an A-range answer—which questions the proverb’s implicit model of causation and shows how chaos and contingency can come apart in practice—from a B-range answer—which applies the distinction correctly but treats the proverb uncritically as a neutral illustration. Do not include any bibliography, reading list, or request for citations.
