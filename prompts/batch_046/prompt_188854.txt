I am working through Book II, Chapter XXVII of Locke's Essay Concerning Human Understanding (first published 1689) and am trying to apply his account of identity to a modern thought experiment about gradual substrate replacement.

Consider this passage from §3:

"In the state of living creatures, their identity depends not on a mass of the same particles, but on something else. For in them the variation of great parcels of matter alters not the identity : an oak growing from a plant to a great tree, and then lopped, is still the same oak ; and a colt grown up to a horse, sometimes fat, sometimes lean, is all the while the same horse : though, in both these cases, there may be"

Locke seems to claim that a living thing's identity is sustained by an organizing principle rather than by the persistence of its material parts. Now suppose we apply this to a human brain in which biological neurons are replaced one at a time with functionally equivalent synthetic substrates, over a long period, with no interruption in consciousness or psychological continuity. At the end of the process, none of the original tissue remains, yet the functional organization and conscious stream appear intact.

Does Locke's framework allow us to say that the "man"—the living organism—survives, because the organizational "something else" has been preserved? Or does his separate account of personal identity, in which sameness of consciousness rather than sameness of substance makes the person, mean that biological continuity is irrelevant and only the uninterrupted stream of consciousness matters?

More specifically, given Locke's distinction between "man" and "person," if the synthetic replacement eventually eliminates every original biological particle, has the original "man" been destroyed even if the "person" continues? And does the gradual, neuron-by-neuron nature of the replacement make any difference on Locke's view, or would he treat gradual substitution as equivalent to instantaneous replacement?

I am not asking for a survey of modern commentators or a bibliography; I want to know whether Locke's own distinctions, taken strictly on their own terms, resolve this case, or whether they leave it genuinely ambiguous.
