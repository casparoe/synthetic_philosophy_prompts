I am giving the opening remarks at a one-day workshop held in honor of my mentor's retirement from the philosophy department; she spent roughly four decades working at the intersection of practical rationality and institutional decision-making. The audience is deliberately split: about half are philosophers of action and decision theorists who will recognize every technical allusion, and the other half are university administrators, family members, and students from other disciplines who will not. I want the remarks built around the absent-minded driver problem—not as a decorative anecdote, but as a structural constraint on what can honestly be said about rational agency. Specifically, I want you to draw out the underappreciated feature that the driver must adopt a policy (taking the correct highway exit) under conditions where full reflective monitoring of that adoption tends to undermine the very success conditions of the policy; this should shape the speech so that I do not fall into praising my mentor for "always knowing exactly which turn to take," since the problem shows that some rational achievements depend on a kind of productive, delegated unawareness rather than on continuous self-knowledge. The idea should do real work in the address: it should set the tone of the praise, define the limits of what I claim about her decision-making, and offer the non-specialists a genuine framework for understanding her career without reducing it to a metaphor.

Please write the address as a ten-minute speech, approximately 1,000 to 1,200 words, suitable for a mixed room. Structure your response in the following sections, in this order:
1. "Opening Remarks" — the speech itself, with clear internal signals for the specialists and for the family and administrators.
2. "Structural Constraint" — about 150 to 200 words explaining precisely how the absent-minded driver problem limits what the speech can claim about rational agency, and why a simpler narrative of constant vigilance would miss the philosophical point.
3. "Audience Translation" — about 100 to 150 words identifying which sentences or moves are aimed at the decision-theory half of the room, which are for the non-philosophers, and flagging any place where philosophical precision was traded for accessibility.

The speech should not include bibliographies, reading lists, citations, or appeals to highly niche theories that a well-read philosopher might not have encountered; it should stand on its own for a listener with no web access.
