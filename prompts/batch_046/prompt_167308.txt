I am preparing a case study for my graduate seminar on rational choice theory, and I was discussing with a colleague over coffee how the absent-minded driver problem is too often taught as a quaint highway parable rather than as a structural tension between ex ante planning and ex post execution under radical self-uncertainty. To help my students feel the paradox in their bones rather than merely labeling it, I would like you to write a short story that dramatizes the logical architecture of this problem in a setting that is not an automobile on a road.

The story should preserve the core decision structure: the agent moves through a linear sequence of points; only one point yields the maximal reward; an earlier point yields a smaller but positive reward; passing all points yields nothing; and crucially, upon reaching any point, the agent cannot determine with any confidence whether it has already passed the correct point or whether it is currently at it, because its self-monitoring mechanism is corrupted or opaque. Rather than a driver, please construct a specific protagonist and environment—perhaps a single-occupant bathyscaphe navigating a chain of hydrothermal vents where pressure-induced sensor degradation erodes navigational memory, or an autonomous archival intelligence routing packets through temporal nodes with a faulty audit log—and render the agent's internal deliberation so that the audience sees both why it was rational, from the planning perspective, to commit to passing the first point, and why, at each point reached, the same rational calculus seems to command taking it immediately.

Please include, either woven into the ending or appended as a brief author's note, an explicit assessment of how confident you are that the narrative faithfully renders the standard formulation of the absent-minded driver problem. In particular, identify the specific interpretive point—whether about the rationality of the plan versus the act, the nature of the agent's self-knowledge failure, or the payoff structure—where you are least certain, and flag any narrative liberties you have taken with the problem's premises for the sake of dramatic coherence.
