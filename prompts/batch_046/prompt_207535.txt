I am giving the opening remarks at my school's philosophy symposium this Thursday. The room will be split between teachers who assigned Bernard Williams's integrity objection and parents with no background in philosophy. I need a five-minute speech that treats Williams's idea about having one thought too many as structural, not decorative: I want to show why our school's new grant-ranking algorithm—which orders student emergency requests by projected future social utility—feels like a moral betrayal even if it produces better aggregate outcomes. Please build the argument around a realistic school scenario, such as a counselor deciding between two specific students for a single emergency grant, and make the wrongness reside in the mental act of comparing their worth rather than in a miscalculation of outcomes. The speech should reach parents through the emotional logic of loyalty to a particular person, not through technical jargon, but it must contain one precise philosophical move that philosophers in the room will recognize as genuinely Williamsian rather than a generic complaint about efficiency. Do not reuse the classic drowning-spouse example. Keep the language appropriate for spoken delivery and avoid any request for citations or further reading.
