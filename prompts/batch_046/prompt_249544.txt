As a PhD student in philosophy of language preparing a diagnostic handout for a graduate seminar on vagueness and the sorites paradox, I want a classification instrument that sorts positions by what they commit to about concrete cases rather than by the labels students have heard. Please construct a decision tree of 6 to 8 leaves, rooted in the question whether borderline cases of a vague predicate have a determinate status, with branches labeled by the claims that split them rather than by theory names. The first-level split should concern the locus of indeterminacy: in the world itself, in our epistemic access, in linguistic meaning, or in conceptual application. The second-level split should concern why the tolerance principle fails—whether because of a hidden sharp boundary, a context-sensitive shift in comparison class, or genuine gradation in the property instantiated. The third-level split should address whether precisification in a formal system eliminates vagueness or merely displaces it to a meta-linguistic level (for instance, when an exact grain-count is stipulated for “heap” in a database schema, but “acceptable entry” remains vague).

Each leaf should carry a named position—epistemicism, ontic indeterminacy, supervaluationism, degrees-of-truth or fuzzy accounts, contextualism, and eliminativism about vague predicates—or be marked “unnamed” with a one-sentence diagnosis of why that combination is empty (incoherent, unattractive, or unexplored). The tree must be tested against three concrete cases: the classic sorites with one-grain removal; a reversed construction in which a clearly non-heap is built grain by grain, forcing a verdict on whether there is necessarily a first grain that makes it a heap; and a synthetic multi-dimensional case, such as applying “cognitively demanding” to a sequence of formal proofs of gradually increasing length and structural complexity, where quantitative and qualitative dimensions vary together. For each leaf, state its verdict on all three cases and identify its most awkward commitment.

Please flag any pairs of positions that converge on all three cases and therefore cannot be separated by this tree alone, and specify what further case—perhaps involving institutional or legal precisification—would distinguish them. The audience is graduate students familiar with basic logic but not with obscure recent literature; do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or author-date citations, and build the tree from the logical space of commitments rather than from a catalog of named theories.
