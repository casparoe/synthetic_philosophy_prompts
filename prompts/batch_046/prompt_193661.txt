I am currently enrolled in an advanced seminar on Donald Davidson’s philosophy of language, and I have become stuck on the relationship between radical interpretation and his broader metaphysics of mind. Davidson describes the radical interpreter as someone who must assign beliefs and meanings to a speaker without any prior knowledge of either, relying instead on behavioral evidence and the principle of charity. Reading recent work that frames cognition in terms of predictive processing, I have started to think that Davidson’s interpreter is essentially constructing a Bayesian predictive model: beliefs are assigned not because they are independently real mental states, but because they maximize the interpreter’s predictive accuracy regarding verbal behavior. If this is correct, then radical interpretation seems to reduce propositional attitudes to instrumental posits—heuristic variables in an observer’s model rather than causally efficacious entities in the speaker’s mind.

Yet Davidson famously insists on the anomalous monism of mental and physical events, which appears to require that beliefs and desires have genuine causal powers independent of any particular interpretive stance. So I want to ask: does Davidson’s framework contain an implicit commitment to a kind of observer-dependent idealism about mental content, where beliefs exist only relative to an interpretive framework? And if not, how can the radical interpreter’s attribution of beliefs be both epistemically prior (in the order of interpretation) and ontologically posterior (as discoveries of pre-existing mental states) without collapsing into a form of behaviorist eliminativism? I ask because it seems to me that if interpretation is genuinely radical—starting from no shared mental content—then the beliefs it yields cannot simultaneously be mere modeling conveniences and real causes of the very behavior being modeled.
