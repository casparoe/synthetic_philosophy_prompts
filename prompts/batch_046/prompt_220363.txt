Read Michel Gondry’s film Eternal Sunshine of the Spotless Mind (2004, screenplay by Charlie Kaufman) through the philosophical debate about the duty to remember and the case for forgetting, particularly as developed by Avishai Margalit in The Ethics of Memory and David Rieff in In Praise of Forgetting. Margalit argues that shared memories constitute the thick relations that make genuinely ethical life possible and that certain past wrongs generate an enduring obligation to remember victims; Rieff contends that both collective and personal forgetting can be morally restorative, breaking cycles of resentment and allowing individuals and communities to begin anew rather than being imprisoned by commemorative duty.

Apply this framework to the film’s central technological premise: a clinical procedure that targets and erases specific episodic memories of a romantic relationship while leaving intact broader semantic knowledge, skills, and emotional residues. Joel and Clementine undergo this erasure, then later encounter traces—audio tapes, inexplicable attractions, fragmentary feelings—without being able to reconstruct the narrative or emotional content that was removed. Your task is twofold.

First, adjudicate whether the film ultimately sides with Margalit (treating the erasure as an ethical failure that severs the relational fabric constitutive of personal identity) or with Rieff (treating the decision to erase, and especially their final choice to begin the relationship again despite knowing the probable pain, as an affirmation that forgetting can be courageous rather than cowardly). Second, introduce and defend a conceptual distinction that neither thinker fully captures: the film imagines what might be called hollow remembrance, a state in which one retains cognitive markers that something important was erased—knowing that a gap exists—without the emotional and narrative substance that would make that knowledge ethically or relationally meaningful. Examine whether the film presents this hollow state as worse than either full memory or complete forgetting.

In developing your argument, analyze at least two specific sequences: the collapsing beach-house scene, in which Joel attempts to hide Clementine within memories not targeted for deletion, and the final encounter in which both have listened to their pre-erasure tapes and nevertheless choose to start over. Does the film suggest that clinical, targeted erasure is ever genuinely available to human beings, or does the persistence of affective residue prove that personal identity is too thickly relational to be surgically edited? Where does the metaphor of selective erasure break down when applied to collective historical memory rather than private romance? And what does the ending—proceeding with full awareness of likely repetition—add to the philosophical debate that abstract argument about obligation and liberation misses?

Reach a definite conclusion about which philosopher the film more nearly supports, but also identify precisely what the film’s dramatization reveals about the limits of both positions: in particular, whether the possibility of hollow remembrance undermines Rieff’s optimism about restorative forgetting and complicates Margalit’s confidence that preserving memory always preserves the ethical relations it is meant to sustain.
