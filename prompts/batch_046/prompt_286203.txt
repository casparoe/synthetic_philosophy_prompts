Following a prolonged argument with a close friend—who insisted that inheriting a nation’s institutions necessarily entails inheriting its corresponding guilt—I have drafted the exam question below for an advanced undergraduate course in moral and political philosophy, along with one student’s response. I am acting as an external grader and need a detached, point-by-point evaluation; my own view is too entangled in the debate to be neutral.

Exam question: Karl Jaspers distinguishes criminal, political, moral, and metaphysical guilt. Can any of these categories be transmitted intergenerationally, such that descendants bear guilt for ancestral state actions? In particular, assess whether political guilt can be inherited in the same manner as citizenship, and whether metaphysical guilt applies collectively across generations rather than remaining confined to solitary individuals.

Student answer: Jaspers clearly separates criminal guilt, which requires individual action and is adjudicated by courts, from the other three forms. Political guilt attaches to citizens collectively because they belong to a state whose actions they enabled; since citizenship is transmitted by birth and naturalization, political guilt is inherited in the same way—descendants of citizens share in the state’s legacy and therefore in its political guilt. Moral guilt is strictly personal and operates through individual conscience, so it cannot be inherited under any circumstances. Metaphysical guilt, however, is not about specific crimes but about universal human solidarity; Jaspers describes it as the feeling that arises in relation to the divine when one survives while others suffer unjustly. Because solidarity binds all people regardless of time, metaphysical guilt can be borne collectively by a nation across generations, particularly when descendants continue to benefit from historical injustices. Thus Jaspers’s framework permits intergenerational collective guilt through both political and metaphysical channels, though not through criminal or moral ones. A common error is to think moral guilt can be transmitted, but Jaspers’s schema blocks that; the real difficulty is recognizing that metaphysical solidarity is diachronic, not merely synchronic.
