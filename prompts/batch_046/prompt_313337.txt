I've been reading up on that genes-vs-groups selection debate and I think I built an argument that proves way too much, but I can't spot the leak. Here goes:

(1) The unit of selection is whatever gets reproduced with diffrences that matter for success.
(2) Genes clearly fit: some versions spread more because they build better bodies.
(3) But bee colonies also fit. Hives with more cooperative workers split and make more daughter hives, so the hive-level trait "cooperation level" gets reproduced differentially.
(4) So groups can be units too, not just genes.
(5) Now think about human cultures. Some groups have norms that reduce free-riding, and those groups grow or absorb neighbors through vertical and horizontal transmission. So culture-level traits get copied, even if group boundaries are fuzzy.
(6) That means human societies are units of selection for behavior like honesty or fairness.
(7) But if the society is the unit being selected, then my personal choice to be fair isn't selected in me — I'm just a vehical carrying the society's trait, like a gene carrying a genome.
(8) So there shouldn't be any deep psychological machinery for fairness inside individuals; it's all just social pressure.
(9) Yet evolutionary psychology seems to show that people have built-in reciprocity mechanisms that evolved at the individual level.
(10) So (7) and (9) clash.
(11) To save (9) I have to deny (6), but then I should deny (3) for bees too, and that feels wrong because people do talk about colony-level selection.
(12) Maybe (1) is too loose? But then I don't know what replaces it.

Where's the mistake? Is there an equivocation in "reproduced" between genes and cultures? I'd really like to keep both the hive idea and the idea that individuals have evolved moral psychology, but I'm worried I'm paying a hidden cost if I do.
