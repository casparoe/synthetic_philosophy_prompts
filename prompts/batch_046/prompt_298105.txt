Write a 90-minute essay exam question for an upper-level undergraduate philosophy of language seminar (word limit 1500, 40% of final grade) on deflationary theories of truth. Frame the question around a specific, constructed objection: the disquotational schema applies with equal formal force to accidental generalizations and to law-like generalizations, so deflationism appears unable to explain why only the latter robustly support counterfactual inferences unless it covertly relies on a substantive notion of truth-making or correspondence. The exam question should ask students to evaluate whether this objection succeeds, requiring them to distinguish minimalism from redundancy theory and from correspondence, and to articulate how a deflationist might separate semantic ascent from explanatory asymmetry without ontological commitment. Then provide: (i) the exam question itself, identifying the exact crux that separates genuine understanding from label-memorization; (ii) a model answer outlining the three specific argumentative moves a top response must make (for example, clarifying that deflationism denies a metaphysical nature to truth rather than denying its semantic utility; showing that explanatory asymmetry can be grounded in modal or causal structure rather than in a truth-property; and diagnosing precisely where the objection conflates truth-aptness with explanatory power); (iii) a marking scheme allocating marks across those moves, with explicit penalties for identifiable confusions—such as treating deflationism as the claim that truth is useless, conflating minimalism with redundancy theory, or assuming that denying a substantive nature of truth entails denying that truth is a property at all; (iv) for any embedded sub-part or distractor, state the misconception it is designed to catch. The difficulty must come from choosing the crux: the prompt should be unanswerable by reciting a standard summary of deflationism. Do not ask for bibliographies, reading lists, or citations, and do not invoke obscure texts, figures, or attributions; keep all references to well-known positions in analytic philosophy and present the objection in your own terms.
