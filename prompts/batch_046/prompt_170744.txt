Question: Three prominent physicists—Steven Weinberg, Stephen Hawking, and Richard Feynman—have each voiced skepticism about philosophy's value for science, though their objections differ in target and tone. Weinberg, in Dreams of a Final Theory, argues that philosophers have consistently failed to provide useful guidance for working physicists and that some philosophical doctrines have actively obstructed scientific reasoning. Hawking, in The Grand Design (co-authored with Leonard Mlodinow), declares that philosophy is dead because it has not kept pace with modern physics and that physics now answers questions that once belonged to philosophy. Feynman, in remarks often summarized by an analogy to ornithology—suggesting that philosophy of science is about as useful to scientists as the study of birds is to birds—implies that scientists need no external philosophical commentary on their methods.

Against these criticisms, philosophers have offered at least two major defenses. The first is conceptual clarification: philosophers analyze interpretations of quantum mechanics, the nature of spacetime singularities, and the meaning of probability in statistical mechanics—work that practicing scientists themselves undertake but rarely systematize. The second is historical and heuristic: philosophical ideas (for example, Mach's critique of absolute space) have sometimes catalyzed scientific breakthroughs, and philosophical training can improve the rigor of scientific reasoning.

Write a response that does the following. First, distinguish the three physicists' criticisms with precision, identifying the specific aspect of philosophy's alleged uselessness each targets—practical guidance, replacement by science, or external methodological commentary. Second, present the two philosophical defenses mentioned above and explain which of the three criticisms each defense is best positioned to answer. Third, evaluate whether these defenses actually meet the physicists' objections or whether they defend a different activity—say, philosophy practiced by scientists themselves rather than by professional philosophers—thereby changing the subject.

Student answer:
The three physicists all agree philosophy is useless, but they say it for different reasons. Weinberg in Dreams of a Final Theory thinks philosophy is harmful because it promotes relativism about scientific truth and stops physicists from believing in objective reality. Hawking said in A Brief History of Time that philosophy is dead because physics has solved all the big questions like the origin of the universe. Feynman compared philosophy to ornithology for birds, meaning scientists don't need philosophers to tell them how to work.

Philosophers defend themselves in two main ways. One is conceptual clarification: they help interpret quantum mechanics and other theories. The other is historical influence: Mach influenced Einstein's theory of relativity, so philosophy can inspire science.

These defenses work differently against each physicist. Against Weinberg, they fail because Weinberg is complaining about current philosophy being useless right now, not about history. Against Hawking, they also fail because Hawking thinks physics has replaced philosophy completely, so clarifying concepts is just more physics, not philosophy. Against Feynman, they succeed: Feynman says scientists don't need outside help, but conceptual clarification is exactly outside philosophers helping scientists understand their theories. So the defenses mostly fail for Weinberg and Hawking but work for Feynman.
