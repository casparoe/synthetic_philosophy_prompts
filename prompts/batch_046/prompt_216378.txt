I'm writing a chapter on the anachronistic reception of medieval nominalism in formal learning theory, and I need to pressure-test an argument I'm developing. Suppose William of Ockham were presented with the modern formalization of inductive inference via Solomonoff's universal prior and Kolmogorov's algorithmic complexity measure—specifically, the claim that Occam's razor receives a rigorous justification because hypotheses with lower Kolmogorov complexity are assigned exponentially higher prior probabilities. Now imagine a further modification that I am considering: a dual-penalty prior in which a hypothesis is penalized not only by the bit-length of its shortest description on a universal Turing machine, but also by a separate ontological-parsimony coefficient that counts the number of distinct universal kinds or relational structures it requires. How do you think Ockham would respond to this hybrid framework? Would he regard the Kolmogorov-complexity component as an acceptable mathematization of his principle that entities ought not be multiplied without necessity, or would he reject it because syntactic brevity does not map onto the metaphysical simplicity he intended? More specifically, would he see the added ontological-parsimony coefficient as a genuine recovery of his nominalist concern with entities, or as a redundant layer that smuggles in the very realist ontology of universals he sought to eliminate? Please ground your reconstruction in Ockham's actual commitments—his rejection of universals outside the mind, his view that simplicity is a criterion of theoretical selection rather than a metaphysical feature of the world, and his nominalist interpretation of the razor—rather than in generic appeals to medieval simplicity.
