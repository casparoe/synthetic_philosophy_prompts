Consider the unilateralist's curse—the tendency of rational agents, each weighing only local costs and benefits, to produce collectively catastrophic outcomes that none of them intend. In my seminar, one student, J., has offered four verdicts on unilateral action in collective-risk settings. First, a state that unilaterally bans a harmful pesticide, knowing that global production will continue and domestic agriculture will suffer sharply, acts permissibly but not obligatorily; the collective harm is essentially unchanged by its withdrawal. Second, a biosafety researcher who publishes a viral-enhancement protocol despite an active, signed voluntary moratorium among peer institutions, reasoning that non-signatory labs abroad will likely replicate the result within a year, commits a serious wrong. Third, a commercial fisher who voluntarily restricts his catch to sustainable levels while competitors overfish, accepting personal ruin without altering the stock collapse, performs a supererogatory act that is praiseworthy but not morally required. Fourth, a manufacturing firm that unilaterally adopts carbon-negative processes at a severe competitive disadvantage, with no detectable impact on global emissions, is permitted but not required to do so.

Now apply this pattern to a new case. An open-source AI collective has signed a voluntary frontier-release agreement with twelve other labs, pledging not to publish architectures that reduce frontier-model training costs by more than thirty percent until a shared safety-evaluation framework is in place. Internal modeling suggests that two well-resourced private laboratories outside the pact will likely achieve equivalent efficiency gains within ten months regardless of the collective's choice. The collective's architecture also incorporates interpretability features that external auditors could study only upon release, which would broaden safety scrutiny. They ask whether they should honor the agreement and withhold publication.

Predict J.'s verdict, identify the closest analog among her four precedents, and articulate the two competing principles—one centered on institutional commitment and explicit cooperative agreement, the other on causal contribution to the acceleration of risk—that both fit her track record equally well. Then specify the further hypothetical case that would distinguish them: for example, a scenario in which a binding agreement exists but unilateral defection would make no causal difference to the collective risk, contrasted with one in which no agreement exists but unilateral action is causally decisive.
