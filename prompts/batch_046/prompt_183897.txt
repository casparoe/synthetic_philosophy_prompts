Here are my gut verdicts on whether particular individuals are in Sartrean bad faith, and I want to know whether a single principle can capture the pattern or whether I must sacrifice coherence for simplicity.

Case 1: A senior civil servant enforces a policy she regards as unjust, telling herself that her rank leaves her no discretion and that she is merely an instrument of democratic authority. Verdict: bad faith. She treats an institutional role as a fixed essence that cancels her transcendence.

Case 2: A person abandons every long-standing commitment—career, family ties, language—overnight, insisting that nothing from her past can constrain her because she is “absolute freedom” and must reinvent herself continuously. Verdict: also bad faith, but here the failure seems to be a denial of facticity rather than of freedom.

Case 3: A carer remains in an exhausting domestic role after sustained reflection, acknowledging that leaving is physically and socially possible but choosing to stay because of bonds she genuinely values. She does not pretend the choice is inevitable. Verdict: not bad faith. She owns both her situation and her freedom to transcend it.

Case 4: A philosopher genuinely convinced by determinist neuroscience lives without regret or excuse, never invoking causation to dodge particular failures; he simply regards the language of choice as metaphysically misleading. Verdict: uncertain. Is this bad faith because it denies radical freedom at the ontological level, or authentic because he does not use determinism as an alibi for any specific act?

Case 5: A professional consistently delegates moral and practical decisions to an algorithmic recommendation system, explaining that “the model knows my revealed preferences better than I do, so following it is just being honest about who I am.” Verdict: bad faith, yet it differs from Case 1: the excuse is not social role but data-mediated self-knowledge.

Case 6: A member of a tightly integrated community rejects the language of individual preference altogether, saying that her self is constituted by shared practices and that asking what she wants apart from “us” is incoherent. She does not invoke this to avoid particular choices; she genuinely experiences her agency as distributed. Verdict: I lean towards not bad faith—perhaps an authentic recognition of intersubjective facticity—but I worry it collapses into a collective flight from transcendence.

The tensions are these. Cases 1 and 5 both outsource agency, yet my intuition is that both are bad faith for subtly different reasons. Cases 2 and 6 both deny the isolated individual, yet I want to call one flight and the other recognition. Case 4 sits uneasily between them.

Please propose either one coherent principle that distinguishes authentic acknowledgment of facticity (and of intersubjectivity) from the use of either as an alibi, or else present the two simplest competing principles, stating precisely which case each must overturn and why. For whichever principle you favour, say what it predicts for a further case I have not listed: a person who, after a traumatic loss, deliberately suppresses all emotional response by treating her grief as “just a biochemical process I observe,” maintaining full awareness that she could choose otherwise but refusing to do so. Please also indicate honestly which of my six verdicts the principle cannot accommodate without revision.
