I'm trying to map my actual commitments about collective choice and individual rights by testing them against concrete configurations, and I'm finding the pattern harder to state than I expected. Consider these cases. Two roommates, A and B, are deciding whether a particular painting hangs in their shared living room: A wants it displayed, B wants it banned as obscene, but B cares more about preventing A from viewing it than about avoiding it himself—if forced, B ranks "neither sees it" above "both see it," which in turn he ranks above "only A sees it." I feel A should have decisive authority over whether it hangs in his own bedroom, but in the shared space I genuinely waver. Now shift to private reading: each has a copy of the same controversial book. Here I think each should choose for himself, and B's preference about what A reads should carry no weight, even though B's preference ordering is structurally identical to the painting case. Add a third person, C, with symmetric liberal rights, and suppose that exercising all three rights simultaneously produces an outcome that every one of them ranks below a single coordinated alternative—everyone prefers uniformity, yet each insists on his personal exception. I still think the rights should prevail, Pareto loss notwithstanding. Then a workplace dress-code vote: the majority prefers strict uniformity, but one employee's religious garment is non-negotiable for her practice; I think she should be exempt even if that slightly lowers everyone's satisfaction with workplace cohesion. Finally, a neighborhood referendum on whether a religious community may build a worship space near residential homes: the residents prefer it elsewhere, but the worshippers want proximity for their own practice; I think the worshippers' claim about their own religious location should dominate the collective preference. Is there a single principle—something like minimal liberalism coupled with a domain restriction—that delivers all of these verdicts, or do I have to drop either the Pareto intuition or the rights intuition? Please state the principle precisely, show which clause handles which case, tell me what it predicts for the shared-living-room case where I hesitate, and be explicit about which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate without implausible contortion.
