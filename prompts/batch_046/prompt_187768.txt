In my graduate seminar on conceptual analysis, a student routinely invokes Wittgenstein's idea of family resemblance to block every attempt to specify necessary and sufficient conditions for being a language game. Her refrain is that, since games themselves share only family resemblances, the concept must be irreducibly open-textured and therefore immune to formalization—including in current debates about whether conversational AI systems participate in language games at all. I want to press her on exactly what thesis is being asserted. Could you lay out the distinct precise claims hidden in that slogan, ranging from the trivial observation that categories have fuzzy borders, through the substantive denial that any shared features exist, to the radical methodological claim that conceptual analysis is futile? For each reading, indicate whether it is defensible, false, or a platitude; whether it genuinely entails that language games resist formal characterization; and which version she is likely relying on when she uses the phrase to dismiss questions about large language models. Present the interpretations as a menu I can bring to our next meeting, and tell me which of my own convictions—that formalization remains possible despite vagueness, and that AI dialogue lacks the right kind of embedded practice—survive under each reading.
