I have been trying to understand why knowing something is meant to be better than merely being right about it, and the usual examples—a clock that happens to show the correct time—feel too tidy to me. What actually worries me is the case where the reason for a belief disappears but the belief survives anyway, like a map that still points north after the magnetic pole has shifted; that seems like true belief that has outlived its justification. By contrast, knowledge feels like it needs a tether: if the world had changed in the relevant respect, the belief would have broken rather than persisted.

Please write a villanelle—nineteen lines, five tercets and a closing quatrain, with an ABA rhyme scheme and two refrains—about this tension. Use the refrain "the belief outlives its reason" for the true-belief line, and "the reason holds the world in place" for the knowledge line. Let the first refrain dominate the opening tercets, circling back without resolution so that the repetition itself enacts the stubborn looping of unjustified true belief. Introduce the second refrain only in the penultimate tercet, and let it fully complete the rhyme scheme in the final quatrain, as if knowledge requires a structural shift that true belief never achieves. Do not resolve whether the tether is genuinely there; instead, make the form show that one state loops endlessly while the other demands a different architecture.
