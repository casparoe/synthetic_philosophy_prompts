I have been working through my intuitions about old evidence in Bayesian confirmation, and I want to see whether they form a coherent pattern or whether I must abandon at least one. Here are my verdicts, ordered from the clear to the conflicted.

First, when a well-known anomaly—such as the anomalous perihelion advance of Mercury—is derived by a new theory like general relativity, I treat that old astronomical record as strongly confirmatory, on a par with a novel prediction.

Second, if a theory is engineered specifically to reproduce a single known anomaly and makes no independent predictions, the anomaly confirms it only weakly; the real evidential work is done by whatever the theory then forecasts about unobserved phenomena.

Third, suppose two theories both entail an old, stable observation. One derives it directly from core principles; the other requires an auxiliary hypothesis introduced solely to save the phenomenon. I think both gain some confirmation, but the first gains substantially more.

Fourth, I distinguish predictive surprise: a theory that assigns high prior probability to an outcome that was in fact old news receives little confirmation from that outcome, whereas one that assigned it low prior probability receives much, regardless of whether the theorist personally knew the outcome beforehand.

Fifth, and here is the tension. Imagine an old dataset from the 1920s that was recorded, then dismissed as noise and forgotten. In the 1970s a new theory predicts precisely that pattern. Because the 1920s experimenters could not have tailored their methods to the 1970s theory, I feel this rediscovered record confirms the theory very strongly—perhaps even more than a contemporaneous replication would. Yet consider a different old record: one that was widely accepted and explained by a rival theory that is now abandoned. If the 1970s theory also accommodates this record, I feel it should gain very little, since the data was already "used up" by the prior explanation. So in both sub-cases the data is old and previously interpreted, but my intuitions diverge sharply: heavy confirmation in the first, negligible in the second.

Sixth, if a theory is initially proposed to explain old evidence A, and then is modified (not replaced) to accommodate old evidence B that originally contradicted it, I have ambivalent feelings. The need for a patch seems disconfirming, yet once patched, the theory's correct accommodation of B seems to restore some confirmation. I am unsure whether B nets out as confirmatory, disconfirmatory, or neutral.

Can you articulate a precise evidential principle—drawing on notions like predictive risk, the independence of data-generating processes from theoretical construction, or prior probability distributions—that delivers verdicts one through four, resolves or adjudicates the tension in five, and gives a determinate answer for six? Please state exactly which of my verdicts your principle must reject if they cannot all be saved, and apply it to one further case: a theory proposed in 2020 predicts an observation recorded in 1900 that was never published because it seemed physically impossible at the time, with the 2020 theorists unaware that the record existed.
