I've been working through why human extinction would be a tragedy even if the end came peacefully, with no suffering involved. I kept returning to Samuel Scheffler's suggestion that part of the harm lies in the destruction of a collective afterlife—future generations who would continue our projects and give our present efforts meaning. But when I tried to formalize this intuition into an argument about deprivation, it seemed to collapse into an absurd conclusion about everyday reproductive choices. Here is the chain as I wrote it out:

(1) If extinction is catastrophically bad and the badness is not just the deaths, then it must consist in the loss of all the good lives that future generations would have lived—an enormous deprivation of existence.
(2) This deprivation is structurally identical whether the missing lives belong to a continuous human lineage or to a branch that never began: in both cases, specific potential people with specific potential goods never come to be.
(3) Therefore, when an individual or couple chooses not to have a child who would have lived well, they inflict the same kind of harm—one good life is absent—merely on a smaller scale.
(4) By aggregation, widespread voluntary childlessness prevents as many good lives as a localized extinction event, so if (1) is true, reproductive restraint should carry comparable moral weight.
(5) Yet it is plainly not wrong, let alone catastrophic, for people to remain childless by choice.
(6) So the reason extinction is bad cannot be the bare absence of future good lives; there must be some other factor, such as the rupture of an actually existing chain of care or the destruction of accumulated culture.

What makes me uneasy is that this reasoning feels valid, which would force me to abandon (1). But I would strongly prefer to keep (1) together with (5) and reject whatever hidden slide connects them. I have a suspicion that the error lies between (2) and (3), where I treat the failure to continue a narrative line as equivalent to the failure to originate one—perhaps an equivocation between continuity harm and origination harm. Where exactly does the argument fail, and if I reject that step, what conceptual cost do I pay? Must I give up the idea that future people matter in themselves, or can I preserve it by distinguishing the moral status of breaking an existing thread from that of leaving a thread unspun?
