Read Oscar Wilde's The Picture of Dorian Gray through the philosophy of originals and forgeries, using a framing drawn from software verification: call it the "source-truth / surface-truth" distinction. Source-truth holds that an object's identity and value depend on its causal provenance and history of production; surface-truth holds that only present, observable properties matter. In the novel, the portrait ages and accumulates moral damage while Dorian's body remains unchanged, so that Dorian presents a perfect surface-copy of youth and the canvas hides the "source" record of his deeds. I want a reading that treats this not as mere supernatural plot device but as a philosophical stress-test: is Dorian the forgery and the painting the authentic original? Which scenes — Basil's first viewing of the altered canvas, the locked-room concealment, the final confrontation with the knife — best illuminate whether authenticity tracks production history or present appearance? Where does the novel resist being read this way, for example by making Dorian's internal corruption invisible except through the portrait? Please take both Wilde's text and the distinction seriously, avoid reducing the novel to an illustration of art-market debates, and conclude with what the book adds to our thinking about originals that philosophical argument about fakes alone does not capture.
