I've been drafting notes for an essay about the common prior assumption—specifically, how Aumann's agreement result is often invoked in rationalist discussions of disagreement as if it applies to any rational divergence in belief, and whether that extension holds up once we distinguish parameter-level disagreement from what I'm tentatively calling "ontology-level" disagreement, where agents don't merely assign different probabilities within a shared state space but actually disagree about which variables belong in the model at all.

To make the point concrete, I'm using a hypothetical case involving two researchers analyzing survey data on policy preferences. Both have access to identical response sets, but one researcher includes "social desirability bias" as a latent causal node affecting reported answers, while the other treats the survey responses as direct measurements of preference without that intermediate structure. They update differently on new data not because they have different priors over probabilities within a shared graph, but because their graphs themselves diverge—so the messages they exchange (the data points) don't map onto the same event spaces. I argue that this isn't just a failure of the common prior assumption in practice; it's a case where the formal conditions for applying the theorem—shared state spaces and agreed-upon interpretation of messages—aren't satisfied in the first place, which means invoking Aumann to say they "should" converge is a category mistake.
