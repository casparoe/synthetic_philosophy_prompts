My friend and I just finished arguing about Machiavelli's account of fortune and virtù. She kept returning to his comparison of fortune to a woman who must be beaten and roughly handled if one wishes to keep her under, using it first to defend reckless boldness in politics, then to claim that Machiavelli denies any real external constraint on human action, and finally to dismiss the image as mere sexist metaphor without conceptual content.

Please lay out the distinct precise theses this slogan could express: that fortune is an active, responsive force rather than random chance; that mastery requires violent domination of circumstance; that the gendered language encodes a Renaissance ontology of active and passive principles; or that virtù succeeds by adapting its methods to the times rather than by perpetual aggression. For each reading, say whether it is trivial, defensible, false, or an anachronistic imposition.

Then identify which reading actually licenses her use of the line to justify constant boldness, and address a less-discussed possibility: that fortune operates as a strategic complement to virtù, rewarding flexible, timely adjustment rather than unceasing violence. Is that interpretation genuinely available in Machiavelli, or is it a modern imposition?
