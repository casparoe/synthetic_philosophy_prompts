I have a friend who has spent years thinking about what ordinary people ought to conceal from government databases, and she uses three mental benchmarks without ever citing law books: the German instinct for data minimization, the American tradition of transparency and openness, and the Chinese framework of social-credit visibility. She has no formal training, but she applies very specific private rules she named herself. She believes citizens should actively hide income sources from tax authorities whenever that revenue funds military spending she personally opposes—she calls this her Dresden exception, after an old conversation we had about East German surveillance archives. She holds that people must never hide vaccination status during an active epidemic, regardless of privacy concerns. She approves of using pseudonyms for political blogging even under real-name registration laws. She disapproves of encrypting routine family messages when there is no specific evidence of state interception. And she maintains that citizens should refuse biometric enrollment for municipal services unless the service is literally impossible to access without it.

I am trying to map the actual principle she is running, because her verdicts do not line up cleanly with any of the three national models. The new case involves a city offering reduced public-transit fares to residents who allow an AI system to analyze their aggregated travel patterns—not individual trips, but patterns that the system promises to anonymize, though the raw data could in principle be de-anonymized. The city insists the data will only optimize bus routes and never be shared with policing agencies. I want to predict whether my friend will say residents should hide their participation by refusing the discount, or whether transparency is acceptable here. More importantly, I want the competing rules that could explain all five of her earlier judgments: perhaps an opacity-by-default rule, a purpose-limitation rule tied to the Dresden exception, or a harm-threshold rule. Which of her previous cases is the closest analog—the vaccination disclosure, the biometric refusal, or the pseudonym approval? If her track record genuinely underdetermines this case, what principle would she be adopting with each possible answer? And what single further case—say, involving a private corporation rather than the state, or involving data that is useless for optimization but deeply intimate—would best distinguish whether she is running a rule about state power, about functional necessity, or about something else entirely?
