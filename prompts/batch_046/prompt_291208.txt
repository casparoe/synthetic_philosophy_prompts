I am directing a small interdisciplinary research group with one three-year grant and no postdoctoral researchers. We are focusing on open questions that emerge directly from the socialist calculation debate—specifically Hayek's knowledge-problem critique, Mises's claim that rational economic calculation requires private property in capital goods, and Lange's mechanism for competitive-market socialism with central investment boards—because we believe these bear directly on contemporary problems of algorithmic resource allocation in decentralized networks and multi-agent planning systems.

Given severely limited resources, which two or three open lines of work in this area should consume our effort, and which should we deliberately deprioritize? I am not asking for a survey of the literature or a bibliography. I want a defended agenda: for each line you rank highly, specify what makes it tractable with present methods, which other questions depend on its resolution, and how an answer would change the actual design of distributed-planning mechanisms rather than merely settling historical interpretation. For each deprioritized line, indicate whether it is conceptually settled, empirically intractable given current tools, or not upstream of any institutional-design problem that matters to us.

Please maintain the debate's internal distinctions precisely: separate Hayek's epistemic-dispersion objection from incentive-compatibility failures, and treat Lange's iterative price-adjustment procedure not as a completed solution but as an open mechanism-design problem with unresolved convergence and information-revelation properties. Avoid recommendations of specific texts or authors to read; the group can locate sources, but we need to know where to direct our labor.
