I sit on the committee of a small housing co-operative, and we have been asked to choose between two emergency-repair funds. In the first comparison, Fund A guarantees that every household receives £500 should a disaster strike, with no risk of failure. Fund B offers an 89 per cent chance that every household receives £500, a 10 per cent chance that every household receives £2,500, and a 1 per cent chance that no household receives anything. I find myself strongly preferring Fund A.

In a separate decision about a different reserve, we must choose between Fund C — an 11 per cent chance that every household receives £500, and nothing otherwise — and Fund D — a 10 per cent chance that every household receives £2,500, and nothing otherwise. Here I prefer Fund D.

A neighbour who has read some economics tells me that my two preferences violate the independence axiom. He says that the 89 per cent outcome common to both pairs ought to cancel out, so I should either prefer B and D, or A and C, and that treating them differently is irrational. But the situations feel genuinely different: in the first I am weighing certainty for all my neighbours against a collective gamble, whereas in the second I am merely comparing two long shots for the group. Is there a philosophically respectable defence for allowing collective certainty to shift my preferences in this way, or is my neighbour correct that consistency demands I change one of my choices? Please reply in British English, and take the underlying theoretical tension seriously rather than simply instructing me to be consistent.
