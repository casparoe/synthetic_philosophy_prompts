You advise an international development NGO that must screen local partner practices—ceremonial gift exchange, kin-based contracting, patronage hospitality, and mutual-aid obligations—against corruption risk, without assuming Western transparency norms automatically apply. You need a reusable decision procedure that a non-specialist compliance officer can run in about fifteen minutes per case, sign off if clear, and escalate to a senior board if not. The board applies Western anti-bribery rules, but the partners operate in low-trust environments where generalized reciprocity often sustains institutions that the state has abandoned.

Construct an ordered checklist with explicit stop points, judgment zones marked ESCALATE—VALUE JUDGMENT REQUIRED, and a reverse-context test (would this practice be treated as corruption if it occurred in a high-trust Western bureaucracy with identical formal roles?). The conceptual backbone must treat corruption as requiring three jointly necessary conditions: (i) a formal institutional role carrying a defined public-purpose duty; (ii) an exchange that is either a direct quid pro quo tied to a specific decision or part of a diffuse reciprocity network that systematically subordinates that duty to private relationships; and (iii) a power asymmetry that makes refusal or exclusion disproportionately costly for the less powerful party. Include a distinct step separating relational capital—generalized reciprocity that enables institutional functioning—from extractive exchange—private appropriation of public goods—without presuming all kinship or gift practices are the latter.

Specify what counts as sufficient at each step, where the procedure stops with NOT CORRUPTION—MANAGE DISCLOSURE/RECUSAL, where it stops with CORRUPTION—PROHIBIT AND REPORT, and where it hands off. Then apply it to three cases:

1. A procurement officer accepts a ceremonial textile (~$300) from a supplier’s family at a religious wedding, six months before any contract is discussed; refusal would rupture a community alliance essential to operations.
2. A health manager directs vaccination supplies preferentially to kin who previously provided unpaid labor and gifts to her family, arguing “we care for our own first” because the state provides nothing.
3. A partner’s advisory board—composed of community elders who approve funding—also receives private loans from the partner’s revolving fund; board membership and loan access are widely understood to be linked, but no contract explicitly ties the two flows.

Run the steps in order for each. At least one case—preferably case 3—must yield a verdict that conflicts with a strict zero-tolerance reading (e.g., AMBIGUOUS/ESCALATE—INSTITUTIONAL CONFLICT RATHER THAN PERSONAL CORRUPTION rather than CORRUPT). Explicitly state what the procedure cannot do: for instance, it cannot adjudicate whether generalized reciprocity in a collapsed-state context is ethically justified, only whether the three corruption conditions are met.

Present the procedure in a numbered flow and the three applications concisely. Keep the entire response under 500 words. End with a brief self-critical note identifying the step most likely to misfire by substituting a formal check for a substantive moral judgment about institutional legitimacy, and state what specific factual or normative assumption—if overturned—would change the verdict in case 3. Finally, say what would change your mind about the overall framework: which argument or feature, if shown wrong, would lead you to abandon the three-condition joint-requirement model for a simpler or stricter rule?
