After a career in experimental physics, I have recently begun studying jurisprudence, and I am trying to understand the exchange between H.L.A. Hart and Lon Fuller. Hart's rule of recognition strikes me as analogous to identifying a stable equilibrium in a physical system: it describes an observable pattern of acceptance among legal officials. Fuller, by contrast, maintains that law must satisfy procedural conditions—generality, promulgation, non-retroactivity, clarity, and consistency—to count as law at all, conditions he associates with an inner morality of law. Here is my confusion: if these procedural requirements are genuinely necessary boundary conditions for any legal system to exist, much as specific temperature and pressure ranges are necessary for a chemical reaction, then does it not follow that Fuller's argument falsifies the separation thesis? In other words, if a minimal procedural morality is structurally inseparable from legal validity, then law and morality are not conceptually distinct in the way Hart insists. I would be grateful for an explanation of where this analogy between physical boundary conditions and Fuller's procedural conditions breaks down.
