Here's an argument I built for a seminar and now find suspicious because the conclusion is not merely false but self-undermining for anyone who reasons. (1) Disjunctivism denies that veridical perception and illusion share any mental common factor, on the ground that only the former involves a direct perceptual relation to an object. (2) By parallel reasoning, belief and disbelief share no mental common factor, since belief relates the mind to a true state of affairs and disbelief to a false one. (3) If belief and disbelief share no mental kind, then there is no single cognitive state of "entertaining p" that remains constant while one weighs reasons for and against p. (4) But rational deliberation requires holding exactly one proposition in mind across opposing attitudes. (5) So consistent disjunctivism implies that rational deliberation is impossible. (6) Rational deliberation is not impossible. (7) Therefore the reasoning in (1) and (2), applied generally, proves too much and must fail. I would prefer to preserve (1) and (6). Where does the analogy between perceptual relation and representational attitude break down, and what does repairing it cost the disjunctivist?
