Having retired after thirty-five years in experimental physics, I have lately been working through philosophy of mind without formal training, and I keep encountering a specific argumentative pattern that I cannot place. The pattern concerns defenses of a singular, unified "point of view" in conscious experience. Typically the defense begins with an undeniable observation: right now there seems to be one place from which I am observing these words. Then, when presented with phenomena that fracture that unity—a split-brain patient whose left hand acts in ways the verbal self denies knowing; long stretches of skilled driving during which no continuous narrative is present; or the retrospective fabrication of motives after a decision has already occurred—the defender does not abandon the unified viewpoint. Instead, each fragment is treated as an independent stream or version of awareness that is still delivered to, and registered by, that original singular observer. The multiplicity is admitted, but only as input; the central convergence point is preserved and merely fed more richly. I have noticed this in academic discussions, in popular neuroscience writing, and in my own introspection when I "come to" after daydreaming and immediately construct a continuous self that was supposedly present all along. Is there an established concept for this precise hybrid position—one that accepts distributed, reconstructed, or fragmented awareness yet insists on a single central registration point? How should it be distinguished from the account that rejects any convergence altogether, treating awareness as genuinely dispersed without a final observer? If no standard term exists, I would welcome a precise definition with clear boundary conditions: specifically, whether the central point is meant to operate in real time or only retrospectively, and whether the fragments are genuinely independent processes or merely partial reports to an enduring observer. Please lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then provide the reasoning, with no preamble and no restatement of the question.
