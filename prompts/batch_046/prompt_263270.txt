I am preparing a three-month internal grant proposal for a small interdisciplinary research collective. The conceptual question I want funded is this: when a decentralized, geographically dispersed online community—say, an open-source protocol network—begins to retroactively designate certain early forum debates or governance votes as “foundational traditions” and to treat dissent from them as a kind of cultural betrayal, is this best understood as an instance of invented tradition in the Hobsbawm sense, or does the absence of a state apparatus, physical ritual, and shared ethnicity make it a structurally different phenomenon? I want to compare this case with how Anderson describes the nation as an imagined community sustained by print-capitalism and shared temporal consciousness, asking specifically whether tradition-invention requires territoriality or whether digital networks generate an analogous mechanism through version history and protocol consensus. The deliverable should be useful to people actually designing governance frameworks for distributed networks—not just a taxonomy, but a clear argument about whether appeals to “heritage” in these spaces are legitimate normative moves or category errors.
