In a philosophy of mind course I am taking, the lecturer has been developing the view that conscious experience is best understood as a kind of user illusion — not a false belief about an internal world, but a functional interface analogous to the graphical desktop of an operating system. The desktop metaphor, originally introduced to make file systems navigable for non-technical users, presents folders, windows, and a trash can as tangible objects, yet none of these correspond to physical structures inside the hardware; they are representational conveniences that allow interaction with complex underlying processes. The lecturer extends this analogy directly to phenomenal consciousness: just as the desktop hides machine code behind icons that have no direct causal interaction with the circuitry, so qualitative properties — the redness of red, the hurtfulness of pain — are representational artifacts that hide neural dynamics behind a user-friendly model. From this, the lecturer draws a strong eliminativist conclusion: there is no inner screen watched by a homunculus, phenomenal properties possess no causal powers of their own, and the hard problem dissolves once we recognize that consciousness is merely the interface through which an organism navigates its own cognitive architecture.

I am trying to assess how far this analogy can legitimately carry the argument, particularly because it seems to import features from the source domain that may not have analogues in the target. Can you lay out an explicit mapping between the desktop interface and conscious experience — what corresponds to the user, to the icons, to the hidden machine code, and to the absence of an internal observer — and then identify the precise points where the correspondence breaks down? I am especially interested in disanalogies that matter for the intended conclusion: for example, computer interfaces are designed for an external user who is ontologically distinct from the machine, whereas conscious representation appears to be self-targeting; and interfaces are causally inert only in a very specific sense, since the user's actions through the interface do eventually cause physical changes in the hardware, which raises questions about whether phenomenal representation might similarly have downstream causal efficacy despite being surface-level. Does the conclusion that phenomenal properties are causally inert representational conveniences survive once these disanalogies are fully on the table, or does it depend on smuggling in an ontology of representation that the analogy itself does not establish? If the desktop metaphor ultimately fails at a critical juncture, what alternative comparison — perhaps drawn from predictive processing, virtual-machine architectures, or self-modeling theories — would better capture the relationship between phenomenal experience and its underlying neural dynamics without presupposing an external user?
