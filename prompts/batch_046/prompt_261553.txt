I'm a graduate student in philosophy of language taking a seminar on linguistic defaults, gender marking, and conceptual analysis. Alongside coursework I work as an assistant copy editor for an interdisciplinary journal that publishes philosophy of science, medicine, and cognitive psychology. Submissions regularly contain belief reports, direct quotations, and methodological descriptions using gender-neutral terms—"the researcher," "the subject," "the patient," "the agent"—and my supervisor has asked me to develop a reusable editorial procedure for flagging cases where substituting a gender-neutral term for a gendered one (or vice versa) changes the substantive claim, or where a gender-neutral term carries a hidden masculine default that distorts empirical or conceptual content.

Ground the procedure in the conceptual analysis of hidden gender defaults and the gap between semantic neutrality and pragmatic interpretation developed in Hofstadter's essay A Person Paper on Purity in Language, but do not summarize the essay. Instead, translate that analysis into an operational checklist or flowchart in words that I can apply repeatedly—roughly ten submissions per week, with no more than fifteen minutes per case, no direct access to authors for clarification, and no authority to rewrite text without senior sign-off.

The procedure should include: (1) a short ordered set of tests I can run on any sentence (for example, substituting "she" or "they" for the neutral term and checking whether the claim becomes false, anomalous, or empirically unsupported; checking whether a methodological description references a "standard subject" whose physiology is implicitly male; and checking nested belief reports where the gender of the believer or agent affects the truth conditions); (2) explicit identification of the hard cases—historical quotations that must preserve original gendered language, dosage or physiological claims masked by substituting "person" for "man," and belief contexts where gender-neutral substitution alters who is being attributed with the belief; (3) clear marking of the points where judgment cannot be eliminated and I must simply flag for senior review rather than resolve alone.

Apply the procedure to three worked cases: one where substitution is safe and preserves meaning; one where it is unsafe and changes the empirical claim (for instance, a clinical protocol describing a 70 kg male reference body where "the subject" is substituted with "the person"); and one where the procedure yields an answer the author will resist—such as revealing that a purportedly neutral account of "the typical user" in a cognition study relies on an unstated male-default model of attention or spatial reasoning. Finally, state explicitly what the procedure cannot do: where it will misfire, what ambiguities it cannot resolve, and why an editor without domain-specific scientific expertise should not attempt to settle the flag unilaterally.
