I'm preparing a unit on classical Indian epistemology for my graduate seminar, and I'm trying to work out a subtle tension in the Nyāya account of pramāṇas that standard surveys tend to smooth over. Most introductory treatments present the four pramāṇas—pratyakṣa, anumāna, upamāna, and śabda—as a tidy taxonomy, noting in passing that Nyāya rejects arthāpatti and anupalabdhi as independent instruments of knowledge, subsuming absence-cognition under either direct perception or inference depending on the commentator. But I want to press a more granular problem: if anupalabdhi is not a standalone pramāṇa, then the cognition of absence—say, recognizing that there is no pot on the ground—must be reconstructed through some combination of direct perception of the ground and an inferential step involving the non-observation of the pot together with its counter-correlate conditions. At the same time, Nyāya treats śabda as yielding immediate cognition of objects that are not present to the senses, provided conditions such as yogyatā (fitness), ākāṅkṣā (expectancy), and tātparya-jñāna (grasp of the speaker's intention) are satisfied. So here is the conceptual knot: how exactly does the Nyāya avoid collapsing the epistemic structure of absence-cognition into a mirror-image of testimonial cognition? Both cases seem to involve knowing something not presently perceived—an absent pot via anupalabdhi, a distant peak via authoritative word—yet one enjoys independent pramāṇa-status while the other is relegated to derivative standing.

Please explain this issue in mechanistic detail, walking through the specific roles of viśeṣaṇa-viśeṣya-bhāva (the qualifier-qualified relation) in perceptual absence, the function of tātparya in verbal cognition, and precisely why the Nyāya think the absence-case lacks the distinctive "fitness" structure that would entitle it to its own pramāṇa. I want an intuitive, gut-level account of the internal differences—not a bibliographic survey—though you should flag any point where my framing might depend on a contested reading of the Nyāya-sūtra or on later Navya-Nyāya revisions that alter the classical position. If my premise is confused—for instance, if the distinction I'm pressing dissolves once we attend properly to the Nyāya theory of negation as a viśeṣya-dependent mode rather than an independent grasp—say so plainly rather than constructing a flattering answer. Be precise in your conceptual reconstruction, but avoid inventing specific sūtra numbers or attributing exact formulations to named authors unless you are fully certain; paraphrase freely, and do not provide reading lists or citations.
