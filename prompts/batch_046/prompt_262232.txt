I’ve been arguing with a friend about whether deplatforming and social sanction are best understood as punishment, risk management, or communal reciprocity, and we kept returning to how a specific body applies its rules in practice. I’m trying to formalize the track record of our university’s Cross-Disciplinary Speech Council, which adjudicates sanctions—ranging from archival annotation and algorithmic demotion to temporary suspension and permanent removal—on a shared research forum. Their past verdicts are these. First, a senior professor delivered a public lecture arguing that a widely endorsed social policy was counterproductive, citing contested but peer-reviewed data; the lecture caused widespread offense, and the council kept it archived with an appended critical commentary but left his posting rights intact. Second, a junior researcher anonymously circulated fabricated allegations of data fraud against a peer; the council permanently revoked his posting privileges and published a correction. Third, a prominent commentator used an unambiguous slur in a clearly derogatory post; the council suspended the account for ninety days and required an explicit acknowledgment of harm before reinstatement. Fourth, a researcher shared a methodologically sound study whose conclusions contradicted a dominant moral belief; the council permitted the post but required a linked rebuttal from a qualified critic. Fifth, a student organized a disruptive campaign to flood the forum and suppress a speaker’s invitation; the council banned her for thirty days for procedural disruption, without addressing the underlying opinion.

The new case is this. A mid-career, non-tenured researcher posts a rigorous statistical meta-analysis that is technically accurate but widely interpreted as undermining the dignity of a protected group. The post contains no false claims, no direct calls to action, and no slurs, but it triggers protests and reports of members feeling unsafe. When the council requests that she add contextual framing or link to a critical rebuttal, she refuses on principle, arguing that accurate data should not require apology.

If the council wants to be consistent with itself, what should it decide—annotate, demote, suspend, or remove? Which of the five precedents is the closest analog? If the precedents genuinely underdetermine the outcome, what principle would the council be adopting with each possible choice? What are the two most plausible rules that jointly fit all five verdicts, what does each predict for this case, which prediction is best supported, and what further hypothetical case would best distinguish the two rules?
