I am working through the literature for a philosophy-of-mind seminar, and I keep returning to Daniel Dennett's heterophenomenology—not merely as a tactical recommendation to treat subjects' verbal reports about experience as fictional narratives or texts requiring interpretation, but as a more stringent methodological principle that fixes in advance what can legitimately count as data in consciousness science. Please describe the most important implications of adopting heterophenomenology in this stronger, normative form. In particular, I want to understand how it would reframe three interconnected problems: the evidential weight we should assign to anomalous first-person reports in which a subject claims to experience a qualitative feature that apparently outstrips their available descriptive vocabulary; the conditions, if any, under which consciousness could be attributed to systems that do not produce verbal reports at all, such as certain non-human animals or artificial architectures; and the precise methodological boundary between heterophenomenological interpretation and third-person neuroscience, especially whether neural findings could ever legitimately correct or override an interpreted report without undermining the anti-authoritarian core of the method.

Please present your analysis with appropriate philosophical care, and then—since some of these extensions go beyond Dennett's explicit formulations—say explicitly how confident you are in what you claim, identifying the specific point on which you are least certain and briefly explaining why that implication is the most fragile.
