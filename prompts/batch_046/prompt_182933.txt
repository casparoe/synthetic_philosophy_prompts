Within the philosophy of AI-generated art—particularly images and text produced by diffusion models and large language models—the standard questions about machine creativity, artistic intention, and copyright are well-rehearsed but rarely sorted by conceptual dependency. I want to treat this as an agenda-setting exercise rather than a survey, using a framing that is not usually made explicit: distinguish process-value theories, on which artistic worth partly resides in an agent's navigational effort through a conceptual space, from artifact-value theories, on which worth resides in the final object's properties alone. Diffusion models complicate both, since the relevant process is distributed across training-data curation, latent-space interpolation, and prompt engineering, with no single locus of effort. Given that framing, which open questions are upstream? Is the metaphysical status of stochastic discovery—finding a structured output in noise—more fundamental than the legal question of copyrightability, or does institutional design constrain what counts as discovery? Does resolving whether these systems can perform transformational creativity, altering the conceptual space itself in the sense Boden described, carry weight for aesthetic theory, or only for intellectual-property doctrine? Which lines are tractable with current philosophical tools and existing technical descriptions of model architecture, and which depend on empirical breakthroughs we do not yet have? Provide a defended prioritization—explicit criteria such as importance, tractability, downstream dependency, and neglect—stating clearly what kind of effort this agenda is meant to guide: a philosophy-of-mind research group, a semester-long course, or a policy draft. Do not provide a bibliography or reading list; I am asking for an ordering of cruxes, not citations.
