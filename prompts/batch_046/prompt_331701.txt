As a senior site reliability engineer with no formal philosophical training, I am required each quarter to sign off on root-cause analyses for production incidents. Analysts increasingly propose novel failure-mode hypotheses and then cite years of archived telemetry—garbage-collection traces, network handshakes, or authentication logs—that align with them. I need a reusable decision procedure that translates the Bayesian treatment of old evidence (the problem that evidence already available before a theory is formulated should not automatically, or should only conditionally, raise confidence in that theory) into a checklist I can run in about fifteen minutes per case, without consulting texts.

The deliverable should be an ordered flowchart in words: the questions to ask in sequence, what counts as a sufficient answer at each node, where the procedure stops with “do not update belief,” where it stops with a qualitative tier of update (weak, moderate, strong), and—explicitly marked—which nodes are unavoidable judgment calls that I must sign off personally. It must function when I lack precise probabilities, using only qualitative ratings, and it must be honest about which steps are genuinely formalised and which are simplified heuristics. Crucially, embed a check I am calling the Pre-Exposure Constraint: before any archived telemetry can count in favour of the hypothesis, I must establish whether the specific patterns in that telemetry were accessible to the analysts, or used in constructing the hypothesis parameters, during its formulation; if they were, the procedure must direct me to treat that telemetry as non-confirmatory regardless of fit.

Apply the procedure step-by-step to three cases, including one where it yields an answer the analysis team will not welcome. The cases are:

1. A proposed garbage-collection deadlock theory predicts a specific stall signature in GC logs; analysts cite eighteen months of archived GC traces showing that exact signature, but those traces formed part of the dataset used to train the predictive model that generated the deadlock parameters.
2. A network-partition theory predicts a precise three-packet handshake failure sequence; this sequence appears in logs from two years prior, dismissed at the time as noise, and no member of the current team had examined those particular traces before the theory was proposed.
3. A security-breach hypothesis predicts an unusual authentication-error cascade; old logs contain the cascade, but only in aggregated dashboards that obscure the individual error ordering, leaving the fit ambiguous.

For each case, show the path through the procedure and the final output.

Finally, state clearly what the procedure cannot do—for instance, assign exact posterior probabilities, resolve disputes about prior plausibility without my input, or replace direct engineering verification of the proposed mechanism. Write the response in British English, with British spelling and punctuation conventions throughout.
