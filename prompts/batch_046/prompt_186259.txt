I'm in an anthropology seminar and we keep circling back to whether farming was actually a bad deal. One student proposed this framing — she called it a kind of caloric payday loan. The idea is that switching to agriculture is like borrowing labor from your future self: you put in massive upfront work clearing fields and building storage, then pay it back with intrest through worse nutrition, more work hours, and bodies that wear out faster. Once you've got surplus tied to a plot and dependants who can't just wander off, you're locked in — like defaulting on a debt where the penalties compound and there's no bankruptcy option. She's using it to push back against the standard progress narrative and say the agricultural revolution wasn't just a mistake but a kind of predatory trap with no exit.

Can you audit how far that analogy actually holds? Give me the correspondance table — what's the principal, what's the intrest rate, who or what is the lender, what does default even look like for a Neolithic community — then tell me where the mapping breaks. Was there actually a borrower with agency, or are we projecting modern contract ideas onto kin-based groups? Does the debt compound like financial interest, or is it just steady overwork? And does the comparison smuggle in assumptions about choice and exit that don't fit societies without markets or banks? Finally, I want your take on whether the trap conclusion survives once those disanalogies are on the table, or if the payday-loan framing is mostly just making the transition sound more intentional and predatory than the archeological record actually supports.
