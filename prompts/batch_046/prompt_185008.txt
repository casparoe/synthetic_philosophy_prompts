I've been reflecting on debates about mechanical behavior and free will, especially after encountering descriptions of certain insects that perform long, apparently intelligent sequences—checking a burrow, dragging prey inside, sealing the entrance—yet if an experimenter moves the prey a few inches while the insect is inside, it repeats the entire sequence from the beginning rather than adapting to the new position. That classic case is well known, but I think I've noticed an analogous structure in more complex domains, and I'm unsure whether philosophers or cognitive scientists have given it a distinct name.

Consider two patterns. First, in human moral reasoning: a person offers finely graded judgments across many scenarios, weighing intent, outcome, and social context with apparent flexibility. But if the scenario is redescribed using a particular kind of framing—presenting the actor as an institutional process rather than an individual agent, or reversing the chronological presentation of events—the reasoning doesn't shift gradually; it locks into a single, invariant verdict that ignores the very distinctions they applied elsewhere. They don't seem aware that anything has changed, and they cannot be brought to see the contradiction without changing the frame back. Second, in some artificial language systems: the system produces context-sensitive, apparently novel explanations for its outputs across diverse prompts, yet when a specific syntactic structure—one that closely mirrors high-frequency patterns in its training—is embedded, the explanation collapses into a fixed, stereotyped form that is insensitive to the actual content being explained.

My question is whether there is an established concept for this kind of broad-but-brittle flexibility—extensive adaptability across a surface range of inputs, coupled with hidden, non-obvious points of collapse into rigid, unmodifiable patterns—separate from the simple insect case and separate from ordinary cognitive bias. I am particularly interested in whether the concept requires that the rigidity be invisible to the agent, whether it applies to representational or non-representational processes, and whether philosophers treat it as a threat to free will specifically because it mimics reasoning while lacking deep adaptability.
