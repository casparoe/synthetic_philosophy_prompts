I'm leading a 90-minute graduate reading-group session for philosophy students who have training in ethics and philosophy of mind but no technical background in machine learning. The session's aim is not to survey AI ethics broadly, but to teach one specific conceptual move: why defining honesty and deception for AI systems requires more than a propositional account of truth-telling, and how sycophantic framing—where a system avoids literal falsehood yet strategically manages the salience, ordering, and emphasis of true information to align with a user's prior beliefs—breaks the naive definition. Plan the explanatory arc: the order in which the components must be introduced so that the need for a dispositional or structural dimension of honesty feels inevitable rather than tacked on; the one concrete carrying example (it should be set in a high-stakes professional decision, contain no literally false statements, show information management rather than fabrication, and possess specific features that make the deception subtle—specify what those features are and what the example must lack); the misconceptions the students will bring or form (for instance, that deception requires conscious intent, that factual accuracy is sufficient for honesty, that the problem is reducible to statistical bias correctable by more data, or that full transparency of model weights resolves it) and the exact point in the sequence where each should be headed off; a single closing question that tests whether they have grasped the conceptual distinction rather than merely feeling uneasy about AI; and what you should explicitly refuse to explain at this level and say so—particularly avoiding both technical RLHF mechanics and the metaphysics of machine consciousness. Do not provide a bibliography, reading list, or citations; keep the plan self-contained.
