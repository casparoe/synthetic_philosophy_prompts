I was trying to sketch out an essay for my blog about whether AI alignment is actually one problem or like five problems wearing a trenchcoat. I think the standard split is between outer alignment (specifying the right objective) and inner alignment (getting the optimizer to actually pursue it), but I'm wondering if we're missing a third thing I want to call "ontological drift" — basically the risk that as a model gets smarter its internal categories for what exists or matters shift in ways that make its earlier values incommensurable with its later reasoning, even if the reward signal stays constant. So my essay argues there are really three layers: preference alignment (what we want it to do), optimiztion alignment (whether it actually optimizes for that), and ontological alignment (whether its conceptual scheme stays stable enough that "what we want" still refers to the same thing over time).

I'm not sure if ontological drift is a real distinct failure mode or just a fancy name for inner alignment plus distributional shift. I also wonder if I'm overstating how much the preference/optimization split actually explains — like, maybe in practice they collapse into each other for large models because the specification problem is mostly about predicting what the optimizer will latch onto.
