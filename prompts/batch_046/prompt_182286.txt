"The analogical argument, originating with Mill and developed by Russell, holds that I observe a constant correlation between my own mental states and my behavior. When I see others behaving similarly, I infer by strict analogy that they have the same mental states. This avoids solipsism because it treats my own mind as one instance of a general class. However, later philosophers showed that this argument actually presupposes direct empathetic recognition of others, which makes Mill's inferential version obsolete. Turning to cross-modal behavioral coherence: this view holds that mental-state attribution is justified when an agent's responses are integrated across sensory modalities and temporal contexts—say, matching color reports to avoidance behavior and emotional reactions consistently over time. This is stronger than mere analogical parallelism because coherence requires structural integration, not just similarity. Both arguments share the weakness of relying on observable behavior to justify claims about unobservable consciousness. Yet the inverted-spectrum objection undermines them differently. For the analogical argument, inverted spectra are fully compatible with behavioral similarity, so the inference collapses. For coherence, however, an inverted spectrum is impossible: if my experience of red were actually green, my cross-modal associations—linking red to danger, warmth, and specific linguistic categories—would become incoherent with my reports. Therefore coherence escapes the objection, while analogy does not."
