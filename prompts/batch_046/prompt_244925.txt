Consider a cooperative dilemma among three normative communities whose foundational commitments are structurally incompatible yet not hostile: Community K treats the instrumentalization of rational agents as categorically impermissible regardless of aggregate outcomes; Community R operates with a hierarchical deontology in which duties are indexed to social role and can be overridden only by lexically prior obligations within that same hierarchy; Community U employs a pluralistic consequentialism that assigns lexical priority to the satisfaction of basic needs but permits trade-offs among higher-order goods. They must jointly administer a scarce resource—say, a water purification system—under conditions where unilateral action by any one community violates its own core norms.

Introduce the concept of a moral exchange rate: a negotiated institutional rule that allows each community to surrender a subordinate norm in exchange for the preservation of a more central one, thereby generating cooperative surplus analogous to economic gains from trade, provided no community is required to violate an axiomatic commitment.

Now evaluate the following five proposed arbitration mechanisms, each designed to sit on the compromise frontier—the set of arrangements where every concession is internally ranked as non-axiomatic by the conceding party:

1. The Rotating Veto Protocol: each community holds absolute veto over any decision that touches its axiomatic commitments, but must justify its veto in terms of its own internal hierarchy; deadlock is broken by temporary suspension of the least-prioritized subordinate norm of the vetoing party.

2. The Lexical Partition Scheme: the resource is divided into three domains—basic survival, social function, and discretionary use—each governed exclusively by the normative framework that assigns it lexical priority, with cross-domain transfers permitted only through unanimous consent framed as moral loans that must be repaid in kind.

3. The Translational Mediator Model: an artificial institutional agent, not being a person and hence not subject to instrumentalization prohibitions, applies each framework's decision rules sequentially, selecting the outcome that maximizes the number of non-violated subordinate norms across all three systems, with axiomatic violations treated as infinite-cost constraints.

4. The Axiomatic Firewall with Subordinate Market: core commitments are declared incommensurable and protected by an absolute firewall; all subordinate norms are pooled into a common normative currency and traded via a weighted voting system where each community's voting weight on a given issue is inversely proportional to the internal rank of the norm at stake.

5. The Nested Consent Architecture: decisions are made at three nested levels—local, interstitial, and global—where consent requirements become stricter as the decision approaches an axiomatic zone for any participant; cooperative surplus is extracted only at the interstitial level, where pairwise compromises can occur without global exposure.

Task: Rank these five mechanisms from 1 (most effective) to 5 (least effective) according to their capacity to realize genuine gains from trade—defined as cooperative outcomes that each community could not achieve unilaterally—without forcing any community into axiomatic violation or reducing its normative system to mere strategic preference. For each rank, provide a one-sentence justification that references the specific structural feature making it succeed or fail. Then, separately, rate each mechanism on a scale of 1–10 for deontic stability, defined as the likelihood that sustained operation of the mechanism will gradually erode the boundary between subordinate and axiomatic commitments within at least one community. Present your answer as a ranked list followed by the five stability ratings in corresponding order.
