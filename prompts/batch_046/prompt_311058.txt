I sit on a five-person municipal arbitration board that reviews development permits every other Thursday; we have forty-eight hours per case, no staff philosophers, and every decision requires all of us to sign the minutes. Our jurisdiction is limited to proposals that would cause irreversible physical change—demolition of buildings older than seventy-five years, paving over designated community spaces, or altering water access—because once those changes happen the prior state is not recoverable. I have no formal training in ethics or economics, but after several contentious hearings I have been trying to think through what philosophers call incommensurable values: situations where two goods, such as the historical continuity of a working-class neighborhood and the accessibility of affordable housing, do not seem to share any common scale by which one could be traded off against the other like dollars against dollars.

In particular, I keep returning to the idea that not all losses are the same kind. Some seem substitutable: if we lose ten units of green space here, we might gain comparable space elsewhere, and the loss can in principle be compensated. Others seem to involve what I have been calling structural rupture—when the change alters the kind of social world that exists in that location, so that compensation elsewhere does not address the specific loss. I have also read that in hard choices of this kind, rational regret is not a mistake to be eliminated but a feature of correct decision-making: even when we choose rightly, we should still register that something genuinely valuable was sacrificed. I want to turn these ideas into a reusable procedure our board can apply case by case, not just an intuition.

Write a step-by-step decision procedure—ordered as a checklist with explicit tests—that a non-specialist panel can run within our forty-eight-hour window and collective sign-off constraint. The procedure should include: (a) an initial filter to separate cases where values are merely hard to quantify from cases where they are genuinely incommensurable, using criteria that do not require economic expertise; (b) a test for structural rupture versus substitutable deficit that relies on observable features of the proposal and the affected community; (c) rules for when the board must reject a framing that treats both values as commensurable trade-offs; (d) a required format for documenting rational regret in our signed minutes, including what must be named and what must not be falsely resolved; (e) worked applications to three concrete cases—demolition of a 1920s public market hall for senior housing; conversion of a long-standing community garden into a parking structure serving a regional hospital; and approval of a technology campus that replaces a working-class social club with no direct housing benefit but substantial tax revenue—where in at least one case the procedure yields an approval that board members will strongly dislike; and (f) explicit marking of the points where judgment cannot be eliminated and where the procedure is likely to misfire. Finally, state clearly what this procedure cannot do—what questions it leaves open, which kinds of conflict it should not be applied to, and why it will not eliminate moral residue.
