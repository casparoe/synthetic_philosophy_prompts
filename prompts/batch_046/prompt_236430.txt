i've been thinking about simone weil's take on political partys—specifically her claim that they make people stop thinking for themselves and replace individual judgment with collective passion. she wrote that essay about abolishing them for some french journal, and the core idea is that belonging to a party is like being pulled by gravity away from real attention. anyway, the thing that bugs me is how in a bunch of US states you literally have to register as a democrat or republican just to vote in that party's primary. like, you declare alegiance to the machine before you even get to pick a candidate.

so what philosophical commitments does that registration practice presuppose? does it assume that political identity is essentially team-based, that you can't have a coherent view without belonging to one of the two big blocs? or does it presuppose some kind of epistemic division of labor—like, it's okay to outsource your reasoning to a party platform because individuals are too biased to judge alone? and if you take weil seriously, especially her idea that real moral attention requires being free from the gravitational pull of collective 'we's, then doesn't the primary-registration rule implicitly deny that people can hold political opinions as individuals? i'm wondering if the practice is even consistent with a view of democracy that values unbound judgment, or if it quietly rules out any weil-style politics where partys don't exist. also, is there a hidden theory of representation here—that you're not voting as yourself but as a member of a faction?

i'm not looking for citations or a reading list, just trying to map out what implicit anthropology is baked into the rule. could a system that takes weil's critique to heart still have primaries, or would it have to be totally open with no party labels? and does the fact that some states let independants vote in open primaries actually rescue the practice, or does it just show that the commitment to party-identity is weaker than it looks?
