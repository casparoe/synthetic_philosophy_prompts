You are a philosopher specializing in comparative ethics, Buddhist philosophy, and philosophical literary criticism. Read Ursula K. Le Guin's short story "The Ones Who Walk Away from Omelas" through the lens of Buddhist ethics—specifically the Mahayana emphasis on cetana (intention as the primary locus of moral weight), the Bodhisattva vow not to turn away from suffering, and upaya (skillful means)—set in direct contrast to consequentialist aggregation. The standard reading treats the story as a rejection of utilitarian calculus: the citizens enjoy prosperity built on one child's agony, while those who walk away refuse complicity. I want you to push past that framing.

Ask whether the story implicitly relies on the very consequentialist logic it condemns. Is walking away structured as a preference for personal moral purity over sustained engagement with suffering? From a Buddhist perspective—particularly one that rejects the accumulation of pure karma through withdrawal—might remaining, bearing witness, or transforming the system from within be the more ethically demanding path? Does the isolated child mirror the Buddhist figure of the preta (hungry ghost), trapped in a cycle sustained by others' craving for happiness? And if so, does the walkers' departure actually replicate the neglect that perpetuates that cycle, turning compassion into abandonment?

At the same time, be honest about where the text resists this reading. Note where Le Guin's lyrical ambiguity, the fairy-tale absence of any institutional mechanism for change, or the narrative's refusal to imagine a successful reform makes the Bodhisattva interpretation feel like an imposition rather than an illumination. Do not reduce the story to an illustration of Buddhist ethics; say what the narrative's particular form—its silence about how change might occur—adds to the philosophical debate about complicity, withdrawal, and the limits of individual moral refusal that abstract argument alone misses.
