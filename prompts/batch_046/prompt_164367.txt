I'm writing from the perspective of a student in an upper-level philosophy of physics seminar on time and relativity. We've been working through the Rietdijk–Putnam argument that special relativity favors a block-universe ontology, but I keep hitting a conceptual knot that the standard expositions seem to glide over. Most presentations move quickly from the frame-dependence of simultaneity—illustrated by the Andromeda-paradox scenario, in which an observer walking toward Andromeda has a plane of simultaneity slicing through events centuries in our future—to the conclusion that all spacetime events must therefore exist with equal ontological status. I don't see why this isn't just conventionalism about coordinate choice dressed up as metaphysics, and I want to understand the specific inferential step that is supposed to bridge the gap.

Could you explain, at an intuitive but conceptually precise level, why the absence of an absolute "now" is taken to undermine presentism rather than merely showing that "now" is relative? In particular, does the argument tacitly rely on a premise that distant events are real if they lie on any valid simultaneity slice, regardless of our causal or epistemic access to them? And does Putnam's formulation introduce a distinct problem about determinism—namely, that if future events are already embedded in the four-dimensional manifold, then the openness of the future is metaphysically closed rather than merely unknown—whereas Rietdijk's version is strictly about the impossibility of global, instantaneous becoming? Please disentangle the ontological claim of eternalism from the coordinate-dependence of "now," clarify whether the inference requires an additional metaphysical assumption about tenseless existence, and indicate exactly where a presentist should object if she wishes to resist the conclusion without rejecting the physics of special relativity.
