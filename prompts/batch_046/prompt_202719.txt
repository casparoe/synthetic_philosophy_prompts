Norman Malcolm maintained that dreams are not experiences — specifically, that no continuous conscious state corresponding to the dream narrative occurs during sleep, and that dream reports fail to meet the intersubjective correction criteria that apply to waking perceptions. Daniel Dennett proposed a cassette model in reply: physiological traces generated during REM sleep are assembled into a narrative script, which is then played back upon awakening, yielding the phenomenology of a continuous experience without requiring that any such experience took place during sleep. Student answer:

Malcolm's core argument is that for any mental state to count as an experience, there must be a logical possibility of being mistaken about it, and there must be some intersubjective criterion for correction. In waking life, if I claim to see a red cup and it is actually blue, others can correct me. But with dreams, if I report flying over Paris, there is no independent check: I am the sole authority. Malcolm concludes that dreams are not experiences but rather self-constituting narratives that generate their own content in the very act of being reported upon waking.

Dennett agrees with Malcolm that dreams are not continuous experiences during sleep, but he rejects the idea that they are mere narrative dispositions. On Dennett's cassette model, physiological events during REM generate a narrative script that is stored like a tape. When the sleeper wakes, the tape is played back, producing the phenomenology of having just experienced a continuous dream. So the dream is not experienced during sleep; it is experienced only at the moment of recall, though its content is causally constrained by REM activity.

However, Dennett's view still treats dream reports as reconstructions rather than descriptions, which means he implicitly accepts Malcolm's premise that there is no direct access to a sleeping mental state. In fact, Dennett's cassette is just a mechanized version of Malcolm's self-constituting narrative view, with the only difference being whether the assembly happens at the moment of reporting or during REM. Therefore, Dennett does not really answer Malcolm; he merely relocates the construction process.

Moreover, because Dennett denies that dreams occur during sleep, he is committed to denying that REM sleep serves any emotional-regulatory function, since there is no experiential processing to regulate. This makes his view implausible given clinical evidence linking REM deprivation to emotional dysregulation. Finally, Dennett's comparison of dream recall to split-brain confabulation is a category mistake: confabulation involves false beliefs about present actions triggered by external stimuli, whereas dream reports concern past mental events triggered by internal REM traces, so the epistemic structures are not parallel.
