As a retired experimental physicist who has only recently begun working systematically through philosophical aesthetics, I find David Hume’s essay on taste to be rigorously structured yet curiously underdetermined in its normative implications. I would like you to draft the joint statement that two opposing camps could both sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration on its interpretation.

The two camps are the Intersubjective Calibrationists and the Sentimental Pluralists. The Calibrationists maintain that Hume’s true judges identify a single, intersubjectively valid standard of taste—normative rather than merely descriptive—such that persistent disagreement is generally traceable to correctable defects in the faculty of sentiment (lack of delicacy, insufficient practice, failure of comparison, prejudice, or weakness in good sense). The Pluralists maintain that Hume’s framework describes how elite consensus forms sociologically, not how it ought to form; the “true standard” is a statistical regularity among a particular class of observers, and intractable divergence across cultures or individuals reveals irreducible pluralism rather than error.

Please produce a formal document with the following parts.

First, a numbered section of agreed claims. These should cover: (i) the formal architecture of Hume’s argument, including the five conditions he places on a true judge—delicacy of sentiment, practice, comparison, freedom from prejudice, and the exercise of good sense—paraphrased without verbatim quotation; (ii) the phenomenology of aesthetic disagreement, specifically that we routinely treat some disagreements as correctable mistakes and others as mere differences in preference; (iii) the circularity problem in identifying true judges by the very agreement they are invoked to ground; (iv) the existence of substantial cross-cultural and historical variation in aesthetic verdicts. In formulating these, you may introduce conceptual distinctions not central to standard commentary—such as the “synthetic connoisseur” and the “silent calibration” scenarios outlined below—provided they are stated in neutral language both sides can accept.

Second, a disagreements table. Each row should present: the Calibrationist formulation of the dispute, phrased so that Calibrationists would accept it as their own view; the Pluralist formulation, phrased so that Pluralists would accept it; and a concise statement of the precise logical or normative divergence.

Third, identify at least three cruxes—sub-questions such that resolving them would move at least one camp. For each crux, provide: (a) a concrete case, argument, or piece of evidence that both sides agree is genuinely relevant and not merely rhetorical; (b) the Calibrationist prediction or interpretation of that case; (c) the Pluralist prediction or interpretation.

One crux must address the synthetic connoisseur: an artificial system trained exclusively on the verdicts of acknowledged true judges, capable of producing aesthetic judgments statistically indistinguishable from human experts, but lacking consciousness, biological sentiment, or embodied experience. A second crux must address silent calibration: a neurologically atypical observer who consistently produces expert-level approvals and disapprovals but cannot articulate reasons, and whose judgments diverge from articulated norms in a predictable, non-random pattern. A third crux must address the resolution of long-standing cross-cultural aesthetic disputes—specifically between traditions that privilege representational accuracy and those that privilege non-representational abstraction—asking whether convergence should be modeled as the correction of prejudice or as the recognition of incommensurable but equally valid standards.

The document should be written in a polished, academic register appropriate for a working paper. It must not contain bibliographies, reading lists, citations, or references to scholars other than Hume where unavoidable. Do not include verbatim quotations from Hume’s text; paraphrase all relevant claims. The deliverable should be a constructive text that both parties could put their names to, with residual disagreement rendered exact rather than rhetorical.
