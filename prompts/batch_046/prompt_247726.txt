Following a long argument I recently had with a friend about whether rational agents ought always to split the difference with epistemic peers, I found myself recalling an even more intricate exchange I had witnessed earlier between two colleagues — Dr. Alistair Chen and Dr. Beatrice Alvarez — during a philosophy of science seminar. I should like an impartial adjudication of their specific moves, as I remain genuinely uncertain where the stronger position lies, and I do not wish to signal any preference of my own.

Their dispute concerned conciliationism versus steadfastness, but it quickly moved into somewhat specialised territory. (1) Alvarez opened by arguing that conciliationism is the only rational response to peer disagreement: if two epistemic peers hold contradictory beliefs and neither can identify a specific flaw in the other’s reasoning, each ought to reduce confidence proportionally, since the disagreement itself constitutes higher-order evidence that one’s own cognitive processes may have misfired. (2) Chen replied that this argument is reflexively unstable: if conciliationism demands that he lower his confidence in conciliationism itself whenever he encounters a peer — such as Alvarez — who rejects it, then the view undermines its own authority, and steadfastness must therefore be permitted, at least for methodological commitments. (3) Alvarez countered that reflexivity can be managed hierarchically: one may conciliate on first-order beliefs while remaining steadfast on the second-order principle that disagreement is evidential, because that principle enjoys independent support from considerations of symmetry and calibration; to treat this as special pleading, she said, is merely to confuse levels of reasoning.

The exchange then shifted to a more distinctive claim. (4) Chen proposed a “domain-specific peerhood” criterion: conciliation is appropriate only within domains where peerhood is genuinely established through shared training, methods, and predictive calibration against independent outcomes — science and medicine, for example — but not in domains lacking such calibration mechanisms, such as ethics or aesthetics, where conceptual frameworks diverge too radically for disagreement to carry evidential weight. (5) Alvarez objected that this boundary is ad hoc unless independently justified; without a principled criterion for why ethics lacks peerhood, Chen is merely insulating his preferred beliefs, and the burden rests upon him to demonstrate the absence of shared expertise rather than to assume it. (6) Chen defended the boundary by appealing precisely to predictive calibration: in domains where peers can be tested against observable outcomes, disagreement indicates probable cognitive error; where no such testing is available, disagreement may instead reflect incommensurable starting points rather than malfunction, so steadfastness becomes rational rather than dogmatic.
