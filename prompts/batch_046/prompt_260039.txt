Design a taxonomy—formatted as a four-level decision tree—for a graduate comparative-philosophy seminar that sorts interpretations of Jain anekāntavāda (the doctrine of many-sided or non-absolutist truth). The root node should ask whether the doctrine is interpreted as epistemic (about cognitive limits), metaphysical (about ontological complexity), or methodological (about discourse norms). The second level should ask whether every naya (standpoint) reveals a real feature or whether erroneous standpoints are possible; the third, whether the saptabhaṅgī (seven-fold predication) functions as logical taxonomy, semantic contradiction-avoidance, or pragmatic qualification; and the fourth, whether the doctrine is compatible with correspondence truth, demands coherence or pragmatic truth, or renders unitary truth inapplicable. Each leaf should be labeled either by its closest named Western analog—moderate perspectivism, ontological pluralism, fallibilist realism, epistemic relativism, methodological pluralism—or marked 'unnamed' if the combination is undefended. The instrument should include: (i) a brief diagnosis for every unoccupied logical combination, indicating whether it is incoherent given Jain premises, unattractive, or merely unexplored; (ii) an explicit flag of any pair of leaves that share identical answers across all four nodes and therefore cannot be separated by the tree; and (iii) a one-sentence statement of each occupied leaf's most awkward commitment regarding expert disagreement or cross-cultural judgment. All branch questions must be answerable without prior knowledge of Sanskrit technical terms, and no leaf should depend on citation or bibliographic identification.
