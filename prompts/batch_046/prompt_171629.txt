I was just in a long, exhausting argument with a close friend about whether the numbers should count in rescue scenarios. She was defending a position in the spirit of Taurek — that if I can save either one person or five, my duty is directed to each individual separately, so I have no stronger reason to save the five, since I cannot discharge a duty to more people simultaneously in the way I can to one. I think this is wrong, but I do not want to retreat to a bare utilitarian sum-ranking. Instead, I want a rigorous argument that the numbers do matter, constructed through two interlocking ideas I have been trying to formulate: first, a principle of parallel moral standing, according to which identical and independent claims on aid, when simultaneously present, do not compete with one another but accumulate structurally; and second, a non-nullification principle, which holds that to select one claim for satisfaction while ignoring the others — when all could be satisfied without violating any independent duty — is to treat the ignored claims as lacking moral weight, which requires an arbitrary distinguishing feature that is absent here. Please argue that in such cases we do have stronger reason to save the greater number, framing the reasoning specifically through these two concepts. Your argument should address the objection that this merely collapses into aggregate utility maximization, should explain precisely where Taurek's pairwise comparison of each versus each mischaracterizes the decision structure, and should show why recognizing parallel standing is not the same as treating persons as interchangeable units. Do not cite authors or texts; build the case conceptually from the ground up.
