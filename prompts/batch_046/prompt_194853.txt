Chang's concept of parity is designed to capture hard choices where options aren't equal but also not ranked. This is different from incommensurability, where values lack a common measure — like liberty versus equality in Berlin's pluralism. If two careers are on a par, they share a value domain but aren't comparable by weight. The IIA objection says that if A and B are on a par, and C is clearly worse, then preferring A over B shouldn't depend on C being present. But Chang can reply that parity isn't a preference ordering at all, so IIA doesn't apply. She uses a non-compensatory framework where reasons don't aggregate, which avoids the collapse.

However, there's a problem. If parity isn't transitive, then rational choice theory breaks down completely, because we need transitivity for consistency. Chang doesn't address this, and it suggests her view is just hidden incommensurability. Also, her appeal to commitment as generating reasons sounds like Sartrean voluntarism — she thinks we create value by choosing — but she's actually closer to Raz's idea of constitutive goods, where the value comes from being part of a practice. So the IIA objection fails because parity is outside standard utility theory, but Chang still owes us an account of how commitment avoids arbitrariness without smuggling in a hidden metric. I think she mostly succeeds, though she needs to be clearer about transitivity.
