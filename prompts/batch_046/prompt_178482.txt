I have no formal training in history or archaeology, but I have been thinking on my own about whether societies really "collapse" or simply change in ways we do not like — specifically the Bronze Age palace economies around the eastern Mediterranean, the Classic Maya urban centers in the southern lowlands, and Easter Island. I keep returning to the suspicion that "collapse" might be less an objective event and more a judgment about which institutions deserve to survive. I run a small community discussion group at a local library; about ten adults with no required academic background, meeting for one ninety-minute session. I want an in-room activity — not a lecture or presentation — that makes participants feel this problem from the inside before anyone names it.

Design the full session for me. Divide the group into three subgroups, each assigned one of the three cases. Give each participant a hidden "observer stance" card that they do not reveal: one stance treats centralized political hierarchy and writing or record-keeping as the essential core of a society; one treats ritual practice and monumental construction as essential; one treats subsistence continuity and stable population as essential. Provide each subgroup with the same brief, non-technical descriptions of what changed in their assigned case — for the Bronze Age, the disappearance of palace-centered redistribution and Linear B; for the Maya, the abandonment of southern lowland cities and the end of dated monuments; for Easter Island, the cessation of statue transport and widespread deforestation — but do not include interpretive labels. Ask each subgroup to vote, using only the criteria implied by their hidden stance, on whether their society "collapsed," "transformed," or "continued," and to record a one-sentence justification. Only after all three groups have presented their classifications should they reveal their stance cards. Then run a structured deliberation in which they must agree on a single label for each case, with the rule that they may not dismiss another stance as "wrong" — they can only argue that one criterion should outweigh another.

Include a simple, non-monetary payoff or scoring scheme: for example, groups earn points for internal consistency between their hidden stance and their classification, but lose points if they change their essential criterion during deliberation. Give me a minute-by-minute timeline for the ninety minutes, the exact materials I need (cards, a one-page case sheet for each society, a scoring sheet), and rules that prevent anyone — especially me as facilitator — from explaining the conceptual point before the group has experienced the disagreement themselves.

Then provide a debrief that maps each stage of the activity onto the conceptual problem: that collapse narratives often smuggle in prior value commitments about which institutional layer counts as the "real" society, and that without such a commitment the archaeological data underdetermine whether we are seeing failure or mere change. Tell me precisely what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this — for instance, participants trying to negotiate away their hidden criteria to reach harmony, or one person dominating with an unsourced historical anecdote — and how to recover without breaking the activity's structure.

Finally, be frank about what this design cannot reproduce: the actual archaeological uncertainty over population numbers, the multi-century timescales that obscure cause and effect, and the environmental or climate data that scholars actually debate. Do not ask participants to read anything in advance, and do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations in your design — everything should come from what is in the room.
