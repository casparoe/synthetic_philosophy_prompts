After a long argument with a friend about whether normative reasons can exist without any reasoner, I tried to formalize the problem and ended up with a chain that seems to collapse free-floating rationales into contradiction. I want to hold onto the idea that suffering is bad even in a mindless universe, but I cannot find the exit. Here is the argument:

(1) A free-floating rationale is one whose normative force does not depend on being grasped, represented, or possessed by any mind.
(2) If such rationales exist, then in a possible world W containing no conscious beings—only physical processes—there can still be reasons, because value (e.g., that suffering is bad) does not vanish with minds.
(3) Now, a reason is conceptually a consideration that counts in favor of some response or state of affairs; if there is literally no entity in W capable of responding, then in W there is nothing for the reason to count in favor of.
(4) So in W, the reason avoid suffering both exists (by 1–2) and does not exist as a reason (by 3), since there is no bearer.
(5) To avoid contradiction, the free-floating rationalist might say the reason exists but is dormant—like an unplayed chess rule.
(6) But dormancy is a modal property of rules within a practice; free-floating rationales are not rules of any practice, so invoking dormancy imports the very agent-dependence that (1) rejects.
(7) Therefore free-floating rationales are incoherent: they must be present as normative facts and absent as reasons.
(8) The only escape is to deny (2)—to say that in W there simply are no reasons—which means suffering in a mindless world has no normative status at all.

If I have to choose, I would rather keep (1) and (2) than accept (8), but I suspect something slips between (3) and (4). Where does this go wrong? Is there a stable distinction between a reason's existence and its application that does not smuggle reasoners back in?
