I am grading final essays for an upper-level philosophy-of-mind seminar, and I need a rigorous point-by-point assessment of one exam response. The exam prompt asked students to consider the scenario—drawn from the family of thought experiments discussed by Douglas Hofstadter and Daniel Dennett in The Mind's I—in which Einstein’s brain is replaced by an enormous book containing a complete functional description of every neuron and synapse, with human operators updating the symbols in real time so that Einstein’s body behaves exactly as before. Students were required to evaluate whether the book itself could be conscious, analyzing the case through three frameworks: functionalism, John Searle’s biological naturalism, and Clark and Chalmers’s extended mind thesis. To prevent rehearsed answers, the prompt added a twist: the updating is performed not by a central team with global understanding, but by a geographically distributed network of operators, each applying only local, context-free rules and unaware of the overall state of the system they collectively maintain.

Student response:

The thought experiment replaces Einstein’s brain with a book containing full functional descriptions of his neurons, updated continuously so his body behaves identically. From a functionalist standpoint, mental states depend on causal roles rather than physical substrate, so if the book replicates the relevant input-output patterns and internal transitions, Einstein’s beliefs, desires, and possibly even consciousness should be preserved. The system as a whole—including the operators—realizes the same functional architecture as a brain, which is all functionalism requires.

Searle’s biological naturalism denies this because consciousness is caused by specific biological processes in brains, not by symbol manipulation. Here the Chinese Room argument is directly relevant: just as the person in the room follows rules without understanding Chinese, the operators update symbols without grasping Einstein’s mental life, and the book itself only shuffles syntax. Because neither the operators nor the book possess the biological causal powers of neural tissue, there is no real intentionality or consciousness in the system.

The extended mind thesis supports the functionalist conclusion. Clark and Chalmers argue that cognitive processes extend into artifacts when they are reliably available and automatically endorsed. The book is continuously coupled to Einstein’s body and plays the exact functional role his brain once played, so it should count as part of his cognitive system. Moreover, because the updates come from a distributed network of operators—each knowing only local rules—the arrangement actually strengthens the analogy to a brain, since individual neurons also lack global understanding yet collectively produce consciousness. Thus the distributed nature removes the worry that a single homunculus is hidden inside the system, and it shows that biological naturalism arbitrarily restricts consciousness to carbon-based tissue when functionally equivalent distributed systems are clearly possible.
