I'm a software engineer without formal philosophical training, but after a long discussion with colleagues about whether algorithms exist independently of their implementations, I've been trying to articulate my actual commitments on abstract objects and mathematical knowledge. I think I hold something close to platonism about mathematical structures—when I design a sorting network, it feels as though I'm mapping onto a pre-existing logical form rather than inventing it. At the same time, I take mathematical knowledge to be essentially formal and syntactic: we justify claims through proof and derivation, not through empirical observation or causal interaction. Yet I also believe that in practice we only acquire this knowledge through thoroughly causal, empirical processes—running tests on physical hardware, debugging outputs, and observing behavioral patterns in computation. I deny that abstract objects can enter into causal relations with brains or silicon; a graph or a number doesn't physically push electrons around. Finally, I think evolutionary debunking arguments show that our intuitive mathematical judgments are unreliable guides to abstract truth, but I still regard reflective equilibrium—balancing particular judgments against general principles—as the correct method for refining beliefs about computation and correctness conditions.

These commitments feel coherent to me in isolation, but I suspect they reproduce a well-known epistemological problem for platonism, and I want to know how badly they conflict. Specifically, if mathematical objects are non-spatiotemporal and causally inert, how can my empirical, hardware-mediated engagement with algorithms count as knowledge of them rather than merely reliable belief-formation? Is there an unrecognized tension between my skepticism toward intuitive mathematical judgments and my continued reliance on empirical testing to validate formal systems? And does my view that executable code is just syntax given physical interpretation commit me, despite myself, to an anti-platonist position about algorithms?

Please audit this package: rank the tensions by severity, identify which pairs are only apparently in conflict, and specify the cheapest revision that would let me keep the idea that algorithms have objective correctness conditions independent of any particular implementation. Also, tell me what this package predicts for a case I haven't considered—if human topological intuition systematically diverged from formal proof in high-dimensional spaces, but the proof systems remained internally consistent, should I side with the intuition, the formalism, or reject the platonist framework altogether?
