I'm trying to sort out my own stance on commemorative justice before a seminar I'm leading next term, and I'd like a consistency check rather than a general lecture. Here is roughly where I've landed:

(1) In deciding whether to retain a statue or street name, we should primarily apply the moral and political norms that were actually operative in the figure's own era, rather than retroactively imposing our present-day framework.
(2) At the same time, if the figure's actions directly established or reinforced structural injustices that remain materially embedded in present institutions—say, legal regimes of dispossession—then there is an independent case for removal that does not depend on anachronistic moral judgement.
(3) The expressive meaning of a monument is fixed by what it communicates about our present values, not by historical intent; therefore, adding explanatory plaques without altering the physical form or location usually fails to remedy the wrong done to those who must live with the symbol.
(4) We ought to distinguish sharply between honouring a specific, bounded achievement (a scientific discovery, an artistic oeuvre, a narrowly defined political reform) and honouring a person's global moral standing; a commemorative object can legitimately celebrate the former while remaining agnostic on the latter.
(5) There is a weighty presumption against erasing commemorative names altogether, because abrupt removal tends to produce a kind of collective amnesia that obscures the historical pathways through which present inequalities were constructed.
(6) The authoritative decision about retention, removal, or renaming should rest with the democratic representatives of the community that actually inhabits the space, rather than with historians, ethicists, or external advocacy groups.

I think (1) and (2) might be reconciled by treating enduring structural harm as its own normative category, but I'm unsure whether (3) and (5) pull in opposite directions—whether the expressive-wrong argument actually undermines the anti-erasure presumption, or whether they address genuinely different concerns. I also wonder whether (4) remains stable alongside (2): if the structural harm is inseparable from the very achievement being celebrated—for instance, administrative genius in organising a harmful system—does the domain-separation collapse? Meanwhile, (6) seems compatible with everything else, yet I worry it might conflict with (3) if a democratic majority can simply vote away an expressive wrong that falls disproportionately on a minority within that same community.

Please audit this package carefully. Rank the tensions by severity, distinguishing genuine contradictions from merely apparent ones. For each real tension, specify which commitment must be revised or qualified, and what that revision costs—particularly whether it forces me to abandon the context-sensitive judgement in (1), the domain-separation in (4), or the democratic-deference principle in (6). I also want to know whether there is a single minimal revision that resolves the most severe conflicts without collapsing the whole framework. Please answer in British English, with specific attention to how these commitments interact rather than offering a broad history of monument debates.
