After a career in geophysical sciences I have begun reading in philosophy of history, and I find my old intuitions about causation colliding with a problem I cannot resolve without help. I want a definite, committed answer, not a survey of options, and I want it delivered in continuous prose only—no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables—so that I may read it as one reads a letter. My problem concerns when, if ever, a non-human entity—a germ, a climate pattern, or a volcano—should be treated as a genuine historical actor rather than a mere causal condition, and whether my judgments on this point are consistent or hide an unacknowledged environmental determinism.

Here are my verdicts. First, when Mount Tambora erupted in 1815 and its aerosol veil lowered global temperatures, disrupting harvests and altering literary culture across continents, I feel firmly that we should not call the volcano an actor. It lacked anything like intention or responsiveness; human societies reacted to it, and to grant it agency is to slide into the kind of determinism that writes human decision out of history. Second, when the bacterium responsible for the fourteenth-century plague spread across Eurasia and reshaped labor relations and state formation, I similarly deny it actor-status; it was a biological mechanism, and attributing historical agency to a pathogen strikes me as a category error, however profound its demographic effects.

But my judgments shift in other cases. The multi-decadal drought and cooling associated with what archaeologists call the 4.2 kiloyear event, which coincided with the fragmentation of the Akkadian Empire and the decline of Indus urbanization, feels to me different. I am inclined to say that this prolonged climate regime did not merely cause change but actively redirected historical trajectories, functioning as something closer to an actor than a background condition, even though I recognize it had no mind. Third, consider an engineered gene drive released into wild mosquito populations: if over decades it propagates autonomously across continents, altering disease ecology and thereby reshaping settlement patterns in ways no individual or government intended, I think I should treat that system as possessing a derived but real form of historical agency—yet this seems to contradict my refusal to grant agency to natural pathogens or volcanic eruptions.

Fourth, there is the emerging evidence that gut microbial communities influence neurological and behavioral states, and that over generations these microbial dynamics might shift collective risk tolerance or social coordination in agrarian populations. If such microbiome effects contributed to large-scale organizational change, I genuinely do not know whether to treat them as actors; my reluctance may be only a disciplinary prejudice left over from reserving agency for entities with nervous systems.

Is there one coherent principle that explains why I deny agency to Tambora and the plague bacterium, feel pulled to grant it to long-duration climate regimes and autonomous engineered systems, and hesitate over microbiome dynamics? Please state that principle precisely, identify which clause explains each of my four cases, and be honest about which judgment it cannot save. Then apply it to a case I have not listed: the 536 CE volcanic winter, which appears to have triggered both severe cooling and the first great plague pandemic, and tell me whether under your principle it counts as an actor. Finally, say whether your account requires a sharp boundary or allows degrees of agency, and defend that feature with reasons rather than leaving the choice to me.
