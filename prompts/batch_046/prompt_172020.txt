I have been thinking for some time about whether, if I had made one different choice long ago, that other person in the "possible world" is literally me or only someone very much like me. As I understand it, counterpart theorists say that across worlds there are only similar substitutes—counterparts—while defenders of transworld identity say the very same individual exists in other worlds with different properties. Rather than stay with the usual personal-identity examples, I want to apply this to an object that has both physical and institutional dimensions, using a scenario I have constructed.

Consider the deep-sea vessel Nautilus-7, registered with an international maritime body and given a continuous mission charter. Over a decade every physical component is replaced with upgraded equivalents, yet the registration number and charter remain intact—except that for six months the registration lapses through bureaucratic error and is then reinstated with an amended charter. Now imagine a counterfactual world identical except that the lapse begins exactly one replacement cycle earlier, so that one specific component is swapped out during the unregistered interval rather than during registration.

Please draft the adversarial-collaboration document that a committed counterpart theorist and a committed transworld-identity theorist could both sign after working in good faith on this Nautilus-7 case and the broader principles it raises. Structure the response in four parts, in this order:

1. Jointly Endorsed Claims (numbered list, about 300 words): claims both sides genuinely accept, including about the structure of modal reasoning, the fixed facts of the Nautilus-7 scenario, and what exactly needs philosophical explanation—without reducing the agreement to empty platitudes.

2. Precise Disagreements (table or structured list, about 250 words): the exact points of divergence, each side's view phrased in wording that side would accept. Separate any purely verbal disagreements from substantive ones, and indicate whether the dispute over Nautilus-7 in the counterfactual world is one of them.

3. Cruxes (bulleted, about 200 words): the sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one party—such as whether institutional continuity can ground cross-world identity when physical continuity is interrupted by an institutional gap, and whether similarity relations are sufficient without literal identity.

4. Tests and Predictions (about 300 words): for each crux, describe one concrete case or argument both sides agree is relevant, then record each side's explicit prediction about what that case or argument shows regarding Nautilus-7 or the general principle.

Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations. Write for someone with no formal philosophical training who has been reasoning through these issues independently.
