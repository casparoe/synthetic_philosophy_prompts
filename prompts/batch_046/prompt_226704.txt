Read Ken Kesey's novel One Flew Over the Cuckoo's Nest, with occasional reference to the well-known 1975 film adaptation where it clarifies a scene, through Ian Hacking's framework of looping effects and interactive kinds in psychiatric classification. Hacking argues that classifications of human beings—unlike classifications of natural kinds—can alter the behavior and self-understanding of those classified, and that these changes feed back to modify or stabilize the classification itself. The institution in the novel sorts patients into categories such as acute and chronic, and these labels shape both treatment regimes and personal identity.

Your task is a critical reading that treats neither the novel nor the philosophy as subordinate. Focus on Randle McMurphy's presence on the ward: does he trigger a destabilizing loop that forces the classification system to adapt, or is he eventually reclassified and contained in a way that reinforces institutional stability? Examine key scenes—the collective vote to watch the World Series, the unauthorized fishing trip, and McMurphy's final lobotomy—asking whether they depict patients actively reshaping the meaning of their diagnoses, or whether the power asymmetry is so severe that interactive looping is effectively blocked. At the same time, consider whether the staff, particularly Nurse Ratched, are themselves caught in looping dynamics: does her administrative identity depend on maintaining the patient categories she enforces, and does McMurphy's resistance alter her own functional role within the system?

Where does the novel resist a Hacking-style interpretation? For example, does Kesey present psychiatric classification as a purely unidirectional mechanism of domination, leaving no conceptual space for the bidirectional feedback Hacking describes? Or does the tragic conclusion suggest that certain loops can be terminated only by annihilating the subject who initiated them?

End by articulating what the work contributes to philosophical debate about psychiatric classification that a purely conceptual analysis—such as Hacking's own—cannot fully provide: perhaps an embodied account of what it feels like to be an interactive kind under institutional control, or a moral insight into the limits of adaptive classification when human agency confronts bureaucratic violence.
