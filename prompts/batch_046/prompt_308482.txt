After a long argument with a colleague about whether multi-level governance can be democratically legitimate while also addressing planetary-scale collective-action failures, I have tried to state my position precisely and now want a consistency check before I present it in a seminar. I hold the following six commitments. First, subsidiarity should be constitutionalized as a justiciable principle: courts rather than legislatures ought to assess whether a higher-level body has discharged the burden of proving that an objective cannot be sufficiently achieved at a lower level. Second, the American shift from dual to cooperative federalism—through conditional grants-in-aid and expansive preemption—was a normative corruption of federalism, reducing states to administrative agents rather than autonomous constitutional partners. Third, Germany’s concurrent-legislation framework under Article 72 of the Basic Law is institutionally superior to American dual federalism, because it preserves Länder initiative in shared domains and permits federal preemption only upon a demonstrated need for uniform living conditions, rather than freezing competences into exclusive zones. Fourth, genuine subsidiarity requires more than legal devolution: subnational units must possess fiscal sovereignty over their own revenue bases and direct democratic mechanisms—such as cantonal popular initiatives—since without these, decentralization becomes merely the downward delegation of blame without power. Fifth, for collective-action problems with genuinely global spillovers, specifically climate mitigation and pandemic preparedness, the subsidiarity logic should be inverted: constitutional design should mandate decision-making at the highest effective level and should authorize the preemption of local veto points, because decentralized rationality produces globally catastrophic outcomes. Sixth, within the EU specifically, the political safeguard clause allowing national parliaments to object to Commission proposals on subsidiarity grounds should be the exclusive enforcement mechanism; granting the ECJ authority to review subsidiarity claims would improperly judicialize contested judgments about effectiveness and democratic preference.
