hey, i'm writing a feature on long-range governance and got stuck on this argument about Bostrom's singleton idea that a source sent me. it feels like it proves way too much, but i can't tell if there's a hidden slide in the reasoning or if i just have to bite one of the premises.

here's the chain as they laid it out:
(1) if technology gets far enough that seperate actors can cause world-scale damage, rational coordination requires a single decision-maker—a singleton—to internalize every externality.
(2) that logic doesn't depend on scale; it applies wherever independant agents share a system and one can wreck it for the others.
(3) so inside the singleton itself, any department, committee, or even cognitive submodule that can act on its own creates the same kind of coordination risk.
(4) therefore the singleton must suppress all internal independance—no dissenting bureaus, no seperate minds, just one unified process with no parts.
(5) but then there's nothing left to coordinate. you've collapsed the very agents the singleton was meant to organize, so the concept becomes self-undermining.
(6) so either Bostrom's singleton is incoherent, or we reject (2) and admit local fragmentation is okay.
(7) but if (2) fails locally, why should it hold globally? that smells like a scope slide, but i can't pin it down.

where does this actually break? is there an equivocation between "can act" and "can cause existential damage"? or a quantifier shift i missed? i really want to keep (1)—scale and distance shouldn't dissolve moral or rational obligations—but i don't want to end up with a monolithic blob with no internal structure. what's the cost if i reject (2) instead?

also, there's this weird fringe idea i've seen called "fractal singleton architecture," where every layer is a singleton relative to the one below, like nested governance dolls. does that fix the collapse problem or just push it up a level? i don't need a bibliography, just want to know if that move is relevant to diagnosing the argument.
