Design a 75-minute structured simulation for a 16-student upper-level political-philosophy seminar on dirty hands, built around a conceptual twist you should call the "retroactive ledger": the idea that a dirty political act incurs a moral debt whose justification depends on goods that may never arrive through no fault of the actor, leaving the wrong committed but the promised redemption unrealized.

Divide the class into four administrations of four: one office-holder, two advisors, and one historian (who records but does not vote). Give each administration the same binary choice: a clean policy yielding a modest, certain welfare gain with no rights violation, or a dirty policy—calculated public deception that violates a procedural right—promising a transformative welfare gain. Before any choice is made, seal one outcome envelope per administration, unknown to the participants, containing one of four worlds: (A) dirty succeeds and delivers the good; (B) dirty succeeds but external contingency destroys the good; (C) dirty fails outright; (D) clean succeeds modestly. Lock in choices after 10 minutes of internal deliberation, then open the envelope. In a second 10-minute round, rotate historians across administrations so each reviews a case where they know the outcome but did not choose; they must assign "justified," "excused," or "condemned," treating identical actions differently based solely on the drawn world. Run two full cycles with different envelopes so every student experiences both decision under ignorance and judgment with knowledge.

The rules should make participants feel the problem before it is named: advisors should split between risk-averse and ends-justifying positions; office-holders should discover they cannot hedge without breaking the rules (if they try to invent hidden conditions, allow it briefly, then identify the move as the "escape clause" real dirty-hands theories deny); and historians should feel the instability of assigning the same verdict to the same act across different envelopes.

Debrief by mapping: the locked-in choice onto decision under moral risk; the envelope opening onto contingency that undermines forward-looking justification; the historian's rotation onto the fragility of retrospective moral accounting; and the advisor split onto distributed guilt. Note honestly that the simulation cannot reproduce the actual phenomenological weight of real-world harm caused by the wrong, nor the long temporal horizon over which political goods unfold—what the envelope symbolizes but cannot deliver.

Participants should leave seeing that dirty hands is not a static "ends justify means" calculus but a dynamic problem of incurring moral debt without guaranteed collateral, where luck retroactively rewrites whether the same act was justified or condemned.
