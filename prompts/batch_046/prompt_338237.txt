I'm preparing a discussion for a philosophy of mind reading group that meets next week, and we've been working through postdictive accounts of conscious experience—particularly the color phi illusion, in which a moving dot that shifts color mid-trajectory is perceived as changing color at the midpoint even though the intermediate position is never actually presented in that hue. We are also interested in how this bears on the temporal structure of experience: if our conscious narrative is assembled after the fact, does that mean the present is always, in some sense, an edited past?

Given all this, I'd like you to do something speculative but rigorous: imagine George Orwell—who died in 1950, well before this phenomenon entered experimental psychology and philosophy of mind—were presented with this debate and asked which model, Orwellian or Stalinesque, better describes the mechanics of conscious perception, and what it implies about the reliability of first-person reports of temporal sequence. Don't simply map Nineteen Eighty-Four onto neuroscience. Instead, draw on his broader commitments: his skepticism about subjective certainty, his analysis of how totalitarian power operates not merely by propagating falsehoods but by restructuring the very conditions under which a falsehood could be detected, and his concern with how linguistic corruption enables conceptual corruption. Would he find the Stalinesque model more terrifying because it removes the very memory of the intermediate state—making resistance impossible because there is nothing to compare against? Or would he see the Orwellian model as closer to the operation of doublethink, where the subject retains both versions and is trained to hold them simultaneously? Please construct a reasoned prediction of his position, including one specific implication he might draw for how we should treat eyewitness testimony or introspective reports in legal and political contexts, given that our perceptual stream might be assembled through one of these two revision mechanisms.
