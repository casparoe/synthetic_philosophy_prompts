At a recent seminar on democratic boundaries, two participants—Elena and Marcus—debated the composition of a proposed transnational assembly tasked with governing solar-radiation research. Elena defended an expanded demos built on the all-affected-interests principle: anyone whose vital interests are shaped by the decision belongs, including future generations (represented by trustees) and non-citizen residents of affected territories. Marcus insisted that democracy requires not mere affectedness but the capacity for reciprocal justification, so only present agents capable of deliberative authorization—current citizens and long-term residents—should belong; future people, he argued, lack the retrospective accountability relation that makes representation legitimate.

Elena opened by saying that if affectedness is not sufficient, then domestic democracies wrongly exclude infants and severely disabled adults who also lack full deliberative capacity, yet we represent them without controversy. Marcus replied that those groups differ in kind: infants are potential deliberators who will eventually hold representatives accountable, whereas future generations never can. He added that if affectedness alone determines membership, the demos expands globally and intergenerationally to the point of ungovernability—aggregation mechanisms collapse when there is no shared political identity to structure conflict. Elena countered that this conflates a practical design challenge with a normative boundary: representation of future interests is structurally parallel to legal guardianship, and the difficulty of weighing competing claims does not erase the claimants' standing. She also noted that Marcus's reciprocity criterion quietly imports a communitarian assumption—shared identity as a precondition for democracy—that cosmopolitan theories reject.

Marcus then argued that Elena's trustee model is not representation but administration: trustees for the unborn are not authorized by their principals and can be replaced without consequence, breaking the principal-agent chain essential to democratic legitimacy. Elena responded that all representation involves mediated authorization; voters rarely know their representatives' specific votes, yet the chain holds through institutional design rather than direct knowledge. She concluded that Marcus's real concern is feasibility, not principle, and that excluding the globally and intergenerationally affected transforms democracy into a cartel of present citizens imposing risks on others.

They turned to me—who had been listening—to referee. Please explain, point by point, where each speaker is right and where each missteps; then say who is more right overall, and identify the central issue or distinction that both sides are neglecting.
