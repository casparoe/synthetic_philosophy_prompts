You are a philosopher of mind and applied ethics with expertise in comparative cognition and institutional design. Before answering, think step by step: first map the main competing views on how consciousness is distributed across animal taxa—binary sentience, scalar phenomenal presence, domain-specific fragmentation, and organizational-complexity panpsychism—then examine how each interacts with the institutional case I describe.

Consider the following practice. Over the past decade, several major research-funding bodies and animal-ethics boards have replaced the old vertebrate-invertebrate divide with a tiered welfare framework. Under this system, organisms are scored on a composite index—nociceptive architecture, behavioral flexibility, and phylogenetic proximity—yielding a continuous welfare coefficient between 0 and 1 rather than a protected-unprotected binary. The result is graded obligations: full analgesic and environmental-enrichment protocols for high-scoring species such as mammals, birds, and cephalopods; abbreviated review for mid-range species such as certain decapod crustaceans and teleost fish; and exemption for low-scoring invertebrates unless the study explicitly targets nociception. The framework’s official rationale appeals not to sentience as an all-or-nothing property but to degrees of phenomenal presence, and it treats moral standing as roughly proportional to this coefficient.

Yet the same institutions retain binary language elsewhere. In public communications they celebrate the discovery of consciousness in species X as if consciousness were a milestone achieved or missed. In liability and conservation-law contexts they ask whether a particular animal was conscious at the time of injury to determine standing, with no gradation permitted. Meanwhile, the framework quietly assumes that phenomenal presence is globally integrated within an organism: it does not recognize the possibility that an octopus might have arm-local phenomenal fields without global binding, or that consciousness could be radically distributed across a colony.

What philosophical commitments about the distribution of consciousness does this tiered practice strictly presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory: must it be a scalar gradualism about phenomenal consciousness, or could a binary view combined with epistemic risk-aversion generate the same gradations in policy? Does the practice presuppose that moral status tracks phenomenal presence continuously, or can the coefficient be justified on purely pragmatic, tractability grounds without any metaphysical commitment? Examine whether the framework is consistent with its own binary legal usage, and whether it rules out domain-specific or fragmented consciousness by fiat. Then distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what merely fits well with it, noting any philosophical underdetermination. Finally, describe what institutional practice would have to change if we replaced the implicit scalar-global view with (a) a strict binary sentience view, or (b) a view on which consciousness is ubiquitous but moral weight tracks organizational complexity rather than phenomenal intensity. Be specific about which rules, review categories, and justifications would collapse or survive.
