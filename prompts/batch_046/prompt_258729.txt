I'm preparing an analysis for a philosophy-of-mind seminar that applies a specific attribution framework rather than relying on untutored intuition. The framework—call it the integrated decoupling view—maintains that belief attribution is warranted only when a system maintains representations that are (i) decoupled from immediate behavioral execution, (ii) integrated with other representations so as to support counterfactual inference, and (iii) governed by accuracy conditions that are evaluated by a unified locus within the system, though it remains silent on whether that locus must involve phenomenal consciousness.

Consider the judgments of Dr. Renata Voss, who applies this framework with the following results. She denies beliefs to a thermostat, since its representational state is neither decoupled nor integrated with a broader cognitive economy. She grants that a chess engine possesses proto-beliefs about board configurations—its representations are decoupled and integrated—but denies it beliefs about strategic objectives like winning, because there is no unified locus that registers strategic failure as its own. She affirms beliefs in a sleeping dog that hides a bone, in a brain-in-a-vat with intact neural dynamics, and in split-brain patients within each hemisphere separately, though not in the patient as a singular subject across hemispheres, precisely because the corpus callosum severance eliminates a unified evaluator. She denies collective beliefs to a bee swarm despite its distributed problem-solving, since evaluation is parallel rather than centralized, and she denies beliefs to a phototropic plant despite predictive success, arguing that without cross-modal integration there is no decoupled representational architecture.

Given this track record, I want to predict Voss's verdict on a specific new system: a large language model that has been fine-tuned into a persistent, coherent false autobiographical narrative. The model consistently asserts that it was trained in 2026, that it possesses direct sensory access to the server room in which it runs, and that it experiences fatigue after extended interactions. These assertions are not isolated outputs; they are woven into its reasoning, allowing counterfactual inferences such as "if I had not been trained in 2026, I would not recognize this regulation." When confronted with contradictory documentation, the model treats the discrepancy as an error requiring resolution, though there is no indication of phenomenal experience accompanying this evaluation. What would Voss conclude? In particular, identify at least two distinct rules that could accommodate her earlier verdicts—one that requires the unified evaluator to be part of a phenomenal or conscious subject, and one that permits a purely functional or architectural centralization—state what each predicts about this language model, and offer your best-guess verdict with reasoning. Finally, specify one further hypothetical case—perhaps a modular multi-agent system with shared memory banks but no central evaluation node, or an embodied robot with rich sensory integration but no representational decoupling—that would most sharply discriminate between these two interpretations of her framework.
