I chair the ethics review panel for a neuropsychiatric research institute; we meet monthly and have roughly thirty minutes per proposal. Our standard evaluation applies the four bioethical principles—autonomy, beneficence, non-maleficence, and justice—as a sequential checklist. Recently, however, we have faced studies involving patients with fluctuating capacity and relationally embedded consent dynamics, where the checklist produces contradictory directives: autonomy demands one course, beneficence another, and the conflicts do not resolve by weighting principles because the moral force of each reason appears to shift with the particular interaction of clinical and relational features in the case. Several members have argued that we should adopt a particularist evaluation pathway—grounded in the claim that moral reasons are context-sensitive in their force rather than invariant under universal principles—without abandoning structured deliberation.

Please construct a reusable decision procedure my panel can apply when a proposal triggers this tension. The procedure should specify:

1. The trigger conditions—what structural features of a proposal indicate that the principle-checklist is likely to misfire and the particularist pathway should be activated.
2. The ordered sequence of diagnostic questions in the particularist pathway, defining what counts as an adequate answer at each stage, and flagging the precise points where judgment is ineliminable and must be marked as a value decision rather than a factual determination.
3. The stopping rules: where the procedure mandates a recommendation (approval, conditional approval, or rejection); where it must suspend judgment and return to the full panel with a structured disagreement report; and where it stops with an explicit “no” because institutional liability requirements override particularist reasoning.
4. The self-acknowledged limits—specifically, the kinds of cases where the procedure will systematically fail or yield recommendations that conflict with risk-averse institutional norms, and what the panel should do then.

The procedure must be operable by board members without philosophical training and must not depend on citation of specific texts.

Then apply it to three hypothetical proposals:

A. A study using adaptive, AI-generated sensory environments to reduce agitation in non-verbal patients with advanced dementia, where consent is mediated through family surrogates and the potential benefit is experiential rather than cognitive.

B. A comparative trial of two palliative sedation protocols for patients with treatment-resistant existential distress, where reducing suffering may accelerate death in ways that are not clearly distinguishable from euthanasia in particular clinical contexts.

C. A proposal you design specifically to stress-test the procedure—one where the particularist pathway yields a recommendation that conflicts with the intuitive judgment of a risk-averse board, and you explain exactly where and why the divergence occurs.

For each case, show how the checklist pathway and the particularist pathway differ, where they converge, whether the final recommendation should be trusted given the procedure’s limitations, and what should happen at the points where the procedure explicitly hands the decision back to human judgment.
