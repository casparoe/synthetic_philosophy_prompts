I'm on the committee drafting this year's MA political philosophy comprehensive exam. Write one 90-minute essay question on Sen's liberal paradox, carrying 25 marks, designed so that a candidate cannot earn high marks by summarizing the standard impossibility result. Instead, present a modified framework—call it domain-relative minimal liberty—in which an individual's protected rights apply only within specifically designated domains (for example, personal consumption or religious practice) and are explicitly waived in cross-domain spillover cases (for example, zoning regulations that simultaneously affect both consumption patterns and religious assembly). Ask the student to construct a concrete three-agent, two-alternative example showing either that the contradiction between the Pareto principle and minimal liberty is reconstructed across these domain boundaries, or that it is genuinely dissolved, and to explain precisely why domain-relativization changes—or fails to change—the logical tension. Provide a model answer that identifies the exact structure of the reconstructed cycle or demonstrates consistency, explains the interaction between Pareto efficiency and domain-bound rights, and addresses whether the spillover exemption functions as an ad hoc rescue or as a principled restriction. Include a marking scheme with five must-hit analytical moves, the marks allocated to each (total 25), and a one-line note on the characteristic mistake that costs marks on each point. The question must be fully answerable by a student who grasps the conceptual machinery without relying on bibliographic knowledge, and it should contain no request for citations, reading lists, or references to specific texts.
