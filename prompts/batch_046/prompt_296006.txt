i was chatting with a friend about Kazuo Ishiguro's The Remains of the Day and we got stuck on whether Stevens is a moral exemplar or a kind of cautionary tale. i'd like to read the novel through the lens of Confucian role ethics — not just the generic idea that people have duties tied to their social positions, but the more specific tension between performing your role with ritual propriety (li) and having the humaneness (ren) to recognize when the role itself is corrupted. there's this Confucian idea, zhengming or rectification of names, that if a ruler stops being a ruler you shouldn't just obey blindly. Stevens treats being a butler almost like a sacred vocation: he suppresses his feelings for Miss Kenton, ignores his father's dying, and stays loyal to Lord Darlington even after learning Darlington hosted Nazi-sympathetic gatherings. so does his absolute self-sacrifice show what it looks like to achieve moral virtue entirely inside a role, or does it expose a pathology — a small person who mistakes the form of ritual for its moral substance? and when the employer's political role turns shameful, does Confucian ethics actually demand Stevens leave, or does hierarchical loyalty still bind him?

i'd want a reading that takes both the novel and the philossophy seriously: which scenes — maybe the conversation with Miss Kenton in the garden, or the moment Stevens realizes Darlington's disgrace — best illuminate the conflict between role-duty and moral correction? where does Ishiguro resist being turned into a case study, and does the book dramatize something about role-bound morality that the academic papers on Confucianism usually miss? i'm writing an essay for a course so i'm not looking for a bibliography, just an honest critical reading that can hold both sides in tension.
