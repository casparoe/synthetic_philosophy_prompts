In my philosophy study group after school, two members—Marcus and Elena—became locked in a debate about whether a human mind could survive gradual replacement of biological neurons with synthetic equivalents. I am writing to ask for a precise adjudication of their exchange, since neither will concede and I want to understand where the argument actually stands.

Marcus opened by defending what he called continuous-substrate independence: if each biological neuron is replaced one at a time by a functionally identical artificial unit, and if psychological continuity is maintained without interruption, then the same person persists throughout. He compared it to repairing a ship plank by plank, except that in this case there is never a gap in consciousness, so there is no moment at which the original person ceases and a copy begins.

Elena replied that functional identity of parts does not guarantee phenomenal or personal identity across substrates. She proposed a specific variation: suppose the left and right cerebral hemispheres are replaced in separate facilities, and during the intermediate stage the corpus callosum is temporarily disconnected so that each synthetic hemisphere operates in isolation, running its own integrated stream of cognition, before being reconnected into a single system. If both hemispheres claim psychological continuity with the original person, then reconnection produces not one survivor but a branching case—two candidates with equal claim. She argued that this possibility reveals gradual replacement to be a process that can generate duplicates, which undermines the claim that it necessarily preserves a single identity.

Marcus countered that Elena's branching scenario smuggles in an assumption that consciousness can be cleanly bisected during replacement. He pointed out that if the brainstem and thalamic nuclei remain biological and continuously integrated, they serve as a single subcortical core that never splits, meaning there is still only one integrating subject even during hemispheric separation. Therefore, he said, no genuine branching occurs, and gradualism survives her objection intact.

Elena then pressed a further point: by invoking an unsplit biological core to prevent branching, Marcus was no longer defending pure functionalism about personal identity, because he was now treating the location and biological substrate of that core as identity-fixing. She noted that if the brainstem eventually gets replaced too, Marcus faces the original problem all over again. Moreover, she argued that the mere possibility of a branching variant—even if avoided in practice—shows that gradual replacement lacks a principled reason to privilege the non-branching outcome as the real survivor. Without such a principle, she concluded, Marcus cannot explain why the gradual path preserves identity rather than merely producing a very convincing successor.

Please adjudicate this exchange move by move. For each of the four moves above, rate whether it is clearly mistaken, partially correct but incomplete, or clearly right, with brief justification. Then state who is right about which specific point, what—if anything—both parties are missing about the relationship between functional continuity and substrate independence, and whether Elena's branching objection genuinely undermines gradual replacement or only exposes a boundary condition.
