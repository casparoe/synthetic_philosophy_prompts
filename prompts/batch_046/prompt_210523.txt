The therapist keeps saying emotions are basically smoke alarms: they detect danger, they can be faulty or oversensitive, and you shouldn't confuse the loud beeping with the actual fire in the room. She uses this to push back against the idea that feelings are judgments or perceptions about the world; she says grief is just a bodily alert system going haywire after loss, not a claim that life is worthless now. I want to audit this analogy for the scene I'm writing, but I also want to avoid repeating some generic argument I've seen everywhere.

Can you lay out the correspondance table — what's the smoke, the sensor, the false alarm, the reset button, maybe the battery — in the emotion version? Then where does the mapping actually break? The therapist's using it to license the conclusion that grief isn't about evaluating the world, it's just a broken alarm. But I feel like the analogy smuggles in a design stance — like someone engineered the sensor with a purpose — and maybe even an intentional stance, since we talk about the alarm 'meaning' danger. There's also this concept my philosopher character would probably bring up, affective intentionality, where the feeling is already directed at the specific loss, not just a generic bodily beep. How does that fit in?

Does the conclusion survive — that emotions are feelings not judgments or perceptions — once these disanalogies are on the table? Or is there a better analogy for what grief actually does? I'm trying to write this so neither character sounds like a strawman, so I need an honest audit of what the smoke-alarm picture actually carries and what it quietly imports about bodies being machines that the emotional target might not have.
