I was discussing institutional commitment dynamics with colleagues in an informal reading group, and I have sketched the following argument for an essay I am preparing. I would value your comments on its coherence, its potential misrepresentations of the underlying model, and whether the conceptual distinction I draw actually holds up under scrutiny.

The essay examines the classic dollar auction game, in which two parties bid for a prize valued at one dollar, but with the twist that both the winner and the loser must pay their final bid. Conventionally, this is treated as a paradigmatic case of escalation of commitment driven by the sunk-cost fallacy: once a bidder has invested, withdrawal feels like an acceptance of certain loss, so rationality is progressively eroded. I want to challenge this interpretation in one specific institutional context—namely, repeated collective-action settings where bidding histories are observable and future cooperation depends on revealed preferences. My central claim is that, under these conditions, escalation is not necessarily irrational but can operate as a costly signaling mechanism. By continuing to bid and thereby accepting greater potential loss, an actor demonstrates high private valuation of the collective good, allowing the group to screen out free-riders. In this framing, the losing bidder does not merely suffer waste; they purchase credible information about their own commitment level. The argument seems to invert the usual normative evaluation of the auction: rather than a trap to be avoided, it becomes a functional screening device.

However, I am concerned that this reframing may illegitimately conflate strategic signaling with the psychological dynamics of commitment escalation that the original model isolates. Specifically, I am unsure whether the irrevocability of bids and the absence of a direct link between immediate loss and future cooperative benefits undermine the signaling interpretation. I also worry that I have implicitly assumed institutional repetition without defending why the short-term losses of the auction translate into long-term reputational advantages. Could you comment on whether this line of reasoning is defensible, point out any hidden premises about institutional design, and suggest whether there is a more precise way to distinguish rational escalation from pathological commitment within this framework?
