I'm preparing a contribution for a graduate seminar on collective responsibility and historical attribution, and I want a consistency audit of my position before I present it. Here are my commitments regarding the First World War and what it means to hold a nation responsible.

(1) I accept Fischer's thesis in its strongest form: the German leadership deliberately exploited the July crisis of 1914 to initiate a war of continental hegemony, treating aggression not as defensive necessity but as calculated opportunity.

(2) I reject methodological nationalism in historiography: I maintain that the crisis was structurally overdetermined by alliance rigidities, mobilization timetables, and systemic diplomatic pathologies, so attributing primary causation to Berlin's intentions alone is an explanatory mistake.

(3) I hold that moral culpability is essentially individual and intentional; collectives such as the German nation lack the unified mental life required for blame, and no aggregation of individual intentions constitutes a collective agent capable of guilt.

(4) I also hold that citizens in representative regimes can acquire what I call institutional liability—a non-moral, repair-oriented form of collective accountability for state actions that proceed through legitimate democratic channels, even when individual citizens actively dissented.

(5) I maintain an absolute separation between causal-historical explanation and moral evaluation: knowing that leaders intended aggression, or that systemic structures enabled it, never by itself licenses any normative verdict about collective responsibility.

Please audit the logical architecture. Specifically: does (1) commit me to the opposite of (2)—does accepting an intentionalist narrative about Berlin entail methodological nationalism despite my explicit rejection of it? Is the tension between (3) and (4) only apparent, given that institutional liability need not presuppose collective agency? Does (4) violate (5) by allowing institutional explanations to generate normative consequences? Order the tensions by severity, specify for each what must be revised and at what conceptual cost, and identify which single revision would resolve the most conflicts. Do not provide a historiographical lecture; I need an audit of whether these five commitments can stand together.
