I am taking a course on modern theories of liberty, and our seminar has focused heavily on the distinction between negative, positive, and republican conceptions of freedom. I have been trying to apply Pettit's account of freedom as non-domination to a situation that was not covered in our assigned readings. Imagine that a university introduces a proprietary automated grading system for final essays: the algorithm's weights and decision rules are trade secrets, students receive only a numerical score with no explanatory breakdown, and there is no mechanism for human review or appeal. The system does not stop anyone from submitting whatever argument they choose, so negative liberty is intact; and because it applies the same rules to everyone, one might think there is no arbitrary interference being exercised over particular individuals.

Please argue that this arrangement nevertheless constitutes republican unfreedom—specifically domination—rather than merely a deficit in transparency or procedural justice. In your answer, clearly distinguish republican liberty from both negative liberty (the absence of interference) and positive liberty (self-mastery or collective autonomy), and explain why the mere absence of actual interference is not sufficient to eliminate domination when an agent stands in a position of unchecked, arbitrary power. To develop the argument, I would like you to introduce a somewhat novel conceptual framing: treat the proprietary grading algorithm not merely as an instrument but as an institutional master that holds students in a structural relation of subordination, and defend the claim that genuine republican freedom requires what might be termed algorithmic contestability—that is, an institutional right to challenge, explain, and revise automated decisions—even in cases where no specific grade has actually been altered. You need not cite specific texts, but your reasoning should be precise and philosophically rigorous.
