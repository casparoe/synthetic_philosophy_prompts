I'm giving the toast at my mentor's retirement dinner next month. She spent thirty years as a clinician and also wrote about philosophy of medicine — specifically she was always pushing back against the statistical-normality view of health, the idea that disease is just deviation from species-typical function. She'd always say that calling something a disease already sneaks in values about what bodies should do, not just what they do do. The room will be half her medical colleagues who know the literature and half her family who don't care about philosophy of medicine at all.

Here's the thing I want the speech to do structurally: I want it to honor her view without making the family feel like they're being lectured, but also I can't just say "she was right about everything" because honestly I'm not sure I buy the full normativist line. There's also this specific case I want woven in — she recently was diagnosed with something that is technically a dysfunction by every statistical measure (it's like a very rare cardiac rhythm occurence that shows up in like 0.02% of people her age) but she insists it's not a disease for her because it doesn't limit her life in any way she values. Meanwhile my cousin, who will be there, has chronic fatigue that every lab test says is "within normal range" but obviously wrecks his quality of life. I want the toast to use these two cases — her statistical outlier that's "healthy" and his statistical normal that's "unhealthy" — to frame her career, but without resolving whether naturalism or normativism wins. The philosophical tension should shape what I can and can't claim: I shouldn't pretend science alone decides what's wrong with my cousin, and I shouldn't pretend her heart thing is "just a social construct" either. I definately need it to land as affection and respect, not as a position paper.

Can you write a five-minute toast? It should feel like something said at a dinner, not a seminar, but the people who know the debate should recognize that the speech is actually walking a tightrope between the two camps. Maybe include one small joke or light moment that works for both audiences — like something about how she always said doctors shouldn't confuse "normal" with "fine" and then applied it to her own medical chart. No citations needed, and please don't ask me to list her publications or anything like that.
