I have just spent a long evening arguing with a close friend about how John von Neumann might have assessed a neurotechnological possibility that arose only after his death. The scenario concerns cortical interfaces that do not merely read neural signals but actively inject probabilistic patterns into the hippocampal–cortical loop; over repeated stimulation, the brain weaves these artificial traces into its autobiographical network, so that a subject sincerely remembers events that never happened, with no subjective marker distinguishing the grafted memories from veridical ones. My friend insists that von Neumann, given his well-known posthumous comparison of the brain to computing machinery, would have treated this as corrupted input data: the brain is essentially an analogue computer with redundancy and error-correction capacities, and these injected traces are noise that ought to be filtered out, which means the synthetic memories are not genuinely mental content but hardware malfunction. I take the opposite view. I believe his emphasis on neural logic being organised through redundant, non-digital coding, together with his openness to self-modifying automata, would have led him to regard the brain’s own plastic authentication of a trace—its integration into reasoning, emotional response, and future planning—as constitutive of mental content, regardless of etiology. In other words, if the brain’s internal mechanisms validate the grafted pattern, then for von Neumann the distinction between “authentic” and “synthetic” mental states would collapse, and we ought to count these memories as real, albeit artificially originated. Please adjudicate our disagreement with care: set out, step by step, what von Neumann’s likely position would have been on whether stochastically grafted mnemonic traces constitute genuine mental content, drawing specifically on his known views about neural redundancy, error tolerance, logical structure, and the brain–machine analogy. Say where my friend and I each go wrong, if anywhere, and identify what both of us are missing. Do not supply a bibliography, reading list, or citations.
