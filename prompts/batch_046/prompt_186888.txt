For the past year, my forecasting group has been stuck on whether epistemic modesty demands uniform deference to outside-view base rates or allows selective reliance on inside-view reasoning. One colleague argues that whenever a detailed forecast diverges from the reference class—say, expert predictions in a domain fail seventy percent of the time—modesty requires suppressing one’s reasoning as probable rationalization. Another insists that this collapses expertise into statistics and proposes a distinction between “structural” predictions, where the reference class genuinely applies, and “diagnostic” predictions, where case-specific evidence changes class membership itself; but we keep recycling the same examples without deciding whether her distinction tracks something real or merely rationalizes overconfidence. A third member suggests the stall is conceptual: “modesty” is being used to mean both “defer to the crowd” and “acknowledge personal fallibility,” and the sides are answering different questions—one normative, one about social epistemology—under one heading. Is this disagreement substantive, about whether reasoning can ever legitimately override statistics; verbal, turning on ambiguous uses of “expertise” and “modesty”; or a case of different questions being treated as one? What—whether a formal result, an empirical calibration study, or a conceptual distinction—would count as genuine progress rather than another round of examples? Please separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who work on judgment and decision-making, answer in American English, and do not present any contested view as the consensus.
