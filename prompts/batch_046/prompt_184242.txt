I am preparing material for an interdisciplinary seminar on comparative sentience, and I have been experimenting with a conceptual metric that I call phenomenal density: the ratio of qualitatively distinct experiential dimensions an organism can integrate, divided by a normalised behavioural-flexibility index. Applied consistently across phyla, this metric yields a non-obvious distribution of consciousness — one in which evolutionary complexity and experiential richness may come apart. Please state your credence that, under this metric, at least one taxon traditionally regarded as paradigmatically conscious — such as corvids or octopuses — scores lower than at least one taxon traditionally treated as reflex-driven — such as certain bivalves or planarians.

Decompose your reasoning into four components: first, the prior probability that phenomenal density is a philosophically coherent and empirically tractable measure for cross-species comparison; second, the conditional probability that available behavioural-flexibility indices are sufficiently calibrated to avoid anthropocentric distortion, assuming coherence; third, the conditional probability that the qualitative-dimensional count for high-consciousness taxa is inflated by mistaking convergent cognitive complexity for experiential richness; fourth, the conditional probability that low-consciousness taxa possess hidden integrative capacities that raise their dimensional count. Give a specific number and a brief justification for each term, show how they combine to yield your aggregate credence, and identify which term is the weakest link. Finally, describe what kind of evidence — whether a particular neurobehavioural finding or a conceptual argument about metric invariance — would most substantially revise your overall figure.
