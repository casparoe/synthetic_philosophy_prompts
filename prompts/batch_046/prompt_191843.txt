My study group reconstructed an argument drawn from Keynes's well-known essay predicting long-run abundance and a post-scarcity good life, and it now seems to prove that this very good life is dynamically unstable. Here are the steps:

(1) Keynes predicts that compound growth and technological progress will eventually solve the problem of absolute scarcity, reducing necessary labor to roughly fifteen hours per week.

(2) Once basic needs are satisfied, he claims the remaining challenge is not further accumulation but learning to live wisely and well—treating work as purely instrumental and pursuing leisure, friendship, and aesthetic experience as ends in themselves.

(3) Keynes also maintains that sustained capital accumulation—the very engine that generates abundance—depends on a social ethos of saving, deferred consumption, and a psychological drive to accumulate, which is rational only when scarcity and future uncertainty make accumulation prudent.

(4) If absolute scarcity is genuinely eliminated, the rational justification for that accumulation ethos collapses, so the ethos should wither.

(5) Without sustained accumulation, the capital stock and technological progress that support the fifteen-hour work week decay over time.

(6) Therefore, achieving the conditions for Keynes's post-scarcity good life destroys the motivational and economic infrastructure required to maintain those conditions.

(7) Hence the vision is not a stable equilibrium but a self-undermining interlude: abundance makes leisure rational, yet leisure undermines abundance.

We think one of these steps must be wrong, but each seems defensible. Which premise or inference should we reject? Explain why it looked plausible—especially the slide from the psychological claim in (3) to the predictive claim in (4), and the causal mechanism in (5)—and then price the alternatives. In particular, if we reject (3) by arguing that institutional design rather than individual psychology can sustain investment, what does that cost Keynes's broader moral psychology? And if we instead reject (2) by insisting that productive work retains non-instrumental value even in abundance, how much of Keynes's specific vision of leisure-centered well-being must we abandon?
