I'm preparing notes for a graduate seminar on early modern metaphysics, and a colleague keeps deploying an analogy I suspect is carrying more argumentative weight than it should. She maps Leibniz's account of world-selection onto a modern multi-objective optimization procedure: the space of all possible worlds is the search space, perfection and simplicity are the competing objective functions, the actual world is the Pareto-optimal point that maximizes their composite, and compossibility is just a feasibility constraint that filters out coordinate conflicts—like removing a point where universal salvation and perfect justice are jointly unsatisfiable. From this she concludes that Leibniz's God doesn't genuinely choose in any volitional sense; there is only a deterministic computation returning the global maximum, so attributing moral responsibility for permitted evil to the deity is a category error, like blaming an optimizer for its output. I want to audit this analogy rigorously: provide an explicit correspondence table mapping each algorithmic element onto its Leibnizian counterpart, then identify where the mapping breaks down. Does the analogy silently import a value-neutral, mechanistic conception of computation that conflicts with Leibniz's rationalist theology? Does it misrepresent compossibility as an external binary filter rather than an internal logical relation among the essences of things? Does the determinism of the conclusion depend on smuggling in premises—say, about the uniqueness of the global maximum or the absence of incommensurable goods—that Leibniz might reject? And if the analogy fails in ways that undermine the conclusion that divine freedom is illusory, what would be a more faithful analog—perhaps drawn from jurisprudence, musical composition, or legislative drafting—that preserves both the rational determination and the non-arbitrary, genuinely selective character of the actualization Leibniz describes? Please separate the disanalogies that matter for her anti-volitional conclusion from those that don't, and say whether any version of the optimization analogy can be repaired or whether it should be abandoned altogether.
