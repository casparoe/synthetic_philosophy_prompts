I’ve been sketching a short essay on teh wrong-kind-of-reasons problem, but I want to push it into a corner that doesn’t get much air in the standard philosophy lit. The usual story is about belief: you shouldn’t believe God exists just because it’d make you happier, since belief aims at truth and happiness isn’t evidence. My move is to ask whether the same structure applies when rationalists talk about “adopting a frame” — like Bayesian updating or causal decision theory — not as literal belief but as a working mental model. If I switch to CDT not because I think EDT is descriptively false, but because CDT makes me sharper in real-world negotiations, is that a wrong-kind reason? Or does the distinction only bite for mental states with a truth-aim, and collapse once the attitude is purely practical?

My draft argues that Hieronymi’s “right kind of reason” requires that the reason bear on the question the mental state answers — for belief that’s truth, for a decision framework that’s maybe instrumental success. So practical success wouldn’t be wrong-kind for adopting CDT; it’d be exactly the right kind, just for a different attitude. But I’m not sure if I’m misreading Hieronymi — I think she wants all reasons for belief to be evidential, but maybe she allows practical reasons for other attitudes? I also want to bring in Parfit’s point about reasons being agent-relative, but I’m fuzzy on whether that helps or just muddies the water. I think the niche payoff is that rationalists constantly “adopt frames” for efficacy reasons, so if WKRP is only about belief, it misses half the psychology.
