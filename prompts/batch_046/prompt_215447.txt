The exam question was: Leibniz distinguishes justice (justitia) from charity (caritas) and argues that moral progress requires what he calls amor illuminatus. Explain the distinction, the cognitive-affective transformation involved, and why perfect knowledge of the moral law might still fail to produce moral action. Then assess whether this renders his ethics merely psychological rather than normative.

One student answered as follows:

Leibniz separates justice, which concerns distributive fairness and the rights of others, from charity, which is universal benevolence oriented toward the perfection of the whole. Moral progress occurs when an agent achieves amor illuminatus: a state in which reason directs affection so that the agent takes genuine pleasure in the happiness and perfection of others, much as a physician might understand medicine yet refuse to take it for his own illness. Without this transformation, even a being with complete knowledge of moral principles remains unmotivated, because knowledge alone does not align the will with universal harmony. Leibniz thinks this transformation is inevitable for any fully rational being, since reason, once cleared of confused perceptions, naturally directs the will toward the greatest good. Consequently, charity supersedes justice entirely: because universal benevolence aims at overall perfection, retributive punishment has no independent place in his system and should be replaced by corrective education alone.
