At a recent workshop on moral responsibility, two junior scholars—Elena and Marcus—entered a sustained disagreement over whether unavoidable structural hypocrisy undermines standing to blame. Elena presented the following case: a university professor who drives a gasoline-powered car, lives in a car-dependent suburb, and regularly books domestic flights for conferences blames a graduate student for taking frequent short-haul flights home to care for an aging parent. Elena argued that the professor lacks standing because she participates in the very carbon-intensive mobility practices she condemns; on her view, standing to blame requires that one not be guilty of the same wrong in kind, and the scale of one's own contribution is irrelevant—what matters is the absence of complicity. Marcus replied that this standard collapses moral criticism in structurally embedded societies, since nearly every agent is implicated in harmful systems they also oppose. He proposed a distinction between direct standing, which is the authority to demand redress for a wrong specifically done to oneself, and advisory standing, which is the authority to point out that a practice is wrong and ought to change. Marcus claimed that unavoidable structural complicity might erode direct standing but need not eliminate advisory standing, so the professor could still legitimately urge the student to reconsider her travel patterns without being a hypocrite. Elena responded that Marcus's distinction merely relabels the problem: if advisory standing remains intact, then the professor can still deliver blame in a normatively authoritative way, which seems intuitively wrong given her own behavior. Marcus then introduced a second distinction—between synchronic hypocrisy, where one's present actions mirror those of the blamed party, and diachronic hypocrisy, where one's past actions do—which he accused Elena of conflating. He argued that only synchronic hypocrisy undermines standing, because it reveals present insincerity or bad faith, whereas diachronic hypocrisy merely shows human fallibility and allows for moral growth. Elena countered that this makes standing far too easy to preserve: anyone can claim to have reformed, and without some principled accounting for past and present complicity, blame risks becoming a tool of the recently converted rather than a genuinely relational practice. In particular, assess whether Marcus's direct/advisory distinction and his synchronic/diachronic distinction actually solve Elena's problem, or whether Elena is correct that they only relocate it. Do not simply declare a winner; also explain what both parties are missing about the relational structure of standing itself.
