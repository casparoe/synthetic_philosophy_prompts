Consider Fernand Braudel's historiographical framework, which treats geography and climate as nearly immobile structures unfolding over centuries—the longue durée—while relegating events, individuals, and political decisions to superficial disturbances he called histoire événementielle. Braudel died in 1985, before the Anthropocene gained widespread scientific currency and before climate science foregrounded rapid, nonlinear tipping points in Earth systems. Today geologists debate whether human activity inaugurated a new geological epoch around 1610 (the Columbian Exchange and biotic homogenization, sometimes called the Orbis hypothesis), around 1945 (nuclear isotopes and the Great Acceleration), or only in the late twentieth century; climate modelers warn that processes such as an Atlantic Meridional Overturning Circulation slowdown or ice-sheet destabilization could unfold within decades rather than centuries; and some policymakers now propose deliberate geoengineering—solar radiation management, large-scale carbon capture—as an exercise of immediate collective will over planetary conditions.

How would Braudel likely classify these phenomena? Address three specific scenarios. First, the claim that the 1610 Orbis hypothesis reveals long-term ecological restructuring as always structural rather than evental. Second, a model predicting an abrupt AMOC shift within thirty years, a tempo far faster than the climatic rhythms he studied in the Mediterranean. Third, the prospect of intentional geoengineering, which treats climate not as a given constraint but as an object of instantaneous political design. For each, indicate whether Braudel would absorb it into geohistory, reject it as mere event, or be compelled to invent an intermediate category, and identify the principle—if one exists—that governs all three judgments.

Then assess whether his core distinction between slow structure and rapid event can survive these cases intact, or whether the Anthropocene forces a genuine revision of his framework. Apply your reconstructed principle to one further post-1985 development you think he would have found conceptually puzzling—perhaps the use of satellite-derived climate data to rewrite regional agricultural histories, or the argument that pandemic mortality patterns belong to biological structure rather than political decision. Finally, state how confident you are in this reconstruction overall, and single out the specific judgment about which you are least sure—whether your uncertainty centers on the Orbis dating, the tipping-point tempo, or the geoengineering case.
