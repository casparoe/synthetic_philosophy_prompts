I am preparing an extended essay for an advanced ethics seminar at my school on the boundaries of state coercion, and I have become convinced that the conventional framing of the Hart–Devlin debate obscures a structural feature of moral norms that should determine when legal moralism is genuinely defensible. I would like you to construct a rigorous, ground-up argument for the following intermediate position: legal moralism is unjustified when the conduct in question merely violates conventional moral preferences or causes diffuse offense, but it acquires limited justification in precisely those cases where the norm violated functions as a focal-point coordination mechanism in a society's shared practical framework. Specifically, defend the claim that Devlin's anxiety about social disintegration ought not to be read as conservative nostalgia or as an appeal to majority disgust, but rather as an implicit functional claim about expectation-stability: certain visible moral norms operate like Schelling points in repeated coordination games, solving collective-action problems precisely because participants treat them as non-optional and mutually expected. On this reconstruction, allowing unpenalized, publicly observable deviation in these specific domains does not merely harm feelings; it risks an epistemic cascade in which citizens can no longer distinguish genuinely shared norms from private preferences, thereby eroding the very background conditions that make high-trust cooperation possible. Your argument should establish three connected theses. First, that Hart's harm principle implicitly treats all moral norms as aggregable individual preferences, and therefore lacks the conceptual resources to recognize norms that have this irreducible coordination-structural role. Second, that Devlin's disintegration thesis is best understood not as an empirical prediction about crime rates, but as a claim about the fragility of shared expectations in domains where alternative coordination mechanisms are unavailable or prohibitively costly. Third, that this reconstructed view yields a sharp boundary for legitimate legal moralism: enforcement is warranted only when a norm's violation is both highly observable and structurally embedded in a coordination game whose collapse would generate widespread, non-localized social costs—such as visible breaches of contractual fidelity norms that underpin economic trust—but it is unwarranted for norms governing private, low-observability conduct, or for norms that can be readily replaced by institutional substitutes without systemic damage. Please develop the conceptual machinery without bibliographic references or reading lists, using concrete comparative cases—contrasting, for instance, the public destabilization risk of openly sanctioning fraud in small-scale commercial networks with the absence of comparable risk in regulating private dietary or religious practices—to show exactly where the boundary falls and why Hart's blanket rejection therefore overreaches.
