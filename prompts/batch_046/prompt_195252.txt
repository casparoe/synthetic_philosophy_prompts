Take the role of a philosopher of biology specializing in functional explanation and conceptual engineering. Before writing, briefly brainstorm step by step: what conditions must hold for a causal-role account to attribute a function that an etiological (selected-effect) account denies, while the physical contribution to the organism remains exactly constant?

Then construct a thought experiment that meets the following specifications precisely.

Domain: etiological versus causal-role accounts of biological function.

Specification of the case:
Build a realistic, present-day laboratory scenario—no science fiction, no hypothetical evolutionary futures—involving a synthetic biological system. The system must contain one component, call it Module X, that currently makes a causal contribution to the survival or reproductive success of its host organism in a specific environment. Because of this contribution, a causal-role analysis assigns Module X the function F. At the same time, Module X must lack the selection history that an etiological account requires for attributing F: it was de novo designed and synthesized in the lab, assembled from genetic parts that may have separate evolutionary histories but arranged in a novel configuration that has never been subject to natural selection for performing F. To isolate the variable, include a brief comparison with a naturally evolved analog—Module Y—in an otherwise identical host and environment, where both theories agree that the function is F.

Constraints:
- One coherent paragraph describing the synthetic case and the natural analog together.
- Realistic biotechnology context (for example, engineered yeast, a synthetic metabolic pathway, or a biomimetic circuit).
- Do not invoke artificial selection, directed evolution, or designer intent as substitutes for natural selection in the synthetic case; the absence of the relevant selection history must be genuine, not replaced.
- Do not appeal to demons, perfect predictors, or exotic metaphysics.

After presenting the scenario, provide the analysis:
1. State the function attribution for Module X according to each account.
2. Identify the method (causal-systemic analysis versus historical selection analysis) and the specific conditions each examines.
3. Explain why each account delivers its verdict, referencing the concrete features of Module X and Module Y.
4. Identify honestly the single feature of the case that does all the work.
5. Explain what happens if that feature is removed—i.e., if Module X had arisen through natural selection—showing that the divergence collapses and both accounts converge.
6. Include a brief defensive reply on behalf of the etiological theorist: how might they attack the case? (For instance, might they argue that Module X inherits function from its constituent parts, or that laboratory assembly constitutes a form of selection?) Note explicitly the weakest point in that defense.

Finally, conclude with a one-paragraph reflection on why this case is meaningful without requiring external literature: summarize the conceptual tension in your own terms, and confirm that the divergence is generated by the theories applied to the scenario rather than stipulated by fiat.

Write in a formal, precise philosophical style. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations. Do not quote verbatim from any published text. If you mention specific theories or thinkers, rely only on widely recognized designations and avoid precise page numbers, dates, or titles that depend on verification.
