In debates over whether we can ever identify why some nations are rich and others are not, comparative historians and economists often rely on an implicit analogy: the historical record is treated as a laboratory, sovereign territories as experimental subjects, geographic endowments or colonial institutional legacies as exogenous treatments, and long-run GDP divergence as the measurable outcome. Proponents of institutional explanations invoke divided territories or differential colonial settlement patterns as "natural experiments" that allow causation to be read off the record the way a scientist reads it from a controlled trial. Critics counter that history offers neither randomization nor replication, and that culture, geography, and institutions co-evolve rather than being separable inputs.

Audit this analogy rigorously. First, lay out an explicit correspondence table: what plays the role of the researcher, the randomization mechanism, the control group, the treatment dosage, the outcome variable, the replication protocol, and the procedure for holding confounders constant? Then identify precisely where the mapping fractures—paying special attention to the non-exogeneity of institutional treatments, the impossibility of holding culture constant the way one holds temperature in a beaker (given that culture is not a separable substrate but the medium through which institutions operate), the path-dependency that makes counterfactual replication impossible, and the fact that institutions and geography reshape each other over centuries rather than being applied to static subjects. Separate the disanalogies that are merely cosmetic from those that actually undermine the institutionalist conclusion that institutions dominate geography and culture. Does the laboratory framing smuggle in an unjustified confidence about causal identification, or can it be rehabilitated as a disciplined heuristic? If the analogy collapses, propose a more honest conceptual framing for how history can and cannot adjudicate among institutions, geography, and culture as drivers of national wealth. Conclude with a single-line verdict in the form: Verdict: <whether the laboratory analogy survives as more than rhetorical ornament>.
