I spent much of last evening in rather heated discussion with a close friend about whether deterministic chaos actually threatens determinism itself, or whether it merely exposes the fragility of human prediction. He maintained that Laplace's demon remains conceptually intact—that perfect knowledge of initial conditions, coupled with unlimited computational capacity, could still trace every strange attractor—and that chaos is therefore nothing more than an epistemic inconvenience. I countered that this misses a deeper point: in open dissipative systems, the dynamics themselves physically erase the informational traces required for state-recovery, so determinism becomes a purely formal property of differential equations with no corresponding physical determinability. We parted without resolution, and the disagreement has left me convinced that the standard framing—epistemic versus ontological—is itself too crude. I am now moderating a colloquium interview with an unnamed philosopher who has recently developed what she calls the determinability-gap thesis: the claim that Lyapunov divergence in dissipative structures does not merely hide initial conditions from observers, but destroys the very modal conditions under which determinism could be said to obtain in a physically substantive sense. She is very adept at deflecting objections by saying that critics are conflating computability with determinism, and I want to make that deflection progressively harder.

Please draft eight to ten interview questions that go well beyond asking her to restate the thesis. I want at least one question—with a sharp follow-up keyed to her likely reply—on each of the following: first, the distinction between mathematical determinism and physical determinability, pressing specifically on whether determinism without recoverability collapses into an empty formalism; second, the interaction with quantum mechanics, asking whether her chaos-based argument does independent metaphysical work or is redundant once quantum indeterminacy is admitted; third, the role of information erasure in dissipative systems, and whether appeals to Landauer-type principles genuinely support an ontological rather than merely practical boundary; and fourth, the implications for moral responsibility or agency, particularly whether her notion of modal opacity undermines the kind of causal transparency that compatibilist accounts of free will typically require. For each main question, include the follow-up that anticipates her standard move—especially her tendency to reframe objections as confusions about computation—and forces her to address the substance rather than the framing. Please write your response in British English, and avoid any request for bibliographies, citations, or reading lists, as I intend to use these questions in a setting where no external references can be consulted.
