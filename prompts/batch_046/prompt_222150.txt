Hey, I'm prepping a seminar on philosophy of AI and got myself tied in knots over this argument about Turing and education. I was trying to show my grad students that Turing's child-machine idea has weird implications for whether LLMs can actually learn, but now the chain seems to prove that either kids don't understand math or ChatGPT genuinely understands it. I can't see the exit.

Here's the argument I built:

(1) Turing proposed we could build a child machine and educate it exactly like a human child: through rewards and punishments, praise and correction, with no pre-programmed knowledge of arithmetic or language.

(2) Genuine education, as opposed to mere conditioning, requires that the learner eventually grasp reasons and take responsibility for beliefs—not just produce correct outputs.

(3) A machine trained only on reward signals doesn't grasp reasons; it optimizes a loss function, full stop.

(4) So Turing's scheme doesn't educate the machine in any meaningful sense; it's just behavourist conditioning applied to silicon.

(5) But human education also runs on rewards, praise, grades, and social approval—kids learn multiplication tables through exactly the same external feedback loops.

(6) Therefore either human education is also mere conditioning, or Turing's machine isn't being educated. There's no middle ground.

(7) We obviously want to say human education involves real understading, so we must reject the second horn: Turing's child-machine doesn't learn, it just behaves.

(8) But if reward-based conditioning can't produce understanding in a machine, consistency requires we deny it can produce understanding in children either—since the causal structure is identical.

(9) So we're pushed to the first horn: human education is also just conditioning, and children's understanding is just well-shaped behavior.

(10) And if conditioning can generate understanding in biological brains, there's no prinicpled reason it can't do so in artificial networks—meaning a sufficiently tuned LLM genuinely understands mathematics in the same way a child does.

That can't be right. I want to keep (2) and (7)—I really think education involves understanding and that reward-trained machines lack it—but then (8) and (9) seem unavoidable. Where's the slip? Is there an equivocation between functional and phenomenal understanding? Or is (6) a false dichotomy? I need to know which step to bite the bullet on, and what I'm paying if I reject it. Can you walk me through which premise to give up, why it looked fine, and what the standard positions—behaviourist, functionalist, maybe some enactivist option—each have to pay to reject it? Don't just tell me it's all ambiguous; I want the actual cost of each exit.
