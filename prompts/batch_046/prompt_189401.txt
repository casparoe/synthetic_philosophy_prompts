I was recently talking with friends about whether a society could ever justify harming one person to save a larger group, and someone mentioned a philosophical objection about the “separateness of persons.” I have no formal background in philosophy, but I have been reading around the topic on my own, and I find the usual explanations unsatisfyingly tidy: they tend to boil down to the slogan that utilitarianism “ignores that people are distinct,” which feels like a label rather than an argument. I want to design an exam question—perhaps for an upper-level undergraduate ethics course, with about 90 minutes allowed and an answer of roughly 1,200 to 1,500 words expected—that forces students to do more than recite that slogan.

The question should center on a scenario I have been turning over: imagine a public-health system that must choose between Policy A, which guarantees every citizen a lifespan of exactly 75 years with minimal variance, and Policy B, which operates a random “life lottery” giving 90 percent of citizens a lifespan of 85 years and 10 percent a lifespan of 45 years. The average lifespan is higher under B, but a specific subgroup bears the entire cost. The prompt should ask students to explain whether the classic separateness-of-persons objection applies to Policy B, and—crucially—whether the objection also applies if Policy B is restructured so that no specific group is designated in advance: every citizen enters the same lottery, with the 10 percent outcome determined only after birth by a genuinely random mechanism.

What I need is an exam question that makes students confront a deeper tension: if the objection depends on there being an identifiable victim who is treated as a mere means, does it dissolve once the risk is universalized and ex-ante equal? Or does it reappear at the level of distributive patterns, regardless of whether any interpersonal boundary is crossed? I want a model answer showing that the best response does not merely cite the objection but diagnoses whether utilitarian aggregation is wrong because it violates boundaries between distinct individuals, or because it permits certain distributive shapes that are unjust even when no boundary is crossed. Please provide the question itself, then a model answer of about 600 words, and a marking scheme out of 20 marks broken into five specific moves the answer must contain. For each move, state the marks it carries, a one-line note on how candidates typically miss it, and a clear standard that separates an answer earning an A from one earning a B—specifically, the B-level answers should be able to summarize the standard objection and apply it to Policy B with the designated group, but should fail to distinguish the interpersonal version of the objection from its structural or pattern-based variant, and should not address whether universalizing risk actually rescues aggregation or merely disguises the same distributive wrong. The difficulty should come from choosing this crux: a student who only summarizes the familiar objection should earn no more than a passing grade.
