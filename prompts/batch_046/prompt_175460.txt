I sit on the allocation panel of a public history fund. Our eligibility criteria require every funded project to centre on either a named state institution or an identifiable elite actor whose decisions are documented in official archives; projects that begin with an anonymous collective practice — for instance, the circulation of informal debt instruments in a single parish, or the transmission of craft knowledge through non-literate apprentices — may only be admitted if they are framed as background conditions for an institutional biography. We justify this by saying that without a named agent and a dated official record, claims cannot be verified or assigned historical weight. What philosophical commitments about evidence, agency, and the ontology of historical change does this framework presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory: does it rest on an epistemic preference for documentary traceability, a metaphysical claim that only intentional state action constitutes historical causation, or a normative view that history's purpose is to assign responsibility to identifiable actors? Assess whether this is the only theory that could licence our rules, or whether rival accounts — for example, one that treats structural patterns or collective experience as primary objects of historical knowledge — could justify a different selection of sources without collapsing into unverifiability. Then set out, in practical terms, what a grant scheme genuinely committed to history from below and microhistorical method would have to alter: not merely in subject matter, but in its evidentiary standards, peer-review criteria, and the kind of narrative coherence it demands of applicants. Please write your answer in British English.
