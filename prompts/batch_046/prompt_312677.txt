At a planning meeting, two councillors, Amara and Ben, disagreed over whether to complete a half-built arts centre. The city had already spent £42 million on a bespoke design that turned out to have serious acoustic flaws; finishing it would cost another £28 million and still yield a substandard venue, whereas demolishing it (£8 million) and building a conventional one (£22 million) would cost £30 million total and perform far better. Amara argued that the £42 million is irrecoverable and must be ignored: only future costs and benefits matter, so demolition is clearly the rational course. Ben replied that this treats the council like a solitary utility calculator and ignores how past commitments restructure present options for a collective body. The expenditure had created contractual obligations to local contractors, planning permissions tied to this specific site, and public expectations of follow-through; abandoning the project would damage institutional credibility and the capacity to undertake long-term partnerships. Amara conceded that those are real future costs of withdrawal, but insisted they are distinct from the sunk cost itself—once you account for credibility damage and contract penalties, you are weighing new consequences, not honouring past spending. Ben countered that for an institution, past investment is partly constitutive of its present identity: the project is “ours” precisely because of that £42 million, and treating it as disposable severs the narrative continuity that makes collective agency possible. He added that people who finish long, difficult projects often do so not because they miscalculate, but because being a finisher is a second-order value that depends on having already invested. Amara responded that this merely relabels the mistake—if the value lies in reliability or narrative coherence, then that value supplies the reason, not the past pounds spent—so Ben has not shown that the sunk expenditure itself carries any rational weight. Ben then asked whether Amara’s view implies that a government should never complete a flawed treaty or infrastructure project once flaws appear, even when breaking it would destroy institutional trust that no future project could easily rebuild. They turned to me, an observer, and asked me to referee.

Please adjudicate point by point: where is Amara correct, where is Ben correct, and what is each missing? In particular, does Ben succeed in showing that sunk costs can themselves supply reasons for collective agents, or has Amara shown that he is always smuggling in independent future values? And who is more right overall?
