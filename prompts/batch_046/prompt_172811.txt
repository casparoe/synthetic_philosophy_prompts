Do not begin with an overall verdict; instead, deliver a point-by-point assessment that assigns specific credit or deduction for each conceptual move, and conclude with a single letter grade (A through F) and a numerical score out of 100. Your comments should attend especially to the distinction between Quine’s circularity objection to synonymy-based definitions and his separate concern about semantic rules in natural language, and they should flag any misapplication of Quinean holism to formal systems or any confusion about whether resolving the first dogma bears on the second.

Question: In Two Dogmas of Empiricism, Quine attacks the analytic-synthetic distinction through several interrelated arguments. Focus specifically on the circularity that arises when analysts try to ground analyticity in synonymy, and on the distinct problem Quine raises for attempts to ground it in explicit semantic rules. Now consider this rescue proposal: an analytic statement is one that is true solely in virtue of the meanings conventionally assigned to its non-logical constants, where those assignments are made by direct stipulation in a formal metalanguage that does not presuppose any prior notion of analyticity or synonymy. Would this proposal escape Quine’s objections? In your answer, distinguish (i) whether direct stipulation avoids the circularity problem associated with synonymy, (ii) whether it addresses Quine’s concerns about semantic rules in natural language, and (iii) whether resolving the first dogma in this way has any bearing on Quine’s second dogma of reductionism, keeping in mind his claim that the two dogmas are identical at root.

Student Answer:
Quine’s central objection to synonymy-based definitions is that explaining synonymy—whether through definition, interchangeability salva veritate, or through semantic rules—already presupposes the notion of analyticity that is meant to be explained, so the account is circular. The direct-stipulation proposal escapes this because it assigns meanings to non-logical constants in a metalanguage by explicit fiat, without invoking synonymy at all. So on point (i) the proposal succeeds.

On point (ii), Quine complains that natural language lacks explicit semantic rules and that any reconstructed rules depend on prior judgments about which sentences are analytic. By placing the assignments in a formal metalanguage where they are literally listed, the proposal removes this worry: the rules are present rather than reconstructed. So it answers part (ii) as well.

On point (iii), Quine’s second dogma is reductionism—the thesis that every meaningful statement can be translated into statements about immediate sensory experience. Direct stipulation does not provide such translations, but it does maintain the analytic-synthetic distinction for artificial languages. Quine, however, argues that the two dogmas are identical at root because both depend on the false idea that individual sentences can be evaluated in isolation for their empirical or conventional status. Since direct stipulation assigns conventional status sentence-by-sentence through the constant-assignments, it actually preserves this isolationist picture. Moreover, Quine’s holism about belief revision applies even here: if we revise one stipulated assignment, consistency demands that we revise others, so the metalanguage is not stable. Therefore rescuing the first dogma through stipulation does not help reductionism; it repeats the same mistake in formal dress. The correct Quinean conclusion is that both dogmas fall together, because even in a metalanguage we cannot isolate the empirical content of individual statements.
