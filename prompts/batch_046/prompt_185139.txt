I'm putting together notes for a philosophy-of-literature seminar I'm teaching next fall, and I keep circling back to Macbeth. I want to read it through Aquinas's framework on tyrannicide -- specifcally the distinction he draws between a ruler who usurps power illegitimately and one who abuses authority he actually holds, plus his insistence that resistance must come from public authority rather than private hands, since private killing is basically sedition. The play gives us both moves: Macbeth kills Duncan, a legit king, and then Macduff kills Macbeth with Malcolm's army behind him, but Macduff is also driven by personal grief since Macbeth murdered his family.

So I'm stuck on whether Shakespeare is actually dramatizing the breakdown of Aquinas's neat seperation between public duty and private vengeance. Even if Malcolm provides the public authority, Macduff's personal rage is still doing the actual sword work. Does the play suggest that political violence is always contaminated by private motive, which would make Aquinas's conditions impossible to satisfy in practice? And what about Duncan's death -- Macbeth is clearly a usurper, but the play never asks whether anyone had a right to stop him before he seized the crown. I'd like a reading that takes both the play's scenes and Aquinas's conditions seriously, and says whether Macbeth ends up more pessimistic about resistance than Aquinas allows. No citations needed, just whether the work adds something the argument alone misses.
