I serve on an institutional ethics review panel at a large research university, and we are required to evaluate roughly a dozen proposals each term for field studies conducted in low-resource settings. These studies are routinely justified by consequentialist reasoning—appeals to aggregate welfare gains, long-term public health outcomes, or the maximisation of knowledge—yet they frequently impose substantial burdens on participants: extended time commitments, emotional disclosure, restricted behaviours, or opportunity costs that disrupt family and livelihood structures. To prevent these reviews from collapsing into unstructured intuition or pure cost-benefit arithmetic, the panel wishes to adopt a reusable decision procedure grounded in the philosophical literature on demandingness objections to consequentialism. The procedure must be operable by panellists without philosophical training, executable within a thirty-minute review session, and transparent enough that a rejected applicant or an approving chair can see which step determined the outcome.

Please construct this procedure as an ordered sequence of questions or tests, specifying for each stage what counts as a sufficient answer, where the procedure terminates with a clear recommendation (approval, conditional approval, or rejection), and where it must explicitly defer to a value judgement that cannot be eliminated by further factual inquiry. The procedure should preserve the key conceptual distinctions that demandingness objections rely upon—particularly the difference between costs that merely burden an agent and costs that undermine the projects and relationships that structure a meaningful life, and the distinction between comparative demandingness (relative to what others do) and absolute demandingness (relative to a fixed baseline of moral expectation)—while allowing these distinctions to be applied without requiring panellists to master the underlying theory. It should also identify the point at which consequentialist calculation becomes insufficient and a non-consequentialist constraint takes precedence.

Then apply the procedure to three hypothetical proposals: first, a six-month nutritional intervention requiring daily dietary logging and restricted caloric intake among food-insecure households, justified by aggregate epidemiological benefit but offering minimal direct gain to participants; second, a twelve-week psychological study requiring trauma survivors to repeatedly recount distressing experiences with no therapeutic component, defended on the grounds that the data will improve future clinical protocols; and third, a community health trial demanding moderate but sustained behavioural change that yields clear personal health benefits for participants but negligible broader welfare impact. Include at least one case in which the procedure produces an answer that will feel unwelcome—perhaps endorsing a study with strong consequentialist credentials but severe personal costs, or rejecting one that appears intuitively acceptable. Finally, state explicitly what the procedure cannot do: for example, resolving foundational disputes about the correct baseline of moral expectation, substituting for democratic deliberation about acceptable risk, or determining whether institutional actors face the same demandingness constraints as individual moral agents.

Please present your answer in British English, using spellings such as 'programme', 'behavioural', and 'judgement' where appropriate.
