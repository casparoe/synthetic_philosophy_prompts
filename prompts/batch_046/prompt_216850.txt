I'm drafting a proposal for a one-semester philosophy-of-mind term paper—roughly five thousand words—on pain asymbolia and whether the standard debate about pain's badness has missed a distinction that the clinical phenomenon actually supports. Pain asymbolia, as I understand it, is the condition in which subjects report feeling pain with apparently normal qualitative character but show little or no aversive behavior, emotional distress, or motivation to avoid or terminate the stimulus. The philosophical literature usually treats this as a direct test case between intrinsic badness views, which hold that the suffering is present but behaviorally masked, and functionalist or motivational views, which hold that without the aversive role the badness is absent or severely diminished. What strikes me is that both sides treat decoupling as a single axis—sensation separated from reaction—when there may be at least two separable failures: one in which the affective valence is retained but the prudential force is lost, so the subject feels the pain is unpleasant yet sees no reason to avoid it; and one in which the affective coloring is flattened but instrumental avoidance remains intact, driven by habit or social expectation rather than felt urgency. If these can come apart, then asymbolia does not settle the intrinsic-versus-functional dispute so much as reveal that "the badness of pain" is not a unitary property.

Write the proposal. State the precise research question: can a taxonomy of decoupling types in asymbolia be constructed that distinguishes affective decoupling from prudential decoupling, and does this taxonomy show that the intrinsic-versus-functional debate rests on an oversimplified model of what is being lost? Explain why existing philosophical and clinical treatments leave this open—namely, that they tend to describe asymbolia as a uniform dissociation between pain sensation and pain reaction without examining whether the retained elements are qualitative, emotional, or normative. Describe the method: conceptual analysis of the phenomenological structure of reported cases, paired with a distinction-drawing exercise that maps possible decoupling combinations onto competing theories of pain's badness, rather than an empirical literature review or clinical study. Say what a useful result would look like: a three-part taxonomy with boundary conditions, an argument that at least one decoupling type is conceptually coherent and clinically distinguishable, and an assessment of whether any version of intrinsic badness survives the separation. Also state what would count as failure—if the two decoupling types collapse into one another, if reported phenomena are too ambiguous to support the distinctions, or if the taxonomy adds no explanatory power over the standard single-axis model. Keep the scope realistic for one semester, avoid any request for bibliographies or citation lists, and do not invent specific clinical studies, patient names, or direct quotations; refer to reported phenomena in general terms only.
