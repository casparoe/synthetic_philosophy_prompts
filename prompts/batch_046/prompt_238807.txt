I have recently become the primary caregiver for my mother, who has progressive dementia, and was simultaneously offered a two-year research fellowship abroad that I turned down to remain near her. This collision of obligations has made me realize that I hold strong but apparently inconsistent views about incommensurable values, and I want to see whether they can be unified. You are a professional philosopher specializing in practical reason and value pluralism. Before answering, briefly list the tensions you see among the cases below, and think step-by-step about whether simplicity or completeness should prevail.

I tentatively propose a principle of domain-specific lexical ordering combined with a personal-commitment override: within each domain—survival, autonomy, fidelity, preservation—values are lexically ranked, but across domains there is no common metric, except that once one has voluntarily assumed a specific, non-transferable duty (such as direct caregiving), that duty acquires a weight for the agent that does not automatically bind others in structurally similar positions.

Here are my verdicts. (1) Funding a medical trial that saves 100 lives is better than preserving a unique historical manuscript archive, yet the loss of the archive is a genuine tragedy rather than a merely smaller quantity of good. (2) Forcing a child to practice an instrument is wrong if the child genuinely lacks aptitude and suffers, but permissible if the resistance is mere laziness and long-term flourishing is genuinely at stake—so autonomy does not always win. (3) Telling a dying friend that their estranged son never called, purely to comfort them, is wrong; disclosure is required only when a practical decision (e.g., writing a will) depends on the fact. (4) I turned down my fellowship and judge my sibling wrong for accepting a comparable opportunity despite having an aging parent—yet I concede I cannot universalize this judgment, since I do not think all caregivers in analogous situations must refuse all career advances. (5) Cloning a deceased pet to extend companionship is wrong, because it instrumentalizes a being as replaceable, but using genetic screening to prevent a hereditary disease in a future child is permissible—both involve biological intervention, yet they feel categorically different.

Is there a coherent principle—perhaps a repaired version of my lexical-ordering idea with the personal-commitment clause—that captures all five verdicts? If not, state precisely which verdict or pair must be dropped in favor of simplicity, predict your principle’s verdict on a case I have not listed (a physician who must choose between performing one risky but innovative surgery with uncertain success versus administering a standard treatment to ten patients with the same prognosis), and be honest about where your reply is weakest. Keep your response to no more than 400 words.
