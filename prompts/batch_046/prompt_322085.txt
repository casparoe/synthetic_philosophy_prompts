As a software engineer without formal philosophical training, I have been trying to formalize a personal view about suffering, pleasure, and rational choice—partly by analogy to system-state monitoring, where certain failure modes are categorically different from ordinary performance degradation. I want to know whether the following commitments hang together, or whether I am implicitly helping myself to incompatible premises.

Here are the five theses I currently hold:

(1) I accept a lexical-threshold form of negative utilitarianism. There exists a qualitative severity level of suffering—analogous to a critical system failure that destroys operational coherence—such that preventing any instance of suffering at or above that level takes absolute priority over producing any amount of pleasure, regardless of scale. Below that threshold, however, suffering and pleasure enter into ordinary quantitative trade-offs.

(2) I deny that pleasure and pain are commensurable on a single quantitative axis. They are phenomenologically distinct states, more like different sensory or feedback modalities (e.g., thermal stress versus structural integrity loss) than opposite values on one thermometer. Consequently, there is no universal "hedonic currency" that permits direct conversion between units of pleasure and units of pain.

(3) I hold that rational choice should maximize a vector-valued welfare function rather than a scalar. The lexically dominant first coordinate is the reduction of above-threshold suffering; the remaining coordinates are reduction of sub-threshold suffering and promotion of pleasure, ordered by some secondary principle.

(4) The threshold in (1) is defined entirely by internal properties of the suffering-state—specifically, whether it undermines the coherence or agency of the subject—not by comparison to any quantity of pleasure. To say that above-threshold suffering is "lexically worse" is therefore a categorical assignment of priority, not a quantitative claim about magnitude.

(5) Despite (2), I believe that sub-threshold choices can be guided by local coherence conditions: if an agent's revealed preferences over sub-threshold states are transitive and consistent, those preferences supply local ordering information without presupposing a global hedonic metric or a unified pleasure-pain scale.

Please audit this package. For each pair of commitments, say whether the tension is genuine or only apparent, and rank the genuine conflicts by severity. For each real tension, identify the cheapest revision that resolves it while preserving the core motivation behind both commitments. Then tell me which single revision—dropping or substantially modifying one of these five—would eliminate the most conflicts with the least damage to the overall view. Be precise about what each thesis actually commits me to, not just about what sounds implausible in isolation.
