I have recently been appointed to the curatorial board of a proposed museum of institutional memory, and I find myself caught in a debate that feels more philosophical than logistical. One group of trustees wants the permanent exhibition to follow a linear, progressive arc—each room demonstrates an ethical or organizational improvement over the last, implying that history moves toward refinement. The opposing faction insists that every institutional form carries the seeds of its own dissolution, so that the museum should be arranged as a repeating cycle of foundation, corruption, collapse, and renewal. Rather than adjudicating between these two narratives, I need to understand the conceptual problem that emerges when both are applied at once to a single artifact or practice.

Could you construct for me a concrete, somewhat elaborate example—centered on an invented but plausible social institution or technological practice—where applying both linear-progressive and cyclical-collapse historiography to the same object produces not merely disagreement but an internal conceptual contradiction? I want the example to be specific enough that one can see exactly where Polybius's anacyclosis, Ibn Khaldun's cycles of asabiyyah, Spengler's transition from culture to civilization, or Toynbee's challenge-and-response framework would each identify a different structural tension. The point is not to rehearse standard classroom illustrations; I am looking for something that forces the contradiction into the open, perhaps by showing how an institution's self-documentation or commemorative architecture must simultaneously claim irreversible progress and inevitable recurrence. Please do not ask me to consult particular texts I cannot access, and do not provide a bibliography—just develop the example and indicate precisely where the conceptual fracture lies.
