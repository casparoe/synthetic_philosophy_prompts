Write a sestina in standard form—six stanzas of six lines each, with the end-words aspect, harmony, earth, soul, measure, and shadow rotating through the prescribed sequence (1-2-3-4-5-6, 6-1-5-2-4-3, 3-6-4-1-2-5, 5-3-2-6-4-1, 1-4-6-5-3-2, 2-5-4-3-1-6), followed by a three-line envoi that pairs them—about Johannes Kepler’s specific account of astrological causation. The repetition should do structural work: each recurrence of aspect should evoke the geometric angles Kepler calculated instead of zodiacal houses; harmony should carry the physical ratios of the Harmonices Mundi, suggesting celestial influence operates like resonant sound rather than symbolic decree; earth and soul should together name the anima terrae, the living planet that receives the influence; measure should track the astronomer’s quantification of celestial positions; and shadow should accumulate the determinism Kepler rejected, the popular error that stars write fixed human fates. In the first five stanzas, construct the view from the ground up: distant bodies arranged in measurable intervals emit a physical sympathy, comparable to lunar tides, that inclines the Earth’s soul toward certain meteorological and temperamental states without compelling individual human choices. Distinguish this from judicial prophecy—there should be no natal chart predicting a particular marriage or death, only probabilistic patterns. In the envoi, resolve all six words into a single statement of Kepler’s actual position: the astrologer measures harmonic aspects cast upon an animated earth, producing forecasts as contingent as weather reports, and only by refusing the shadow of determinism does the art remain physically grounded rather than superstitious. The poem should be accurate enough that a historian of early modern science recognizes Kepler’s rejection of zodiacal determinism, his reliance on geometric aspects, and his analogy to tidal physics, without requiring any citation or bibliographic reference.
