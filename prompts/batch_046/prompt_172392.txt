Consider an institutional policy governing synthetic-neuron replacement clinics: a patient’s biological neurons may be replaced one at a time with functionally identical artificial substrates over ten years, and throughout that gradual process the patient retains full legal continuity—contracts, property, criminal liability, and medical consent rights remain intact. If, however, the identical total replacement is performed in a single surgical session, the governing statute treats the result not as the original patient but as a legal successor entity, even when all psychological properties and functional connectivity are preserved. The policy makes no reference to consciousness, narrative selfhood, or bodily integrity; it hinges solely on the rate of replacement. What theory of personal identity does this rate-sensitive boundary presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit view—whether it is a temporal-parameter version of psychological continuity, a disguised bodily-continuity criterion, or something about narrative coherence being disrupted by rapid change—and say whether it is internally consistent. Then compare it with the same jurisdiction’s treatment of rapid organ transplantation, where identity is not split, and ask what would have to change in clinical practice and contract law if the rate-sensitive rule were replaced by a straightforward functional-continuity standard that ignored the speed of replacement entirely.
