I'm taking a course on the philosophy of formal systems and keep running into a particular argumentative pattern in popular and semi-technical writing that I can't quite classify. A writer invokes Gödel's first incompleteness theorem—specifically, that in any consistent formal system capable of expressing arithmetic there are true statements unprovable within the system—to argue about non-mathematical domains like legal codes, organizational bylaws, or ethical rulebooks. They observe that these practical "systems" also face cases they can't resolve, and then they claim that Gödel's result formally demonstrates that such incompleteness is unavoidable and, crucially, that it necessarily requires an external supplement: judicial discretion, executive intuition, or some meta-authority outside the rules. What's distinctive is the slide: they treat the need for this supplement not as a practical or institutional problem, but as a direct formal consequence of Gödel numbering, as if the theorem itself proves that any consistent framework must be completed by something outside it. The pattern differs from ordinary appeals to legal indeterminacy, and also from Lucas-style or Penrose-style anti-mechanist arguments, because it applies Gödel's machinery—truth, provability, self-reference—to domains where none of those notions are formally defined, yet it insists on the analogy with mathematical rigor. I've seen this used both to defend flexible governance and, conversely, to argue that hierarchical authority is mathematically inevitable. Is there an established name for this specific reasoning pattern? If it is recognized as a misuse, what exactly distinguishes it from legitimate analogies between formal and practical limits? And if there is no precise term, can you define it in a way that separates it from plain category mistakes about formalization and from genuine arguments about the incompleteness of rule-based reasoning?

Please separate what is settled about Gödel's actual theorems and their legitimate scope from what is genuinely contested among philosophers and logicians regarding analogical applications, and do not present any particular interpretation of those analogies as if it were the consensus view.
