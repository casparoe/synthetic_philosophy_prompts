I'm writing this for a philosophy of action seminar I'm taking this term, where we've been working through Buridan's ass and whether rational choice requires a complete preference ordering. After a long discussion with my study group about revealed preference, incommensurability, and how agents resolve choices without clear winners, I've arrived at a cluster of commitments that I want to check for coherence before I present them. Here is my profile: (a) I think Buridan's ass is only a paradox if one assumes rational agents need a decisive reason for every selection; I reject that assumption, so I hold that choosing arbitrarily between genuinely equal options is fully rational. (b) I think revealed-preference theory is basically correct: an agent's values are shown by their consistent choices, not by introspecting private mental states. (c) At the same time, I think some pairs of options—say, a life centered on artistic creation versus one centered on scientific discovery—are genuinely incommensurable: there is no common measure that makes one better, and no utility assignment can rank them. (d) Even in these incommensurable cases, I think rational agents can and should choose, but not by inventing a false comparison; rather, they choose by appealing to second-order commitments about the kind of person they want to be. (e) Finally, I think any adequate decision theory must be complete: for every pair of alternatives, a rational agent must be able to deliver a determinate verdict—either one is better, they are tied, or one realizes an identity-constituting commitment—without leaving the choice genuinely open.

Please audit this package. Which pairs are in genuine tension, which only appear to conflict, and what is the least costly revision for each? I am especially concerned that (c) and (e) pull in opposite directions, since incommensurability seems to deny exactly the determinacy that (e) demands, and that (b) may sit badly with (d) if revealed preference collapses the distinction between first-order value and second-order identity. I also wonder whether (a) and (c) are in tension—if equality requires comparability and incommensurability denies it, do they describe competing phenomena? Rank the tensions by severity, specify what has to give in each case and at what philosophical cost, and tell me whether there is a single revision—perhaps weakening (e)—that resolves the worst problems without abandoning my core conviction that rational choice survives both equality and incommensurability.
