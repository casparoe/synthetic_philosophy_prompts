The debate in metaphysics over material constitution—exemplified by the statue and the clay—has stalled in a way that looks like agreement disguised as disagreement. Both sides grant the same physical facts: a single lump of marble is shaped into David, composed of atoms arranged in a particular form, and if squashed into a ball the artistic object is destroyed while the material persists. The coincidence view holds that two distinct objects share every part but differ in essential properties—the statue is essentially formed, the clay essentially material—while the identity view holds that there is only one object, differently characterized by different sortals. Over decades the literature has cycled through appeals to counting, to the worry that infinitely many part-arrangements generate infinitely many coincident entities, and to ontological parsimony, yet neither side has conceded ground. I want a structural diagnosis, not an adjudication. Is the impasse verbal—do the sides use “object” differently, with one treating “statue” as a functional kind and the other treating “clay” as a material substrate—so that they answer different questions under one name? Or is there a hidden formal question, perhaps about whether identity conditions for artifacts depend on origin or on continuous form, that neither side has resolved? Could it be bedrock intuition about persistence through change, immune to argument?
