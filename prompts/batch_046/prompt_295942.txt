I have just come out of a prolonged argument with a close friend about whether scientific thought experiments—Einstein's accelerating elevator, Maxwell's demon, Schrödinger's cat—actually yield knowledge or merely dramatize commitments we already hold. My friend, persuaded that in silico experiments have made them obsolete, insisted that any epistemic labor they once performed is now executed more rigorously by simulation. I found myself defending a more nuanced position: that thought experiments operate either diagnostically, exposing internal tensions in a theoretical framework, or generatively, constructing modal dependencies that physical apparatus cannot isolate because the relevant variables are inseparable in nature. We stalled, however, on which of these functions justifies sustained philosophical attention, and on where the real open problems lie—particularly regarding how thought experiments interact with computational models and whether their conclusions survive systematic perturbation of the imagined conditions, a feature I will call counterfactual robustness.

Treat this as an agenda-setting question, not a survey or a reading list. In the study of how scientific thought experiments yield knowledge, which open questions or lines of work actually matter most? I want you to identify the two or three that should command priority for a researcher with about three years, conceptual-analysis skills, and no laboratory access—defending the ordering with explicit criteria such as tractability, dependence of other questions on this one, and potential to reshape scientific practice rather than merely tidy philosophical taxonomy. Please address specifically: the relationship between thought experiments and simulation; the diagnostic versus generative distinction I sketched; and the problem of counterfactual robustness. Which directions are overrated, which depend on false premises about how science functions, and what would make you reorder the priorities? I am not asking for citations or a bibliography; I want a defended agenda that explains what makes a problem worth years of limited effort.
