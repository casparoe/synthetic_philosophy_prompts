My colleague in aesthetics evaluates cases of what she calls the paradox of tragic enjoyment—whether negative emotional responses to art can be genuinely pleasurable—assigning each to one of three outcomes: dissolved (no real paradox, only misdescription), resolved (a mechanism explains the pleasure alongside the pain), or persistent (the combination remains genuinely puzzling). Her track record on five cases is as follows. (1) Watching a live theatrical production of Sophocles’ Antigone, with full awareness of actors, stage, and fiction: she judges the paradox dissolved, holding that formal mastery and cathartic release replace suffering rather than accompany it. (2) Viewing a horror film that uses jump scares and visceral imagery in a safe theater environment: she judges it resolved, because fear operates as arousal that converts into excitement and post-threat relief. (3) Listening alone at home to a solo instrumental requiem with no narrative program, lyrics, or visual component: she judges the paradox genuinely persistent, since the listener reports profound sadness coupled with deep satisfaction and she finds no framing mechanism that transforms or redirects the grief. (4) Visiting a museum installation that projects documentary footage of an actual historical disaster, accompanied by curatorial wall text framing the work as ethical testimony: she judges the paradox dissolved, arguing that institutional framing redirects emotional response into moral attention and contemplative distance. (5) Reading a first-person fictional narrative in which the protagonist suffers a loss structurally parallel to one the reader has experienced: she judges the paradox resolved, because identification produces a feeling of being understood, a distinct positive affect that accompanies the sadness.
