Read Peter Howitt’s 1998 film *Sliding Doors* through the philosophy of path dependence and historical “lock-in.” The premise is well known: the protagonist Helen’s life splits into two timelines that diverge solely on whether she catches a particular London Underground train—one branch leading her to discover her partner’s infidelity and begin a new relationship, the other leaving her unaware and the affair intact—yet both timelines eventually converge on strikingly similar crises, hospitalizations, and resolutions. Analyze this narrative architecture philosophically. Does the film genuinely dramatize path dependence, in which an initially trivial contingency is amplified and institutionally or psychologically “locked in,” or does it instead depict a form of macro-level convergence—what we might call “basin attraction”—in which deeper structures (professional ambition, relational patterns, urban environment) pull individual biography back toward a common trajectory regardless of micro-contingency? In particular, examine whether applying the concept of “lock-in,” developed for technological standards and institutional history, to a single life commits a category mistake, or whether biography is precisely the domain where path dependence is most vivid. Where the film’s emotional logic insists that the missed train matters profoundly—where it resists being read as mere superficial divergence—does that resistance expose a limit in the philosophical theory, or does it reveal that the theory has never properly accounted for phenomenological significance? I want a reading that takes both the film’s dual-timeline structure and the debate over historical inevitability seriously, and that concludes by saying whether *Sliding Doors* adds something to that debate—perhaps about the scale at which contingency operates, or about the difference between causal and narrative significance—that formal argument about path dependence alone does not capture.
