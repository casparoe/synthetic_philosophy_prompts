I spent much of yesterday evening arguing with a close friend about the therapy/enhancement distinction, and we kept circling back to a hypothetical case he dubbed “synaptic rebalancing” — a neural intervention that restores degraded mnemonic pathways to an individual’s prior baseline, yet also selectively amplifies signal-processing efficiency in those same pathways to levels the patient never naturally possessed, but only in cognitive domains where she previously tested above the population mean. My friend summarized the standard philosophical account like this: therapy is defined as restoring a function to the statistically typical range for the species, while enhancement pushes it past that range; since the boundary of “typical” is just a conventional cutoff on a distribution curve, the distinction carries no intrinsic ethical weight and objections to enhancement are merely status-quo bias. He applied this to our case, saying that if synaptic rebalancing returns your memory to baseline while also making it sharper than ever, labeling the second effect “enhancement” only expresses an arbitrary preference for the old bell curve. I suspect he is conflating the statistical tool used to classify functions with the normative reasoning that is supposed to justify treating the two categories differently, but I want to be precise about where the error lies. Is this a fair summary of the debate? Please distinguish what is accurate, what is a common simplification, and what subtly misrepresents the relationship between descriptive classification and moral justification — especially regarding whether an intervention that restores an individual baseline can simultaneously be an enhancement, and whether that possibility undermines the ethical distinction rather than complicating it.
