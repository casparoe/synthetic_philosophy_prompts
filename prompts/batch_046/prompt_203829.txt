I'm working through the reductionism debate in testimony for a seminar paper, and I've built an argument against anti-reductionism that now strikes me as proving far too much. The steps are:

(1) Anti-reductionism holds that being told p can, by itself, justify a hearer's belief that p.
(2) If being told p can justify belief by itself, then there must be some feature F of the utterance-event that is epistemically accessible to the hearer and that rationally supports p.
(3) In pure testimony—where the hearer lacks any background knowledge about the speaker's competence, sincerity, or track record—the only accessible feature is the bare fact that the speaker said p.
(4) But that bare fact gives the hearer no more reason to accept p than to reject it, absent prior beliefs about the speaker.
(5) So in pure testimony there is no accessible supporting feature; hence anti-reductionism is false, and testimony justifies only through inference from prior evidence about the speaker.

Then the overreach:
(6) The same reasoning applies to perception: the bare occurrence of a visual experience gives no reason to believe there's an object present unless one already has justified beliefs about one's visual reliability.
(7) It applies equally to memory: a seeming-memory of p supports p only if one already has justified beliefs about one's mnemonic reliability.
(8) So if testimony reduces to prior inference, then perception and memory must also reduce, leaving no basic sources of justification.
(9) But that generates a vicious regress: if every justified belief requires prior justified beliefs about the faculty's reliability, then justification never gets started.
(10) Therefore one of (1)–(5) must be wrong, or the analogy in (6)–(7) fails.

I'd like to keep (2) and (4)—they seem to capture the accessibility requirement on justification—but I'm unsure whether the fault lies in (3) (perhaps pure testimony never actually occurs), in the slide from (5) to (6) (maybe I'm conflating source-reductionism with justification-reductionism), or in treating (6)–(7) as parallel rather than disanalogous. Where does the argument actually break, what does fixing it cost for the reductionist, and which premise is least painful to surrender?

Please respond with a clear diagnosis of the weakest step, an explanation of why it initially looked sound, and a pricing of the alternatives. Also, please say how confident you are in your diagnosis and single out the specific point where you are least sure.
