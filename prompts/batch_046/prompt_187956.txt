Having recently begun mentoring junior scholars and finding myself repeatedly reaching for biographical accounts of leadership to think through questions of professional integrity, I have drafted the outline of an essay on Plutarch’s Parallel Lives—specifically the pairing of Pericles with Fabius Maximus—and on what I take to be their distinctive moral pedagogy. My central claim is that Plutarch’s comparative structure operates as a form of “stereoscopic moral optics”: by placing two exemplary figures in parallel rather than isolating a single hero, the text forces the reader to hold competing models of greatness—democratic magnanimity versus republican austerity—in simultaneous tension. I argue that this juxtaposition is deliberately designed to block the kind of uncritical admiration that modern single-subject biography, functioning as a kind of secular hagiography, tends to encourage; the reader’s task is not to imitate Pericles or Fabius but to calibrate her own judgment in the space between them. I then propose that contemporary biography of scientists, activists, and statesmen has largely abandoned this comparative frame, substituting narrative identification for moral calibration, and that this structural shift explains why modern readers often emerge from such works with heightened admiration but without the self-corrective distance Plutarch intended. I am especially uncertain about two points. First, whether my reading of the Pericles-Fabius pairing holds up against the actual narrative dynamics of those lives—particularly how Plutarch treats Pericles’ political failures and Fabius’ strategic patience—rather than imposing my “stereoscopic” framework onto texts that may be doing something closer to straightforward praise and blame. Second, whether the conceptual distinction I draw between “moral calibration” and “narrative identification” is genuinely philosophically sharp, or whether it collapses into a simpler formal claim about comparative versus singular narrative structure, with the normative weight I assign to the difference being largely projected. I would welcome precise comments on the historical plausibility of my framing, on the coherence and usefulness of the “stereoscopic” concept, and on whether the essay’s move from ancient paired biography to modern secular hagiography is defensible or anachronistic.
