Write a branching taxonomy—built from answer-combinations rather than from named philosophers—that sorts positions on whether pleasure and pain are commensurable, using lexical-threshold negative utilitarianism as the organizing frame. The tree should have four branch-points, each phrased as a concrete claim a respondent could accept or reject without already knowing the position-labels: (i) whether any magnitude of pleasure can ever morally outweigh a single severe pain; (ii) whether sub-threshold pains can aggregate across persons or over time to become lexically significant; (iii) whether the incommensurability, if present, runs in one direction only (pain always dominates pleasure) or is symmetric; and (iv) whether a context-sensitive “reversibility threshold”—where accumulated small goods might eventually license accepting a large harm—can be coherently defined without collapsing back into standard scalar weighing.

At each leaf, place the view (for instance: scalar negative utilitarianism, lexical negative utilitarianism, strong one-directional incommensurabilism, or the unnamed reversibility position) and in one sentence state its most awkward commitment in everyday moral choice. Identify every empty branch—combinations of answers that are logically available but apparently undefended—and briefly diagnose each gap as due to incoherence, practical unattractiveness, or mere neglect. Explicitly flag any pair of leaves that your tree cannot genuinely separate, explaining why the distinction fails in practice.

Then take a committed stance: defend one specific branch as the most defensible, argue that at least one apparently available leaf should be treated as incoherent rather than merely unexplored, and identify which of the four distinctions actually does the substantive work in separating views that otherwise look similar. Do not supply bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; work entirely from the concepts and cases specified.
