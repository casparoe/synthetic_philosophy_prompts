I am a doctoral student in moral philosophy preparing materials for a graduate seminar on action and omission, and I have become convinced that the standard trolley-and-rescue framing obscures a genuinely different problem: what I am calling “structural omission,” in which a life-saving resource is withheld not through any discrete decision by an identifiable agent but through an institutional protocol, algorithmic filter, or bureaucratic default that systematically excludes recipients without anyone performing an observable act of refusal. For the handout I need a decision-tree taxonomy that sorts philosophical positions on whether killing remains morally worse than letting die specifically within this distributed-agency context, and I want the instrument to work for respondents who can answer concrete case-questions but do not already know the philosophical labels.

Please construct a tree with three binary branching nodes, yielding eight logical combinations, of which exactly seven should carry named positions and one should be flagged as unoccupied or incoherent. The root node should ask whether the moral asymmetry between killing and letting die depends on a metaphysically robust distinction between positive action and mere absence—specifically, whether that distinction survives intact in structural-omission cases where no individual agent performs a direct act of withholding. The second node should split on whether counterfactual dependency—the fact that altering the protocol would save lives—is sufficient by itself to convert structural omission into a form of killing. The third node should split on whether rights against being killed can be violated purely by omission, or whether such rights necessarily include a positive-interference condition. Each leaf must carry the name of a position—examples might include “Agent-Causal Deontology,” “Consequentialist Collapse,” “Rights-Based Positive-Duty Account,” “Institutional Structuralism,” “Counterfactual-Equivalence View,” and “Unnamed/Incoherent”—together with a single sentence stating its most awkward commitment when applied to the structural-omission scenario. Phrase every node as a concrete yes/no or either/or claim about the protocol case that a respondent could answer without knowing which label it leads to. Where two named positions share identical answers across all three nodes and therefore cannot be separated by the tree, flag them explicitly and indicate what further distinction would be required to pull them apart. Finally, for the one empty combination, briefly diagnose whether its absence reflects logical incoherence, mere theoretical unattractiveness, or an unexplored space in the literature.
