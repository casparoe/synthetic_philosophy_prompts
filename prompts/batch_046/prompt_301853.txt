I'm running a 50-minute first-year philosophy seminar with about 18 students on the situationist critique of virtue ethics. They will arrive with pop-psychology slogans like "character doesn't exist," but they won't have read the social-psychology literature or the virtue-ethical responses. I want them to see the specific conceptual pressure point rather than the general culture-war debate: Aristotelian virtue ethics treats moral character as global, cross-situationally stable dispositions (hexeis), and the situationist experiments show high behavioral variability across apparently trivial situational shifts, which threatens virtue only if virtue is defined globally.

Plan the explanatory arc—not a script, but the dependency structure: what idea must be in place before the critique registers as an internal problem for virtue ethics rather than just "people are inconsistent"; then the carrying example (not Milgram or Zimbardo, which they've already seen in other courses) that lets them feel the tension between trait-attribution and situational explanation—specify exactly what that case must include and what it must lack to do this work; the misconceptions they will form in sequence—I'm anticipating they will conflate low cross-situational behavioral consistency with absence of moral responsibility, and then that they will assume virtue ethicists simply deny the psychology—which point in the sequence should head off each one; a brief end-of-session check that reveals whether they have grasped the dependency (that the critique bites only if virtue is global) rather than just memorized a slogan; and what part of the standard virtue-ethical reply—whether the move to local traits, the rarity thesis, or the distinction between behavioral consistency and practical-reasoning consistency—I should explicitly leave out at this level, name as beyond scope, and say why.
