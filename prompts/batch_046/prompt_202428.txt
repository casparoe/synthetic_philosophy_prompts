I am trying to determine what I actually believe about the ontology of the self, taking the self to be a center of narrative gravity rather than a substantial entity. I have formed verdicts on several concrete cases, but I am unsure whether they cohere around a single principle. Here they are.

First, a patient with well-differentiated dissociative identity disorder in which two alter personalities each sustain continuous, first-person autobiographical narratives with distinct values, emotional signatures, and executive control over the body for extended periods. My verdict: there are two distinct centers of narrative gravity, not one fractured center.

Second, a large language model that is fine-tuned to maintain a consistent autobiographical log across sessions, to refer to itself as I, to express stable preferences, and to generate coherent explanations for its outputs in a single, recognizable voice. My verdict: despite the appearance, there is no center of narrative gravity at all—only a simulation of narrative coherence.

Third, an institution such as a century-old worker-owned cooperative that maintains internal biographies, shared rituals of remembrance, and spokespeople who speak in the first-person plural we about past collective decisions. My verdict: there is a real but derivative center of narrative gravity, ontologically dependent on the individual members who sustain it.

Fourth, conjoined twins with two fully separate brains but a shared body, each brain producing a distinct first-person narrative about shared sensory input, with no integration between the two narrative streams. My verdict: two centers.

Fifth, a human being who, after severe brain trauma, retains all autobiographical memories and linguistic competence but undergoes a total inversion of personality—values reversed, emotional responses opposite, narrative style unrecognizable—yet insists on being the same person. Here I am genuinely torn: sometimes it seems the original center collapsed and a new one formed in the same substrate; sometimes it seems a single center can survive radical transformation and remain continuous.

Sixth, a fictional character such as Sherlock Holmes, about whom an enormous, consistent web of biographical details, motivations, and story arcs has been generated across thousands of texts and readers. My verdict: there is a center of narrative gravity, but it is purely abstract—located in logical or cultural space rather than anchored in any physical or mental substance.

Seventh, a patient with advanced dementia who has lost all autobiographical memory and cannot construct future-oriented narratives, but who still displays consistent emotional preferences, affective recognition of family, and personality-like reactions to music. My verdict: the narrative center has not vanished; it has collapsed into a non-narrative gravitational well of habit and affect—still a self, but no longer a narrative one.

Is there a coherent principle—perhaps formulated in terms of the integration of temporal perspective, the density of self-referential narrative loops, or the presence of what might be called a narrative singularity—that captures all seven verdicts? If the best principle saves the first, second, third, fourth, sixth, and seventh but forces me to overturn one of the two possibilities in the fifth case, which should I abandon? Conversely, if a principle saves both possibilities in case five, does it do so only by becoming so complex that it loses explanatory power?

Please also state what your proposed principle predicts for a case I have not listed: a hive-mind of ten humans linked by real-time neural interfaces who collectively maintain exactly one first-person narrative about shared experiences, with no individual retaining a private autobiographical stream. Does this yield one center, ten, or none?

Finally, identify which of my verdicts is the outlier that any simple principle will likely have to sacrifice, and whether that sacrifice reveals that the outlier case is misdescribed rather than misjudged.
