I've been arguing with a friend about whether you can really seperate the art from the artist, and we kept circling back to The Picture of Dorian Gray because it's almost too on-the-nose—the painting literally absorbs Dorian's sins while staying beautiful. I want a reading that takes both the novel and the actual philosophical debate seriously: does Wilde dramatize the idea that aesthetic perfection and moral corruption can sit in the same object? I'm especially interested in the portrait as an artwork inside the fiction—Basil calls it his masterpiece, yet its presence corrupts everyone who stares at it too long. Does the novel end up siding with some kind of autonomism, where beauty is independent of morality, or with moralism, where the corruption eventually taints the art itself? And where does Wilde's own biography—his trial for gross indecency, the way people read his fiction as autobiographical—fit into this? Not asking for a biography lecture, just: if we strictly seperate author from work, are we missing something the novel itself says about how art and morality get entangled?

I'd also like you to dig into whether there's a useful distinction between the portrait's aesthetic value and its moral effect on Dorian. Is it good art that happens to cause harm, or is it bad art precisely because it causes harm? The standard autonomist/moralist divide feels too flat here, since the portrait is both technically brilliant and actively destructive. Does Wilde complicate that divide, or does the ending—where the portrait is destroyed and Dorian dies—vindicate one side? I'd prefer a reading that doesn't just use the novel to illustrate a position, but actually says whether Wilde adds something to the debate that pure argument misses, and where an honest critic has to admit the novel resists being read that way.
