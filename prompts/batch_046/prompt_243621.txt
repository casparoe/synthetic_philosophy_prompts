I was discussing with a close friend until quite late about the ethics of voting, specifically whether casting a ballot for the lesser of two evils is best understood as an expression of autonomy or as an instrumental act that carries unavoidable complicity. We kept finding that both the expressive and the consequentialist accounts left something out: a vote for a flawed candidate does not merely select an individual, but also lends symbolic weight to that candidate's entire platform, including elements the voter explicitly rejects. To capture this, I want to work with a framing I am calling the "residual mandate" — the idea that even a strategic, harm-minimizing vote generates an unredeemed symbolic endorsement of policies and values the voter opposes. With that in mind, could you provide an example of a conceptual issue in which this residual mandate creates a genuine paradox for voting ethics? I am especially interested in a constructed case where the usual distinction between a deontological duty to abstain and an expressive duty to participate breaks down entirely, rather than being neatly resolved in favor of one side. The example should be original rather than drawn from familiar historical elections, and it should show precisely why the tension between expressing one's moral commitments and minimizing harm through a lesser-evil choice resists dissolution into either pure consequentialism or pure deontology. I do not need bibliographic references or a list of further readings; I simply need a sharp, philosophically constructed illustration that I can use to continue our argument.
