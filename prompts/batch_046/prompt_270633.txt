I'm drafting an essay for my seminar on moral responsibility, and I would appreciate critical comments on the argument outlined below rather than on a full draft. The essay takes up P.F. Strawson's familiar contrast between reactive attitudes—resentment, gratitude, moral indignation, and their kin—and the objective attitude we adopt when we regard someone as outside the sphere of moral interaction. Rather than treating this distinction as a single binary switch, I propose decomposing it along two independent dimensions. The first dimension concerns affective register: whether one's response operates in the participatory-reactive mode characteristic of interpersonal engagement, or in a detached, clinical, or instrumental mode. The second dimension concerns normative stance: whether one continues to hold the agent to a standard of appropriate conduct, irrespective of the emotional coloring of that holding. I contend that Strawson's presentation tends to conflate these dimensions, so that moving to the objective attitude is understood to entail not merely a change in emotional posture but a complete suspension of normative expectation. I defend a hybrid category that Strawson's framework appears to exclude: an agent can be subject to a "thin" form of normative address—being held accountable in a minimal, non-reactive sense—while being approached with an objective affective register. I then apply this hybrid to cases of what I term structural excuse, in which an agent retains basic reasons-responsive capacities but was formed under severely coercive or depriving conditions that make full reactive attitudes seem unfair. On my account, the correct moral response is neither full reactive blame nor complete exculpation through the objective attitude, but a calibrated attenuation of affective intensity coupled with retained, though thinned, normative address. I would particularly welcome comments on three points: first, whether this two-dimensional decomposition is textually defensible or whether it imposes an artificial structure on Strawson's view; second, whether the hybrid stance is conceptually coherent, or whether holding normative expectations without reactive affect undermines the very idea of moral address; and third, whether my notion of structural excuse actually distinguishes cases that standard excusing conditions—such as ignorance or duress—do not already cover.
