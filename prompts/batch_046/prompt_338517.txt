Write a villanelle — nineteen lines, five tercets and a closing quatrain, with refrains alternating in the A1bA2 pattern — that takes up split-brain consciousness through a rarely foregrounded tension: imagine a corpus-callosotomy patient whose left hand consistently selects harm-avoidance images while the right hand selects reward-seeking images, and whose left-hemisphere interpreter, cut off from the right hemisphere's reasoning, confabulates a single moral justification that covers both choices. Let the first refrain be the interpreter's first-person claim to unified agency ("I meant both, and both are me"), and let the second refrain be the silent hemisphere's non-verbal acknowledgment of separation ("the hand moves, and I have no word"). Structure the poem so that the rhyme scheme itself performs the philosophical problem: the refrains are forced into sonic proximity despite semantic divergence, and in the final quatrain they must share a single line without resolving whether they belong to one consciousness or to two. The poem should be accurate enough that someone familiar with the unity-of-consciousness debate recognizes the empirical setup — severed hemispheres, confabulation, contradictory preferences across hands — without naming researchers, experiments, or texts. Do not resolve the metaphysical question in the conclusion; let the formal constraint of the villanelle stand in for the missing neural bridge, so that the rhyme does the unifying work the brain no longer can.
