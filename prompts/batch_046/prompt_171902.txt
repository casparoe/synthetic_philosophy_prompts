I chair the evidence-review board for an interdisciplinary research institute that adjudicates claims about pre-modern technological transmission—claims that typically combine archaeological artifacts, reconstructed causal arguments, analogies to better-documented cultures, and attributed textual descriptions. Our reviewers currently have no shared framework for weighing these evidence types when they conflict, and they often import unstated assumptions about whether physical presence, logical derivation, comparative similarity, or authoritative attribution should dominate. I want a reusable decision procedure, grounded in classical Nyāya epistemology’s four pramāṇas—pratyakṣa (perception), anumāna (inference), upamāna (comparison or analogy), and śabda (testimony)—that the board can apply to any submitted claim without requiring knowledge of Sanskrit texts.

The procedure should be specified in ordered steps. First, provide operational criteria for classifying each piece of submitted evidence into exactly one of the four pramāṇa categories, including boundary conditions that prevent misclassification (for instance, distinguishing an analogical inference from a direct perceptual identification, or separating reconstructed logical entailment from authoritative textual attribution). Second, state a conflict-resolution rule for cases in which two pramāṇas yield incompatible conclusions about the same claim, specifying the conditions under which a default hierarchy applies and the explicit conditions—such as hidden auxiliary premises or qualified-object failure—under which that hierarchy is suspended. Third, list the recognized failure modes or defeat conditions relevant to this domain: contradiction between results, absence of a properly qualified object of knowledge, overextension of a pramāṇa beyond its proper scope, and dependence on an unverified mediator. Fourth, identify the precise decision nodes where the procedure must halt and defer to human judgment because the resolution depends on normative weighing rather than epistemic classification, marking these nodes clearly. Fifth, include a brief validation check that asks whether the claim as a whole is pramāṇa-supported or falls into one of the defeat conditions.

Then apply the procedure to three submissions. Case one: a claim that a specific irrigation technique traveled from Region A to Region B based solely on structural similarity in canal remains, with no direct artifactual link and no textual attestation. Case two: a claim that a metal alloy formula described in an attributed historical treatise was actually produced in antiquity, where laboratory analysis of recovered artifacts contradicts the textual proportions, and the treatise’s attribution to its named author is itself disputed. Case three: a claim supported by direct observation of a functioning modern replica built exactly from textual instructions, but where the original historical and geographical context of the technique is entirely unattested by either physical remains or comparative parallels.

For each case, state the procedural verdict at each step, identify the specific point where judgment is unavoidable and why, and describe one way the procedure could misfire if applied mechanically without that judgment. Finally, state clearly what kinds of evidentiary conflicts or claim-types this procedure is not designed to settle, and flag any normative questions about technological value or cultural ownership that lie outside its scope.
