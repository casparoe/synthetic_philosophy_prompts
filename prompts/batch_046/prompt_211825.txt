I'm currently drafting a chapter of my dissertation in the ontology of art, and I've become stuck on a variant of the classic statue-and-clay problem that my advisor suggested I work through more rigorously. In the standard formulation, we ask whether a statue is identical to the lump of clay that constitutes it, particularly once the clay is reshaped or destroyed. I want to push the problem in a slightly different direction: imagine that a museum holds only fragmented remnants of an ancient terracotta figure—so little material remains that no continuous spatiotemporal path connects the original intact object to any present substrate. Conservators then reconstruct the figure using clay extracted from the very same geological stratum as the original, fired using documented ancient techniques, yet they necessarily incorporate modern stabilizing compounds that alter the crystalline lattice of the material at the molecular level. The resulting object is phenomenologically indistinguishable from the original, occupies the same institutional and art-historical role, and is displayed as the restored work, but every particle of the original clay has been replaced, and the new substrate is chemically distinct.

Write an essay addressing whether this reconstructed figure is numerically identical to the original statue, or whether it is merely a perfect replica. In your essay, weigh the main metaphysical positions—material essentialism, spatiotemporal continuity theories, and institutional or art-historical accounts of object identity—against one another in light of this specific reconstruction scenario. You should reach a definite conclusion about which framework best handles the case and justify that verdict with clear reasoning. I am interested purely in the conceptual argument itself, so please do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations.
