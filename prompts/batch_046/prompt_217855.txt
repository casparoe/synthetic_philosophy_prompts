A university’s Academic Equity and Integrity Board has, over the past decade, issued a consistent pattern of rulings on examination accommodations and assistive technology. It routinely approves extended time, separate testing rooms, and human scribes for students with documented cognitive or physical disabilities; it approves standard calculators for students with dyscalculia in exams where arithmetic is not the primary skill assessed, but rejects symbolic-computation software in calculus exams; it denies requests for unprescribed cognitive stimulants taken specifically for testing, even when students claim they merely restore baseline attention; and it classifies real-time augmented-reality fact-retrieval devices as unauthorized aids rather than accommodations, regardless of disability status. Now the Board faces a novel request: a student with a diagnosed working-memory deficit asks to use a personalized AI “cognitive prosthetic” during a closed-book philosophy exam on moral theory. The system does not generate answers; it stores and retrieves, via natural-language prompts, only the student’s own previously composed notes and conceptual maps. The student argues this externalizes memory in the same way a scribe externalizes handwriting or a calculator externalizes arithmetic, thereby restoring species-typical functioning rather than enhancing beyond it.

What theory of the therapy/enhancement distinction—normalization to a species-typical baseline, a welfarist account focused on well-being regardless of baseline, or an equality-of-opportunity framework—does the Board’s pattern of approvals and denials implicitly presuppose? Is the pattern genuinely committed to one theory, or is it philosophically underdetermined, with rival theories licensing the same rulings for different reasons? Identify the closest analog among the Board’s precedents for the AI prosthetic case, predict whether consistency demands approval or rejection, and explain which principle the Board would be adopting with each choice. Finally, if the Board replaced its implicit framework with one that permitted cognitive enhancement, which of its existing distinctions—between human scribe and AI retrieval, between calculator and symbolic solver, between prescribed medication and exam-day stimulant—would become unstable, and what would a policy rebuilt on that alternative view have to change?
