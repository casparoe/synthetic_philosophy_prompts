I am not a philosopher or academic; I am a local resident who was asked to deliver the opening remarks at tomorrow’s community reconciliation workshop. The gathering is for neighbors divided by the closure and proposed redevelopment of the old Riverside pool. Roughly half the room will be mediators, counselors, and people familiar with Adam Smith’s moral philosophy; the other half are residents who lived through the conflict and have no particular interest in an eighteenth-century economist.

Over the past months I have been thinking on my own about Smith’s idea that sympathy is not merely feeling what another person feels, but a kind of internal judgment in which an “impartial spectator” within us assesses whether another’s emotions fit their situation. But I have also come to believe that Smith left something important unstated: it seems to me that this spectator can only operate if we genuinely do not already know what we are going to hear. If I enter a conversation convinced that I understand exactly why my neighbor is angry, the spectator does not correct me; it merely ratifies the view I brought with me. So I want to propose, in plain language, that real sympathy requires what I call structured ignorance—a deliberate refusal to finalize one’s judgment before listening in a way that could actually change it.

Please write the opening remarks, about six to eight minutes spoken—roughly 950 to 1150 words. The philosophical idea should do real structural work: it must explain why this workshop is designed with silent paired-listening rounds before any open debate, and why I am asking people not to arrive with rebuttals prepared. It should shape what I can and cannot say about forgiveness and moving forward—so I should not offer cheap calls for “more empathy” that ignore the conditions that make empathy possible. At the same time, the speech must land for the residents who have no background in philosophy; they should feel addressed, not lectured. The mixed audience should understand why the format of the day is itself part of the moral argument, not just decoration around it. I do not want bibliographies, citations, or reading lists; I want the concept rendered clearly and used to govern the shape and limits of what I say.
