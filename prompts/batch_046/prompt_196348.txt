I'm preparing remarks for a metaphysics seminar on persistence and identity, and I have constructed an argument that starts from Black's symmetric universe but seems to me to collapse into an absurd denial of personal continuity. The steps are as follows. (1) The Identity of Indiscernibles states that if x and y share every property, then x is identical to y. (2) In Black's scenario, sphere A and sphere B share all intrinsic features and differ only in spatial position—A is situated to the left of B. (3) One standard reply to this counterexample is that spatial relations are genuine properties, so A and B are not qualitatively identical after all, and the principle survives. (4) But if we take that reply seriously, then a solitary sphere in an otherwise empty universe lacks those very relational properties, so by (1) it cannot be numerically identical to sphere A in Black's world. (5) That result is unacceptable: it makes cross-world identity impossible for any object whose relational environment changes, yet we routinely judge that the very same sphere could have existed alone. (6) Therefore the relational-properties reply must fail, and the spheres differ only in bare spatial position, which is not a qualitative feature. (7) By parity of reasoning, if bare spatial position distinguishes A from B, then bare temporal position distinguishes my coffee cup at t₁ from the same cup at t₂ whenever its intrinsic properties remain constant. (8) Hence accepting Black's spheres as genuinely distinct forces us to deny that any enduring object survives qualitative stasis; every interval without change produces a numerically new object. (9) But then the agent who made a promise at t₁ is not the agent who bears the obligation at t₂, which undermines moral responsibility and practical reasoning. (10) Since this conclusion is intolerable, some premise must be rejected.

I would prefer to keep both that Black's spheres are metaphysically possible and that enduring objects can persist through qualitative stasis, but I am unsure whether saving those commitments requires abandoning the Identity of Indiscernibles entirely, restricting its scope to intrinsic properties only, or finding a hidden flaw elsewhere in the chain. Where does the argument actually fail, and what is the least costly revision?
