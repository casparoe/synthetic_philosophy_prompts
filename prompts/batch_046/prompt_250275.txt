I am facilitating a philosophy workshop for roughly eighteen graduate students in cognitive science and philosophy of mind, convening for a single 75-minute session. We have been working through the problem of personal identity, and I want the group to feel rather than merely analyze the possibility that a self might be genuinely distributed across brains—the view associated with Douglas Hofstadter that what persists is not a particular physical substrate but a high-level pattern, a soul in his loose sense, that could in principle be instantiated in more than one brain at once. To keep the exercise from feeling like a recycled teletransporter variant, please inject a less familiar framing: a clinical trial of a bidirectional working-memory bridge linking two consenting adults, originally designed only to share low-level sensory buffers, that has begun inadvertently merging their first-person narrative centers. Each participant now reports some of the partner's autobiographical memories as genuinely belonging to them, and the coupled pair must make joint ethical and legal decisions despite retaining separate bodies.

Design a structured role-play or forced-choice deliberation—assigning roles such as the coupled pair (each defending a different claim about where the self resides), an ethics review board, a legal arbiter, and silent observers—governed by explicit rules about how decisions must be reached, for instance unanimous vote or weighted authority tied to whose memories are under discussion, and carrying small but real stakes such as the allocation of a single shared resource or a binding vote on whether to disconnect the bridge. The rules and incentives should be arranged so that participants run into the coordination failure and the distributive puzzle themselves, discovering that asking who is the self determines rights, obligations, and suffering, before the facilitator names the philosophical position. Include a precise timeline, a debrief that maps specific moments of the activity onto elements of a pattern-based account of persistence, an honest admission of which feature of distributed selfhood the exercise cannot reproduce and must be supplied verbally, and a frank prediction of what will predictably malfunction the first time you run it together with suggestions for managing it. The students should leave seeing that identity under distribution is not only a metaphysical curiosity but an urgent coordination problem with practical consequences.
