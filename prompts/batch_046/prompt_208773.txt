A philosophy-of-language blog offers the following summary of Paul Grice's theory of speaker meaning and implicature:

"Grice divided speaker meaning into two strictly separated layers. The first layer, 'what is said,' is fixed entirely by linguistic convention—syntax and lexical semantics—without any contribution from speaker intention or contextual disambiguation. The second layer, 'what is implicated,' is recovered by the hearer through the Cooperative Principle, according to which rational speakers aim to be truthful, informative, relevant, and perspicuous. Whenever an utterance appears to violate one of these maxims at the level of what is said, the hearer derives a hidden meaning by assuming the speaker is still cooperating at a deeper level. Because this derivation is purely a matter of reasoning about intentions, every implicature is cancellable without contradiction: a speaker can always deny the implicated content and remain consistent. Grice also held that conventional implicatures—such as the contrast carried by 'but'—are in fact merely conversational implicatures that have become conventionalized, since they depend on the Cooperative Principle rather than on the literal meaning of the term. Consequently, speaker meaning is ultimately subjective: to mean something is just to possess a private mental intention, and the Cooperative Principle serves only as a psychological heuristic listeners employ to guess at those hidden states."

The blogger then applies this framework to two examples. First: "If I say 'Some of the guests arrived,' I implicate that not all arrived; this is cancellable, since I can consistently add 'in fact, all of them did.'" Second: "If I say 'He is poor but honest,' the word 'but' conventionally signals a contrast; yet if I immediately deny that contrast—'and I do not mean to contrast poverty with honesty'—my denial is contradictory, which proves that conventional implicature is not cancellable and therefore cannot be reduced to conversational implicature."

Please audit this summary for fairness to Grice's actual position. Separate what is accurate, what is a defensible simplification, and what Grice would reject. Specifically address: (i) whether "what is said" is entirely free of context and speaker intention; (ii) whether all implicatures are cancellable, and whether conventional implicatures can be analyzed as conventionalized conversational implicatures; (iii) whether Grice's account of speaker meaning reduces it to private mental states; and (iv) whether the blogger's treatment of the "poor but honest" case correctly applies the distinction between conventional and conversational implicature, particularly with respect to cancellability.
