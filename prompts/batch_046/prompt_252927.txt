I am designing the reading list for a graduate seminar on metaethics, and I have grown skeptical of how the literature frames indirect normativity—especially those accounts that derive reasons for action from the deliverances of an idealized deliberative procedure. Before I ask students to defend or attack any particular version—whether Railton-style idealized desire satisfaction, the rationalist models associated with Michael Smith, or recent contractualist reconstructions—I want the conceptual ground cleared. Write the full list of desiderata that a theory of indirect normativity grounded in idealized deliberation must satisfy to count as adequate, not merely familiar. For each requirement, specify the phenomenon it must capture or the distinction it must preserve: for instance, how the theory handles agents with systematically false beliefs about their ends without collapsing into direct instruction; how it distinguishes procedural rationality from substantive rationality without letting the latter do all the normative work; how it explains divergence in reasons between agents who share identical actual psychologies but differ in counterfactual rational capacities; and how it maintains normative authority for the actual agent without requiring omniscience or generating systematically alienating reasons. Behind each desideratum, state the motivating case or conceptual pressure. Then mark which requirements are genuinely non-negotiable—those that, if abandoned, dissolve the very idea of indirectness—and which reflect camp-specific preferences, such as internalist demands on motivation or the rejection of substantive constraints on rationality.

I also want you to flag any subset of these desiderata that looks jointly unsatisfiable—particularly whether the accessibility constraint (that normative guidance must be in principle available to the agent) can be reconciled with the anti-collapse constraint (that the idealized procedure not reduce to direct evaluation of actual reasons)—and to identify the single requirement that does the most damage to existing proposals. Keep the weight on the criteria and their tensions rather than on a scorecard of named theories, though a brief indication of where the canonical accounts stand is welcome. Finally, please state explicitly what would change your mind: which argument about the structure of human deliberation, or which empirical result about practical reasoning, would lead you to treat one of your non-negotiable requirements as dispensable, and where in your reasoning is the vulnerability greatest?
