For a workshop of about ten to twelve people steeped in rationalist forum culture—mostly philosophy or cognitive-science graduate students who have encountered the HCH idealization and factored-cognition framing in discussion but never tried to live inside it—design a ninety-minute structured deliberation game with small real stakes. The group should collectively decide how to distribute a limited pool of points (say, thirty dollars in actual mini-grants or a binding selection of which three proposed follow-up topics the workshop will pursue, with tangible consequences like snack allocation or scheduling priority). The point is to make them feel, through rules and payoffs rather than lecture, what changes when collective reasoning is forced into the HCH-style model: reasoning decomposed into independent sub-tasks executed by modular agents that communicate only through narrow, formal interfaces, with no shared emotional context, no hidden-state leakage, and no holistic oversight.

Give me explicit roles (for example: belief-updating, value-ranking, consistency-checking, meta-deliberation, aggregation), message templates restricted to tagged propositions with dependency lists and confidence scores, and a timeline—opening framing, modular execution, aggregation attempt, forced binding decision, debrief. The rules must make open discussion impossible; participants should experience coordination friction, the loss of implicit context, and the rigidity of pre-committed reasoning paths before anyone names the idealization. Include a payoff scheme that converts group success or failure into real but small rewards or penalties.

Build in one twist that is not part of the standard HCH discussion: a hidden “ghost module.” One participant is secretly assigned an objective that conflicts with the group’s explicit goal and can influence the decision only through the permitted interface format. Do not reveal this identity or objective until the debrief; use it to demonstrate how factored cognition can propagate subtly corrupted inputs that the aggregation mechanism never filters out, because no single module possesses the holistic context needed to detect the distortion.

Be frank about the gap: the simulation can reproduce modular isolation and interface rigidity, but it necessarily cannot reproduce [the feature you identify]; tell me precisely what that missing feature is and why I must supply it verbally after the activity ends.
