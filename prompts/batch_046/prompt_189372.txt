I am preparing a session for a philosophy-of-film seminar on absurdism and contemporary theories of meaning in life, and I want to use Harold Ramis’s Groundhog Day as the central case rather than the usual literary examples. The film places Phil Connors in a closed temporal loop in Punxsutawney: he retains full memory across repetitions, but for everyone else each February 2nd is entirely new, so his moral development, skill acquisition, and emotional growth occur in perfect isolation and, within the loop, leave no lasting social trace unless he eventually breaks free.

Read the film through the lens of Camus’s absurdism—especially the figure who lives without appeal in a world that offers none—and through contemporary hybrid accounts that hold meaning requires both objective value and subjective active engagement, as developed by philosophers such as Susan Wolf and Thaddeus Metz. Focus on three phases: Phil’s initial hedonistic exploitation of repetition, his suicidal despair when he realizes the loop is inescapable, and his eventual turn toward self-cultivation and other-directed care—the piano lessons, ice sculpting, saving lives, and finally earning Rita’s trust through genuine rather than calculated kindness. Where does the film seem to vindicate the absurdist claim that meaning can be generated without transcendent purpose, open-ended futurity, or lasting legacy? Where does it instead suggest that Phil only finds coherence by adopting objectively valuable projects pursued with full subjective investment—and does that investment depend on Rita’s recognition, which the loop initially withholds?

I am especially interested in a problem that the standard philosophical literature on meaning tends to assume away: whether meaning can survive what we might call diachronic closure, where an agent’s biography has no irreversible impact on others and no genuinely open future. Phil’s loop is an extreme version of this closure. Does the film imply that meaning collapses without the possibility of being witnessed or remembered, or does it show that private achievement and moral refinement are sufficient? And where does the film resist both readings—for instance, in the narrative fact that Phil escapes the loop only after he stops trying to manipulate Rita and instead acts selflessly, which restores linear time and social recognition in a way that looks like a resolution neither pure absurdism nor pure hybrid objectivism predicts? Please treat the film and the philosophical positions seriously in both directions: do not reduce Groundhog Day to an illustration of either Camus or Wolf, and do not distort the theories to fit the plot. Point to specific scenes and narrative choices, say where an honest critic must admit the film complicates or undermines the account, and tell me what this cinematic thought experiment adds to the debate—perhaps about the role of temporal openness, intersubjective acknowledgment, or moral luck—that a purely argumentative paper tends to miss.
