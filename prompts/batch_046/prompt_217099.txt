I'm working on a feature about why democratic budget processes always seem to have hidden loopholes, and I'm running a workshop for about 18 grad students in public policy — one session, 75 minutes — to make them feel the Gibbard-Satterthwaite issue rather than just reading about it. The setup is a climate-adaptation budget game: three competing projects (coastal defense, drought-resistant crops, urban heat mitigation) with a shared research fund. Each participant gets a private preference card plus a hidden "local knowledge" card that reveals something about another stakeholder's true priorities, but nobody knows the full map. One person plays the treasurer/fascilitator and applies a Borda-count rule in round one; everyone ranks the projects and earns credability points based on proximity to their true preference. In round two they can revise rankings after seeing partial tallies, using the same rule. The goal is for them to discover organically that strategic misreporting always pays off for someone, and that closing every loophole forces the mechanism into either dictatorship or collapsing the choice set down to two options.
