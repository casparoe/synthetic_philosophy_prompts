People often invoke "nullum crimen sine lege" against the Nuremberg and Tokyo tribunals, treating it as an obvious knockdown of retroactive charges—especially crimes against peace. But I think the slogan conceals several different objections, and I want them separated.

Could you lay out the precise theses "nullum crimen sine lege" could stand for in this context—distinguishing, for example, claims about enacted positive law, about customary international law existing in 1945, about subjective fair notice to defendants, about the moral wrongness of punishment absent prior prohibition, and about the institutional legitimacy of a court that defines its own jurisdiction? For each thesis, say whether it actually condemns the aggression charges at Nuremberg, whether it also condemns the parallel charges at Tokyo (where the legal charter came from MacArthur rather than a multilateral treaty), and whether it extends to war crimes and crimes against humanity that rested on prior conventions like The Hague and Geneva.

There's also a tension I keep running into. If the maxim demands explicit statutory enactment, then even well-established customary prohibitions on aggressive war wouldn't save it; but if it only demands moral foreseeability, then many ordinary domestic retroactive statutes survive too, which seems to undermine the special force of the objection against victors' justice. Which of the readings preserve a sharp asymmetry between international tribunals and ordinary legal development, and which collapse that distinction?
