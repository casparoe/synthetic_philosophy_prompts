Write a short story set in a near-future society where every citizen carries a calibrated sensor that displays, in real time, the exact marginal contribution of any action they take to an ongoing collective harm—measured not in vague carbon tons but in precisely quantified increments of systemic damage, visible to anyone who looks. The protagonist is a moral philosopher who initially embraces this transparency as the final refutation of the inefficacy objection: since the display confirms that her individual commute, meal, or vote shifts the aggregate needle by less than a rounding error, she concludes that personal obligation dissolves into statistical irrelevance. Over the course of the story, however, she discovers a hidden layer in the data: the collective system contains unpredictable, transient "threshold windows"—brief intervals during which the same marginal increment that was previously negligible suddenly becomes the decisive factor that pushes the harm past an irreversible tipping point. Because these windows are undetectable in advance and vanish without trace, she cannot calibrate her behavior to avoid them. The narrative should explore her attempt to formulate a coherent moral stance in response—whether constant ascetic restraint is rational if the dangerous windows are unknowable, whether moral responsibility requires actual causal efficacy or merely the reasonable risk of it, and whether perfect knowledge of one's own causal insignificance ultimately undermines or transforms the concept of individual duty. Treat the philosophical problem seriously rather than allegorically: the story should examine whether the inefficacy objection fails not because individuals do matter, but because the conditions under which they matter are structurally opaque to the agents involved.
