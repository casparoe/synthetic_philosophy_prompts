Analyze the 1991 testimony of Anita Hill before the Senate Judiciary Committee during Clarence Thomas's Supreme Court confirmation hearings as a case study in epistemic injustice, treating the episode not as a generic gendered credibility deficit but as an institutional decision structure in which the same body served simultaneously as evidentiary evaluator and political stakeholder with an interest in confirming the nominee. Examine whether Hill faced testimonial injustice in the allocation of credibility, and whether she faced hermeneutical injustice given that the concept of sexual harassment was already in legal and academic circulation by 1991—so that if a hermeneutical failure occurred, it was not an absence of conceptual resources but an institutional refusal to authorize their deployment. Apply two analytical distinctions: first, between "preemptive hermeneutical closure," in which the framing of the hearing (character assessment versus workplace conduct) constrained what could be articulated before testimony could reshape the terms, and "retroactive credibility calibration," in which the committee's evaluation of Hill's testimony was adjusted after delivery to preserve the legitimacy of the confirmation outcome; second, examine "distributed epistemic labor"—who within the institutional setting bore the burden of rendering Hill's experience intelligible, and whether that labor was acknowledged or erased. For each feature, state what the framework of testimonial and hermeneutical injustice explains well, what it misdescribes or leaves silent, and whether the episode confirms the theory, reveals an unanticipated variant (institutional authorization suppression rather than resource absence), or exposes a limit in applying individual-level credibility analysis to collective decision-making bodies. Keep the mapping disciplined: identify the specific players, the options available for belief and action at each stage, and the evidential standards the committee implicitly applied. Conclude with what the case shows about whether epistemic injustice requires a missing concept, or whether it can operate through the suppression of available concepts within stakeholder contexts where the evaluator and the beneficiary of the belief are structurally identical.
