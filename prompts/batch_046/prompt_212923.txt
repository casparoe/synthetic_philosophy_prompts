You serve on the governing board of a small residential cooperative that has long operated by unanimous consent for any expenditure over fifty pounds. One founding member, who has lived there for fifteen years, recently authorised a fifty-two-pound repair to the shared water pump during an emergency without convening the others. The board must now decide whether to enforce expulsion for breach of procedure, but members want to avoid weighing direct harms and benefits in an ad hoc way. Instead, they are attempting to determine the right course by asking what rule a fully rational, completely well-informed, and unbiased deliberator—one who knows every relevant fact about the cooperative’s history, the member’s past contributions, and the risks of the broken pump—would endorse for a community with exactly these people and this shared past. You have noticed that board members disagree not about the facts, but about how to construct that hypothetical standpoint: some insist that emotional attachments and particular relationships must be stripped away so that members are treated as interchangeable rational agents, while others argue that removing the specific history of trust and mutual reliance eliminates precisely the information that gives the decision its point. There is also disagreement about whether this kind of reasoning belongs only to formulating general institutional rules, or whether it can legitimately settle an exceptional individual case without committing a category mistake. You want to understand whether there is a principled boundary around what should be included inside the idealisation and what should be excluded, and how to proceed without mistaking one contested interpretation of this approach for the settled view. Please advise on the philosophical structure of this disagreement, clearly separating what is broadly accepted about reasoning through an idealised deliberative standpoint from what remains genuinely contested among those who work on these issues, and do not present a controversial position as if it were the consensus. Write your response in British English.
