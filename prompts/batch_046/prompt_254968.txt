Design a 90-minute essay question for an upper-level undergraduate seminar on ancient economic thought, centered on Aristotle’s account of money, exchange, and acquisition. The prompt must not ask for bibliographies, citations, or reading lists, and it should be answerable by a student who has studied the relevant texts but has no access to external sources. To prevent mere recitation of standard summaries—such as the oikonomia/chrematistike distinction, the barrenness of money, or the Midas story—build into the question a constructed hypothetical practice called "custodial circulation": goods are never permanently possessed but rotate through temporary custodians, each of whom receives a grain-denominated "holding increment" that is compulsorily reinvested into further circulation rather than consumed. Ask students to determine whether Aristotle’s framework can consistently condemn this practice as unnatural chrematistike without also undermining legitimate market exchange, and require them to identify the precise point in Aristotle’s analysis—whether the natural limit to acquisition, the measuring function of nomisma, the telos of the household, or the use-value/exchange-value distinction—where the judgment turns, and why alternative readings of that point fail.

Provide a model answer of approximately 700 words, followed by a marking scheme totaling 25 marks divided among five must-hit analytical moves. For each move, state the marks allocated, the specific content required, and a one-line description of the typical student failure. In both the model answer and the marking scheme, clearly separate what is settled in Aristotelian scholarship—such as the distinction between natural and unnatural acquisition and the conventional status of money—from what remains genuinely contested—such as whether Aristotle’s critique of interest (tokos) rests on a substantive metaphysics of sterility or can be reconstructed functionally, and whether his normative anthropology is indispensable to the argument. Do not present any contested interpretive position as the scholarly consensus.
