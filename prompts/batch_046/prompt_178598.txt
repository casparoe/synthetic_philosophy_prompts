I have been working through the stalemate between the Self-Sampling Assumption and the Self-Indication Assumption in anthropic reasoning, particularly where it intersects with Bostrom's well-known 'Presumptuous Philosopher' objection. At first glance, the dispute appears to be a technical disagreement about belief updating under cosmological models that predict vastly different numbers of observers: the SSA counsels reasoning as though one were randomly drawn from one's reference class, whereas the SIA takes one's own existence as direct evidence favouring theories that predict more observers. Yet despite decades of discussion—spanning multiverse cosmologies, Boltzmann brain scenarios, and various formal treatments of observer selection—the two positions remain entrenched, and the 'Presumptuous Philosopher' is treated either as a decisive reductio of SIA or as a question-begging appeal to third-person intuition that SIA need not respect.

My working theory is that the disagreement is not, at bottom, about probability calculus, but about whether reference classes are discovered or constructed, and whether indexical uncertainty is a feature of reality or merely an artefact of our epistemic position. The SSA camp tends to treat the sampling frame as a methodological constraint that must be fixed independently of the cosmological theory being evaluated, while the SIA camp treats observer existence as theory-laden evidence, which seems to presuppose that there is no neutral reference class prior to the update. Meanwhile, objections to the 'Presumptuous Philosopher' often turn on a distinction between first-person and third-person probabilities that SIA defenders reject as unmotivated. To complicate matters further, I have been wondering whether both frameworks tacitly assume a binary conception of observerhood—an entity either counts in the reference class or it does not—whereas if observer-moments can vary in phenomenal weight, or if artificial and biological consciousness form overlapping reference classes without a clear cardinality, then 'random sampling' may be undefined without an arbitrary metric of conscious intensity. This suggests that part of the stall may come from applying a crisp formal apparatus to an ontologically graded domain.

I am not looking for a survey of interpretations, but for a diagnosis of the disagreement's structure. Is it verbal—do 'evidence', 'update', and 'reference class' carry different meanings across the camps? Is there a hidden empirical or formal question—perhaps about the measurability of observer populations across theories, or about whether phenomenal weight can be quantified—that is actually doing the work? Or is it a clash of starting intuitions about indexical reasoning that neither formalisation has captured? Please engage concretely with what SSA and SIA proponents actually say about the role of priors, about whether the 'Presumptuous Philosopher' genuinely undermines SIA or merely exposes an unpalatable consequence that any adequate principle must accommodate, and about whether graded or overlapping observer classes dissolve the problem or merely relocate it. Finally, tell me what kind of development—a conceptual distinction that separates first-person evidence from sampling bias, a formal result linking reference-class choice to predictive accuracy, or an argument that anthropic reasoning is ill-posed when observerhood lacks sharp boundaries—would count as genuine progress, or whether we should expect permanent stalemate and why.
