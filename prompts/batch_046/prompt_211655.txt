I'm moderating a podcast interview with an unnamed philosopher who defends a restricted, transparency-bounded variant of the Lucas-Penrose anti-mechanist argument. Rather than claiming that human cognition exceeds every formal system, she holds that for any formal system S an agent can fully survey—what she terms an epistemically transparent system—the agent can recognize that if S is consistent then S cannot derive its own Gödel sentence, and that this recognition is not itself reducible to a derivation within S. She uses this to block mechanist accounts that treat mathematical insight as computation inside a fixed formal architecture, while conceding that opaque or dynamically revising architectures might escape the argument.

Draft eight to ten interview questions that press well beyond restating the view. Include at least one question on each of the following, with a follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give: (1) the consistency objection—that if S is inconsistent it proves its Gödel sentence, so her "insight" might just be an implicit detection of inconsistency; (2) how her surveyability condition distinguishes the human case from a computer running a proof-checker, and whether surveyability is a logical feature or merely a psychological limit; (3) the fallibility-parity objection—that humans can also be inconsistent—asking whether her view then applies only to idealized rational agents; (4) what her thesis implies for artificial systems deliberately engineered to be transparent, particularly whether the system's designers could predict its Gödel-recognition from outside; and (5) whether her restriction saves mechanist accounts that rely on non-transparent, evolving architectures, and if so whether the anti-mechanist claim is emptied of substantive content. Anticipate her standard replies—appeals to prior consistency commitment, to first-person epistemic access, to the difference between knowing and computing—and make each follow-up force her to either defend the distinction or absorb the cost. Keep the tone colloquial but precise, suitable for an audience familiar with Gödel's first incompleteness theorem but not requiring specialist bibliography.
