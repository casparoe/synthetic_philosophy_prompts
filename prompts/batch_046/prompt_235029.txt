I am enrolled in a graduate seminar on comparative economic systems, and during our recent session on interwar planning theory a fellow student offered a summary of the socialist calculation debate that struck me as suspiciously tidy. According to her account, Ludwig von Mises’s initial objection was strictly arithmetical: without market prices, socialist planners lack a common denominator to compare heterogeneous capital goods, which makes rational allocation logically impossible rather than merely inefficient. She then said that Oskar Lange answered this not by denying the need for prices but by proposing an iterative trial-and-error mechanism—essentially a simulated Walrasian auctioneer—through which a central board could adjust administered prices until supply and demand equilibrated, thereby solving the very same system of simultaneous equations that decentralized markets solve. From this she inferred that Lange had dissolved Mises’s logical impossibility claim entirely. Finally, she characterized Friedrich Hayek’s subsequent intervention as a retreat from logic to pragmatics: Hayek, she claimed, conceded that socialism was arithmetically feasible but argued that dispersed, tacit knowledge makes central computation prohibitively slow, so that markets win only on latency and parallel-processing grounds, not on any deeper epistemic principle. She concluded that the entire debate therefore collapses into an engineering comparison between centralized and distributed optimization algorithms, with no residual philosophical disagreement about the nature of economic knowledge.

I am skeptical that Hayek’s point about local, contextual knowledge can be reduced to a complaint about processing speed, and I also wonder whether Lange’s iterative model genuinely captures the dynamic, unforeseen adjustments that occur in actual markets rather than in a static equilibrium framework. Could you audit this summary claim by claim—Mises as pure arithmetic, Lange as solving identical equations, Hayek as latency—indicating which parts a historian of economic thought would accept, which involve subtle conflation or overstatement, and whether the conclusion that the debate is merely about algorithmic efficiency survives once the mischaracterizations are corrected? I would especially value your view on whether the residue—the parts that do not fit the optimization-algorithm framing—points to something genuinely missing in my classmate’s vocabulary or to something that was never as central to the original arguments as she assumes.
