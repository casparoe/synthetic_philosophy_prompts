I work as a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy, and a technically literate colleague recently summarized Hilary Putnam’s Twin Earth thought experiment and the doctrine of semantic externalism for me. The summary runs roughly as follows. Putnam asks us to imagine Twin Earth, a planet molecule-for-molecule identical to Earth except that its lakes contain XYZ rather than H2O. An Earthling and her doppelgänger on Twin Earth share identical brain states and psychological histories, yet when each utters “water is wet,” the Earthling refers to H2O and the Twin Earther refers to XYZ. From this, my colleague concludes that the meaning of natural-kind terms is not fixed by any internal mental state, but is determined entirely by causal-historical chains linking speakers to their environments. He further claims that the result generalizes: if two agents can be in identical internal configurations yet differ in reference, then no mental content—not even beliefs about logic or mathematics—can be purely internal. Finally, he presents Twin Earth as an empirical counterexample to internalism, analogous to running identical source code in different runtime environments and observing different outputs because of external dependencies.

I am inclined to accept the broad outline, but I want to check whether I can rely on it. Specifically, I am unsure about three points. First, does Putnam’s argument actually extend from natural-kind terms to mental content in general, or is that an overgeneralization? Second, is reference fixed entirely by external causal chains with no contribution from the speaker’s conceptual competence, stereotypes, or descriptive intentions? Third, is Twin Earth best understood as an empirical refutation of internalism, or does that mischaracterize the status of a conceptual thought experiment? More broadly, does the analogy to distributed software systems—where identical code behaves differently under different environment configurations—illuminate the philosophical point, or does it smuggle in assumptions about determinism and modularity that distort the original argument?

Could you audit this summary for fairness? Please identify which parts accurately represent Putnam’s view and standard semantic externalism, which parts are subtle misrepresentations or overstatements, and whether the conclusions drawn survive once the errors are corrected. I am especially interested in whether the internalist has a viable response that my colleague’s summary obscures, and whether the software-environment analogy is philosophically apt or misleading.
