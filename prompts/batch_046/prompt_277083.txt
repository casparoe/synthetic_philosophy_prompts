Here is an argument I constructed for a seminar on rational choice under ambiguity, and I now find it suspicious because its conclusion seems to evacuate normative guidance altogether. (1) If one's evidence about a proposition does not determine a unique precise credence but only fixes a convex set of probabilities—say, an interval over the likelihood that a particular non-human animal experiences morally significant suffering—then rational belief should be represented by that entire set rather than by an arbitrarily selected point within it. (2) Under imprecision, the canonical rational-choice constraint is E-admissibility: an action is permissible just in case there exists at least one precise distribution within the belief set for which that action maximizes expected utility. (3) Consider three options regarding ecosystem management: aggressive intervention to reduce predation (A), strict non-intervention (B), and targeted intervention only in human-modified environments (C). Given the current ambiguity about animal sentience, ecological dynamics, and long-term welfare outcomes, the expected-utility intervals for A, B, and C overlap for every convex belief set that respects the biological evidence. (4) By (2), A, B, and C are therefore all E-admissible; none is uniquely rational, and none is ruled out. (5) The same structural feature generalizes: in medical triage with ambiguous prognoses, climate policy with ambiguous damage functions, and distributive justice with ambiguous interpersonal comparisons, imprecise credence sets will almost always contain members that rank each competing option first. Hence imprecision does not produce localized indecision but a global permissiveness—whenever guidance is most needed, rationality permits everything. (6) A decision theory that systematically yields global permissiveness in precisely those domains where normative direction is required has failed its purpose. (7) So either imprecise probabilities misrepresent ambiguous belief, or E-admissibility is not the correct decision rule. I would prefer to preserve imprecision and reject (2), but I fear that abandoning E-admissibility forces an arbitrary collapse—whether to a maximally cautious maximin rule or to an unjustified point-estimate—that betrays the epistemic humility that made imprecision attractive. Which step should I reject, and what does each rejection cost?
