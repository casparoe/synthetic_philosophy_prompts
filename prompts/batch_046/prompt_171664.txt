I am convening a 75-minute seminar for eighteen final-year philosophy undergraduates, and I want a classroom activity that makes the doctrine of double effect feel conceptually necessary rather than merely intuitive, deliberately avoiding the overused trolley switch-and-push framing that students have already encountered in popular media. Design a structured role-play in which the group acts as a hospital resource-allocation committee facing four successive triage scenarios during a simulated supply crisis; each student receives a hidden role card—Clinician, Administrator, Patient Advocate, or Ethicist—and must vote and justify decisions in real time using only non-consequentialist vocabulary (for instance, terms such as 'directed at', 'foreseen but not intended', or 'instrumentalised'), without being told that any formal doctrine governs their reasoning. The payoff scheme should operate through credibility points: every participant begins with ten points, risks losing two if the group detects logical inconsistency in their stated reasons across cases, and can earn collective bonus points if the committee achieves unanimous justification for a decision; no real money should be involved. Crucially, case one and case three must present numerically identical outcomes—saving five patients at the cost of one death—but case one requires actively withdrawing a shared life-support resource from the single patient to redirect it (where the harm to the one is intended as a means to the saving), whereas case three involves altering the flow of the same resource so that the single patient dies as a foreseen side-effect of the redirection that saves the five. The rules and role incentives should be arranged so that participants feel the pressure to distinguish the two cases on grounds other than outcome, reconstructing something like the conditions of intention, proportionality, and moral acceptability of means through their own deliberation before any theory is named. Include a precise timeline, the exact instructions printed on each role card, a debrief that maps specific moments in the committee's deliberation onto the four conditions of double effect, an honest assessment of which feature—most likely the metaphysical individuation of intention within collective bureaucratic agency—the activity cannot reproduce and you must supply in closing discussion, and a frank prediction of what will go wrong on the first run together with how to manage it.
