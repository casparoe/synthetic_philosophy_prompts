I have been thinking about Simone Weil's argument that political parties are harmful because they demand a kind of total allegiance that replaces individual judgement with collective slogans. In conversations with politically active friends, one group insists she is describing a real psychological danger: party identity suppresses independent moral reasoning, even in well-meaning people. The other group replies that collective action is impossible without some surrender of private judgement, so Weil is attacking the necessary price of democracy. For months the discussion has not moved; everyone accepts the same examples of partisan behaviour, yet the two sides draw opposite conclusions. My own suspicion is that the stall comes from answering two different questions under one heading — one about whether parties damage individual cognition, the other about whether democracy can function without them — but I am unsure whether that is the real structure or whether we are facing an unarguable disagreement about whether loyalty to a group can ever be a virtue. Could you diagnose the kind of disagreement this is: verbal, substantive, hidden empirical, or bedrock intuition? Please explain what each side actually treats as evidence, whether a distinction or a historical case could break the impasse, and be honest about whether progress should be expected at all. Write your answer in British English.
