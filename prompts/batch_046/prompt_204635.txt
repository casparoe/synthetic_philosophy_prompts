You are a philosopher of institutions with particular attention to conservative political thought and the epistemology of institutional reform. Before answering, please take a moment to distinguish carefully what a practice strictly presupposes from what merely happens to fit it well, flag any points where the practice is philosophically underdetermined, and consider whether rival theories could license the same behavior.

Consider a pattern visible in the governance of selective universities in the United States and elsewhere. Defenders of legacy admissions preferences—the practice of giving favorable consideration to applicants with family alumni ties—routinely invoke reasoning analogous to what is often called Chesterton's fence: they argue that the preference should not be abolished until reformers can fully explain the hidden coordinating functions it may serve for alumni loyalty, institutional culture, and long-term resource stability, functions that present administrators might not be able to articulate or measure. At the same time, these very institutions have repeatedly eliminated long-standing core curriculum requirements—mandatory study of classical languages, comprehensive examinations, or distribution requirements spanning natural science and humanities—often on the basis of committee reports citing changing student needs, pedagogical fashion, or administrative efficiency, and without requiring proponents of elimination to demonstrate that they understand the original purposes those structures served.

What implicit theory of institutional knowledge, temporal authority, and the burden of justification does this asymmetry presuppose? Does it commit the university to a Burkean view that certain social arrangements are intergenerational partnerships carrying tacit practical wisdom that present reason cannot fully articulate, while treating other arrangements as purely instrumental means to current educational ends? Or can the asymmetry be defended by a consistent principle—perhaps about the reversibility of error, the kind of harm done by removal versus retention, or the distinction between private association and public curriculum—that does not depend on treating alumni relations as specially sacrosanct? Reconstruct the implicit philosophy, evaluate whether it is coherent or merely ad hoc, and describe what university governance would have to change if it consistently applied the fence principle to all inherited structures, or if it consistently rejected that principle and treated every legacy practice as open to instrumental revision whenever present goals shift.
