I am preparing a paper for a graduate seminar on historical method, and a line of reasoning my study group reconstructed now strikes me as suspicious because its conclusion threatens to dissolve the very project it was meant to serve. Here is the argument as we have it laid out.

(1) The quest for the historical Jesus aims to settle, with reasonable confidence, who Jesus actually was, as distinct from later theological constructions.
(2) The standard criteria of authenticity—dissimilarity from contemporary Judaism, dissimilarity from the early church, multiple independent attestation, coherence, and embarrassment—are the principal instruments designed to achieve that settlement.
(3) If these criteria are applied with full logical rigor and treated as jointly exhaustive filters, then any saying, action, or trait that Jesus shared with other first-century Jews, or that was retained and developed by his followers, must be judged inauthentic.
(4) A figure reconstructed exclusively from material that survives every one of these filters would therefore bear no meaningful relation to first-century Palestinian Judaism and no continuity with the movement that preserved his memory.
(5) But a figure with no such relations is not historically identifiable as Jesus of Nazareth at all; the criteria, pushed to their limit, dissolve the object they were designed to recover.
(6) Therefore, either the criteria cannot, even in principle, settle who Jesus was, or they must be applied selectively and probabilistically, which abandons their claim to objective arbitration.
(7) Yet scholars routinely claim that the criteria yield a meaningful, if minimal, profile of the historical Jesus, which suggests the quest is not self-defeating.

We are stuck between (4) and (6). The conclusion feels absurd—surely the criteria do not annihilate their own subject—but I cannot name the slip. Is there a hidden modal slide between treating the criteria as filters and treating them as conditions for identification? Or does the error lie in assuming they must be jointly satisfied rather than weighted probabilistically? Which premise should I reject, and what does that rejection cost? I would prefer to keep (1) and (2)—that there is a genuine quest and that these criteria are its central tools—so I am especially interested in whether the fault lies in (3)'s rigor or in (5)'s inference from alienation to non-identity. If you have to choose, I would rather preserve the idea that the quest can settle something substantive about Jesus, but I want to know exactly what I am paying for that preservation.
