I am drafting an essay for my advanced philosophy elective on the debate between internal and external reasons for action, and I want to move beyond a standard survey of the positions. My central claim is that Bernard Williams's internalist framework overlooks a distinct class of reasons that I call adoptive internal reasons: these are not discovered within an agent's pre-existing psychological makeup, nor are they purely external demands that apply regardless of her mental states; rather, they are reasons that an agent actively makes her own through a reflective process of commitment and practical-identity restructuring. To illustrate this, I discuss the case of a young composer who adopts a principle of formal austerity—stripping her work of ornament and decorative harmony—not because she already possessed a desire for simplicity, but because she judges artistic restraint to be worthy and then integrates that judgment into her self-conception. Once integrated, the principle motivates her without external coercion, functioning like an internal reason, yet its origin lies in reflective endorsement rather than in a prior desire or interest. I then consider the objection that this category collapses into externalism, since the initial judgment of worthiness appears to be an external reason that the agent merely internalizes. I respond that the act of adoption transforms the normative status of the reason, analogously to how taking ownership of a promise transforms an external expectation into an internal obligation, though I worry this reply may presuppose exactly the kind of identity-based authority it needs to establish.

Could you please comment on whether this middle category is genuinely coherent, or whether it inevitably collapses into either standard internalism or externalism? I am particularly interested in feedback on whether my example of the composer actually supports the distinction, and whether my reply to the collapse objection succeeds or relies on an unstated assumption about personal identity. Additionally, I would welcome observations about logical gaps in the argument as sketched, whether the framing is interesting enough to sustain a full essay without becoming repetitive, and whether there are hidden ambiguities in the notion of integrating a judgment into one's practical identity. Please do not provide a bibliography, reading list, or citations; I am working only with the conceptual resources I have already studied and want to test whether the argument stands on its own.
