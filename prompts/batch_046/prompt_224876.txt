In recent niche work on what some call “autopoietic artifacts,” a debate has deadlocked over a specific scenario: a large clay statue is built from a bio-ceramic composite substrate that continuously metabolizes and replaces its own particles. One camp—the Continuists—argues that the original statue survives this process because material constitution for artifacts is governed by continuous preservation of form and artistic function, not by shared matter; on their view, the statue is a functional kind that tolerates total material turnover. The opposing camp—the Replacementists—insists that without at least some direct material continuity or a causal chain anchored in the original clay substrate, what exists after full replacement is numerically a different statue, however qualitatively identical; they treat constitution as requiring overlapping material parts or spatiotemporal continuity of the original substrate.

Both sides agree completely on the physical and biological facts of the replacement process, and both concede that ordinary clay statues do not behave this way. The disagreement has persisted for years without either side conceding ground. My working hypothesis is that the dispute is not genuinely ontological but verbal: the Continuists use “statue” to denote a functional-artistic kind allowing total turnover, while the Replacementists use it to denote a material-object kind tied to origin and substrate continuity, so they are answering different questions under a single heading.
