Write the desiderata that an adequate philosophical theory of collective national responsibility for the outbreak of the First World War must satisfy. The theory should clarify what it means to hold a nation—rather than only particular decision-makers—responsible, in light of the Fischer controversy over intentional German planning, the fact that the architects of the Schlieffen Plan and the July crisis constituted a narrow elite unrepresentative of the wider population, and the problem of intergenerational attribution when present citizens are not identical to those of 1914. For each desideratum, provide the motivating case or conceptual distinction: for example, whether the theory must separate causal responsibility for mobilization orders from normative responsibility for the alliance structure; whether it must distinguish blaming the enduring state apparatus from imposing guilt by association on ordinary citizens; and whether it must allow a successor regime to repudiate war aims without dissolving the moral identity of the state itself. Flag any pair of desiderata that appear jointly unsatisfiable—especially those that require both a persisting collective moral agent across generations and a refusal to transfer liability to citizens who never consented to the conflict. Then briefly score three approaches against the list: an intentionalist view that traces blame to deliberate elite planning in the Fischer tradition; a structuralist view that distributes responsibility across systemic alliance dynamics rather than national will; and a contractualist view, injected here, that makes a nation's inherited responsibility depend on whether democratic successors have explicitly endorsed or repudiated the original war aims. Keep the emphasis on the criteria, their internal tensions, and the cases that motivate them rather than on a full historical chronicle. Write the answer in American English.
