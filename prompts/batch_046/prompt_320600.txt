I have been sketching the moral landscape for a novel set in a near future in which biotechnology can intervene directly on the qualitative structure of suffering—not merely suppressing symptoms, but reshaping the neural and affective architecture that generates negative experience. In trying to determine what my protagonist ought to endorse, I have arrived at a cluster of intuitive verdicts that seem to point in one direction and then suddenly fracture, and I want to know whether there is a coherent principle behind them or whether I am merely holding inconsistent commitments.

Consider the following cases. First, a germline edit that transforms human nociception into a purely informational signal, stripping physical pain of all negative valence while preserving its warning function, strikes me as not merely permissible but morally required: the suffering was redundant. Second, a voluntary post-bereavement therapy that collapses the emotional weight of grief into a detached, gentle recognition within forty-eight hours also seems humane and defensible—grief is suffering, and suffering of this kind is precisely what such technologies ought to eliminate. Third, extending analogous interventions to wild animals—re-engineering predator neural dynamics so that predation no longer involves the prey's terror, and so that starvation registers as neutral lethargy rather than agony—feels like a legitimate moral expansion rather than an arrogant overreach into nature.

Yet the pattern breaks in ways that trouble me. A civic biotechnology designed to suppress moral outrage—transforming the experience of injustice from an affective wound into a calm intellectual puzzle—feels deeply wrong, even though it would plainly reduce aggregate suffering and social friction. Similarly, a generational edit that eliminates the capacity for existential dread—the vertiginous awareness of mortality and cosmic insignificance that fuels certain philosophical and artistic temperaments—strikes me as a kind of theft, despite sparing future generations profound anguish. There is also a case on which I keep reversing myself: a targeted intervention that eliminates empathic distress, so that witnessing another person's pain produces no internal suffering in the observer. At first this seems like a kindness to the witness, but then I worry it severs the motivational bond that makes us respond to others in the first place.

Is there a principle—perhaps concerning the functional integration of affect with agency, or the distinction between suffering that serves a necessary relational purpose and suffering that is pure waste—that captures my approval of the first three interventions and my resistance to the latter two? Must I abandon one of my verdicts to preserve coherence? I am especially uncertain about the empathic-distress case: does the best version of such a principle predict that I should reject this intervention, accept it, or treat it as categorically different from the suppression of moral outrage? And what does the principle imply for a scenario I have not yet considered—say, a biotechnology that eliminates the suffering of unrequited love by rendering attachment purely volitional, leaving no residue of loss?
