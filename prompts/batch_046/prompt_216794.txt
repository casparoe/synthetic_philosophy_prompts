The Platonia dilemma involves 20 people and an eccentric trillionaire. If exactly one person sends a telegram by noon they get a billion dollars, but if nobody or more than one sends it then nobody gets anything and cooperation is banned. The superrational solution is that each person sends the telegram with probabillity 1/20, because all 20 agents are symetric and share the same reasoning so they coordinate on the same randomization. This gives about a 37 percent chance that exactly one sends it, which is optimal.

The Luring Lottery was the Scientific American contest where readers mailed postcards with a number indicating how many entries they wanted. The prize was one million dollars divided by the total number of entries, and a random entry won. To maximize expected value you should submit a number of entries equal to the total submitted by everyone else, but that creates a runaway reaction where everyone submits astronomically large numbers. The superrational fix, as Hofstadter explained, is to roll a die with sides equal to the expected number of responders and submit a 1 only if you roll 1. This is essentially the same logic as Platonia but applied to postcards instead of telegrams.

For the reverse pool with the $10 entry fee, the situation is isomorphic to Platonia because there are still 20 symetric agents, exactly one winner, and cooperation is forbidden. Therefore the superrational strategy is still to enter with probabillity 1/20. The entry fee doesnt matter because superrationallity only looks at the reasoning structure, not whether money flows in or out.
