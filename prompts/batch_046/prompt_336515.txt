You are a professional moral philosopher specializing in normative ethics, decision theory, and rational choice under normative uncertainty. I want you to help me reach reflective equilibrium on my own judgments about how to act when I am uncertain which moral theory is correct. Please proceed in three stages. First, brainstorm at least three candidate principles that could unify my verdicts below: include one complex principle that saves every verdict, one simpler principle that forces me to overturn at least one verdict, and one intermediate option. Second, state each principle precisely, map its clauses onto the specific cases, and show what it predicts for an unlisted case. Third, defend the principle you find most plausible and state honestly which of my original verdicts it cannot accommodate and why that revision is the least costly.

Here are my concrete verdicts on intertheoretic moral uncertainty—uncertainty about which normative framework is correct, not empirical uncertainty about facts.

Case 1 (anonymous charitable allocation): I assign 70% credence to a broadly consequentialist view that values saving lives proportionally and 30% to a deontological view that prohibits coercive recruitment methods even when they save lives. Charity A saves 100 lives per thousand dollars but uses mildly coercive recruitment. Charity B saves 20 lives per thousand dollars with fully voluntary participation. My verdict: I should donate to Charity A, computing expected moral value across theories without a special deference rule.

Case 2 (coercive climate policy): I assign 60% credence to a consequentialist climate framework and 40% to a rights-based view with strong property rights that prohibit uncompensated land seizure for collective goods. Policy C seizes private land to build solar infrastructure, saving millions of future lives, but violates property rights if the rights-based view is correct. My verdict: I should oppose Policy C. Despite my higher credence in consequentialism, the risk of this particular rights-violation crosses a threshold that blocks action.

Case 3 (long-term institutional design): I am roughly 50/50 between a biological-substrate view of moral status and a functionalist view. Option X imposes strict biological-only safeguards on computing development, preventing potential suffering of digital minds but stifling innovation and forgoing benefits. Option Y permits development, risking massive suffering if functionalism is true. My verdict: choose Option X, applying a maximin rule—minimize the worst-case outcome across the two theories—rather than averaging expected outcomes.

Case 4 (interpersonal deception, easily reversible): I am 75% confident that white lies are permissible to prevent unnecessary emotional harm and 25% confident that all deception violates autonomy regardless of consequence. A close friend asks whether I like their poor artwork, which they will display publicly. Telling the truth causes embarrassment but preserves autonomy; lying spares embarrassment but deceives, and the error is correctable later. My verdict: I should lie, going with my highest-credence view and giving no special protective weight to the minority autonomy-based view.

The pattern feels unstable. Case 1 uses expectation, Case 2 uses a hard threshold despite 60/40, Case 3 uses maximin at 50/50, and Case 4 reverts to simple credence-weighting with no protection for the minority. I have considered whether reversibility explains the split—Cases 2 and 3 involve irreversible harms, Case 4 is correctable, and Case 1 involves distributed anonymous harms—but that still does not explain why Case 2 gets a threshold while Case 1 gets averaging, since both involve serious harm. Is there one coherent principle—perhaps combining reversibility-sensitive weighting with a domain distinction between coercive state action and private charitable allocation, or between structural and parametric moral risk—that delivers all four? Or is the pattern genuinely pluralist?

Please also apply your leading principle to this unlisted case: You are a juror in a capital trial. You assign 65% credence to a consequentialist theory of punishment that permits execution if it maximally deters, and 35% to a retributivist-absolutist view that prohibits execution whenever factual innocence is not metaphysically certain. The evidence strongly suggests guilt but does not eliminate all doubt. The defendant will be executed if convicted. What is your verdict?

Conclude by listing which of my four original verdicts your best principle must overturn, explaining the precise clause of the principle that conflicts with it, and why overturning that particular verdict is less damaging to my overall moral outlook than overturning any of the others.
