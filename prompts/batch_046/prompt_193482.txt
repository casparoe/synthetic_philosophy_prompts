I just had a long argument with a friend about panpsychism and whether treating sentience as fundamental rather than emergent forces us to reckon with suffering distributed across physical processes we normally ignore—specifically, whether quantum decoherence or vacuum-level field interactions could involve something like micro-experience, and whether our usual ethical distinction between acting and allowing harm collapses once every physical system is a potential moral patient. I want to move past abstraction. Design a classroom activity for an upper-level philosophy seminar—twelve students, ninety minutes—that makes participants feel the practical pressure of this idea before they know its name. Arrange roles or forced choices with real, small stakes (no money required) so that students discover for themselves that if sentience is truly ubiquitous, ordinary action becomes ethically paralyzing in ways that mirror the aggregation problem. Include rules that generate the coordination or decision failure without a lecture up front, a debrief that maps moments in the activity onto the panpsychist argument and its standard objections, a frank admission of which feature—most likely the absence of subjective boundaries or the aggregation problem—the exercise necessarily cannot reproduce and must be supplied by the facilitator, and an honest prediction of what will predictably go wrong the first time it runs and how to correct for it. Keep it fully self-contained: I need a runnable game or staged deliberation, not a bibliography or reading list.
