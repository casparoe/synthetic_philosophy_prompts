I spent my career in experimental physics, and since retiring I have been working through some problems in political morality that seem to have the kind of structural complexity I used to enjoy in physical systems. One issue that keeps returning is what is usually called the problem of dirty hands in politics—situations in which a person with public responsibility appears to do something morally wrong in order to achieve a necessary collective good.

I have formed tentative judgments about particular cases, and I am trying to see whether they can be captured by a single principle or whether they force me to abandon some of the judgments. Here are my verdicts.

Case 1. A democratically elected leader authorizes the torture of a captured terrorist who possesses information about an imminent bombing that will kill thousands. The information is accurate; the bombing is prevented; the leader never reveals the authorization. My verdict: this is a clear case of dirty hands. The leader has done wrong, but the wrong was unavoidable for the public good, and she retains a moral remainder—she should not pretend her hands are clean.

Case 2. A senior bureaucrat is required to enforce an immigration statute she considers deeply unjust. She complies with the letter of the law but uses every discretionary tool available to minimize harm, saving some individuals from wrongful deportation while carrying out thousands of others. My verdict: this is also dirty hands, but of a structurally different kind. The wrong is distributed across an institution rather than concentrated in a single act, and her mitigation efforts do not fully cleanse the participation.

Case 3. A mayor takes a large cash bribe from a developer and directs the entire sum, without personal retention, to build a free clinic in an underserved district. The clinic saves lives. The mayor's family benefits indirectly from the developer's later projects. My verdict: this is not dirty hands at all; it is ordinary corruption. The presence of a public benefit does not transform corruption into political morality, especially when private gain runs through the chain.

Case 4. A revolutionary leader orders the execution of ten innocent hostages to compel a dictator to surrender and end a civil war that statistical projections suggest would otherwise kill millions. The dictator surrenders; the war ends. My verdict: this should count as dirty hands, but I am uncertain whether the category applies at all. The wrong seems so extreme that calling it merely dirty hands feels like an understatement; yet treating it as a completely different moral phenomenon seems to lose the structural similarity to Case 1.

Case 5. A judge knowingly applies a technically correct but morally draconian sentencing statute to a minor offender, refusing to exercise available judicial discretion that could have reduced the sentence, solely because she fears that leniency will trigger a political backlash that threatens her court's independence. My verdict: this is not dirty hands. The judge has failed in her duty of courage and discretion, but she has not done something morally wrong for the sake of a greater good; she has done something wrong for the sake of institutional self-preservation.

These five judgments feel mostly coherent, but I can see a tension between Case 2 and Case 3. In Case 2, participation in an unjust system with partial mitigation is dirty hands; in Case 3, participation in corruption with a public benefit is not. The distinction seems to turn on whether the wrong is intrinsic to the institutional role, but I am not sure I can make that precise.

I also have a tentative principle that I have been testing. Dirty hands occur if and only if: (i) the actor performs an intentional act that violates an ordinary moral prohibition; (ii) the act is genuinely necessary—no morally permissible alternative could secure the substantial public good at stake; (iii) the public good falls within the actor's legitimate institutional responsibility; and (iv) the actor retains a moral obligation to acknowledge, explain, or repair the wrong rather than conceal it permanently.

This principle captures Cases 1, 2, and 5 well, but it struggles with Case 3 (it might incorrectly include corruption if clause iii is interpreted loosely) and with Case 4 (clause i seems satisfied, but the scale of wrong may break the framework). I would like to know whether this principle can be repaired—perhaps by adding a clause about proportionality or about the absence of private interest—or whether it forces me to drop one of my verdicts, particularly Case 4 or Case 3.

Please also apply the resulting account to two cases I have not listed. First: a public health official deliberately delays the publication of accurate but alarming epidemiological data for two weeks to prevent mass panic that she judges would cause more deaths than the disease itself, then publishes the full data with an explanation. Second: a diplomat negotiates a treaty that requires her nation to abandon a vulnerable ethnic minority to an oppressive neighboring regime, but the treaty prevents a nuclear conflict that intelligence assessments suggest is otherwise probable. Does the principle classify these correctly, and does it predict that the official or diplomat retains a moral remainder?

Please provide your answer in American English. State the principle or candidate principles precisely, identify the clause that each of my listed cases motivates, indicate which of my verdicts must be abandoned if the principle is to remain simple, and be explicit about what the principle predicts for the two new cases.
