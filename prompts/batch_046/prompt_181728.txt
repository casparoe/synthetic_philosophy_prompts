Examination Question: Chomsky’s poverty-of-the-stimulus argument maintains that children acquire the capacity for recursive syntactic embedding—exemplified by center-embedded relative clauses—despite receiving linguistic input that is finite and largely lacking explicit negative evidence for ungrammatical strings. Critics respond that domain-general statistical or Bayesian learning mechanisms, operating over distributional regularities in heard utterances, could recover hierarchical structure without invoking an innate Universal Grammar. Focus specifically on the comparison between (a) true recursive embedding in relative clauses, and (b) hierarchical nesting of prepositional phrases within noun phrases. Does the poverty-of-the-stimulus argument apply with equal force to both? Student Answer: Chomsky’s core point is that children learn grammatical rules they were never explicitly taught and never heard corrected, especially for complex recursive structures like nested relative clauses—consider sentences such as the rat the cat the dog chased killed ate the malt, which people understand despite almost never encountering them in speech. This gap between sparse input and rich competence proves that Universal Grammar must be innate. Turning to prepositional phrases inside noun phrases, as in the book on the table in the room, the nesting is really just linear grouping guided by adjacency: each preposition sits right next to its noun, so a Bayesian learner can track these co-occurrence patterns without any built-in syntax module. Because the hierarchy here is shallow and surface-driven, the poverty argument does not apply; statistical learning handles it easily. Furthermore, Chomsky denies that any statistical mechanism plays a role in language acquisition at all, confining innate structure strictly to clause-level recursion. Consequently, even if Bayesian methods account for prepositional nesting, that success leaves his clause-level innateness thesis untouched, since he never claimed innate knowledge governs phrases. So the argument is strong for recursive clauses but weak or inapplicable for prepositional nesting.
