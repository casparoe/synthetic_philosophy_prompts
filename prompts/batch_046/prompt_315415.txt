I'm drafting a novel about a neolithic elder who refuses to plant seeds because she remembers the old ways, and my writing group got sidetracked arguing whether agriculture was actually humanity's worst mistake. Rachel was convinced it was: she talked about hunter-gatherer societies having more leisure, better diets, and flatter hierarchies, referancing that famous essay on original affluence and Diamond's argument about the worst mistake. She said once people start storing surplus, accumulation is automatic, then guards, then chiefs -- inequality is baked into the storage technology itself. Marcus pushed back hard. He said that's romantic projection onto sparse populations; farming allowed exponentialy growth, and judging the past by modern leisure metrics ignores that those "affluent" foragers had brutal infant mortailty and constant mobility demands. He also argued framing storage as "baking in" hierarchy is deterministic, since some agricultural societies stayed relatively egalitarian for centuries.

Rachel then brought up this distinction between immediate-return and delayed-return economies, saying it's not just about food storage but a total shift in temporal ontolgy -- in immediate-return societies the future literally doesn't exist as a planning domain, so comparing "welfare" across the two is comparing incommensurable worlds. Marcus rolled his eyes and said that's just poetic jargon that makes her argument unfalsifiable. They turned to me, the novelist, to referee, but I was honestly more interested in whether my elder character's refusal makes psychological sense given all this. So could you adjudicate point by point? Specifically: is the leisure and nutrition evidence strong enough to call farming a mistake? Does Marcus's population and inevitability point defeat Rachel's qualitative argument? Is the immediate-return versus delayed-return distinction doing real work or just relabeling?
