I've been reporting for several years on the regulatory architecture governing progressive cortical prosthesis, and a philosophical knot that clinicians treat as mere administrative routine has begun to seem unavoidable to me. In the neuroprosthetic consortia I cover—particularly the frameworks adopted in Northern European long-term degenerative-care programs—patients undergo what the protocols term continuous substrate migration: biological neural tissue is replaced sequentially by synthetic analogues over years, with each phase separated by at least a six-month integration window and constrained to preserve synaptic-weight mapping within a 98 percent functional-equivalence band. Under these rules, a patient retains full legal personhood, consent authority, and property rights regardless of how much cortex has become synthetic, provided the velocity of replacement remains below the institutional threshold. Yet the same framework contains an emergency surgical clause: if more than fifteen percent of a functional network is replaced in a single event, even with perfect functional replication and intact autobiographical memory, the patient is administratively classified as a successor entity and must petition for reinstatement of legal standing.

This strikes me as embedding a theory of personal identity inside a surgical-scheduling regulation. The practice appears to presuppose that continuity of the self is not a matter of psychological profile or functional architecture alone, but depends critically on the spatiotemporal velocity and distributed continuity of the replacement process—something that sits uneasily with both strict biological and standard psychological-continuity accounts. At the same time, the framework permits near-total biological replacement as long as it is gradual, which undermines any purely substrate-based criterion. I find myself wondering whether the rule is philosophically coherent: does it implicitly commit to a velocity-sensitive variant of psychological continuity, to a narrative-identity view that requires uninterrupted biographical threading, or is it simply an underdetermined risk-management heuristic that has accidentally acquired ontological weight?

Could you reconstruct the implicit philosophical commitments—about personal identity, moral status, and the boundaries of the self—that make this institutional pattern intelligible? Specifically, does the distinction between the six-month gradual rule and the fifteen-percent emergency clause strictly presuppose a particular metaphysical view, or is the practice compatible with several rival theories of identity? And if the underlying view turned out to be mistaken—if, for instance, identity tracks functional architecture regardless of replacement speed—what elements of the clinical and legal framework would have to be redesigned? I'm especially interested in whether the tension between these two clauses reveals an internal inconsistency in the institution's implicit ontology, or whether there is a principled way to resolve it without abandoning the core intuition that slow transformation preserves the patient in a way that rapid substitution does not.
