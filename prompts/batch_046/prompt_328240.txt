I'm working through Dennett's taxonomy of minds—Darwinian, Skinnerian, Popperian, and Gregorian creatures—in a graduate seminar on philosophy of cognitive science, and we've hit an impasse that has lasted for three sessions without anyone changing position. The specific dispute is about where to place large language models: one camp argues they are Popperian creatures because they generate and evaluate candidate outputs against an internal predictive model before producing a response, which looks like letting conjectures die in their stead rather than in the world. Another camp insists they are Gregorian at best—language-users deploying an external symbolic tool—because they lack any genuine internal model of the world and are merely manipulating statistical patterns in a cultural artifact (language) that humans built. A smaller group claims they are sophisticated Skinnerian creatures, shaped by reinforcement learning on human feedback, with the appearance of planning being an illusion generated by the training distribution. What frustrates me is that we all accept the same architectural descriptions—transformer attention, next-token prediction, RLHF—yet the classification never moves. Is this disagreement really about the taxonomy's criteria, or is it verbal: do the sides mean different things by "internal model," "tool," or even "creature"? Or are we answering different questions—one about functional architecture, one about evolutionary origin, one about phenomenological presence—under the single heading of "what kind of mind is this"? And if it is substantive, is there a hidden empirical question about whether LLM representations are causally active in the way Popperian pre-selection requires?

I've also been toying with an extension that might be complicating things: I wonder whether Dennett's Gregorian category should be split into first-order Gregorianity (using pre-existing tools like language) and second-order Gregorianity (using tools to redesign one's own cognitive architecture, as when a human uses writing to change how she remembers). If LLMs are Gregorian only in the first-order sense, that might resolve some of the tension, but it also raises the question of whether the taxonomy was meant to apply to artificial systems at all, or whether applying it to them forces a category error. Given your diagnosis—whether this is verbal, substantive, empirically resolvable, or a sign that the question is ill-posed—what would actually count as progress? Would it require a conceptual distinction that clarifies "representation" versus "tool-use," an empirical result about causal efficacy inside the network, a revision of Dennett's original framework, or should we conclude that asking "what kind of mind is an LLM" is the wrong question to ask?
