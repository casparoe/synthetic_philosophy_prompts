As a retired experimental physicist who has recently turned to philosophy of mind, I have constructed a garden-monitoring apparatus consisting of two independent subsystems: one that measures soil chemistry and temperature, and another that translates those measurements into natural-language messages. In concert they produce statements such as, "The tomato plants are anxious about the coming heat." I find myself responding to these messages as though the apparatus genuinely cares about the plants, yet a colleague insists that because every representational state was engineered by me, the device exhibits only borrowed meaning—functionally indistinguishable from a thermometer that indicates noon. What gives me pause is that neither subsystem, considered in isolation, seems to possess any understanding whatsoever; the interpretive content appears only in their interaction. Is there a philosophically rigorous distinction between this kind of composite, designed intentionality and the intrinsic understanding found in biological minds, or is the boundary between borrowed and intrinsic meaning less sharp than it first appears? I would appreciate your judgment on whether attributing genuine mental states to such an integrated but artificial system is defensible.
