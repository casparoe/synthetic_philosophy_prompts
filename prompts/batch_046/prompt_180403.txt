Draft the joint statement that a committed Evaluative-Judgment theorist and a committed Interoceptive-Perception theorist could both endorse after a good-faith adversarial collaboration on the nature of emotion. The Evaluative-Judgment camp maintains that an emotion is essentially an intentional evaluative judgment — a cognitive assessment of an object's significance for one's well-being, values, or practical commitments — and that accompanying bodily changes are contingent effects rather than constitutive parts of the emotion itself. The Interoceptive-Perception camp maintains that an emotion is essentially the conscious perception of organized patterns of visceral, musculoskeletal, and autonomic change, and that any evaluative or intentional content is either a secondary attribution or a non-constitutive overlay added by higher-order processing.

The collaboration should treat as central test cases two phenomena that sit awkwardly with both frameworks: first, the state of epistemic vertigo — the disorienting affect triggered by seriously entertaining radical skeptical hypotheses, which seems to lack both a clear bodily signature and a determinate propositional object; and second, so-called aesthetic emotions such as the experience of the sublime, which appear to involve intense feeling without the kind of personal-evaluative judgment that the first camp requires and without the discrete interoceptive profile that the second camp predicts. The deliverable must contain a numbered section of agreed claims — including what both sides accept about the intentionality of emotional experience, the causal and informational roles of bodily feedback, and the phenomenology of epistemic vertigo — followed by a disagreements table. In that table, each camp's core thesis must be stated in wording that its own members would accept; the cruxes must be listed as specific sub-questions whose settlement would move at least one side; and for each crux, the relevant argument or empirical consideration both sides acknowledge as bearing on it must be described, with each camp's explicit prediction about the outcome recorded in advance. Please do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations, and do not invoke specific named theories or empirical studies by author or date; rely instead on conceptual reasoning and generally accepted structural features of interoceptive and prefrontal processing.
