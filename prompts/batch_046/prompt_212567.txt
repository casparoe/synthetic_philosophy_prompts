I cover science and climate policy for The Meridian Quarterly, and I serve on its editorial advisory board. Our climate editor has proposed that we adopt a formal intergenerational fairness protocol for all major climate coverage: every feature must include an explicit assessment of impacts on people who will live after 2100, weighted equally with present stakeholders, and must avoid framing that implicitly discounts future welfare through present-biased cost-benefit analysis. The protocol also directs editors to treat the non-identity problem—the fact that different climate policies produce different future populations, so no specific future person can be said to be made worse off by our emissions—as a reason not to rely solely on person-affecting harm claims, but instead to ground obligations in impersonal values such as the quality of the inherited environment. Our publisher objects that this embeds a substantive moral framework into what should remain journalistic neutrality, and that requiring editors to apply distinctions between person-affecting and impersonal reasons exceeds both our competence and our mandate. The board must issue a recommendation—adopt the protocol outright, reject it, or adopt it with specified conditions—before next month’s issue-planning meeting. Draft the advisory board’s decision memo. It should recommend a concrete course of action with an implementation timeline if adopted, engage seriously with the publisher’s neutrality concern rather than dismissing it, and address the philosophical tension between using impersonal reasons to justify strong climate obligations and the intuitive pull of the claim that we wrong future people by degrading the world they will inhabit. The memo should also state clearly what philosophical commitment the board would be endorsing by choosing adoption, rejection, or conditional adoption, and identify the point at which further editorial judgment must take over because the question becomes one of values rather than procedure.
