Is there a name for this? In practical decision-making, when an agent faces two options that are genuinely equivalent by every criterion they originally cared about—same expected value, same moral weight, same practical cost—they rarely stay paralyzed like Buridan's ass. But they also don't always resort to bare randomization. Instead, they often generate a new tie-breaking principle that was not part of their preference structure before the deadlock appeared: deciding they will always take the left-hand option in ties, treating the result of a coin flip as binding authority rather than mere chance, or suddenly appealing to an aesthetic symmetry they never valued and do not plan to value in other contexts. The principle seems to exist solely to dissolve the stalemate, not to express a prior commitment or hidden preference. Is there an established concept for this specific move—manufacturing a decisive rule ex nihilo specifically for a preference deadlock? If so, how does it differ from rationalization, from arbitrary selection, and from genuine pre-commitment strategies like randomization? If not, can you coin and precisely define a term for it, including boundary cases—such as whether it still counts when the agent permanently adopts the new principle, or only when it is invoked transiently for this single choice?
