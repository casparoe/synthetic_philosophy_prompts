I'm attempting to determine what principle governs my judgments about commemorative removal and retention, testing them against concrete cases. Removing a public statue of a colonial administrator from a central civic plaza and destroying it: this seems correct. Renaming the same figure's street, which functions as the primary postal and navigational reference for thousands of residents: more ambiguous, perhaps wrong if done without preserving functional continuity. Relocating the statue to a museum with extensive curatorial framing that explicitly repudiates the figure's actions: acceptable, perhaps preferable. Stripping a scientist's name from an institute he founded, given his advocacy of eugenics, while retaining his papers in the library's archival collection: I lean toward doing so, though I'm uncertain whether this is consistent. A municipality that dismantles a monument to an industrialist who exploited child labor, then sells the bronze to endow a scholarship bearing his name: this strikes me as morally incoherent. Renaming a school after a living donor in direct exchange for funding that prevents its closure: not permissible, yet I struggle to say whether the wrongness is the same as in the industrialist case. Is there a unifying principle—perhaps a distinction between honorific and archival uses of names, or between collective endorsement and historical documentation—that captures all of these? Or must I abandon one of my verdicts? Apply your analysis also to the case I return to repeatedly: a town retains a street named after a revolutionary leader implicated in local atrocities solely because altering it would disrupt the only geographic reference system used by emergency responders. Does this functional dependency justify retention, or does it compound the wrong?
