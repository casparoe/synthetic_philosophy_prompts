Professor A. Reed, a philosopher of mind who accepts the extended mind thesis, has rendered consistent verdicts on whether particular artifacts, devices, or processes are constitutive parts of a subject's cognitive system. Her judgments are as follows. First, she holds that a person with severe memory impairment who relies on a meticulously organized, self-built notebook—consulting it automatically, treating errors in it as errors in her own knowledge, and feeling disoriented without it—is engaged in extended cognition, with the notebook functioning as part of her memory system. Second, she holds that a student who actively decides to search a digital archive of lecture notes, compares the results to his internal understanding, and never treats the archive's outputs as automatically authoritative is not exhibiting extended cognition. Third, she holds that a cochlear implant that restores auditory processing, is experienced transparently, and generates a felt deficit in spatial awareness when deactivated is part of the subject's perceptual-cognitive system. Fourth, she holds that a chef who keeps a handwritten recipe journal but consults it only before cooking, relying entirely on internal skill and taste during execution, is not extending cognition through the journal. Fifth, she holds that a mathematician who has used the same physical abacus since childhood, manipulates it automatically without conscious calculation, reports that she feels computations through her fingers, and experiences reasoning as disrupted when it is removed—though she can eventually compute slowly without it—is engaged in extended cognition through the abacus.

Now consider a new case. Professor Reed is evaluating Professor K., a historian of science who has developed a unique working method over two decades. Professor K. never writes down her own working hypotheses. Instead, she maintains a long-term collaborative arrangement with two research assistants whom she has trained extensively. They maintain a shared, spatially organized filing system of conceptual distinctions and prior arguments. Professor K. thinks aloud in their presence; they correct inconsistencies, retrieve relevant claims, and reorganize files based on her habitual gestural cues. She treats contradictions they present as errors in her own reasoning, reports that her thinking feels thin and incomplete when she works alone in a different office, and describes her reasoning as occurring through them. However, the assistants are autonomous conscious agents with independent beliefs; the system requires active social interaction and interpretation rather than direct access to an artifact; and Professor K. can, with substantial time and effort, reconstruct any specific distinction from her own published papers.

If Professor Reed wants to be consistent with her own track record, what should she decide about Professor K.'s case? Identify the closest analog among her five earlier verdicts and explain why. Then identify at least two distinct principles that could jointly explain her earlier verdicts, show how each principle applies to Professor K.'s case, and state which principle Professor Reed should adopt and why. Finally, specify what further case—one not listed above—would clearly distinguish between these two principles. Be honest about whether her precedents genuinely underdetermine the outcome. Write your answer in American English.
