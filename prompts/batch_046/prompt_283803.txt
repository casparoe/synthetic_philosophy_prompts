I am drafting a chapter in my novel about Dr. Elara Voss, a mathematician who has withdrawn to a coastal archive and spends her evenings constructing philosophical instruments she never publishes. For one scene, I want her to design a diagnostic taxonomy—essentially a decision tree—that sorts positions on the Church-Turing thesis according to their underlying epistemic architecture, not according to which academic department defends them. The tree should begin with a root question about whether "effective calculability" is anchored in physical dynamics, in a normative model of rational agency, or in an abstract formalism with no intended interpretation. From there, each branch should split on the treatment of counterexamples: specifically, whether oracle machines, analog continuous processes, and noisy quantum circuits are conceptually incoherent, mathematically legitimate but physically unrealizable, or genuinely open empirical possibilities.

The final level should distinguish the thesis's epistemic status—empirical conjecture, definitional convention fixing the term "algorithm," or modal boundary claim about all logically possible computing devices—so that the leaves represent combinations of these commitments rather than lists of named philosophers. Wherever the literature actually offers a defender, assign a provisional name, such as the classical physicalist interpretation, the definitional conventionalist view, or the principle-based physical stance; wherever a combination seems coherent but unoccupied, mark it "unnamed" and append one sentence on its most awkward commitment, for example the position that accepts physically realizable oracle computation yet denies this undermines the thesis's normative authority. Please also flag any two leaves that your tree genuinely cannot separate, particularly those that differ only in rhetorical framing about "effective" procedures rather than in substantive commitments regarding noise, continuity, or modal possibility. Every node should be answerable without knowing philosophical labels in advance, and the instrument must avoid any request for bibliographies, citations, or reading lists, since Dr. Voss intends this as an exercise in pure conceptual cartography.
