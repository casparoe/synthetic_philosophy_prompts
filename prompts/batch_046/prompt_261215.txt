Please compose a satirical, deadpan bureaucratic manual—approximately 700 words, formatted as an official inter-agency standard operating procedure—issued by the fictional Office of Material Hermeneutics (OMH, Form 27-B). The manual governs field protocols for assigning "institutional belief states" to recovered artifacts when reconstructing defunct administrative or religious institutions. The target of the satire is the epistemic overreach common in archaeological inference: the tendency to treat material patterns as transparent windows onto collective intentionality, despite the fact that objects systematically underdetermine the beliefs and institutional structures attributed to them.

To ensure conceptual precision without dependence on obscure named theories, construct the manual around three invented but philosophically rigorous categories that you define in situ:
(i) "Indexical residues"—objects that causally indicate institutional presence but bear no conventional propositional content (e.g., standardized weights, abrasion patterns);
(ii) "Symbolic residues"—objects that conventionally encode specific propositional or doctrinal content (e.g., inscribed seals, standardized ritual vessels);
(iii) "Continuity artifacts"—objects materially identical to earlier institutional forms but physically preserved and reused by successor communities with divergent practices, generating false positives for institutional persistence.

The manual should prescribe explicit decision rules for classifying recovered items into these categories and for computing a quantitative "belief-attribution confidence score" (BACS). The rules must be internally consistent and technically precise, yet they should quietly yield absurd consequences: for instance, a broken seal and its corresponding clay impression must receive divergent BACS ratings despite being causally co-produced; a continuity artifact must receive a high institutional-persistence score precisely because it has survived intact, even when the manual's own classification criteria indicate doctrinal discontinuity.

Maintain a strictly deadpan voice—no explicit acknowledgment of the satire, no authorial winking. A specialist in material culture studies should recognize the exaggeration of actual practices (inferring theological commitment from ceramic distribution, reading hierarchical authority into storage architecture, multiplying unobservable collective mental states to resolve ambiguity). A non-specialist should understand the bureaucratic logic while sensing that treating broken pottery as direct evidence for a specific institutional belief is epistemically suspect.

Do not include bibliographies, citations, footnotes, or references to named scholars or theoretical frameworks. You may invent form codes, institutional titles, and classification rubrics. Keep the analysis in the conceptual weeds: address specific borderline cases, such as a seal impression executed after the dissolving of the authority that originally commissioned the seal matrix, or a standardized metrological system adopted by an institution whose surviving non-material records explicitly reject quantification—without quoting any actual historical texts. The final document should read as a genuine administrative protocol that has subtly, but unmistakably, gone wrong.
