Having spent a career working with differential equations and boundary-value problems in physics, I have recently begun reading in the metaphysics of time and find the standard framing of presentism versus eternalism unsatisfyingly detached from the structure of physical law. I would like you to argue for a specific position: that presentism can be defended not by appealing to a mysterious idea of a moving present, but by treating the present as an ontological boundary condition—structurally analogous to the initial or boundary conditions required for a well-posed dynamical system to have a unique solution. On this view, temporal becoming is not an illusion superimposed on a static four-dimensional manifold, but the necessary asymmetry that grounds why physical laws function as initial-value problems rather than as symmetric constraints across an equally real past and future.

Specifically, please construct the argument that eternalism fails precisely because it renders all temporal slices equally real and complete, thereby eliminating the ontological distinction that allows causation to operate directionally and that permits the determinacy of future states from present conditions. You should address the objection that general relativity describes a four-dimensional spacetime manifold that seems to treat all points equally, and explain why treating that manifold as a mathematical representation rather than as an ontological inventory does not commit one to presentism's denial of the reality of past or future events, but rather to a more restricted claim about which events possess determinate being. Keep your argument conceptual rather than empirical: do not cite specific experiments, and avoid appeals to authority or bibliography. I am interested in whether this boundary-condition reconception of the present can bear the philosophical weight usually assigned to it, and where its weakest points lie.
