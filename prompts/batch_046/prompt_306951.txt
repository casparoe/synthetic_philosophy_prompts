After a long argument with a friend about which philosophical thought experiments genuinely illuminate and which merely manipulate our gut reactions, I want to test whether my verdicts form a coherent pattern. Here are my judgments.

The trolley switch strikes me as legitimate: it isolates whether directing an existing threat differs morally from introducing a new one, and my intuition survives careful reflection.

The footbridge push strikes me as misleading: the visceral aversion to physical contact contaminates the judgment, yet people treat the emotional difference as if it revealed a deep moral boundary.

The violinist analogy for bodily autonomy strikes me as illuminating, but only if one already accepts that sovereignty over one’s body works like property rights; without that framing it begs the question rather than pumping intuition.

A scenario I constructed: imagine a brain replaced neuron-by-neuron with functionally identical silicon analogues, done over fifty years versus in a single second. I feel the slow version preserves personal identity and the fast version does not, despite identical functional profiles at every intermediate stage. I think this reveals that our concept of persistence tracks narrative continuity rather than functional organization, yet I worry the pump secretly relies on an unexamined assumption that temporal binding carries metaphysical weight.

The argument that a system manipulating symbols by rules alone lacks understanding—often illustrated by a person in a room following a rulebook—initially persuades me that syntax is not semantics. On reflection, however, I think it misleads by asking us to imagine understanding being absent inside a system that already operates with perfect syntactic rules, which presupposes exactly the separation it claims to demonstrate.

A gradient argument for moral status asks us to imagine organisms arranged along a continuous spectrum where the capacity for suffering diminishes gradually with no sharp cutoff. I think this legitimately undermines essentialist species boundaries, but my friend insists it merely exploits our discomfort with vagueness rather than revealing anything about moral status.

Is there one principle that distinguishes the pumps I accept from those I reject, or must I abandon one of these verdicts? If a principle exists, what does it predict about the case I keep oscillating on—whether observing another person’s pain through a perfect real-time neural simulation counts as direct empathy or as technologically mediated representation? And if no single principle saves them all, which verdict is the outlier?
