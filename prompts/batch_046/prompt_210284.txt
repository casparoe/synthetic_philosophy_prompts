For a graduate seminar I am convening on comparative moral metaphysics, I am preparing a classification handout on karma conceived strictly as a theory of moral desert—that is, the claim that there exists a non-instrumental, backward-looking relation between the moral quality of an agent's actions and the subsequent quality of that agent's experience, operating independently of any social or legal enforcement mechanism. Rather than organizing the discussion by religious tradition, I want the handout to take the form of a decision tree that sorts positions according to answers to concrete, label-independent diagnostic questions. The root node should ask whether one accepts any such non-instrumental desert relation at all, which I will call the Desert Thesis. Those who reject it should branch toward either consequentialist denial or institutionalist reduction, in which apparent desert is merely socially constructed reward and punishment. Those who accept it should then face a split between causal mechanisms—where moral behavior shapes future experience through psychological feedback, reputation, or social causation—and acausal mechanisms, in which the link between act and consequence requires no spatiotemporally continuous mediator.

Within the acausal branch, the tree must further divide on whether the transmission of desert requires the persistence of a substantial self or a continuous stream of consciousness across the gap, or whether a "loose" karmic transmission can attach the consequence to a merely causally related successor without strict personal identity. At every leaf, whether occupied by a named view—such as Theravada karmic realism, secular moral-luck compatibilism, or eliminativist desert-scepticism—or left unoccupied, please include a one-sentence statement of that position's most awkward commitment. In particular, I want you to flag whether the taxonomy can genuinely separate strict retributivism, in which specific wrongs merit specific sufferings, from a quantitative "moral accounting" model in which virtue and suffering balance as fungible quantities rather than as paired responses, and to note any pair of leaves that the tree cannot cleanly distinguish. The audience is familiar with basic meta-ethics but not with Indological scholarship, so avoid appeals to untranslated technical vocabulary, and please do not supply bibliographies or citation lists, since the point is to map the logical space rather than to direct further reading.
