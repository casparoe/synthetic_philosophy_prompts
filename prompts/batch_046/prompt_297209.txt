Read Akira Kurosawa's Rashomon (1950) through the lens of peer disagreement, specifically the debate between conciliationism and steadfastness in epistemology. The film presents four incompatible first-person accounts of a single violent encounter in a forest—those of the bandit Tajomaru, the samurai's wife Masako, the dead samurai Takehiro (spoken through a medium), and the woodcutter who claims to have witnessed it. Each narrator is, in some respects, an epistemic peer to the others: none has obvious cognitive superiority, all have direct access to parts of the event, and all have powerful incentives to distort.

First, take the standard conciliationist view: when you discover that an epistemic peer disagrees with your judgment, rationality requires you to split the difference or at least reduce your confidence. Does Rashomon show that conciliation is impossible or undesirable here? The witnesses do not revise their beliefs upon hearing the others; they harden their narratives. But the film also suggests that their disagreement is not merely about a discrete fact—whether the dagger or the sword delivered the fatal blow—but about the moral and explanatory framework that makes the event intelligible: honor, shame, desire, survival. Ask whether conciliationism applies differently when the disagreement concerns not just what happened but the narrative that renders it coherent. If the disagreement is at the level of explanatory framing rather than isolated propositional content, does the conciliationist have any guidance, or does steadfastness become the only rational posture?

Second, consider the woodcutter's account. He is initially silent at the gate, then offers a version that undermines every other testimony without elevating himself. Is he a peer who has withheld judgment, or a non-peer whose detachment gives him authority? The film ends not with resolution but with the woodcutter's act of adopting an abandoned infant—an ethical choice that seems disconnected from the epistemic chaos. Does this ending imply that in cases of radical peer disagreement, the correct response is not to adjudicate belief but to act despite uncertainty? And if so, does the film vindicate a form of practical steadfastness—standing firm in moral action—while remaining epistemically conciliationist?

Finally, ask what Rashomon adds to the philosophical debate that armchair argument misses. The film does not merely illustrate disagreement; it dramatizes how self-narrative, shame, and the need for dignity make belief-formation inseparable from identity. Where the literature often treats peer disagreement as a problem about credence allocation over shared evidence, Kurosawa shows that to conciliate might require abandoning the story by which one lives. Does the honest critic have to say that the film therefore resists the conciliationist framework—not by refuting it, but by showing that its conditions (detached, interchangeable peers assessing common evidence) are rarely met in human experience? Or does the film ultimately support a modified conciliationism that demands humility about one's own narrative framing?

In particular, address whether the disagreement among the four witnesses is genuinely a case of peer disagreement in the philosophical sense, or whether the film shows that the conditions for peerhood collapse once belief is bound up with self-preservation and moral identity.
