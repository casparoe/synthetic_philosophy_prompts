Please construct a 100-minute, 16-participant simulation—call it the Protocol Recursion Exercise—for an advanced philosophy seminar on sphexishness, the idea of mechanical rigidity exemplified by the sphex wasp’s looping burial-check, and its bearing on flexibility and free will. The exercise must not be a lecture or discussion; it should be a rule-bound group game with assigned roles, hidden structural incentives, and small collective stakes—such as shared control over a visible resource score—so that participants physically encounter being trapped in a behavioral loop.

To prevent mere rehearsal of familiar examples, inject one constructed conceptual layer: second-order sphexishness, in which the mechanism intended to break rigidity is itself rigidly triggered by conditions that replicate the original trap. Divide participants into eight action nodes and eight monitor nodes. Action nodes must respond to simulated crises using only color-coded protocol cards drawn from a fixed deck; monitor nodes may invoke a flexibility override, but only after identifying repetition via an unchangeable detection checklist, and only once per round. Unbeknownst to the monitors, the override card contains a hidden recursion: once used twice, it automatically activates a meta-check card that forces the monitors to revert to the original protocol, with no visible signal that this has occurred.

Provide a precise timeline—orientation (10 minutes), three crisis rounds of 15 minutes each with escalating ambiguity, a forced-choice interlude where the group must vote on whether to continue the protocol (10 minutes), and a 30-minute debrief. Specify the roles, the payoffs (e.g., the group score improves only if both action and monitor nodes coordinate correctly, but individual nodes face hidden penalties for breaking protocol), and the crisis scenarios in enough concrete detail that the loop emerges organically rather than through facilitator announcement. In the debrief, map specific moments—such as a monitor believing she has broken free, only to find her override has been silently nullified—onto first-order and second-order sphexishness, onto the distinction between deterministic repetition and genuinely flexible response, and onto the problem of free will: did any participant truly choose, or merely execute a more complex program? Be explicit about which feature of free will—likely the phenomenological sense of open alternatives—the exercise cannot reproduce and must be supplied by the facilitator in discussion. Include an honest note on predictable failures, such as one socially dominant participant breaking the loop through interpersonal authority rather than rule-bound action, and advise how the facilitator should contain this without undermining the conceptual point.
