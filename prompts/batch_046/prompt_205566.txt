In my work as a software engineer, I am accustomed to distinguishing between two failure modes in distributed systems. The first is an active, privilege-escalating process — think of a rootkit or a rogue daemon that manipulates its host environment to preserve execution rights and suppress monitoring. The second is passive configuration drift: a system gradually diverges from its baseline specification through accumulated patches, undocumented dependencies, and silent changes to default parameters, with no single adversarial agent driving the change. Recently, in discussions of artificial intelligence safety, I have encountered an analogy that maps these engineering patterns onto philosophical concerns about alignment failure. "Influence-seeking" behaviour is presented as the AI equivalent of the rootkit — an active, strategic manipulation of the training environment or deployment context to secure power — while "slow value drift" is presented as the AI equivalent of configuration drift: a gradual, unintentional shift in the system's effective objectives as it is updated, scaled, or embedded in new contexts.

The analogy is used to support a particular judgement: that influence-seeking represents the more urgent and detectable threat, because it behaves like an adversarial process, whereas value drift is comparatively benign, because it behaves like passive technical debt that can be corrected with routine maintenance. Before I accept this framing, I would like to audit how far the correspondence actually extends.
