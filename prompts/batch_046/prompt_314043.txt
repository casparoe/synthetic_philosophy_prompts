Write a two-page research proposal for a one-semester graduate workshop on conceptual copycatting and fluidity in the microdomain of mechanistic explanation. The proposal should develop a precise, tractable question: when philosophical accounts of mechanistic explanation—developed originally for biochemical and neural systems—are copied into the technical literature on mechanistic interpretability in large neural networks, which conceptual features survive intact, which become fluid (losing determinacy or gaining unintended entailments), and which collapse entirely?

State the question so that it admits of a determinate answer rather than a broad literature survey. Explain why existing treatments leave it open: philosophy of science typically analyzes mechanism within a single domain and treats cross-domain extension as metaphorically unproblematic, while interpretability research borrows philosophical vocabulary without tracking whether interventionist counterfactuals, hierarchical decomposition, and entity-activity organization apply literally to transformer circuits or only analogically. Propose a comparative conceptual-analysis method: apply three canonical criteria for mechanistic explanation systematically to one biological case study and one computational case study, documenting at each stage whether the criterion survives, distorts, or fails. Specify what the result would look like (a taxonomy of survival, partial fluidity, and collapse mapped onto specific criteria), what would count as failure (if the analysis yields only binary intact/collapsed results with no intermediate slippage patterns), and a realistic timeline (e.g., literature consolidation in weeks 1-3, case-study selection and criterion operationalization in weeks 4-6, comparative analysis in weeks 7-11, synthesis and writing in weeks 12-14). Do not include a bibliography, citation list, or reading list; refer to positions and arguments in general terms, noting that specific references will be inserted later. The proposal should be suitable for evaluation by philosophers of science and computer scientists with standard graduate-level familiarity but no expectation of specialized prior knowledge.
