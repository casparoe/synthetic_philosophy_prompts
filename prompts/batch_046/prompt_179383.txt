I'm leading a single seminar for a philosophy-of-science reading group of graduate students who have worked through basic epistemology and some philosophy of science but have never studied archaeology; they will arrive thinking that material patterns largely speak for themselves if one is sufficiently careful, and that archaeology is mainly a technical matter of preservation, stratigraphy, and dating. I want them to grasp how archaeologists reconstruct institutional beliefs—shared, authoritative norms about value or obligation—from silent material culture alone, specifically the inference from standardized artifacts to a collective commitment that the artifacts have a regulated, non-utilitarian status. The conceptual dependency I need them to see is this: first, one must establish that repetition is not enough to distinguish individual habit from social rule; second, one must show that standardization without writing still requires an implicit theory of authority to explain why deviations are corrected; and only then can one claim to have reconstructed a belief rather than a behavior. To carry this, I want to build on one concrete case: a sealed chamber containing thousands of identical uninscribed clay tokens, some heavily abraded on a single face, others pristine, clustered with no domestic debris or waste—so that the group must decide whether this is ritual hoarding, administrative record-keeping, or something else, and see that the answer depends on prior theoretical commitments about wear patterns and spatial context rather than on the objects alone. Plan the explanatory arc: what idea has to be in place before the inference problem even becomes visible, how to introduce the token example so that its ambiguity is productive rather than confusing, the misconceptions they will raise in sequence—likely that careful observation removes interpretation, that standardization automatically implies bureaucracy, and that the absence of texts means the absence of institutions—and how the arc should absorb each in order, the check that would reveal whether they understand the dependency structure rather than just repeating the vocabulary, what should be deliberately omitted at this level and acknowledged as such, and where the reasoning is most fragile. Write entirely in continuous prose, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, as one might write in a careful letter. Please also state what specific argument or observation about the teaching sequence would change your mind, and identify the point in your reasoning that is most likely to fail.
