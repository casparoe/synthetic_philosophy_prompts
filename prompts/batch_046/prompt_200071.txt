I have been thinking about the Sleeping Beauty problem and the broader puzzle of self-locating belief—situations in which what you ought to believe depends not merely on what the world is like, but on where you are situated within it. I grasp the familiar half-versus-third dispute and the intuition that being awakened on Monday gives Beauty some kind of evidence about the coin toss, yet I keep returning to a configuration that seems under-examined: cases in which several agents, all epistemically identical and all aware of the same centered rules, must coordinate a collective decision before they know whether they are duplicated instances or unique ones. Imagine a committee whose members know that, depending on a random process, there may exist ten copies of each member reasoning in parallel on Tuesday, or only one; they must agree on a policy—say, how to distribute a shared resource—while each member asks not just “what should I believe?” but “what should we all believe, given that any one of us could be any copy?” This multi-agent extension seems to me to sit at a strange intersection of anthropic reasoning, collective rationality, and institutional design, but I am unsure whether it is a genuine open frontier or just an application of answers we already have.

Given that framing, I would like a defended prioritization of where the intellectual effort should go. Among the live questions in this area—the formalization of centered worlds and indexical probabilities; the resolution of the halfer-thirder disagreement; the connection to cosmological anthropic reasoning; the decision-theoretic problem of how to act when your payoff depends on your location; and the kind of collective coordination problem I have just sketched—which are upstream of the others, in the sense that settling them would dissolve or reshape the rest? Which are tractable for philosophers working with conceptual tools rather than empirical data, and which should be deprioritized because their resolution would change little downstream? I am asking for an agenda, not a bibliography: please state the criteria you are using—tractability, dependency, importance for practice or for further theory—identify the trade-offs between them, and say what kind of result over the next few years would cause you to reorder the priorities. In particular, tell me whether the multi-agent coordination issue I described is a serious independent problem or a derivative one, and where it belongs in the queue.
