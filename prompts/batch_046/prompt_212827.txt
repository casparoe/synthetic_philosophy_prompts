My partner and I are both men, and after fifteen years of shared household and finances we are now considering legal marriage—not because our commitment has changed, but because a recent medical crisis made us acutely aware of how fragile our legal standing is around hospital visitation, inheritance, and end-of-life decisions. In talking it through, however, we have discovered a deeper disagreement about what marriage actually is, and whether a same-sex partnership like ours can genuinely realize it. I hold that marriage is an institution with an internal form: it is a comprehensive union of lives that is inherently oriented toward family formation and long-term mutual care, and the state's legal framework exists to recognize and stabilize that specific kind of union rather than to invent it from nothing. My partner thinks this is romantic mystification; he argues that marriage is essentially a modular contract that bundles default rights—property rules, tax treatment, healthcare proxy, immigration sponsorship—and that any two consenting adults should be able to opt into this bundle for purely instrumental reasons, regardless of whether they share a romantic, sexual, or reproductive life. To sharpen the disagreement, we have been arguing about a concrete hypothetical: suppose one of us entered a legal marriage with a close, non-romantic friend solely to secure that friend's immigration status and medical decision-making authority, with an explicit private agreement to dissolve the union once those practical goals were met. Would this be a legitimate, if unconventional, deployment of the institution, or would it violate something conceptually essential to marriage—making it not merely risky or emotionally hollow, but philosophically incoherent? Furthermore, if there is indeed an internal structure to marriage that excludes such purely instrumental arrangements, does that structure depend on the biological complementarity of the spouses, thereby excluding same-sex couples, or can it be fully instantiated by partners of the same sex? I want an analysis that addresses the conceptual architecture of marriage rather than its legal mechanics, and that explains whether an argument for an essential purpose can be maintained without either collapsing marriage into an arbitrary bundle of state benefits or excluding partnerships like ours.
