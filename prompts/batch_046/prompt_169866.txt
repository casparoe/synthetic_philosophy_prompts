I recently completed a graduate course in algebra in which Zorn's lemma was invoked repeatedly—to guarantee maximal ideals, bases for arbitrary vector spaces, and the existence of algebraic closures—always via the same brief ritual: verify that every chain has an upper bound, invoke the lemma, and declare the object to exist. The instructor never noted its equivalence to the Axiom of Choice, never contrasted it with constructive alternatives, and never addressed whether an object whose only warrant is non-constructive can genuinely explain anything in applied mathematics or computation. Having since noticed that this pattern is standard in textbooks and qualifying examinations, I am struck by its philosophical silence. What commitments about mathematical existence, explanation, and ontology does this institutional practice presuppose? Must it rest on a platonist view that non-constructive existence is fully real and explanatory, or can it be sustained by formalism that treats the lemma as a mere syntactic license? Is the practice compatible with intuitionism, which rejects such existence proofs, or does it quietly rule intuitionism out? And if a mathematics department adopted a policy requiring every existence claim to be paired with an explicit construction or algorithmic extraction—say, in redesigned qualifying exams—would that constitute only a pedagogical shift, or would it force a change in the underlying philosophy of mathematics? Reconstruct the implicit theory, distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what merely accompanies it, and identify where it remains philosophically underdetermined, so that several rival views could license the same ritual.
