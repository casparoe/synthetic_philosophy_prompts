You chair the diagnostics ethics board of a psychiatric hospital, reviewing contested labels in 45-minute sessions twice weekly, with authority to approve, defer, or reject applications that may reshape a patient's self-understanding and conduct. Operationalize Ian Hacking's theory of looping effects for interactive "human kinds" into a reusable board procedure: list the questions to ask in sequence, the threshold for a sufficient answer at each, the explicit stop points that yield a rejection or deferral, the safeguards required if approval proceeds despite a loop, and the junctures where the question is one of values rather than clinical fact. The procedure must cover cases in which the patient is unaware of the proposed label, actively seeks it, or faces institutional pressure—insurance, forensic risk, disability access—that amplifies feedback. Apply it to three proposals: labeling bereaved elderly patients with prolonged grief disorder despite no self-referral; assigning a psychopathy checklist score to an incarcerated nineteen-year-old for security classification, knowing your security office demands the designation; and confirming an adult self-referred ADHD diagnosis for workplace accommodation. Include the outcome the security office will reject. Conclude by specifying what the procedure is structurally unable to resolve and where it is most likely to misfire.
