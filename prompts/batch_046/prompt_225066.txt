I'm working through the Browning-Goldhagen debate for a chapter on collective moral responsibility and have constructed an argument that seems to dissolve individual culpability by treating causal overdetermination as automatic exculpation. The premises are:

(1) If an action is overdetermined—produced both by an agent's internal disposition and by external situational pressures—then neither factor alone has exclusive causal priority, and attributing the action solely to either is false.

(2) Browning's evidence on Reserve Police Battalion 101 indicates that situational pressures (peer conformity, authority structures, an available opt-out with minimal penalty) were sufficient to produce participation in killing, even among men who showed little prior antisemitic conviction. Goldhagen's evidence indicates that a pervasive culture of eliminationist antisemitism was also sufficient to motivate participation. So the killings are overdetermined.

(3) If two factors are each fully sufficient, then counterfactually removing either should still leave the action intact.

(4) Removing the agents' antisemitic dispositions (on Browning's characterization) does not prevent the killings, since the situational structure remains sufficient. So the disposition is not a necessary condition.

(5) By parallel reasoning, weakening the situational pressures (on Goldhagen's characterization of ideological saturation) does not prevent the killings, since the ideology remains sufficient. So the situation is not a necessary condition.

(6) Moral responsibility requires that at least one causally necessary factor belong to the agent.

(7) Therefore, for these perpetrators, neither factor is necessary; hence no individual basis for moral responsibility remains. Generalized, institutional violence wherever culture and situation overlap becomes entirely non-culpable at the individual level.

This proves far too much—I clearly want to preserve some form of individual responsibility. I am inclined to defend (1) and (3) as correct descriptions of causal structure, but I cannot see how to block (6) or (7) without abandoning the counterfactual test that makes (4) and (5) plausible. Where exactly does the reasoning fail? Is there a hidden equivocation between causal necessity and the conditions for moral attribution, or is one of the empirical premises in (2) being misread?
