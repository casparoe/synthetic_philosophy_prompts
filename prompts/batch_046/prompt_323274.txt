In philosophy-of-mind discussions about collective cognition—particularly when people invoke the framing of an ant colony as a single subject, Aunt Hillary, with individual ants playing roles analogous to neurons—there is a persistent analogy: the colony stands to its ants as the brain stands to its neurons. This correspondence is often used to argue that distributed, substrate-independent processes can realize a unified mind, and that our reluctance to grant mental status to the colony reflects only scale prejudice or biological chauvinism rather than a principled distinction. Audit the analogy systematically. First, lay out the explicit correspondence table: what plays the role of the neuron, the synaptic connection, the action potential, functional clusters, the global integration mechanism, and the single subject of experience? Second, identify where the mapping fractures—consider the temporal dynamics of ant lifespans versus neural firing, whether pheromone gradients carry representational content, how error-correction operates without a centralized predictive model, and what conditions actually license attributing one mind rather than many interacting agents. Third, examine what features of neural architecture the analogy quietly imports—plastic individual learning, continuous conscious integration, evolutionary selection for unified behavior—and which of these the colony genuinely possesses versus which are projected onto it. Fourth, determine whether the conclusion—that there is literally one mind here rather than a swarm—survives once these disanalogies are explicit, or whether the brain comparison was carrying the intuitive load all along. Finally, if the analogy collapses, propose whether a different structural comparison—say, to a distributed computing cluster, a market-price mechanism, or a federated learning network—better captures what an ant colony actually does without importing mentalistic baggage.
