As I have been preparing lectures for a graduate seminar on rational belief revision, and as I recently faced a practical decision that required me to update on ambiguous introspective signs—uncertain whether my own reasoning was temporarily impaired—I have settled into the following package of commitments. I want an audit of whether they hang together.

(1) I accept Jeffrey conditionalization as the correct rule for all uncertain evidence: when experience does not conclusively establish any member of a partition but shifts probabilities over it, one updates prior beliefs by assigning new probabilities to the members and revising proportionally.

(2) I extend this rule to reflexive, second-order partitions—cases in which the ambiguity concerns not external states of affairs but the reliability of one's own belief-forming processes, such as ambiguous evidence that one's judgment is fatigued or distorted.

(3) I hold that rational updating must be path-independent: processing the same total evidential content in different chronological orders must yield identical final credences.

(4) I maintain that when evidence is maximally ambiguous—uniform across the partition with no differential support—Jeffrey conditionalization does not apply, and the correct response is to suspend judgment by preserving one's prior distribution intact.

(5) I reject decision-theoretic justifications of epistemic norms: coherence requirements and conservation of expected epistemic accuracy are legitimate grounds for updating rules, but appeals to long-run utility maximization or pragmatic success are not.

Please examine these commitments pairwise. Identify which tensions are genuine and which are only apparent, rank the real conflicts by severity, specify for each what must be given up and at what cost, and then state which single revision would resolve the greatest number of conflicts if my priority is to preserve the reflexive application in (2).
