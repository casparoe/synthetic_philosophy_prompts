I'm a journalist who mostly covers science and ideas, and lately I've been digging into democratic theory for a feature — specifically the boundary problem, like who actually belongs to the demos and who doesn't. I was having this arguement with my editor last week (he thinks I'm overcomplicating democracy, which, fair) about whether there's any principled way to include people who are affected by a decision but don't stick around long enough to vote or pay taxes. I ended up sketching out what I'm calling the "transient stakeholder principle" — basically, brief but intense affectedness counts for demos membership only if the affected party can articulate a grievance through some institutional proxy, not just exist as a data point.

For my draft, I want to rank five different boundary rules by how defensible they are as standalone criteria, not just how popular they are in the literature. Here's the list: (1) strict territorial citizenship, (2) the all-affected-interests standard, (3) stakeholder reciprocity where you need both affectedness and some capacity to contribute back, (4) temporal presence — you're in if you're physically there during the decision window — and (5) that transient stakeholder principle I mentioned. Could you sort them into tiers — S, A, B, C, F — with a quick sentence for each explaining why it lands there? I'm especially curious if the transient idea holds up or if it collapses into one of the others, since I definatly don't want to build my whole article around a concept that doesn't actually do any work.
