I've been thinking on my own about those moments where two paths both seem genuinely good but pull in completely different directions—like deciding between staying close to family and pursuing a vocation that demands constant relocation. It feels as though the values on each side—say, loyalty and self-development—simply don't share any common measure; you can't convert loyalty into units of growth, or vice versa. Philosophers call this incommensurability, and I tried to work out what it really means for whether we can choose rationally. But I ended up with an argument that seems to collapse the whole idea of hard choices. Here is the chain:

(1) If two values are genuinely incommensurable, then there is no fact of the matter about whether one is greater than, equal to, or less than the other.
(2) Rational choice between options requires knowing which option realizes the greater value, or at least knowing which is not the worse one.
(3) So if the values behind option A and option B are incommensurable, there is no fact about which option is rationally superior.
(4) Yet people clearly do make these choices in a rational way: they don't flip coins, they commit thoughtfully, and they defend the choice as the right one for them rather than as mere whim.
(5) Therefore, in these cases there must be some hidden common scale—perhaps overall life coherence or narrative unity—that makes one option genuinely better, even if we can't articulate the weights.
(6) If every hard choice is resolved by appeal to such a hidden scale, then true incommensurability never actually occurs; we only have difficulty seeing the scale.
(7) But the lived experience of a hard choice is precisely that no deeper scale resolves the conflict—if one did, the choice wouldn't feel hard, just complicated.
(8) So either our experience is illusory and all choices are calculable on a hidden scale, or rational choice doesn't require that there be a fact about rational superiority.
(9) If rational choice doesn't require rational superiority—if it's enough that the choice structurally fits who I am—then "rational" just means "consistent with my preferences," and there is no meaningful difference between a hard choice and an easy one where one option is obviously best.
(10) But that destroys the very distinction we started with, and it makes "rational" too cheap to be useful.

I feel like something slipped between the claim that there's no common measure and the claim that there's no rational basis at all. I want to keep the idea that hard choices are real, that people can choose well without being able to justify it with weights, and that rational choice isn't just arbitrary. But I also want to avoid saying that every choice is secretly easy. Which step should I reject, and what does giving it up cost me? Is there a way to save both the reality of hard choices and the idea that rationality demands more than personal consistency?
