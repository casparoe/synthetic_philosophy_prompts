I am a retired natural scientist who has recently turned to philosophy, and I am preparing to present a paper at an interdisciplinary seminar on moral epistemology and the classical tradition. My thesis is that Plutarch's Parallel Lives should not be read as hagiography or as a direct gallery of imitable exemplars, but as a form of comparative moral spectroscopy: by pairing a Greek and a Roman figure and allowing their virtues and vices to refract through one another, Plutarch establishes what I call differential exemplarity—a calibrated narrative distance that makes moral judgement possible without collapsing into prescription. I contend that later hagiographic biography abandons this comparative tension in favour of univocal sanctity, which is why it tends to produce admiration rather than practical moral learning. The audience will be analytic moral philosophers committed to the is-ought gap and sceptical of exemplarist virtue ethics; classicists protective of Plutarch's literary and historical specificity; and intellectual historians wary of flattening religious and political contexts into functionalist schemas. Please prepare me for the objections they will actually raise, ordered from the most probable and damaging to the least, phrased in the vocabulary each group would use. For each, provide a concise reply I can deliver in under two minutes, together with an honest note on where that reply is weakest. Please also identify the objection I most hope nobody asks—namely, the charge that my distinction between Plutarch's comparative structure and hagiographic univocality rests on a false dichotomy, since medieval and early modern hagiography also employs comparative pairing and contrasting saints' lives—and advise me how to concede part of the point without surrendering the core claim. Finally, please state how confident you are in this preparatory analysis, and single out the specific point about which you are least sure, whether that concerns the historical accuracy of my characterisation of hagiographic practice, the plausibility of my reply to the is-ought objection, or another element. Write in formal British English, and include no requests for bibliographies, reading lists, or citations.
