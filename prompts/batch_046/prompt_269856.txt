I'm presenting a paper at an interdisciplinary workshop on democratic governance and artificial intelligence, defending the claim that public-sector AI procurement should be governed by what I term threshold satisficing rather than welfare maximization. Specifically, I argue that democratic legitimacy requires agencies to establish explicit, non-tradable minimum thresholds for fairness, transparency, and predictive accuracy in systems used for welfare eligibility and predictive policing; any system meeting all thresholds is admissible, and selection among admissible systems proceeds by a simple, publicly auditable heuristic rather than by optimizing an aggregate social-welfare function. I frame this not as second-best rationality forced by cognitive limits, but as bounded rationality by design — a normative architecture that protects against runaway optimization in resource allocation and preserves space for democratic contestation over values that resist quantification.

The audience consists of about eight people: two democratic theorists who will worry that this licenses technocratic paternalism and undermines the deliberative legitimacy of the procurement process; a public-choice economist who will insist that my thresholds are just shadow prices in disguise and that the heuristic selection hides implicit maximization; a computer scientist specializing in algorithmic fairness who will ask whether my thresholds are jointly computable given well-known impossibility results; and an administrative-law scholar concerned with whether such rules can survive arbitrary-and-capricious review under existing administrative procedure doctrines. I need a speaker prep sheet ordered by how damaging each objection is and roughly in the sequence it will arise: each objection phrased as that discipline would phrase it; what I should concede without damaging the core thesis; what I should hold at all costs; the weakest point in my reply that I should prepare to defend rather than hope they overlook; and finally the one objection I am hoping nobody asks — the one that connects my framework to documented failures of good-enough environmental regulatory standards in the 1970s and asks whether satisficing in procurement will reproduce those pathologies.
