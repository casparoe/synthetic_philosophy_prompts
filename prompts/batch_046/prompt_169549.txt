I have no formal training in philosophy or cognitive science, but over the past several months I have been turning over a problem about personal identity that seems to sit in an uneasy space between Douglas Hofstadter’s idea of the self as a strange loop and the more familiar puzzle of whether one mind could be split across two bodies. As I understand it, Hofstadter treats the “I” not as a single physical substance locked inside one skull, but as a high-level, self-referential pattern—a loop—that can in principle be partially realized in other brains, as happens when one person’s habits, judgments, and memories continue to shape the thoughts of someone who knew them intimately. From this starting point I have developed a speculative notion I am calling a distributed first-person singular: a situation in which two physically separate brains, through sustained shared deliberation and reciprocal modeling, come to function as one authoritative center of practical reasoning, not by dissolving into an undifferentiated collective consciousness, but by maintaining distinct sensory streams and bodily perspectives while jointly issuing intentions and taking responsibility.

The specific scenario I imagine involves two people who, over many years, achieve such tight cognitive coupling that neither can complete a practical reasoning chain without implicitly anticipating the other’s contribution, yet both remain individually conscious of separate bodies and points of view. Rather than describing them as having “merged” or become “one mind in two bodies,” I want to propose that a new functional entity—a loop that connects them—comes into existence as the genuine subject of their shared agency. The criterion I suggest is phenomenological and behavioral: if one brain were suddenly destroyed, the survivor would not experience mere grief for a lost partner, but a specific kind of cognitive deficit, as though part of the very apparatus that constituted their own first-person agency had been amputated, analogous to the alienation a split-brain patient might feel toward an uncontrolled hand. At the same time, the survivor’s stream of consciousness would remain continuous, which I take to indicate that the “I” was genuinely distributed across both substrates rather than merely duplicated in each.

I have drafted these thoughts into a short essay arguing that such a distributed self is conceptually coherent and does not collapse into either standard substance dualism or eliminativist reductionism, but I am uncertain whether I have misinterpreted Hofstadter’s loop idea, whether my “amputation” test actually distinguishes distributed identity from profound attachment, and whether the whole construction quietly assumes that first-person authority must be singular in a way that begs the question. I am not asking for a bibliography, a reading list, or citations—I know I must find those on my own—but I would value your direct comments on the conceptual architecture: where the reasoning is weakest, whether the distinction between a shared pattern and a genuinely shared self holds up under pressure, and whether there is an obvious alternative framing that dissolves the problem entirely. I am especially interested in whether you think sustaining a single “I” across two brains requires something like a shared working memory that neuroscience would rule out, or whether it can be maintained purely at the level of functional organization without violating what we know about how brains operate.
