I am preparing an essay for my literature and philosophy course on Dostoevsky's Grand Inquisitor, specifically the parable that Ivan Karamazov presents in The Brothers Karamazov. Rather than repeating the standard reading that the Inquisitor merely exchanges freedom for security, I want to propose a more precise interpretation: the Inquisitor treats freedom not only as a psychological burden but as an epistemic failure mode. He assumes that ordinary people lack the cognitive and moral architecture to interpret their own choices without producing debilitating anxiety, and therefore that genuine happiness requires outsourcing moral interpretation to an authoritative structure. On this reading, the Inquisitor is not simply anti-freedom in a political sense; he is anti-hermeneutic, claiming that self-interpretation is psychologically unsustainable.

My essay notes outline three moves. First, I treat miracle, mystery, and authority not as three separate temptations but as a single pipeline: miracle produces dependency, dependency requires mystery to remain unquestioned, and mystery is sustained by authority. Second, I argue that Christ's refusal of these temptations is not primarily an affirmation of human dignity but an acceptance of hermeneutic responsibility—he forces individuals to become interpreters of their own moral lives, even at the cost of suffering. Third, I propose that the Inquisitor's conception of happiness is therefore not pleasure but interpretive closure, a state in which moral ambiguity is eliminated not by resolving it but by delegating it to an institution.

I worry that this framing imposes a contemporary vocabulary—epistemic, hermeneutic, cognitive—onto a nineteenth-century religious text in a way that distorts Dostoevsky's intent. I also worry that I may be conflating the Inquisitor's specific argument with Ivan's broader rebellion against theodicy, since the parable appears within Ivan's refusal to accept a world built on the suffering of children.

Could you comment on the conceptual coherence of this interpretation? Specifically, I would value feedback on: (1) whether treating the three temptations as a pipeline rather than a triad is defensible within the internal logic of the parable, or whether it forces an anachronistic structure onto the text; (2) whether my claim that Christ offers hermeneutic responsibility rather than freedom captures something genuinely present in the dialogue, or whether it merely projects modern autonomy theory onto a theological argument; and (3) whether there is a sharper way to describe the Inquisitor's happiness that avoids both psychological reductionism and vague religious language. I am not seeking citations, a bibliography, or reading recommendations—only conceptual clarification and suggestions for tightening the argument's internal consistency.
