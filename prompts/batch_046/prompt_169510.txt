I had a long and somewhat heated discussion with a colleague over coffee about whether Max Weber’s Protestant ethic thesis can be salvaged as a genuinely causal claim within contemporary philosophy of the social sciences. She takes the standard line that without a specified causal mechanism linking ascetic Protestant theology to the accumulation of capital, Weber has not offered an explanation at all—only a redescription of correlations that risks becoming tautological if we treat “values” as both cause and effect of economic behavior. I want to defend a more nuanced position: that culture can indeed be a cause, but only if we reject the Humean constant-conjunction model and instead understand values as what I will call structural selectors—they do not push agents like physical forces, but they constrain the space of meaningful action so that certain economic practices become intelligible while others become unthinkable. Please construct a careful argument for this position, being explicit about what distinguishes this normative selection from both efficient causation and mere correlation, and specify exactly what kind of empirical counter-evidence would genuinely threaten the claim rather than merely complicating it. In particular, address my colleague’s objection that if values genuinely cause behavior, we should expect isomorphic economic outcomes in every culture with structurally similar theological commitments—something history clearly does not show—and explain why the absence of perfect replication does not defeat normative causation but instead clarifies its proper scope and limits.
