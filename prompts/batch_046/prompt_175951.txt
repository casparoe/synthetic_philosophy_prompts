I've been thinking about Dostoevsky's legend of the Grand Inquisitor — the part where he tells Christ that people are too weak for freedom and prefer miracle, mystery, and authority, because freedom forces them to bear the weight of choosing between good and evil without any guarantee. I tried to work out the logic for myself, and I built an argument that seems to prove every stable community must either be secretly free or secretly decaying. I can't see the exit, but the conclusion feels wrong.

Here is the argument.

(1) The Inquisitor claims that freedom is essentially the burden of moral choice without external guarantee, and that human beings are constitutionally unable to endure it; they will always trade it for security and happiness.

(2) If that is true, then any mechanism that removes the burden of choice at the moment of action, while leaving the result intact, delivers exactly the happiness the Inquisitor promises.

(3) In my own life I do this constantly: I bind my future self. I choose a career path now so that later I do not have to choose; I enter commitments that make certain actions unthinkable; I build habits and environments that decide for me before I feel the weight of decision. This is not coercion from outside — it is a free decision to make future decisions unnecessary.

(4) Therefore, binding my future self is the individual version of the Inquisitor's offer: I surrender future freedom in exchange for present and future peace.

(5) But the act of binding is itself a free choice, made with full awareness. So to achieve the Inquisitor's happy surrender, I must first exercise the very freedom I intend to eliminate.

(6) The same holds for societies. Any community organized around comfort, tradition, or authority — any system that relieves its members of daily moral anxiety — must have been founded by people who freely chose that organization.

(7) Once the founders are gone, the next generation inherits the structure without having made the founding choice. At that point the system rests not on freedom but on habit and socialization.

(8) Yet the Inquisitor insists that happiness requires more than habit; it requires the conscious surrender of freedom to a living authority that offers miracle and mystery. Without that conscious surrender, there is only routine.

(9) So any Inquisitor-style society faces a dilemma: it must either constantly renew its founding freedom — which means its members remain free — or it loses the conscious surrender that sustains happiness and collapses into mere routine, which the Inquisitor says is intolerable.

(10) This proves too much. It implies that every lasting institution, every tradition that genuinely comforts people, is either secretly sustained by hidden freedom or is already decaying. That cannot be right. Many communities seem stable and genuinely happy without any hidden reservoir of choice.

If the argument is flawed, which step should I reject? I would prefer to keep (1) — that freedom is a real burden — and (5) — that binding my future self requires an initial free choice. But I need to know what giving up either of them costs, and whether there is a different step that saves the rest without making the Inquisitor's insight trivial.
