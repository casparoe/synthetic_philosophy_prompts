Here is an argument I sketched for a seminar on environmental causation and now find suspicious because the conclusion erases human choice. (1) Define a historical actor as any entity indispensable to explaining an outcome in terms that do not reduce entirely to the intentions of human agents within the chain. (2) The 536 AD volcanic winter, the Black Death, and the 1815 Tambora eruption each satisfy this: the restructuring of Mediterranean trade, European labor markets, and post-Napoleonic famine politics are not intelligible without them. (3) So climate, pathogens, and volcanoes are genuine historical actors. (4) To be an actor in this sense is to possess agency, understood here—not as intentional action, but as the capacity to alter the trajectory of an assemblage, a concept drawn from posthumanist historiography. (5) Once we grant non-sentient entities this kind of agency, there is no principled boundary preventing us from treating human agents as mere nodes within larger climate-microbial-geological assemblages. (6) Hence recognizing volcanoes and germs as actors commits us to environmental determinism: human intentions are always secondary expressions of deeper non-human dynamics. (7) But determinism is false, since human institutions and choices produce radically different outcomes under identical climatic or epidemiological conditions. (8) Therefore we must reject either (4) or (5). Yet if we reject (4) and deny that altering an assemblage counts as agency, then "historical actor" collapses into metaphor and climate and disease become mere background conditions, which contradicts (2). Where is the slip? If I must choose, I want to keep (3) and (7). Which step should I abandon, and what does it cost?
