In discussions of Jefferson's slaveholding, Churchill's imperial policies, or Columbus's treatment of Indigenous peoples, I repeatedly encounter the phrase "he was a man of his time" used to halt moral evaluation rather than to advance it. I want you to treat that sentence as a compressed menu of distinct theses rather than a single argument. Separate out at least four precise readings: (1) an epistemic-availability claim about which moral concepts the agent could actually have accessed; (2) a normative-indexing claim that the correct standard for judging an act is the one dominant in the agent's society; (3) an excuse claim that blame requires the capacity to have done otherwise, which itself requires epistemic access to the relevant moral reasons; and (4) a statistical-descriptive claim that the agent's beliefs matched the modal view in his cohort, with an implicit slide from statistical normality to exculpation. For each reading, say whether it is a platitude, whether it is substantive and defensible, and whether it actually blocks present-day condemnation. Then apply a further distinction I am trying to maintain: diachronic accessibility, meaning whether the agent could have arrived at our current moral view using only the intellectual resources available in his era, versus synchronic accessibility, meaning whether anyone in his era actually held that view. Which of the four readings depend on conflating these two? Finally, identify which reading people typically invoke when they deploy the phrase to end an argument, and report whether that reading survives once you require the defence to specify whether it concerns statistical belief, normative authority, or the conditions for blame.
