I'm a journalist who covers science and philosophy for a general audience. Recently I observed an extended exchange between a moral psychologist and an affective neuroscientist—both credible, neither a strawman—about the ontology of emotion. I want to understand the precise fault lines in their debate, not merely who "wins" overall.

The moral psychologist argued that emotions are fundamentally evaluative judgments. To fear something is to judge it dangerous; to feel moral outrage is to judge that a wrong has been committed. She emphasized that emotions are subject to rational assessment: someone who fears a harmless spider is making a mistake structurally analogous to holding a false belief. She also appealed to emotional granularity—the capacity to distinguish precisely between annoyance, anger, and indignation—as evidence that emotional experience requires conceptual discrimination. On her view, people with finer emotional vocabularies make better practical and ethical decisions precisely because they are making more nuanced judgments about value and importance.

The neuroscientist rejected this framework. He contended that emotions are perceptual or predictive states rather than propositional judgments. He pointed to phobic responses: a subject can explicitly judge a spider harmless while still experiencing intense fear. If fear were literally the judgment of danger, this direct contradiction should be impossible. He argued that saving the judgment theory by positing a "hidden" or non-conscious judgment renders the theory unfalsifiable. Instead, he proposed that emotions are interoceptive predictions—constructed inferences about bodily states that are categorized through learned concepts. Moral disgust, on this account, is not a judgment about impurity but a predicted physiological pattern that culture teaches the brain to apply to social violations. He further noted that manipulating false heart-rate feedback can shift emotional experience without altering any explicit evaluative belief, which he took to favor a predictive-perceptual model over a judgment-based one.

The psychologist responded that predictive processing actually strengthens her case: if emotions are predictions shaped by conceptual categories, then conceptual judgment is performing essential work. She argued that emotional granularity improves decision-making not because it organizes raw sensation—like a wine expert learning to taste—but because finer categories yield more accurate evaluations of what matters. She also pressed a challenge: if emotions are purely bodily predictions about anticipated social punishment, then distant moral outrage—anger at injustices that pose no personal threat—becomes inexplicable. The judgment of wrongness, she insisted, is doing real explanatory work that a bodily prediction alone cannot supply.

The neuroscientist countered that distant outrage is fully explicable through internalized social norms that become part of the predictive model—anticipated bodily and social states are simulated even without direct personal risk. He conceded that concepts organize emotional experience, but compared their role to language organizing color perception: they structure a non-conceptual perceptual field rather than constituting the emotion itself. He granted that judgments can accompany and influence emotions, but denied they are identical to them.

Please adjudicate this exchange point by point: specifically on (1) the phobia objection, (2) whether emotional granularity supports judgment or predictive organization, (3) the false-feedback evidence, and (4) whether distant moral outrage requires genuine evaluative judgment. For each, say which party has the stronger argument and where each is overstating. Then identify what both seem to be missing about how judgment, interoceptive prediction, and conceptual categorization might interact in emotional experience. Please provide your answer in American English.
