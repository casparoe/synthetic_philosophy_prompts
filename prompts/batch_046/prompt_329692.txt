You are an expert in normative ethics and moral psychology. Before drafting the instrument, please spend a brief moment brainstorming the logical space of positions on moral worth: map the familiar divides (whether motives matter independently of results, whether explicit moral belief is necessary, whether wrong actions can carry credit), but also inject one somewhat niche diagnostic axis that standard surveys usually collapse—specifically, whether an agent's motivating state must be counterfactually stable across shifts in self-interest and social visibility, and whether non-cognitive emotional attunement can substitute for occurrent moral reasoning. Then construct a taxonomy of positions on what makes an action morally worthy, formatted as a decision tree whose branches are concrete cases and whose leaves are named or unnamed positions.

The tree should open with a root scenario: an agent performs an objectively right act (pulling a stranger from danger) but is motivated entirely by a desire for social praise. From there, each branch must be labeled by a concrete claim or case on which positions genuinely diverge, not by philosophical jargon. Cover these four splitting questions in order: (1) Can a wrong action—one that fails objectively—still possess moral worth if the agent acted from sincere compassion? (2) Must the agent hold an explicit belief that the act is morally required, or can a cultivated virtuous disposition or emotional resonance suffice? (3) If the agent acts rightly but partly from an immoral motive (e.g., returning lost property both because it is right and because she enjoys humiliating the owner), is the worth fully present, partially diminished, or nullified? (4) Does counterfactual stability matter: if the non-moral incentive vanished, would the agent still act, and does that change the verdict on worth?

The audience is advanced undergraduate philosophy students. Design exactly six diagnostic cases, each presenting a concrete situation with two or three answer options, phrased so a respondent can choose without knowing technical labels. For each leaf, provide the position name if it is recognized in the literature; if the combination is unoccupied, label it "unnamed" and state its most awkward commitment in one sentence. Explicitly flag any pair of positions that the tree fails to separate, with a brief diagnosis (convergence on tested cases, metaphysical divergence without practical difference, etc.). Do not ask for bibliographies, reading lists, or citations, and do not include them. Conclude with a scoring key that maps answer-pattern combinations onto the corresponding leaf positions.
