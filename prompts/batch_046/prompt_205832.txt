I'm moderating a colloquium interview with an unnamed political anthropologist who has recently advanced the thesis that corruption is not a cross-culturally stable moral kind but rather an artifact of institutional translation: practices that Western audit cultures classify uniformly as bribery or clientelism often function locally as legitimate moral economies of reciprocity, recognition, and obligation. She draws a sharp conceptual distinction between what she calls allocative corruption—the diversion of public goods or authority toward private ends in ways that undermine formal distributive rules—and recognitional corruption, which she defines as the distortion of social visibility, status, and interpersonal acknowledgment within patron-client networks, arguing that Western anti-corruption frameworks systematically conflate the two and thereby misread relations such as South Asian jajmani obligations or Mediterranean clientelism as purely extractive rather than as structured reciprocity. She further maintains that gift-giving crosses into corruption only when it violates the explicit procedural grammar of a particular institutional setting, not merely because it involves an exchange of favors or loyalty.

Write me a sequence of eight to ten interview questions that press her position rather than allowing her to restate it. I want at least one question probing whether her framework can distinguish genuinely harmful structural patronage from benign reciprocity without covertly importing Western liberal norms of formal equality; one question about what her account of recognitional corruption implies for democratic elections in which voters actively seek personal access, protection, or status through patronage networks rather than through anonymous procedural rights; and, for each of these, a follow-up keyed to the reply she is most likely to offer—probably that the harm lies in a decoupling between formal institutional equality and lived reciprocity, or that visibility itself is institutionally coded. Include also a question that forces her to confront the possibility that local participants themselves experience a practice she classifies as legitimate reciprocity as coercive or extractive, and end with one question asking what specific ethnographic or institutional finding—rather than a Western auditor's disapproval—would genuinely count against her view. Keep the tone formal and suitable for a public philosophy colloquium, and avoid any generic biographical or career-oriented questions.
