I have been trying to work out on my own whether an ant colony can literally have a mind, using the old image of Aunt Hillary and the ant fugue as a starting point rather than a proof. The more I think about it, the more the argument seems to circle without anyone changing position: one camp points to the colony’s sophisticated problem-solving, its memory stored in pheromone trails, and the way damage to one region is compensated by others, and concludes that something like cognition is happening at the collective level; the other camp replies that every behavior reduces to individual ants reacting to local chemical gradients, and that calling the whole thing a “mind” is just a poetic mistake, since there is no single place where experience could occur and no unified point of view. Both sides agree on the biology, yet neither budges.

My own suspicion is that the stalemate is not really about ants at all, but about an unstated condition for being a mind—specifically, whether consciousness requires what I would call a singular temporal frame, a unified “now” in which information is bound together, rather than merely being processed in parallel across thousands of disconnected bodies. The colony integrates information across space, but it seems to lack any central clock or synchronized present that could serve as the subject of that integration. If that is the hidden fault line, then the debate might be partly verbal—“mind” meaning “functional information system” for one side and “phenomenologically unified subject” for the other—while also containing a substantive disagreement about whether temporal binding is a necessary feature of mentality.

But I am not sure whether this diagnosis is correct, or whether there is instead a hidden empirical question we have not settled—perhaps about whether ant colonies exhibit a form of non-hierarchical convergence that is genuinely irreducible to individual behavior, or whether they merely approximate it. So I want to ask: is this disagreement verbal, substantive, or a case of two groups answering different questions under one heading? If it is substantive, is the core issue the necessity of a unified temporal frame, or is that just my own invention? And given whatever diagnosis you think is right, what would actually count as progress—an observation about chemical synchronization in colonies, a formal result about the limits of distributed representation, a conceptual distinction that separates functional integration from phenomenal unity, or an admission that the question is ill-posed because “mind” was never meant to apply to entities without a single body? I am not asking for a survey of positions; I want the structure of the disagreement diagnosed with reasons, and an honest answer about whether anything could move it.
