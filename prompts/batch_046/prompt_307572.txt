Exam question (Philosophy of Rational Choice, final exam):

In sequential decision-making under uncertainty, the Kelly criterion advises wagering a fraction f = 2p − 1 of current wealth on a favorable gamble—probability p of doubling the stake, 1−p of losing it. For p = 0.6 this yields f = 0.2. Critics object that in any single round expected monetary value is maximized by betting everything (f = 1), so Kelly rationality appears to sacrifice one-shot optimality for long-run growth. Defenders reply by appealing to “geometric rationality”: the normative claim that rational agents should avoid strategies that are almost surely outgrown by some alternative in repeated play.

First, derive f = 0.2 by maximizing expected log-wealth. Second, evaluate whether geometric rationality offers a genuinely independent normative standard, or whether it collapses into expected-utility theory with logarithmic preferences applied across rounds. In your evaluation, address the specific objection that Kelly rationality is irrational for an agent who cares only about the present round.

Student answer:

The Kelly fraction f* = 0.2 solves max_f [0.6 ln(1+f) + 0.4 ln(1-f)], with derivative 0.6/(1+f) − 0.4/(1-f) = 0, hence f* = 0.2. In one round, EV(W) = W[0.6(1+f) + 0.4(1-f)] = W[1 + 0.2f], so f = 1 maximizes EV at 1.2W, confirming the critic’s point. The geometric-rationality defense, developed by Merton and Samuelson in the continuous-time portfolio literature, holds that a strategy must maximize the asymptotic growth rate G = lim (1/n) ln(W_n/W_0). The Kelly strategy achieves G = 0.6 ln 1.2 + 0.4 ln 0.8 ≈ 0.020, and any other strategy is eventually outperformed almost surely. But this is not an independent standard: geometric rationality is just intertemporal expected-utility maximization with a log-utility function, repeated at each stage. So if an agent genuinely cares only about the present round and adopts linear utility, there is no internal contradiction in betting f = 1; geometric rationality only binds agents who have already accepted intertemporal consistency with log preferences. Thus the debate is not about rationality per se but about whether sequential consistency demands logarithmic risk aversion.
