Write a short story in the third person, set in the evaluation chamber of a state bureau that issues Certificates of Meaningful Existence. Under the governing statute, an applicant must satisfy a hybrid standard drawn from two well-known philosophical accounts: Camus’s conception of meaning as conscious, unyielding revolt against the absurd, and Susan Wolf’s requirement that a meaningful life involve active engagement with projects that possess objective value. The protagonist is a senior evaluator who has administered this two-part test for years without doubt. The applicant before him is an elderly engineer who has spent two decades reconstructing, by hand, a railway viaduct that spans a valley between two towns that have been uninhabited since a flood. She reports no subjective fulfillment, denies that her labor serves any external purpose, and insists that she would continue even if every rail were immediately torn away again. The evaluator must conduct the interview, apply the framework, and discover that her project seems to fail both prongs—there is neither evident objective value nor the affirmative subjective stance of revolt—yet something in her description resists categorization as mere futility. Focus the narrative on their dialogue and on the evaluator’s growing suspicion that the statutory framework contains an unacknowledged third possibility. End the story with the evaluator leaving the certificate unsigned and quietly revising a single clause on the evaluation form, without stating whether the revision expands, restricts, or abandons the concept of meaning altogether. Do not provide an authorial explanation of which philosophical view is correct; let the tension remain embodied in the scene.
