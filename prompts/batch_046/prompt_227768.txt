hey, i work for a state budget office and every quarter we have to score like 12 inheritance-tax amendments — family-farm exemptons, dynasty-trust rules, step-up basis tweaks, etc. my team are economists, not philosophers, but we're supposed to check whether a proposal lets dynastic wealth screw up equal oppertunity. the guiding idea is "equal opportunity shouldn't be undermined by inherited advantage," but that's useless in practice — we argue for hours about what counts.

i want a reusable decision procedure — a flowchart in words with ordered questions — that we can actually run in about 20 minutes per bill. it should be built around a slightly weirder framing i picked up: dynastic harm comes from three things, not just size. (1) liqidity viscosity — how stuck the asset is in forms that can't become general opportunity (cash is low viscosity, private land and dynasty trusts are high). (2) positional scarcity — whether the good's value depends on others being excluded (elite school seats, zoning exemptions) vs scalable goods (public health, infrastructure). (3) intergenerational option-luck debt — whether the wealth was accumulated using structures that pushed costs onto others (aggressive tax avoidance, regulatory capture).

for each proposal we run:
- Q1: what's the primary asset class being transmitted?
- Q2: rate its viscosity and positional scarcity (high/medium/low, with thresholds).
- Q3: assess option-luck debt (present/absent/unclear).
- Q4: does the proposal include a conversion mechanism (tax, time limit, public-access condition)?
- Q5: net effect on opportunity distribution over one generation?

i need clear stop conditions ("reject" or "passes filter"), clear hand-off conditions where it's a values call not a fact (the director has to sign off on those), and honest notes on where the procedure will misfire.

then run it on three proposals:
a) exempt family farms up to $15m if heir operates it for 10 years.
b) allow unlimited dynasty trusts if they pay a flat 2% annual wealth tax.
c) eliminate step-up basis for inherited stock, but keep full exemption for inherited patents and IP.

make sure one of these comes out in a direction we'll hate — like the procedure approves something we instinctively reject or kills something we like. name which one and explain the misfire.

also list what the procedure flat-out can't do — for example comparing non-wealth advantages, measuring across more than two generations, or handling cultural capital.

write casual, no citations or reading lists needed. end with exactly one line: Summary: [one sentence capturing the procedure's bottom line].
