The special composition question—when, exactly, do material parts compose a single further object?—has been debated across metaphysics for decades, with universalists, restrictivists, and nihilists all well represented, yet the disagreement shows little sign of convergence despite shared formal apparatus and recurring appeals to intuition. I want a diagnosis of this stall rather than another survey of positions. My own hypothesis is that the dispute is not a single substantive disagreement but a cluster of cross-purposes: universalists often treat composition as a static, synchronic relation governed by spatiotemporal continuity and functional integration, whereas restrictivists and nihilists frequently answer diachronic questions about persistence conditions and ontological dependence over time without marking the shift. There may also be an unacknowledged verbal divergence about whether 'object' functions in an ontologically innocent, quantificational role or in a robust, property-bearing role. In addition, I suspect that a rarely explicit view—mereological essentialism applied selectively to artifacts but denied for organisms—is silently structuring restrictivist resistance to arbitrary composition, though this commitment is seldom defended outright. Given this framing, tell me: which of these sources of disagreement are genuine, which dissolve on inspection, and what is the hidden disagreement that neither side is naming? Is there a bedrock clash about naturalness that makes progress impossible, or could a precise formal result—say, about diachronic parthood relations—actually move the debate? Identify the objection each camp is hoping its opponents do not raise, and be explicit about where your diagnosis is weakest.
