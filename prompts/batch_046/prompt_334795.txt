I am currently enrolled in an upper-division seminar on moral problems in public life, and our discussion section has been constructing a running consensus on dirty-hands scenarios. So far we have reached four judgments. First, a mayor who authorized warrantless surveillance to stop a confirmed bombing plot and then delivered a full public admission of the legal breach: we agreed that dirty hands apply, but that she should retain office provided she issues a complete accounting. Second, a legislator who accepted a bribe to secure emergency health funding, then donated the sum and self-reported: we agreed that dirty hands do not apply, because the corruption was fully self-corrected and made transparent. Third, a diplomat who forged treaty documents to prevent an imminent war, kept the forgery permanently secret, and gained substantial personal prestige: we agreed that dirty hands apply and that removal is warranted, since concealment of a serious integrity breach blocks democratic accountability. Fourth, a police chief who used a false-threat interrogation tactic to prevent an attack and then immediately published a report explaining the deception: we agreed that dirty hands do not apply, because the deception was procedural, immediately transparent, and left no lasting rights harm.

We now face a case that does not map neatly onto these. A city councilor secretly negotiated with a developer to alter zoning laws in exchange for a legally binding, executed promise—not personal payment—to build affordable housing units that saved a specific vulnerable community from displacement. She never disclosed the meeting, arguing that this was a case of preemptive transparency failure: she withheld information not to deceive permanently, but to prevent an anticipated democratic veto that she believed was driven by misinformation and would kill the project. The covenant was executed at once, the housing was built, and she received no financial benefit. The only moral wrong appears to be the secret negotiation itself and the resulting appearance of a conflict of interest, since disclosure now might trigger the very backlash that would destabilize the community she protected.

If our section wants to stay consistent with ourselves, what should we decide about this councilor? Please identify the closest analog among our four precedents, then state whether our track record genuinely underdetermines the case. If it does, lay out at least two plausible rules that jointly explain our four verdicts, say what each predicts here, and name the further case that would best distinguish them; if it does not, say which single principle we are implicitly adopting and why. Present your answer in three labeled parts—Verdict, Closest Analog, and Principle or Divergent Rules—and keep the entire response to no more than 350 words.
