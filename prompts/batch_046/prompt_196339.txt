Construct a thought experiment in the style of Hilbert's hotel, but replace the usual linear sequence of rooms with a binary-tree arrangement: each room is a node with exactly two child-rooms, and arrivals occur in discrete waves corresponding to tree levels, so that accommodating wave n requires filling every node at depth n-1 and shifting those occupants downward into available child-rooms according to a fixed deterministic rule. Engineer the protocol so that, if the process is executed through all finite levels, the root node ends up either occupied or vacant in a way that divides the three following positions: (i) a Cantorian actualist who accepts completed actual infinities and standard cardinal arithmetic must judge that there is a definite, determinate answer to whether the root is occupied after all waves are finished; (ii) a strict finitist who denies that an infinite totality can ever be completed must judge that the question is illegitimate because the accommodation procedure never reaches a terminal state; (iii) a potentialist who accepts that the process can continue indefinitely but rejects any completed infinite totality must deliver a verdict that differs from both (i) and (ii) on whether the root's status is determinable. Present the scenario in one paragraph of ordinary language, with no appeal to computation, physics, or formal notation beyond the tree metaphor. Then demonstrate that your design meets the specification: state each position's verdict, show why the tree structure (rather than the linear Hilbert arrangement) forces the divergence, identify the feature that does the work, and explain how a finitist would accuse the Cantorian of treating a potential process as if it were a completed object. Finally, address the collapse condition: if the branching factor is reduced to 1, returning the hotel to the standard linear case, does the three-way divergence disappear, and if so, exactly which judgment converges?
