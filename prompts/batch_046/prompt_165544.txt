Two colleagues of mine—Sofia, who works on early modern science studies, and David, a medievalist—got into an extended argument at a departmental lunch about whether the standard tripartite division of Western history into the Middle Ages, the Renaissance, and the Scientific Revolution tracks anything real or is essentially a retrospective invention. They were designing an undergraduate survey together and could not agree on whether the course should be organized around these periods at all. I was sitting nearby and heard the whole exchange without taking sides.

Sofia opened by saying that all three labels are anachronistic impositions. The term “Renaissance” was popularized in the nineteenth century; “Scientific Revolution” is a twentieth-century coinage; and “Middle Ages” began as a humanist slur meant to delegitimize everything between antiquity and the recovery of classical learning. Because the people who lived through these centuries never used these categories for themselves, and because historians constantly shift the boundaries—some placing the Renaissance from 1300 to 1600, others from 1400 to 1700—there are no natural joints being carved. The divisions, she said, are pedagogical conveniences that have hardened into a false ontology, serving mainly to flatter modernity by casting everything before it as darkness or transition.

David replied that retrospective naming is not the same as retrospective fabrication. The collapse of the Western Roman Empire in the fifth century produced institutional, linguistic, and economic discontinuities that contemporaries experienced as rupture, not merely as modern interpretation. Likewise, the recovery and wide dissemination of Greek and Latin texts in fifteenth-century Italy, together with new secular patronage structures and the reform of university curricula, created a cluster of practices discernibly different from those of the preceding centuries. The label may be later, he said, but the cluster is not imaginary; it explains why certain manuscripts were annotated differently, why specific artistic commissions changed in content, and why particular questions became central.

Sofia pushed back by arguing that continuity undermines even these clusters. Economic and administrative structures persisted across the fifth and sixth centuries; Italian humanists in the fifteenth century worked inside medieval guild and university frameworks; and the printing press circulated texts that had long existed in manuscript. Even the so-called Scientific Revolution, she claimed, is an artificial aggregation of local, disconnected practices—court alchemy here, astronomical observation there—unified only by twentieth-century historians to construct a Whiggish narrative of inevitable rational progress. The social and material conditions, she insisted, remain continuous, so the claimed discontinuities are selections made after the fact.

David conceded that social structures often persisted but insisted that conceptual frameworks can shift within them. Galileo may have worked under church patronage, but he articulated a mathematized conception of motion and a methodological separation of primary and secondary qualities that lacked a precise medieval precedent. The institutionalization of replication—through correspondence networks that later formalized into bodies like the Royal Society and the Académie des Sciences—was not mere social-club window dressing; it changed how claims were validated and how knowledge was credited. These are not just labels, he said, but explanatory tools: treating 1450–1600 as a coherent cluster predicts which texts will carry humanist glosses, which artists will receive secular commissions, and which universities will revise their statutes. If the category were pure fiction, it would lack that predictive grip.

Sofia then argued that this predictive success is illusory because the selection is circular. Historians highlight Galileo and Boyle because they fit the narrative, while ignoring the vast body of seventeenth-century natural philosophy that remained Aristotelian, hermetic, or theologically embedded. The “institutionalization” David described was largely the formalization of existing networks, not a rupture in them. More fundamentally, she said, these categories do ideological work: the “Dark Ages” legitimates modern secular rationality, and the “Scientific Revolution” justifies contemporary science funding and authority. Once you recognize that the divisions serve political and institutional functions, you should be skeptical that they serve descriptive ones.

David did not deny the ideological weaponization—he agreed that every periodization has been misused—but he distinguished misuse from non-existence. “Democracy” is ideologically loaded and still denotes real institutional forms. The proper test, he said, is whether refusing the cluster makes explanation harder. A flat timeline with local nodes is itself an ontology, not a neutral alternative; it still requires criteria for what counts as a node. Sofia had shown that boundaries are fuzzy and internal diversity is high, but that is true of every historical generalization, including “the Roman Empire” or “the Victorian era.” The burden, he concluded, was on her to show that continuity is so total that no macro-level clustering is justified—and she had only demonstrated that edges are indistinct, which is not the same as demonstrating absence.

Sofia’s final move was to say that if boundaries are that fuzzy and internal variation that high, then the category collapses into a family-resemblance concept with no sharp edges—which is exactly what “invention” means. One does not need “real ages” to build useful pedagogy; a continuous web with localized nodes explains just as well. David’s defense, she suggested, rested on an intuitive preference for macro-narrative coherence rather than on a historical finding. Until she was shown a principled reason why “institutional cluster” or “epistemic shift” is less legitimate a basis for grouping than “political dynasty,” she said, she would treat the tripartite scheme as a convenient fiction—harmless for surveys, but misleading if taken as ontology.
