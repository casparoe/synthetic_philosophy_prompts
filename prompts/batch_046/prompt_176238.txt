Construct a branching taxonomy of positions on the permissibility of belief-formation without sufficient evidence, taking the James–Clifford debate as its backbone but extending it into applied territory. The root should pose whether belief is ever subject to direct voluntary control. From there branch on: (a) whether indirect cultivation of belief-forming dispositions can still be morally evaluated; (b) whether evidential sufficiency is a necessary condition for permissible belief; (c) if it is not necessary, whether pragmatic license is restricted to options that are live, forced, and momentous, or whether it also covers therapeutic contexts (e.g., sustaining hope against a clinical prognosis) and collective identity narratives (e.g., founding myths that stabilize cooperation despite lacking empirical verification); and (d) whether the wrongness of belief depends on its consequences, on disrespect for epistemic norms, or on both. At each leaf, supply a label—named if established (strict Cliffordian evidentialism, Jamesian pragmatism, doxastic involuntarism with indirect duty, pragmatic pluralism) or 'unnamed'—and one sentence stating its most awkward commitment, such as having to condemn a dying patient's hopeful belief or having to license politically useful but empirically unsupported collective narratives. Identify any logically possible but unoccupied combinations, with a brief diagnosis of whether they are incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Frame every branch as a concrete claim or scenario that a student can answer without knowing the philosophical labels, and design the tree for an audience familiar with James's essay The Will to Believe but not with specialized doxastic-ethics literature. Keep the tree to six or eight leaves, and explicitly flag any pair of positions the tree cannot cleanly separate.
