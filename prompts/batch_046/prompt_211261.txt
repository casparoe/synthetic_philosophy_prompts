Consider the institutional practice in long-term disability and workers’ compensation systems: conditions that present with “objective” biomarkers—abnormal imaging, elevated inflammatory markers, or tissue damage visible on biopsy—are granted benefits with minimal dispute over impairment, whereas conditions diagnosed primarily through patient-reported symptoms and functional limitations, such as fibromyalgia, myalgic encephalomyelitis/chronic fatigue syndrome, or post-viral cognitive impairment, are routinely subjected to adversarial review, mandatory psychiatric evaluation, or denial on the grounds that “subjective” experience lacks corroboration, even though clinical taxonomy recognizes these as legitimate diseases.

What conceptions of health and disease does this bifurcated practice presuppose? Does it commit the institution to a naturalist view—where disease is defined by biological dysfunction independent of normative judgment—or can a normativist account, in which disease involves a harmful departure from species-typical functioning or a failure to realize valued capacities, explain the disparity? Reconstruct the implicit theory: are objective biomarkers treated as necessary conditions, sufficient conditions, or merely as epistemic proxies for social legitimacy? Examine whether the practice is internally consistent with the same system’s acceptance of psychiatric disorders, where subjective suffering is often the primary diagnostic criterion, and with its treatment of addiction, which is medically classified as a disease but administratively handled through punitive exclusion. If the implicit view were replaced by a purely normativist framework that granted equal standing to subjectively experienced functional limitation, what specific institutional mechanisms—evidentiary rules, expert-witness structures, appeals processes—would have to change? And identify where the practice is philosophically underdetermined, so that both naturalism and normativism could license the same administrative outcome for different reasons.
