I teach a graduate seminar on Adam Smith’s moral psychology, and whenever I press students on whether the impartial spectator can genuinely adjudicate deep cross-cultural moral disagreement, they fall back on the formula sympathy is putting yourself in another’s shoes as if it dissolves the problem. The phrase is being made to do specific argumentative work: it is meant to establish that moral judgment is always available through imaginative projection, and therefore that no further normative or institutional apparatus is required for cross-cultural evaluation. I want a discussion sheet for our next meeting that exposes exactly how many different theses that formula conceals.

Please lay out, as an ordered menu running from the most anodyne to the most metaphysically ambitious, the distinct precise claims that sympathy is putting yourself in another’s shoes could be expressing within a recognizably Smithian framework. Then indicate, for each reading, whether it actually licenses the dismissal of my specific worry—that cross-cultural moral adjudication requires resources beyond imaginative projection—or whether it leaves that worry fully intact. Structure the answer so that my students can see which interpretations are trivial, which are substantive and defensible, and which are false; do not include bibliographic recommendations or reading lists, since the sheet must be usable without library access.
