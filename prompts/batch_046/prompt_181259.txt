The examination question was as follows: "Explain J. L. Austin's distinction between constative and performative utterances. Set out the three felicity conditions he labels A, B and Γ, and apply that framework to the following case: in an automated legal platform, a speaker who does not in fact hold a particular lease utters 'I hereby assign my lease', and the smart-contract system executes the transfer regardless of the speaker's authority. Analyse whether this represents a misfire or an abuse, with reasons."

Below is one candidate's response.

Austin distinguishes constative utterances, which describe states of affairs and can be assessed as true or false, from performative utterances, which perform an action in being uttered—such as "I bet you" or "I name this ship". A performative is neither true nor false, but can be felicitous or infelicitous according to three conditions. Condition A requires an accepted conventional procedure with a particular conventional effect. Condition B requires that the particular persons and circumstances be appropriate for invoking that procedure, and that it be executed correctly and completely. Condition Γ requires that the participants have the requisite thoughts, feelings and intentions, and that they conduct themselves consistently afterwards. If A or B fail, the result is a misfire; if Γ fails, it is an abuse.

Applying this to the smart-contract case, the speaker lacks ownership of the lease, yet the algorithm completes the transaction. This is an abuse, because the speaker does not genuinely intend to transfer property they do not possess, which violates condition Γ regarding appropriate intentions. Nevertheless, the digital system verifies the syntax and executes the command, so condition B is satisfied through algorithmic authentication rather than through human authority. Austin's own taxonomy of illocutionary acts—assertives, directives, commissives, expressives and declarations—classifies this utterance as a declaration, since it brings about an institutional change purely by being spoken. Searle's preparatory conditions correspond closely to Austin's A and B, though Searle separates the sincerity condition more explicitly. Because the conventional procedure was carried out correctly by the system, there is no misfire; the failure lies solely in the speaker's insincerity and lack of entitlement, making it an abuse.
