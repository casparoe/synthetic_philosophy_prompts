I facilitate a monthly philosophy-of-economics reading group—about ten to twelve people, mostly graduate students and researchers steeped in the rationalist-blog tradition of treating "utility" as a formal object that still has to answer to intuition. We have been working through the formal structure of intertemporal choice: the decomposition of a discount rate into a pure time-preference component, a growth component, and a risk component, and the normative argument that the pure component ought to be zero on grounds of temporal neutrality. For our next 90-minute session I want something that does not begin with a lecture on why δ should be zero, but instead makes the group physically experience both the normative pull of treating goods at T, T+1, and T+4 as equivalent and the stubborn descriptive resistance to doing so, with no risk of non-delivery, no anticipated change in preferences, and no interest mechanism to hide behind.

Design a structured activity—part forced-allocation game, part role-play—that divides the session into three "time-slices" (this meeting, one week later, and four weeks later) and asks participants to distribute identical, fungible benefits across them using real but small stakes internal to the seminar, such as control over the opening discussion topic, exemption from a minor summary task, or selection of the snack/refreshment budget. The rules must be arranged so that any unequal allocation can only be defended by appealing to pure temporal location, and participants should feel the discomfort of trying to justify that defense aloud. Include concrete rules, materials, a payoff or procedural-consequence scheme that carries weight without real money, and a minute-by-minute timeline for the 90 minutes.

Then provide the debrief: map specific moments in the activity onto the four standard sources of discounting (pure preference, risk, growth, and changing tastes) so the group can see which ones have been eliminated by design and which one remains; predict exactly what will go wrong the first time—especially how participants will smuggle in hidden justifications about forgetfulness, social coordination, or "future-me won't appreciate it"—and explain how to handle that in the room without shutting down the experiment. Finally, be explicit about which feature of pure time preference the activity cannot reproduce—such as the long-horizon asymmetry across generations or the aggregation problem in social-choice settings—so I know where I must supply the argument verbally rather than expecting it to emerge from play.
