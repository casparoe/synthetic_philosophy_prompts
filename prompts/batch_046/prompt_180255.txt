The exam prompt was: "Contrast counterfactual (Lewis-style) and interventionist (Pearl/Woodward-style) accounts of causation with respect to overdetermination in settings where direct manipulation is impossible—such as attributing a policy failure to one of several sufficient institutional failures. Specifically, address (a) how each theory defines causal relevance, (b) why modularity might appear to restrict interventionism to experimental contexts, and (c) whether counterfactual similarity metrics avoid that restriction. Be precise about structural equations versus possible-world similarity."

The student wrote:

"Counterfactual theories define causation in terms of counterfactual dependence across possible worlds ordered by overall similarity. If C had not occurred and E would not have occurred in the nearest world where C is absent, then C causes E. Interventionism, developed by Pearl and Woodward, replaces worlds with structural causal models: X causes Y if an intervention on X—using the do-operator—changes Y's distribution while holding other mechanisms constant via modularity.

In overdetermined institutional cases—say, a policy failure caused jointly by regulatory capture and legislative gridlock—counterfactual analysis struggles because removing either factor still leaves the other sufficient; neither is necessary. Lewis resolves this by appealing to causal influence, tracked by quasi-dependence across worlds, rather than to binary causation. Interventionism handles the case more cleanly: applying the do-operator to one variable in the structural model isolates its contribution even when other sufficient causes remain, provided modularity holds.

Because modularity requires that mechanisms be independently alterable, interventionism seems confined to laboratory or engineered systems. Historical and institutional explanations lack such separability, so counterfactual theories are actually better suited to them, since they rely only on comparing world-similarity rather than decomposing mechanisms. The counterfactual theorist can weigh the small miracle needed to remove one institutional factor without altering others through standard similarity metrics. Therefore, for non-experimental domains, counterfactual accounts retain an advantage that interventionist formalism cannot match without importing statistical assumptions foreign to its core causal logic."
