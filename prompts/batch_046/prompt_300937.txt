I am currently enrolled in an advanced undergraduate seminar on the philosophy of perception and consciousness, and I am attempting to work through a conceptual difficulty that arose during our discussion of temporal experience. We examined the color phi phenomenon, in which two differently colored stimuli presented in rapid succession at adjacent spatial locations are experienced as a single object that moves across the gap and changes color at an intermediate point, despite the absence of any physical stimulus at that midpoint. I have also encountered the distinction between Orwellian and Stalinesque revisions of conscious content—broadly, whether earlier phenomenal states are retroactively altered once later information arrives, or whether an intact but fragmentary record is merely reinterpreted in the present. Could you explain, in strictly conceptual terms, how each of these two revision models applies to the intermediate color experience reported in the phi phenomenon? Specifically, does the Orwellian model require that the subject's earlier experience of the first dot is literally replaced in consciousness upon arrival of the second, or does it allow that only the narrative report is rewritten while the original phenomenal content remains unchanged? To probe this further, I want to consider a hypothetical dissociation: imagine that the interstimulus interval is lengthened just past the threshold for apparent motion, so that observers report seeing only two discrete, stationary flashes, yet they still report a brief, isolated experience of the intermediate color occurring precisely at the spatial midpoint and temporally between the two flashes. How should each revision model treat this separation of motion interpolation from color interpolation? Additionally, please clarify the conceptual dependencies involved: must one already commit to a particular account of the specious present, or to a predictive-processing framework, in order for the Orwellian/Stalinesque distinction to carry explanatory weight here? I am particularly concerned to avoid the misconception—common among my peers—that both accounts are merely different descriptions of the brain filling in missing sensory data, and I would like to understand exactly where that misconception fails for each model.
