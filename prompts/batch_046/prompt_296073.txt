I'm defending a seminar paper next week titled "Dummy Predicates and the Grammar of Consciousness" to a philosophy-of-mind reading group that meets in our department's graduate seminar room. My thesis is a specific version of illusionism: phenomenal properties don't exist, but not because experience is "all illusion" in the perceptual sense; rather, first-person reports deploy what I call dummy phenomenal predicates—syntactic structures that inherit the subject-predicate form from physical-object talk but apply to nothing, analogous to how "the present King of France" behaves grammatically without referring. Once we see that "what it's like" is not a property attribution but a grammatical placeholder organized by introspective reporting rules, the hard problem becomes a question about the evolutionary utility of this vocabulary, not about ontology. To make this concrete I argue that phenomenal concepts are not empty in the way "phlogiston" was empty—because phlogiston was meant to name a substance—whereas phenomenal predicates were never meant to name properties at all; they function like agreement markers in introspective discourse.
