Hey — I'm putting together a grad seminar on Ulysses contracts and constitutional precommitment, and I've been noticing this one failure mode that I can't tell is a recognized thing or just my own obsession.

Picture a constitutional hard constraint that's meant to bind future legislatures — not a vague aspiration, but a real precommitment, like a debt-ceiling lock with a supermajority release. Over time nobody repeals it; they still invoke it in speeches. But what happens is this gradual layering: an emergency-borrowing authority delegated to an independent board, an accounting rule that reclassifies certain obligations as "contingent liabilities" rather than debt, a sovereign-wealth vehicle that borrows on its own books. Each layer gets its own justification — "this preserves fiscal flexibility without touching the constitutional text" — and none of them technically violate the clause. But the upshot is that the constraint only applies in the easy cases. The original rule is still on the books, almost ceremonial, but it's been hollowed out from the outside by parallel structures that do exactly what the rule was meant to prevent.

So: is there a specific concept for this? I know about constitutional workaround and shadow constitutionalism, but those feel broader. I'm asking about the particular pattern where a Ulysses-style precommitment survives in form precisely because everyone agrees not to formally kill it, while they build an entire shadow system that makes it irrelevant. How's that different from ordinary amendment-by-interpretation, or from just running up debt? And if there's no established term, what's the sharpest way to draw its boundaries — especially the line between a legitimate exception and the kind of layered exception that collectively defeats the precommitment?
