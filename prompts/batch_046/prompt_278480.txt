I've been working through the decision-theoretic literature with a local rationalist reading group, and I want to stress-test a strong normative reading of Good's result on the value of information: that whenever information can be acquired without direct cost, rationality not merely permits but requires gathering all of it before committing to any irreversible action, and that any framework licensing action under residual uncertainty is thereby defective. I take this to hold even in embedded, resource-bounded settings, provided we account for computation costs separately. To ground it, consider cases of structural coupling between measurement and outcome—say, a diagnostic assay for a treatment-responsive disorder whose biochemical mechanism marginally alters the physiological state it reports on—so that information acquired at zero monetary cost is never fully orthogonal to the decision it informs.

Given this framing, what would actually count against the position? Deliver the answer as exactly five numbered items, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion. Each item should identify a specific vulnerability—formal result, empirical pattern, conceptual counterexample, or defensive qualification—sorted roughly by decisiveness, distinguishing evidence that would be merely awkward from evidence that genuinely defeats the view, including what defenders of the theorem themselves concede as problematic, and assessing whether the position has been insulated by ad hoc qualifications that risk unfalsifiability and whether that insulation is itself a theoretical vice.
