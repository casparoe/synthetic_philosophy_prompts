Following a lengthy and somewhat exhausting debate with a friend this evening about moral responsibility, I am trying to isolate the precise point where our reasoning diverged. We were examining Galen Strawson's Basic Argument—that true moral responsibility is impossible because nothing can be causa sui, and therefore no agent can be ultimately responsible for the mental nature from which actions flow. My friend proposed that this regress only works if we assume a linear, backward-looking model of causal explanation: to be responsible for my present character, I must have intentionally produced it at an earlier stage, which requires an earlier self, ad infinitum. He then suggested that if we adopt a non-linear, emergentist model of the self—where character is constituted by a dynamic, recursive feedback loop in which present evaluative choices continuously restructure the very conditions that shape future choices—the regress dissolves. The self need not be a substance that created itself ex nihilo; it can be a process that modifies its own structural parameters from within. I countered that even on this model, one never authored the initial conditions or the governing rules of the loop, but I now worry I conceded too quickly. Does Strawson's argument genuinely presuppose a substance-based or linearly temporal metaphysics of the self? Or does the core point hold regardless—namely, that on any architecture, whether linear, circular, or emergent, one remains unable to be the ultimate originator of one's own mental constitution, so that the impossibility of being causa sui is not escaped but merely redescribed?
