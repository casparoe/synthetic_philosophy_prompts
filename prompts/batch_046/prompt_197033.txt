I'm working on a framework for how disability-adjudication boards and emergency-triage protocols should classify patients when the sensory and affective dimensions of pain come apart. The clinical literature on pain asymbolia documents patients—often following lesions to the insula or posterior parietal cortex—who report that a noxious stimulus "hurts" using ordinary pain vocabulary, yet display no withdrawal reflex, no facial grimace, no distress behavior, and often refuse analgesics because they do not experience the sensation as unpleasant or as motivating avoidance. At the same time, nociplastic conditions such as fibromyalgia produce severe suffering and functional limitation in the absence of detectable tissue damage. Despite this dissociation, the institutional practices I am examining—Social Security disability evaluations, workers'-compensation assessments, and standardized ER triage scores—treat "pain severity" as a single axis that either tracks objective tissue damage or aggregates subjective self-report, never systematically separating nociceptive intensity from affective valence.

What philosophical theory of the badness of pain does that unified-axis framework implicitly presuppose? Does it commit these institutions to a hedonic account (pain is bad in virtue of feeling bad), a motivational account (pain is bad in virtue of driving avoidance), or a hybrid view? Reconstruct the implicit theory, distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what merely fits it well, and explain whether rival theories—such as one that treats aversive motivation as constitutive of pain rather than as a separable response—could license the same institutional rules. Then describe what a disability or triage system built on the dissociation would have to change: specifically, how it should classify an asymbolic patient with clear tissue damage but no suffering versus a nociplastic patient with intense suffering but clean imaging, and where the revised framework would have to leave the decision to human judgment rather than algorithmic scoring.
