Exam question: Reconstruct Newton’s bucket argument for absolute rotation, distinguishing carefully between the kinematic and dynamic phases of the experiment. Then present Mach’s principle as a relationalist response, being precise about whether Mach targets absolute space, absolute motion, or both. Finally, evaluate the following variant (the isolated-shell case): imagine the bucket and water are enclosed within a massive, spherically symmetric shell of matter that is initially at rest relative to the bucket; the bucket is then rotated relative to the shell, eventually dragging the water with it after friction acts. Would Mach’s principle predict concavity in the water’s surface, and does general relativistic frame-dragging vindicate, complicate, or replace the Machian explanation in this specific configuration? What, if anything, does this case show about the viability of substantivalism?

Student answer: Newton’s bucket argument begins with a bucket of water suspended by a twisted cord. When released, the bucket spins but the water initially remains flat because it is not yet moving relative to the bucket. Once friction causes the water to co-rotate with the bucket, the surface becomes concave, climbing the sides. Newton observes that in both phases the water’s motion relative to the bucket is the same—first none, then none—yet the dynamical effect appears only in the second phase. He concludes that the concavity tracks the water’s true motion through absolute space, not its relative motion to the bucket. This establishes that accelerated, rotational motion is absolute, though uniform linear motion remains undetectable, anticipating Einstein’s equivalence principle.

Mach’s principle offers a relationalist reply by denying that inertia is intrinsic or space-related. Instead, Mach proposes that the inertial properties of a body depend on its relation to all other matter in the universe. Applied to the bucket, the concavity is not caused by rotation relative to an empty spatial substratum but by rotation relative to the distant fixed stars. Thus Mach undermines Newton’s inference: the effect is relational, but the relevant relations are cosmic, not local.

In the isolated-shell case, the shell provides the distant matter. Initially the water and bucket are at rest relative to the shell; then the bucket rotates relative to the shell, eventually dragging the water. Once the water is co-rotating with the bucket, it is also rotating relative to the shell, so Mach’s principle predicts concavity, just as in Newton’s original scenario. This shows that the presence of the shell replaces the role of absolute space: there is no need to invoke a substantial space because the shell supplies the reference frame. Moreover, general relativistic frame-dragging demonstrates that a rotating massive shell literally drags local inertial frames, which experimentally confirms Mach’s idea that distant matter determines local inertia. Therefore the isolated-shell case supports relationism and suggests that substantivalism is redundant. The only limitation is that if the shell itself were absent, Mach’s principle might predict no concavity, but this is not empirically testable.
