I'm taking a course on the philosophy of logic and we got into a proper row about Carroll's tortoise — the dialogue where Achilles and the Tortoise keep pushing modus ponens back into the premises and never reach bedrock. Half my seminar thinks this proves deduction is primitive and asking for its justifcation is like asking why 2+2=4, while the other half thinks it shows logic needs a conventional or pragmatic foundation or we're stuck in infinite regress. We've argued for three sessions now and nobody's moved an inch, which feels strange since every move is well known.

I wonder if the stall is deeper than stubbornness. Both sides seem to assume reasoning works by explicitly representing rules and then applying them, but what if that's the wrong model entirely? Maybe human inference is more like a direct mental transition without an intermediate 'rule' premise at all, in which case Carroll's tortoise demands a justification for something that doesn't exist in cognition — not a defence of logic, but a defence of a false picture of how minds work. So is the disagreement actually about whether rules are explicit representations, and if so, is it substantive (about cognitive architecture), normative (about what counts as rational), or just verbal, with 'justifcation' meaning one thing to the normativists and another to the descriptivists? I want an honest diagnosis — is there a conceptual distinction or an empirical result that could break the deadlock, or are we answering different questions under the same heading?
