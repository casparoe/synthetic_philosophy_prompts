I've been participating in a long-running rationalist forum thread about whether Gödelian anti-mechanist arguments retain any bite against modern computational theories of mind, particularly in light of large language models that appear to perform mathematical reasoning without explicit formalisation. At one point, a participant summarised the Lucas–Penrose position with the line: "Gödel shows that any formal system powerful enough to do arithmetic can't prove its own consistency, yet I can see that I'm consistent; therefore I can't be a formal system."

I'm sure this slogan is doing several different jobs at once, but I can't tell which ones are actually load-bearing. Could you disambiguate the precise claims hiding in that one sentence? Specifically, I want a menu of readings that distinguishes:

- the logical claim about self-reference and provability within a fixed formal system;
- the epistemic claim about what it means for a mind to "see" or "know" its own consistency;
- the metaphysical claim about the ontology of minds versus algorithms;
- the comparative claim about whether human mathematicians actually do possess the kind of consistency-knowledge that Gödel's theorem places beyond formal reach.

For each reading, indicate whether it is trivial, substantive but defensible, substantive but false, or simply a category error. Then tell me which reading is required to get the anti-mechanist conclusion, and whether that reading survives the standard objections (for instance, that human reason might be inconsistent, or that a system could prove the consistency of a subsystem rather than itself). Please answer in British English, and do not ask me to consult specific texts or provide bibliographies.
