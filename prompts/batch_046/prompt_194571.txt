I have been asked to deliver the opening remarks at the inauguration of a museum wing that houses two adjacent galleries—one documenting the Holodomor, the other the Nanjing Massacre—deliberately arranged without any comparative plaque, numerical ranking, or curatorial statement of relative severity. This is not an abstract exercise for me: my grandmother survived the engineered famine in Ukraine, and my partner's family was killed in Nanjing. The audience will be mixed—historians who study comparative genocide, descendants of survivors, and local officials expecting language about shared dignity—so the remarks must work for specialists without leaving everyone else behind. I need you to draft the speech itself, not lecture me on the ethics. The governing structural image should be the physical architecture of the adjoining rooms: proximity without fusion, acknowledgment without a wall or hierarchy inscribed between them.

The philosophical problem that should shape every paragraph is whether ranking historical evils is conceptually possible or morally obscene, and I want you to treat this not as a simple yes-or-no question but as a tension between two modes of comparison. On one side is quantitative aggregation—mortality figures, geographic scope, temporal duration—which invites a calculus of magnitude. On the other side is what I will call the “normative grammar” of an atrocity: the idea that each event violates a distinct structural logic—starvation weaponized as state administration versus militarized civilian annihilation—so that translating either event into the comparative metrics of the other risks conceptual distortion rather than illumination. The speech should therefore avoid both the false comfort of declaring all suffering identical and the obscenity of declaring one history the greater evil; it should include one precise passage addressed to the historians present about why comparison can be epistemically necessary and morally dangerous at once, followed by a closing gesture that honors both family lines separately without collapsing them into a single narrative of victimhood. Please do not include citations, reading suggestions, or references to specific philosophers by name; the argument should stand entirely on the reasoning visible in the speech. The tone should be formal, restrained, and appropriate to a memorial-adjacent occasion.
