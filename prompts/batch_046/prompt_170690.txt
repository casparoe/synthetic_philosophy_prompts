In philosophical and market discussions of art forgeries, a recurring analogy compares an undetectable perfect forgery to counterfeit currency: both replicate every observable feature of the genuine article, both can perform the same transactional or aesthetic function, yet we treat the counterfeit as worthless or criminal and the original as uniquely valuable, which suggests that the value of the original derives purely from institutional authorization and provenance rather than from any intrinsic property. Proponents use this to argue that our attachment to originals is essentially a fetish for origin, comparable to our trust in a central mint. Audit this analogy in detail. First, provide an explicit correspondence table mapping elements of the monetary system—the mint, legal tender status, the counterfeiting act, transactional utility, state confiscation upon discovery—onto elements of the art object and its market—the artist's studio, exhibition history, material substrate, aesthetic experience, authentication protocols. Second, identify the disanalogies that bear on the intended conclusion: does an artwork possess a "function" analogous to exchange value, or is its value partly constituted by being the very object that bore the artist's intentional marks? Does the currency analogy illicitly import a social-convention theory of value that applies to fiat money but collapses when applied to aesthetic or historical significance? Third, separate the breaks that merely complicate the comparison from those that undermine the normative claim that originality is only genealogically valuable. Finally, state whether the conclusion—that reverence for originals is an institutional prejudice with no deeper grounding—survives the audit, and if not, suggest whether a different technical analogy—perhaps cryptographic provenance, manufacturing dies, or biological cloning—captures the original-forgery distinction more faithfully without smuggling in the same normative load.
