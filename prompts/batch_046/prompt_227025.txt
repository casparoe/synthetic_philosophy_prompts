You are an expert in the philosophy of archaeology and the logic of inference from material culture, particularly the distinction between functional behavior and institutionally governed practice. I am a graduate student in an archaeology seminar focused on reconstructing belief systems from non-textual remains, and I need a rigorous, reusable decision procedure that a field director or post-excavation analyst could apply repeatedly without requiring philosophical training or library access.

Please think carefully about the conceptual translation before constructing the procedure. Start with a brief brainstorming phase: identify the three most common inferential errors archaeologists make when they move from broken objects to claims about shared institutional beliefs—such as assuming that deliberate damage always signals ritual rather than practical decommissioning, or treating spatial clustering as sufficient proof of symbolic arrangement. For each error, state the corrective principle that should govern the procedure.

Then design a step-by-step operational protocol for analyzing an assemblage of deliberately modified objects—specifically ceramic vessels or tools that have been rendered non-functional through either selective thermal shock or mechanical fracture, then deposited near a water source or boundary feature in arrangements that violate purely utilitarian spatial logic. The procedure must include:

1. An entry gate: the observable physical properties that must be documented before any institutional inference is even permissible, with explicit thresholds (e.g., what counts as uniform wall thickness, what distinguishes thermal from mechanical fracture patterns at the macro scale).
2. A sequence of ordered tests, running from the most easily verified in a standard lab or field setting to the more interpretive, each with a specified sufficient answer and a clear stop point where the answer is "functional only" or "insufficient evidence for institutional claim."
3. The judgment nodes that cannot be eliminated by further data—particularly the decision about whether similarity in treatment across multiple objects indicates a shared prescriptive rule or merely independent practical reasoning under similar constraints—and a note on who must sign off at each such node.
4. A frank statement of where the procedure will systematically misfire, especially with hybrid assemblages in which ritual decommissioning and practical discard occur in the same stratigraphic layer, so that spatial proximity may indicate chronological coincidence rather than institutional association.

Apply the completed procedure to three cases:
- Case A: thermally fractured ceramic vessels with no cooking wear, uniform wall thickness, arranged in a semi-circular cluster near a spring, with no utilitarian reason for the location.
- Case B: mechanically shattered tools with clear use-wear, scattered in a midden with no spatial patterning and no boundary association.
- Case C (the case where the procedure should yield an answer the practitioner will not like): a mixed layer where half the objects show ritual-style thermal treatment and half show functional mechanical breakage, arranged linearly along a slope that could be either a processional path or a drainage feature.

The deliverable should be something a practitioner can run in under an hour per assemblage. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citation requests. Be explicit about what the procedure cannot do—particularly whether it can ever distinguish a deeply internalized institutional rule from a widely shared but unreflective practical habit.
