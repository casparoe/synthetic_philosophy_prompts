I advise an exhibit-design and documentary-consulting team that must classify every proposed claim about the historical Jesus into one of three display categories: critically established, plausible but biographically underdetermined, or speculative reconstruction. We apply the standard historical-critical criteria—multiple attestation, dissimilarity, embarrassment, coherence, and linguistic traces—but we keep hitting a structural problem: a detail can satisfy every criterion and still only tell us what kind of first-century figure Jesus resembled, not who he uniquely was. Write a reusable decision procedure my team can run on any proposed claim without requiring specialist judgment at every step. The procedure should present ordered questions, specify what counts as a sufficient answer at each stage, flag the points where calculation ends and human values judgment must take over, and deliver a clear classification or a deliberate stop. Build into the design a committed, argued position on whether authenticity criteria can ever settle biographical identity as opposed to social role—do not present both options neutrally; defend one view and let it shape the weights in your procedure. Then apply the procedure to three cases: Jesus’s baptism by John the Baptist; the claim that Jesus was literate and could compose texts in Koine Greek; and the claim that he directed an armed insurrectionist cell in Jerusalem. Include at least one case—preferably the literacy or insurrection claim—where the procedure yields a classification that conflicts with a widely held popular or devotional narrative, and explain exactly how the team should present that unwelcome result. Conclude by stating explicitly what the procedure cannot do, where it is likely to misfire, and which distinctions it deliberately drops in order to remain usable by non-specialists.
