I'm writing a comparative study of how administrative bodies construct the boundary of the "undeserving poor" without explicitly invoking moral desert, and for the past eighteen months I've been tracking every published ruling of the Regional Welfare Appeals Board. Their record is as follows. First, when a claimant concealed occasional freelance income totaling about fifteen percent of her benefit, the Board ordered repayment of the overpayment but rejected the agency's request for a twelve-month disqualification, distinguishing negligent omission from strategic deception and noting that she eventually corrected her records herself. Second, they overturned a sanction against a single parent who refused a mandatory work placement requiring a three-hour daily commute, holding that conditions must satisfy a "reasonable proximity" test, though they explicitly affirmed that refusal of a genuinely accessible position would justify suspension. Third, they upheld a sanction against a claimant with a vocational IT certificate who turned down a cleaning job twenty minutes away, ruling that a claimant's subjective assessment of occupational suitability does not override the obligation to accept available paid work. Fourth, they reduced but did not suspend benefits for a claimant who hid his adult son's salary-level income, reasoning that total suspension would penalize dependents and that the deception was partial rather than total. Fifth, they waived the standard automatic suspension for a claimant who missed three consecutive reporting interviews due to documented anxiety and depression, substituting instead a single in-person assessment with a social worker.

The Board must now decide a case that does not map neatly onto any of these five. A claimant enrolled in the mandatory Rapid Re-employment Program has attended every required session and submitted the full quota of real job applications, but every application was for an unpaid museum-curation internship rather than for any paid position listed in the program's job bank; a caseworker discovered the pattern when she noticed that all submissions went to nonprofit cultural institutions. The claimant argues that her specialized degree makes these internships her only structurally viable path to long-term employment, and that forcing her into the program's low-skill retail targets amounts to a category error rather than a genuine opportunity. The agency seeks full suspension for misuse of program resources and failure to pursue paid employment. If the Board wants to remain consistent with its own precedents, what should it decide, which of the five rulings is the closest analog, and does the track record genuinely underdetermine the outcome? Lay out the competing organizing principles—perhaps one centered on contractual reciprocity to the program's explicit purpose, one on structural mismatch between claimant profile and program design, and one on procedural good faith—say what each predicts in this case, give your best-guess verdict with the reasoning that favors it, name the principle the Board would be adopting if it went the other way, and specify the one further case that would best tell these principles apart.
