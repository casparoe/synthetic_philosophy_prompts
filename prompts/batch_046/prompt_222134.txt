I'm trying to pin down this move I keep seeing in debates about whether societies should keep commemorating old atrocities. One side says we have a duty to remember the victims, otherwise we let the perpetrators win by erasing the record. The other side says constant remembrance keeps the wound open and prevents the descendents of victims from living fully in the present. But then there's a third position that isn't quite either: they say the problem isn't remembering vs forgetting, but that the remembering has turned into something ceremonial and state-sponsored, like museums that get funding precisely because they keep the trauma visible, and this creates an incentive to never actually resolve or integrate the past. Meanwhile, people who want to 'move on' are accused of being in denial, even when they explicitly acknowledge what happened and just want to stop organizing public life around it. Is there a specific name for this deadlock where remembrance becomes baked into institutions in a way that makes genuine integretion impossible, and where the call to forget is misread as denial? I feel like it's adjacent to Margalit's ethics of remembering and maybe Rieff's critique of therapeutic culture, but neither quite captures the funding-incentive part. Or am I just describing something that doesn't have its own label?
