I am delivering the opening remarks at the retirement dinner for Elena Vasquez, who directed our county's land-use mediation office for thirty-two years. The audience is split: about half are urban planners, policy staff, and lawyers who worked with her and understand technical trade-offs; the other half are family members, neighbors, and volunteers who know her only through the outcomes of her work. I have no academic background, but I spent fifteen years as a community organizer sitting across the table from her, and I have been thinking on my own about the ideas people call satisficing and bounded rationality. I want a speech, roughly eight minutes, that uses those ideas not as decoration but as the actual architecture: it should explain that Elena never tried to maximize every variable, and that this was not laziness or resignation but a disciplined way of deciding. Here is the part I want you to weave in carefully: I have come to believe that once a person accepts a solution that is merely adequate, they do not simply stop looking for better ones; they develop an unspoken loyalty to the very imperfection they settled for, and they begin to protect it as if it were a principle. I do not know whether philosophers have named this, but I want the speech to treat it as real. The speech must honor her without invoking an afterlife or suggesting she will be watching over the office, and it must be intelligible to her family without talking down to her colleagues. Please write it in American English, formally but warmly, and do not include citations, bibliographies, or reading lists.
