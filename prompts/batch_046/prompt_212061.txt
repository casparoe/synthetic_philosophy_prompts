I'm working through a problem at the intersection of metaphysics and philosophy of science that I haven't seen stated cleanly: what should we demand from a theory that identifies the correct ontology when the information that would distinguish competing ontologies is empirically inaccessible in principle? Rather than asking for a survey of existing positions, I want the full specification—the desiderata that any adequate theory of ontology identification must satisfy under conditions of permanent epistemic inaccessibility.

Please construct the requirements list with the conceptual motivation behind each. The theory needs to cover cases where two ontologies are observationally and structurally identical at every accessible level but diverge in their commitments to unobservable entities, properties, or compositional structure—standard quantum-mechanical underdetermination, certainly, but also more exotic scenarios like categorically equivalent mathematical structures with different internal ontologies, or worlds containing permanently screened-off hidden variables versus worlds lacking them entirely. For each desideratum, specify what pressure generates it: must the theory preserve strict empirical adequacy, must it avoid arbitrary ontological stipulation, must it permit revision if access conditions were to change, must it respect a distinction between genuine ontological indeterminacy and mere epistemic limitation, and must it deliver a unique ontology or allow for plural acceptable answers? Mark which requirements are non-negotiable for any serious contender and which are camp-specific preferences—say, the anti-realist demand that inaccessibility entails ontological indefiniteness versus the realist demand that structural or abductive criteria can still single out one ontology.

Crucially, flag any subset of these desiderata that you take to be jointly unsatisfiable given standard constraints—particularly whether the demand for unique ontological identification and the demand for empirical grounding can both be met when the distinguishing information is permanently inaccessible. Then, briefly, score how structural realism, ontic structural realism, and a deflationary view on which there is simply no further fact of the matter fare against your list, but keep the weight firmly on the criteria and their motivating cases rather than on the scorecard. I want the list itself to do the conceptual work: to reveal which demands are genuine constraints and which are prejudices of particular camps, and to show whether the problem of inaccessible ontology identification is a tractable research program or collapses into a tension among its own requirements.
