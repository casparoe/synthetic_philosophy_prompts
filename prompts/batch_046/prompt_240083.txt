I am a high school junior preparing to lead a single fifty-minute discussion for a study group of peers who have no formal philosophy background but are familiar with science-fiction films involving body-swapping and artificial intelligence. Our focus is Dennett's brain-transplant puzzle, often referred to as Where Am I, in which a philosopher's brain is extracted, placed in a vat, and remotely linked to a body, so that the question of where the self is actually located becomes unstable. I do not want an explanation of the puzzle itself; I want a committed teaching plan that makes definite choices and gives its reasons. Specifically: lay out the exact order in which the components must be introduced so that the conceptual problem emerges rather than being assumed, identifying precisely what must be in place before the brain-in-vat scenario is even intelligible as a philosophical challenge rather than as a horror plot. Choose the one concrete scenario or variation that should carry the entire session—specifying what it must include and what it must deliberately lack—rather than surveying alternatives. Map the misconceptions this audience will predictably bring from popular culture—such as conflating the puzzle with cloning, assuming the brain is simply the pilot of the body, or treating the answer as intuitively obvious—and specify exactly where in the sequence each misconception should be headed off, with reasons for that placement. Design one final question I can ask to determine whether they have grasped the problem of personal location rather than just recalled the narrative. Finally, state explicitly what elements—such as the full history of personal-identity theories, technical neuroscience, or Dennett's broader functionalist commitments—must be left out at this level and said so. Take a definite stance on each of these choices; do not present them as equally valid options for me to choose between.
