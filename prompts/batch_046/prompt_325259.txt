Read Jorge Luis Borges's 1941 short story "The Garden of Forking Paths" through the lens of the Everett, Copenhagen, and Bohm interpretations of quantum mechanics. The story's central conceit is Ts'ui Pên's unfinished novel, which is also a labyrinth: instead of selecting one outcome for each event, it describes every possible branching future simultaneously. Stephen Albert interprets this as a single work in which every person becomes lost among infinitely forking paths, and he cites Ts'ui Pên's statement that he leaves "to several futures (not to all) my garden of forking paths." Most readers take this as a literary anticipation of Everett's many-worlds interpretation, but I want you to resist that easy equation and work with a more specific problem. In Everettian mechanics, the "preferred basis problem" asks why decoherence selects particular macroscopic variables—positions of particles, states of devices—as the axes along which worlds split, rather than splitting along arbitrary superpositions. Consider whether Borges's narrative structure—where branches are defined by discrete narrative events (the train arriving or not, the spy being caught or escaping, the murder being committed or not)—implicitly provides a "narrative environment" that solves or sidesteps the preferred-basis problem. Does the story treat branching as ontologically real in the Everettian sense, or does it treat the branches as epistemic possibilities that remain unrealized except for the one line we read?

Then turn to the Copenhagen interpretation. In Copenhagen, a quantum superposition collapses to a definite outcome only upon interaction with a classical measuring apparatus that lies outside the quantum formalism. In the story, the labyrinth is observed and reconstructed by Albert (the sinologist) and narrated by Yu Tsun (the spy). Does their act of interpretation function like a Copenhagen measurement, collapsing the infinite drafts of Ts'ui Pên's novel into a single coherent narrative? Or does the story resist that collapse—note that Tsun himself moves between timelines without experiencing a single collapsed history, and that Albert smiles and says there is at least one timeline where Tsun is his enemy? Finally, consider Bohmian mechanics: here the universal wavefunction evolves deterministically, guiding particles along definite trajectories, so there is only one actual world, but the wavefunction contains "empty branches" corresponding to outcomes that never occur. Could Borges's image of paths that exist in the novel but are "not taken" in any given reader's experience map onto Bohm's empty waves? Or does the story's insistence that every branch is equally real and inhabited contradict Bohm's ontology of a single realized path?

Throughout, do not reduce the story to an illustration of physics. Say where the literary form—its nested narration, its framing by Captain Liddell Hart's historical record, its ending in which Tsun's murder communicates a strategic message—complicates or enriches the philosophical debate in ways that physical argument alone does not. What does Borges add that a physics paper on decoherence or hidden variables misses?

Please say what would change your mind: which argument about the story's ending, or which technical point about decoherence, hidden variables, or the measurement problem, would lead you to revise your reading? And identify the specific point in your reasoning that is most likely to fail—whether it is your interpretation of Albert's smile, your mapping of narrative choice onto quantum branching, or your assumption that the story's labyrinth can be read as a physical model at all.
