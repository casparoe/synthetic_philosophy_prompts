Consider the following argument, which a study group developed to show that epistemic modesty—understood as the demand that one weigh one's first-person reasoning (the inside view) against statistical base rates drawn from a reference class (the outside view)—is ultimately self-defeating.

(1) Epistemic modesty requires that, when I assess whether my belief that p is justified, I compare my inside-view reasoning with the outside-view track record of some reference class R that includes me.

(2) The belief being assessed is not merely a descriptive claim—e.g., that people with property F tend to hold p—but a centered, de se belief: that I, from my present epistemic position, am justified in holding p.

(3) Outside-view statistics are necessarily descriptive; they state frequencies over classes defined by third-person properties such as training, disciplinary background, or past predictive accuracy.

(4) Because centered beliefs contain an essential indexical that descriptive categories omit, a descriptive frequency over R cannot by itself determine the probability that my particular centered belief is true.

(5) To apply the outside view, one therefore needs a translation principle: if members of R are wrong about p-type claims at rate r, then I should lower my credence in my centered belief by approximately r.

(6) But identifying R and endorsing that translation principle are themselves exercises of first-person judgment: I must decide that I belong to R, that R is the relevant class, and that the correlation applies to this case.

(7) Consequently, every use of the outside view to constrain the inside view presupposes prior inside-view judgments about reference-class membership and relevance.

(8) If those prior judgments are themselves subject to the demand for modesty, then constraining them requires further inside-view judgments, generating a regress.

(9) The regress can be halted only by granting some inside-view judgments unconditional authority—exactly what the universal demand for modesty prohibits.

(10) Therefore, consistent epistemic modesty either collapses into an infinite regress or forces the abandonment of all substantive belief; since neither result is acceptable, the argument must contain a hidden flaw.

We are convinced the conclusion is wrong—modesty does not destroy itself—but we cannot agree on where the mistake lies. Which step should be rejected, and why does it initially look plausible? In particular, is the error in treating centered beliefs as irreducible to descriptive ones (step 4), in assuming that selecting a reference class is an inside-view judgment rather than an adoptable methodological rule (step 6), or in inferring a vicious regress rather than a benign circularity (step 8)? Explain the cost of giving up your chosen step: what must the defender of epistemic modesty concede, and does that concession weaken the original motivation for privileging the outside view? Provide a point-by-point assessment that justifies your diagnosis.
