I am preparing notes for an essay in an undergraduate epistemology seminar, and I would value your critical comments on the coherence and originality of the argument I am developing. Rather than rehearsing the familiar Gettier cases in which a justified true belief rests on a false premise, I want to examine what I am calling redundant-path failures: situations in which an agent's belief is indeed true and supported by an independent, sound justification that she does not possess or employ, yet her actual reasoning proceeds through a false intermediate claim. Consider a historian who concludes that a treaty was ratified in 1648 solely because she misinterprets a corrupted manuscript; entirely separate from this, an unconsulted archival record confirms the same date, but she is unaware of it. Here the belief is true, a reliable path exists in principle, but the cognitive route she actually traversed is epistemically defective. I suggest that standard analyses — whether reliabilist, virtue-theoretic, or those adding a no-false-lemma condition — fail to isolate the specific problem, because they focus on whether some available justification is sound rather than on whether the justification actually operative in the agent's reasoning is sound.

I therefore propose that knowledge requires not merely that no false premise be present somewhere in the agent's epistemic environment, but that the particular chain of reasoning the agent activates be free of false premises, or at least that the agent be able to distinguish which premises are doing the justificatory work. I am concerned that this condition might collapse into a redundant mental-state requirement, or that it might conflate epistemic luck with the kind of structural redundancy I have described. Could you assess whether this distinction genuinely separates redundant-path cases from ordinary Gettier cases and from pure epistemic luck? I would also appreciate comments on whether my proposed condition is too demanding, whether the historian example is ambiguous in ways I have missed, and whether the notion of the justification actually employed can be made precise without presupposing a particular theory of mental causation.
