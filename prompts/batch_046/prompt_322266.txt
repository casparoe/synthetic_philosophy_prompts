I have been working through Plato's Meno problem and constructed the following argument, which seems to show that knowledge is not genuinely more valuable than true belief, though the conclusion feels wrong to me. (1) In the Meno, Socrates answers that knowledge exceeds true belief because it is bound down by an account or reasoning, while belief can drift away. (2) If this binding is what produces the surplus value, then any instance of knowledge must contain it. (3) Consider a case of distributed epistemic dependence: a researcher accepts a complex result because a trusted colleague explains it, but the researcher herself never grasps the proof; the reasoning remains anchored in the colleague's mind rather than her own. (4) Because the reasoning does not reside in the researcher, her belief is not bound down by her own account. (5) From (2) it follows that she does not have knowledge. (6) Yet we ordinarily treat such well-supported testimonial acceptance as knowledge, so (5) must be wrong. (7) Hence the binding account is not necessary for knowledge, and therefore cannot be the source of its extra value. (8) With no other distinguishing feature in sight, knowledge and true belief share the same value. (9) Consequently the Meno problem rests on an illusion. I am inclined to preserve both the thought that testimony can yield knowledge and the thought that knowledge is genuinely more valuable than a lucky true belief. Which premise or step should I abandon, why did it seem plausible, and what does giving it up cost me?
