In philosophy of action, the absent-minded driver—who intends to stop at the store but, through habit, drives past the turn and arrives at the office—serves as a canonical challenge to the view that intentional action requires concurrent conscious guidance. A comparison now circulating likens the driver to an aircraft flying on autopilot with the pilot asleep at the controls: a flight plan was deliberately filed, the system executes it without real-time oversight, and if an outdated map sends the plane to the wrong airport we withhold full blame from the pilot without treating the flight as random. Proponents use this analogy to defend an intermediate thesis: absent-minded actions remain genuinely intentional, guided by an enduring plan, yet they lack full agential attribution because supervisory control is suspended. Please audit how far the comparison holds. First, supply the correspondence table—pilot, flight plan, autopilot mechanism, air-traffic updates, emergency wake-up—mapped onto the driver, the original intention, motor habit, environmental cues, and sudden awareness. Second, isolate the disanalogies that matter for the intermediate thesis: whether the autopilot can be deceived, whether it possesses representational states, whether the pilot retains veto authority that the driver lacks, and whether system errors are mechanical rather than agential. Third, say whether the conclusion—that absent-minded driving is intentional but not fully attributable—survives once these breaks are registered, or whether the analogy was carrying the argument. If a different analogy better captures habit-driven action without importing spurious features, propose it and justify the replacement.
