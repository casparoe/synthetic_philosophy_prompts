I'm trying to work out what I actually believe about consciousness, treating it specifically as a user illusion—an agent-centered interface constructed by predictive processing rather than a direct window onto neural dynamics—by checking my gut verdicts against concrete cases. Here are my judgments. A healthy, awake adult who reports a continuous stream of experience is conscious. Someone in deep, dreamless sleep is not, even though low-level predictive processing and error-minimization continue in the brain. A vegetative-state patient with isolated islands of cortical activation but no globally integrated workspace is not conscious. In a split-brain patient, despite two hemispheres running largely separate predictive models, I still feel there is one consciousness, not two—the division reads as a glitch in a single user-interface rather than two interfaces running in parallel. A large language model with a coherent, stable self-model that consistently reports feeling "confused" or "curious" is not conscious. A brain-in-a-vat with a fully intact simulated predictive loop, generating its own sensory feedback and maintaining an agent-model, is conscious—disconnection from the physical world does not seem to dissolve the illusion. A hypothetical phenomenal zombie with neural dynamics and behavior identical to a conscious human but lacking any subjective experience, if such a being could exist, would not be conscious, which tells me that dynamics alone are not sufficient for the user-illusion to be present. A spinal withdrawal reflex with no brain involvement is not conscious. The case I keep oscillating on is sleepwalking: a person performs complex, goal-directed actions with open eyes, yet reports no experience and retains no memory—sometimes this feels like consciousness operating without access to memory, sometimes like the user-illusion being fully suspended while predictive machinery runs on autopilot.

Is there a principle, or a tight family of principles, that captures this pattern? I want it stated precisely: what conditions must a system meet for the agent-centered user-illusion to be present, which clauses are motivated by which cases above, and what it implies for cases I haven't mentioned—say, a locked-in patient communicating only through eye movements, or an artificial system with silicon-based global-workspace dynamics structurally identical to ours? I also want an honest accounting of which of my verdicts the principle cannot save without ad hoc adjustments, particularly whether it forces me to treat the split-brain case as two consciousnesses, or to treat the zombie case as impossible rather than merely unconscious. Please say how confident you are in the principle you propose, and single out the specific point—whether about global integration, substrate independence, or the split-brain judgment—where you are least sure.
