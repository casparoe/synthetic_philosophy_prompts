I'm putting together study materials for a philosophy-of-computation reading group, and I'm dissatisfied with the usual binary framing—"the Church-Turing thesis is a mathematical theorem" versus "it is only a conventional definition." I want a decision tree that sorts the actual positions people hold when they disagree about analog hypercomputation, quantum computation, and whether the thesis could be empirically falsified by physics. The tree should begin from concrete commitments a student can answer without recognizing any philosophical label: yes-or-no questions about whether there is a precise, pre-formal notion of "effective procedure" independent of Turing machines; whether continuous or probabilistic physical processes could in principle compute a function that no Turing machine computes; and whether the thesis is protected from counterexample by definitional fiat rather than by fact. Each node should be phrased as a standalone question the student could answer on its own terms, with branches leading toward named positions.

The leaves should include at least the "mathematical identification" view, the "physical-law" interpretation, the "conventional definition" stance, the "falsifiable conjecture" position, and an "epistemically indeterminate" view, alongside any genuinely unoccupied combinations of answers—each empty slot carrying a one-sentence diagnosis of whether it is incoherent, conceptually unattractive, or merely unexplored. Please flag any pair of leaves that the tree cannot cleanly separate, such as whether the physical-law view collapses into the falsifiable-conjecture view once one allows that physical laws themselves are conjectural. To keep the instrument in the weeds without requiring outside sources, inject one niche framing branch: ask whether stochastic or probabilistic computation should count as an "effective procedure" if it produces non-Turing-computable outputs with non-zero probability, and show exactly where that commitment lands in the tree, even if it yields a contested or unoccupied leaf. The audience is advanced undergraduates familiar with Turing machines but not with the philosophy literature, so do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations, and do not ask the student to name authors they have not read.
