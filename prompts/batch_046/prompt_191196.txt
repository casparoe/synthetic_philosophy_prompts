You serve on a university’s graduate fellowship committee. We must award three identical, indivisible grants, but our six finalists are tied on every evaluative metric—portfolio quality, references, and structured interviews—such that no defensible ranking exists. One member argues we should draw lots, invoking Buridan’s ass: when reasons are perfectly balanced, rational choice requires an arbitrary tie-breaker, and random selection respects the equality of reasons. Another insists that randomization violates meritocratic responsibility and that we must invent a further criterion, however artificial, to preserve the idea that selection tracks desert. A third proposes splitting each grant into sequential half-year terms, but the funding agreement prohibits division. Please draft the brief written opinion you will circulate before Friday’s meeting: it should recommend one concrete course of action with a clear implementation timeline, treat the tie not as a measurement failure but as a genuine case of choice without preference, and engage seriously with the strongest argument for the losing position—specifically the claim that arbitrary selection undermines the normative authority of the committee itself.
