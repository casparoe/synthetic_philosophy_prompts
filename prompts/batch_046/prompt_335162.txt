I have constructed an argument extending Sen's liberal paradox into constitutional design, and I believe it proves too much—namely, that liberal democracy is conceptually incoherent—yet I cannot identify the precise misstep. I would be grateful for a diagnosis.

(1) Sen's condition of minimal liberalism requires that for each individual i there exist at least one pair of social states differing only in i's private choice over which i's strict preference is socially decisive.
(2) The Pareto principle requires that if every individual strictly prefers state x to state y, society ranks x above y.
(3) Sen's original case shows that (1) and (2) conflict when preferences are interdependent—when citizens hold preferences about others' private choices.
(4) One proposed resolution is constitutional domain restriction: partition the social state space so that (1) governs within each citizen's designated private domain D_i, while (2) governs within the public domain.
(5) However, the boundary of D_i—what counts as i's private choice—is not self-defining; it must be settled collectively, or the domains become arbitrary.
(6) Applying (1) reflexively, each citizen i should be decisive over the boundary of D_i, because that boundary determines whether i's sovereignty is protected.
(7) Applying (2) to the choice of boundaries, if there is unanimous agreement on a particular partition, that partition ought to be adopted.
(8) Preferences over domain boundaries are necessarily interdependent: the scope of D_j affects i's welfare and feasible set, so every citizen will hold preferences over every other citizen's boundary.
(9) Consequently, for any proposed partition, (6) demands that i control i's boundary, while (7) and (8) together imply that collective preference may override i's choice of boundary.
(10) Thus no partition can consistently satisfy both conditions.
(11) Without a partition, (1) and (2) apply globally; given (8), they conflict in every society.
(12) Since preference interdependence is unavoidable—utility is always context-dependent—no constitutional framework can simultaneously guarantee minimal liberal rights and democratic unanimity.
(13) Liberal democracy, which asserts precisely this combination, is therefore conceptually incoherent.

I am especially reluctant to abandon (1) and (5), though I could tolerate weakening (8) or (12). Which step is the weakest link? Please explain why it initially seemed sound, what giving it up costs for the broader argument, and whether there is a principled repair that allows me to retain minimal liberalism without the reductio. Please reply in British English. I also ask that you say how confident you are in your diagnosis, and that you explicitly identify the point about which you are least certain.
