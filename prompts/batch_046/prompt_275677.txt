Write a Petrarchan sonnet — an octave that establishes a conceptual problem and a sestet with a volta that turns rather than resolves — on Pascal’s claim that justice and force are inseparable yet never genuinely coincide without artifice. In the octave, establish the two orders he distinguishes: justice belongs to the order of charity and reason, yet within the order of the body it is powerless unless it borrows the armour of established force; conversely, naked force is tyrannical until custom and law dress it in legitimacy. Use rhyme and metre to keep the two orders audibly separate but structurally interdependent. At the volta, introduce the more unsettling corollary Pascal draws: that the only distribution which avoids both envy and conflict is allocation by lot, since merit always invites dispute and lot removes the ground of comparison. Let the closing couplet hold this tension in suspension — do not resolve it into a modern liberal defence of rights, nor into a cynical celebration of power — but allow the form itself to show why the orders cannot be unified. The poem should be precise enough that a reader familiar with the Pensées recognises the specific tension between the ordre du corps and the ordre de charité, rather than encountering a generic meditation on law and violence.
