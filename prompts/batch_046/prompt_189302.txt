I just moved into this neigborhood where they're trying to put up a four-story mixed-use building on the empty corner lot, and suddenly half the block is talking about "shadow impacts" and "parking stress" even though the place has been vacant for years. I'm pretty sure most of them don't actually care about the sun; they care that the planning board okayed it without their informal veto. I'm taking this urban ethics seminar and our prof asked us to design a 75-minute workshop for about 14 people that makes that specific tension visceral — not the usual build-versus-block debate, but the feeling that your procederal standing got ignored even if the housing outcome is actually okay.

I want a structured game or role-play where participants hold "shadow veto tokens" — they can only stop a proposed project by invoking a procedural rule (like "insufficient notice" or "wrong committee"), never by saying they just don't want the building — and then they have to live with whatever gets built through those rules. Can you lay out the roles (maybe some are renters, some are homeowners, one is the planner, one is a developer with a hidden budget), a timeline for the 75 minutes, the payoff or scoring rules, and then a debrief that maps what actually happened onto the idea of procedural NIMBYism? Please be honest about which feature of real-world zoning politics this can't reproduce — especially the part where richer neighborhoods have lawyers and poorer ones don't — and how you'd handle the predictable meltdown when people realize they've been forced to use procedural excuses for what are really distributive objections.
