As a high school student examining the ontology of social categories, I hold that generational labels—Boomers, Millennials, Gen Z—are not real kinds but institutional artifacts produced by census and marketing conventions. I want to advance a more specific claim: that a cohort becomes ontologically significant only as a “position-cohort,” defined by members occupying structurally identical positions in economic history—such as entering the labor market during a systemic credit contraction—while birth-year ranges remain mere correlates. What would have to be true for this position-cohort account to fail? I am asking for the observations, statistical patterns, or conceptual arguments that should genuinely change my mind, sorted by decisiveness: for instance, whether attitudinal traits cluster more tightly around birth years than around economic-entry points, whether intra-cohort variance consistently overwhelms inter-cohort differences in ways the model cannot accommodate, or whether defenders can redefine “structural position” post hoc to absorb every anomaly. Please distinguish decisive defeaters from merely awkward ones, state what a defender of position-cohorts would themselves admit counts against the view, and assess whether it has been insulated into unfalsifiability—if so, whether that is fatal for an ontological claim of this kind.
