hey, I'm drafting a column on science and ideas, and I keep hitting the same wall when I talk to people about unrealistic models. Last month I was at a workshop on climate surrogates—those neural-net emulators where every internal parameter violates physics but the ensemble still tracks temperature curves—and I watched a philosopher of science and a climate modeler go in circles about Friedman's old F-twist. The modeler said unrealistic assumptions don't matter if predictions hold and interventions work; the philosopher said that's bare instrumentalism, and without representational accuracy you can't claim causal knowledge. I've seen the same exchange in three different panels now, and nobody's budged.

My hunch is they're not fighting over facts but over what scientific progress even looks like—predictive calibration versus structural transparency. But there's also this buried empirical worry: if your assumptions are unrealisitic at every layer, does calibration survive when you push the model past its training range? So I want you to diagnose the stall: is this a verbal dispute about 'realism', a substantive disagreement about epistemic standards, or are they answering different questions—prediction versus explanation—under one heading? And whatever the diagnosis, what would count as actual progress? A formal result on robustness, a conceptual distinction that splits the cases, or is it just bedrock intuition about what science owes us? Don't just survey positions—really dig into why the argument won't move, and whether it should.
