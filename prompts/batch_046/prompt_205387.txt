Here are my verdicts on whether self-deception occurs in concrete situations, and I want to know whether one principle can unify them. (1) A legislator privately writes that her bill will harm the poor, yet sincerely defends it as just; I call this self-deception. (2) A spouse with clear evidence of infidelity insists “nothing is wrong” and actively avoids checking; I call this self-deception. (3) A researcher knowingly cherry-picks data for publication, fully aware of the bias; I call this dishonesty, not self-deception. (4) A patient with anosognosia denies his paralysis despite direct demonstration; I think this is a cognitive deficit, not self-deception. (5) A committed determinist who reacts with authentic moral outrage when cheated, despite his theoretical denial of responsibility, strikes me as self-deceived about the practical authority of his belief. (6) A person who genuinely forgets an embarrassing failure and, when reminded, expresses surprise and reconstructs the past; I think this is mere memory distortion, not self-deception. The hard tension is between (1) and (3): both involve motivated belief-management, yet I treat them differently. Is there an account—say, in terms of divided cognitive architecture, motivated ignorance, or practical versus theoretical belief—that saves all six verdicts? If not, which should I overturn? And please state what your best account predicts for an unlisted case: a manager who sincerely claims not to envy her peer, yet systematically undermines that peer’s projects while attributing each act to independent, legitimate reasons.
