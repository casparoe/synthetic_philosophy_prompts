My thesis is that the phrase does not name a coherent tradition but instead glues together three normative structures that are historically separate and actively conflict: first, procedural liberalism, the idea that legitimate public rules must be justifiable without appealing to any comprehensive doctrine; second, epistemic individualism, the conviction that knowledge is secured through private doubt rather than inherited authority; and third, affective autonomy, the emotional ideal that moral maturity requires feeling personal guilt for one's actions rather than communal shame. I claim these come from distinct lineages—post-Reformation constitutional settlement, Cartesian and Protestant inner certainty, and the post-Romantic cult of authenticity—and that their hidden tensions collapse whenever the phrase is used in cross-cultural argument. For instance, procedural liberalism asks citizens to bracket deep commitments in public reason, while affective autonomy treats those same commitments as personally owned and therefore non-negotiable, so invoking both at once is contradictory.
