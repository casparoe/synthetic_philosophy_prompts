In a forecasting seminar I attend, two colleagues—Mara and Leo—have been deadlocked over how to assign credence to a specific claim: that a proposed carbon-tax-and-dividend scheme will reduce national greenhouse-gas emissions by at least thirty percent within ten years in a particular mid-sized industrial democracy.

Mara defends an inside-view analysis. She has built a detailed causal model of legislative mechanics, industry lobbying dynamics, and technology-adoption curves specific to that country’s institutional context, and she reports a credence near sixty-five percent. She argues that Leo’s outside view commits the reference-class problem: there is no well-defined class of “structural economic reforms with this precise design feature and institutional embedding,” so selecting a base rate requires exactly the kind of contextual judgment the outside view is supposed to replace. She also notes that Leo’s choice of OECD comparators is itself an inside-view judgment about which institutional features are causally relevant.

Leo defends the outside view. He cites historical data showing that only about fifteen percent of comparable structural economic reforms in advanced democracies have hit their ten-year targets, and he insists that Mara’s sixty-five percent substitutes narrative coherence for predictive validity. He frames the outside view not as a rival causal model but as an epistemic-modesty device: because Mara has no calibrated track record of predicting legislative outcomes in this domain, her confidence should be anchored to the base rate and adjusted downward, not upward.

Mara’s counter is that Leo’s appeal to humility is self-undermining. If outside-view skepticism applies to her contextual judgments, it must also apply to Leo’s selection of the reference class and to his interpretation of “comparable.” Applied consistently, that skepticism should lower Leo’s confidence in his own fifteen-percent figure, pushing him toward agnosticism rather than a specific low number. She claims he is selectively deploying modesty against her position while treating his base-rate choice as immune.

Leo replies that this misses the asymmetry. His fifteen percent is not a point prediction but a prior anchored to observable frequencies, and rational updating requires specific, identifiable evidence rather than a general appeal to institutional complexity. He argues that Mara’s model, by contrast, offers no falsifiable intermediate predictions—only a final outcome ten years away—so there is no mechanism by which she could be calibrated or corrected before then.

Please adjudicate this exchange move by move: rate Mara’s inside-view defense, her reference-class objection, her self-undermining charge, Leo’s humility-as-device framing, and Leo’s asymmetry reply, ordering them from clearly mistaken to clearly right and briefly justifying each rating. Then say what, if anything, both parties are missing—whether a hidden empirical question about calibration in policy forecasting, a conceptual distinction about how modesty applies when reference classes are contested, or a deeper disagreement about what counts as an admissible update.
