I am writing from a philosophy of science seminar on reduction and emergence, and I want to move beyond the standard textbook taxonomy by focusing on a specific conceptual knot that has come up repeatedly in our discussions. Consider what I will call a "mechanism sketch" case: scientists observe a robust macro-level regularity—say, a particular flocking dynamics or protein-folding pathway—for which they possess a genuinely predictive, causally adequate explanatory model at the macro-level, but one that deliberately abstracts away from microphysical details and for which no unique, complete microphysical realization is specified or even specifiable in principle given current physics. The sketch predicts and supports intervention; it is not treated as a temporary placeholder for future reduction, but as a permanently underdetermined mapping.

Please construct a decision tree that takes a student from the commitment "Such mechanism sketches are genuinely explanatory, not merely heuristic" to a named philosophical position on reduction and emergence. Each internal node should be a single claim or commitment—phrased as a question answerable without knowing the philosophical labels—about the status of these sketches: whether explanatory autonomy requires ontological novelty; whether downward causation is possible if the sketch is causally closed at the macro-level; whether the absence of unique realization is an epistemic limitation or a metaphysical feature; whether the sketch's predictive success licenses treating macro-properties as real kinds. Each leaf should carry the name of a position (e.g., reductive physicalism, non-reductive physicalism, emergentism, eliminativism, or "unnamed" if the combination is unoccupied) together with one sentence describing its most awkward commitment specifically regarding mechanism sketches.

Also flag any pair of leaves your tree cannot genuinely separate—perhaps because two positions converge on all sketch-related commitments despite diverging elsewhere—and suggest what missing branch (concerning, for instance, the modal status of the sketch or its relation to intertheoretic reduction) would be needed to split them. The aim is not to reproduce the familiar taxonomy but to see whether this sketch-case exposes hidden fault lines that the usual labels obscure.
