here's the exam question: In his essay "Of Suicide," Hume argues that suicide does not violate our duty to God. Focusing specifically on the providential-order argument, explain Hume's reasoning about general laws versus particular divine volitions. Then apply the distinction between "structural interference" (rearranging conditions within an existing system) and "causal usurpation" (substituting human will for divine causation in a specific event) to show why suicide counts as the former rather than the latter. Finally, note one limitation of extending this reasoning to duties we owe to society or to ourselves.

student answer:
Hume denies that suicide insults providence because God governs through general laws, not by micromanaging each particular event. Killing yourself is just altering conditions—like diverting a river or planting trees—so it counts as structural interference rather than causal usurpation. In fact, Hume uses the river-diversion analogy in the social-duty section to show that changing social conditions isn't harmful either. Since structural interference is always permitted (we do it when we take medicine or build dams), suicide is never wrong from a providential view, and Hume therefore establishes that it is morally neutral in every case. The only real limit is that it might hurt friends, but that belongs to the social argument, not the providential one, so the providential argument stands completely on its own.
