Glaucon's ring of Gyges renders its wearer invisible to other people, and he deploys the story in Republic II to ask whether anyone—just or unjust—would refrain from wrongdoing if every risk of detection and punishment were eliminated. He seems to conclude that justice is valued only instrumentally, for the reputation and security it brings, not for its own sake. Yet I keep wondering whether the thought experiment smuggles in a metaphysical assumption rather than a psychological one. If moral realists are correct that wrongness is an objective feature of actions, independent of anyone's beliefs or perceptions, then the ring's power to conceal the agent should be irrelevant to whether an unjust act remains wrong—after all, moral properties are not the sort of things that need to be "seen" to exist, unlike shadows or social contracts. So is Glaucon actually challenging the intrinsic value of justice, or is he implicitly treating moral obligations as socially constituted relations that literally dissolve when observation is removed? In other words, does the ring argument presuppose moral anti-realism, and if so, can it still pose a genuine problem for someone who thinks moral facts are non-natural and mind-independent?
