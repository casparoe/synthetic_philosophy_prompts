Hi, I'm a retired physicist who only started reading philosophy seriously after I left the lab, so forgive me if I'm mixing up terms. I've been chewing on Bradley's regress — the worry that if a relation R connects A and B, you then need some further relation to connect R to A and R to B, and then you need relations for those relations, and so on forever. I jotted down notes for a short essay I'm tentatively calling "Propositional Unity as Closed Topology," and I'd love your comments on whether the idea is even coherent.

My thought is that Bradley is treating relations like little beads that have to be strung together with more beads. But in physics we don't think interactions are extra objects sitting between particles; they're part of the structure itself, like the geometry of a field. So what if we think of a proposition not as a bag of parts plus a glue-relation, but as a closed loop in a relational graph? The predicate is just a node with a fixed number of slots; the relata plug in; and once every slot is filled the loop closes. There's no separate "binder" that needs its own connection, because the unity is just the absence of open ends. I call this "topological saturation" — the proposition is unified not because something holds it together, but because there's nowhere left for it to fall apart.

I'm especially unsure about three things. First, does this actually stop the regress, or am I just hiding the problem in the idea of a "slot"? Second, if relations are just structural slots, do I loose the intuition that relations are genuinely real? And third, I tried comparing this to how gauge fields work in physics, but I'm not sure the analogy does any real work — it might just be decorative. Is the comparison helpful, or is it a red herring?
