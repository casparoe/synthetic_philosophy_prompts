If consequentialist cluelessness arises because the long-run causal chains of our actions are too complex to rank outcomes with any confidence, the problem appears structurally parallel to moral uncertainty—the situation in which we do not know which normative theory is correct. Both involve decision-making under profound ignorance, yet the standard responses diverge sharply. Moral uncertainty is typically managed by assigning credences to competing theories and maximizing expected choice-worthiness, whereas consequentialist cluelessness is usually taken to defeat direct act-utilitarian calculation altogether, pushing us toward indirect strategies or heuristic rules rather than expected-value reasoning. Is this divergence actually justified? In particular, consider a decision about embedding a specific ethical architecture into an autonomous system’s long-term objective structure: here our ignorance is partly empirical—what states of the world will result?—and partly normative—what values ought to persist across potential value shifts? One might think we could construct a single decision matrix that treats empirical opacity and normative disagreement as two dimensions of uncertainty to be weighted together. But does consequentialist cluelessness contain a deeper asymmetry—perhaps involving the non-comparability of worlds across fundamental value change, or the impossibility of assigning stable probabilities to outcomes that depend on which values eventually dominate—that makes the moral-uncertainty toolkit inapplicable? Or is the refusal to unify them merely a failure of imagination?
