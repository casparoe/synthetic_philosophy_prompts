I am drafting an essay for a philosophy of technology seminar that challenges the usual framing of AI alignment as a single technical problem. Instead of the familiar inner/outer distinction, I propose that what we call alignment fractures into at least three structurally different questions that happen to share a vocabulary. The first is what I call ontological congruence: whether the system's internal representations of value-laden concepts—justice, harm, consent—map onto ours in ways that survive translation across contexts, or whether behavioral compliance masks deep categorical divergence. The second is institutional containment: whether we can design governance mechanisms that remain effective when the underlying system's reasoning is opaque, which seems less like fixing an agent's goals and more like building a cage whose bars do not depend on knowing what the occupant wants. The third is phenomenological parity: whether alignment requires that the system possess some analog of moral experience or intentionality, or whether perfect behavioral mimicry is sufficient regardless of inner life. I worry that collapsing these into one problem invites category mistakes—applying reinforcement learning to a governance failure, or demanding transparency from a system that is behaviorally compliant but representationally alien.

Please comment on these notes, particularly on whether the three-way split holds together, whether phenomenological parity belongs in this cluster at all, and whether I am unfair to standard technical approaches by treating them as answers to the wrong question. Respond in exactly five numbered items, each one or two sentences long, with no introduction or conclusion. Address in order: the coherence of the taxonomy, the legitimacy of separating institutional containment from the other two, the risk of importing philosophy-of-mind debates that do not apply, whether reinforcement learning is genuinely mischaracterized here, and one concrete scenario that illustrates the category error I describe.
