I retired from marine biology about five years ago and have been slowly chewing through philosophy of action and ethics—mostly because my old lab partner kept sending me papers. I recently got into Bernard Williams's integrity objection, especially that idea about having one thought too many when you're deciding wether to save your spouse versus a stranger. It struck me because in field work we used to have these unspoken loyalties to particular study sites that never showed up in the grant proposals.

There's a modern issue that I don't think existed when Williams was writing, and I'm curious how he'd handle it. Some eldercare startups are now building companion robots that develop long-term relational histories with specific patients—like the robot remembers Mrs. Chen's favorite stories and adjusts its gait to match hers—while running on a backend that can remotely override those local adaptations with a global welfare calculus. So if a new patient with greater need is admitted, the robot's central framework might instruct it to drop its established routine with Mrs. Chen and re-allocate all its interaction time, even though the robot has no conscious suffering either way.

Would Williams see this as a mechanized version of the integrity problem? That is, does the robot's accumulated project with Mrs. Chen create a kind of artificial integrity that should resist the utilitarian override, or is the analogy broken because integrity requires human agency? And if he were alive today, do you think he'd extend his objection to algorithimc systems that are deliberately designed to suppress local character in favor of impartial optimization?

I'm not asking for a bibliography or a list of who wrote about robot ethics—just trying to figure out whether the framework applies naturally or if there's a hidden assumption about personhood that makes the whole comparison fall apart.
