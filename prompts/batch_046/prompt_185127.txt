But that reading feels a little too clean to me, like I'm using the novel as a decorative illustration rather than letting it push back.

What I actually want to explore is whether Pi's defense of the animal story as the better one inverts Hume's logic in a way the essay doesn't anticipate. Hume argues that uniform experience should always outweigh testimony to a miracle, because the prior improbability is overwhelming. Pi, though, doesn't defend the tiger story by producing stronger witnesses or corroborating evidence—he offers no testimony at all, really, just his own account—and yet he asks the interviewers (and us) to prefer the miraculous version precisely because it breaks the expected pattern in a meaningful, almost redemptive way. The strangeness isn't a liability to be overcome by evidence; it's the very reason the story is worth telling. So I'm wondering: does the novel suggest that narrative form—explanatory richness, symbolic coherence, emotional fit—can legitimately shift the burden of belief in ways Hume's probabilistic calculus ignores?
