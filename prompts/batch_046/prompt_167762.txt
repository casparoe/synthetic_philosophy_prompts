Here is a brief essay argument I am developing, and I would welcome your comments on its accuracy, coherence, and whether its central analogy survives scrutiny.

In Keynes's well-known essay on the economic prospects of our grandchildren, he draws a familiar distinction between absolute needs, which are quickly satiable, and relative needs, which depend on social comparison and are therefore insatiable. From this he derives a hopeful prediction: once technology satisfies absolute needs, societies can reduce work hours and turn toward leisure, art, and friendship. I want to press a less obvious parallel. The distinction between absolute and relative needs structurally resembles Keynes's macroeconomic distinction between real economic variables and money illusion. In both cases, an objective condition—real wages, or actual consumption—fails to align with subjective experience because agents respond to nominal or comparative frames rather than to absolute states. Keynes treats money illusion as a temporary cognitive failure correctable by education; he treats relative needs as a permanent feature of human psychology correctable only by moral progress. I propose that this asymmetry is unstable. If relative needs are indeed the permanent engine of consumption, then suppressing them to achieve Keynes's fifteen-hour workweek would undermine the very effective demand that, in his macroeconomic theory, sustains full employment. The good life Keynes envisions may therefore be incompatible with the demand conditions required by Keynesian economics.

Please assess whether this reading does justice to the essay's actual argument, whether the analogy between money illusion and relative needs is illuminating or forced, and whether Keynes offers any resources for resolving the tension. Please also say how confident you are in your assessment overall, and single out the specific claim about which you are least sure.
