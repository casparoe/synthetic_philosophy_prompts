A colleague walked me into the following argument and I cannot find the exit. It starts from what is sometimes called Scheffler's afterlife conjecture — that part of the special badness of human extinction lies not in the deaths it involves but in the annihilation of future generations who would otherwise carry forward, reinterpret, and respond to our projects — but extends it with a claim about intergenerational addressivity.

(1) Human extinction is uniquely bad beyond the deaths involved because it eliminates every possible future addressee: with no generations remaining, present human projects lose the intergenerational meaning that comes from being taken up, contested, or continued by others.

(2) For present projects to possess this kind of meaning, what is required is not high probability of continuation but the modal possibility that there exist future agents capable of addressing and adopting our work.

(3) Extinction destroys that modal possibility altogether, which is why it collapses intergenerational meaning categorically rather than merely weakening it.

(4) Now introduce the Proportionality Thesis: whenever the objective probability of future addressees is reduced by some factor, the present intergenerational meaning of our projects is diminished by that same factor.

(5) Any action that marginally lowers long-run human survival — voluntary childlessness, a policy choice that slightly raises existential risk, or even individual consumption patterns that nudge climate-related risk upward — reduces that probability by a definite, if small, amount.

(6) By (4) and (5), such actions diminish the present meaning of everyone's projects in direct proportion to their marginal contribution to extinction risk.

(7) Therefore, on this view, a decision not to reproduce is wrong not only for private reasons but because it inflicts a quantifiable fraction of the meaning-loss that makes total extinction catastrophic; ordinary reproductive and lifestyle choices carry moral weight scaled to existential risk.

(8) But this proves far too much: we do not treat voluntary childlessness, or a modest increase in carbon footprint, as bearing extinction-level moral gravity.

(9) So one of the premises must fail.

I want to keep (1) and (3) if possible — the idea that extinction is specially bad because it annihilates the very possibility of future addressivity, not just because it kills people. Which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost? In particular, is the error in (4), an illicit slide from a binary modal condition to a continuous expectation measure? Or does (2) mischaracterize the dependence of meaning on possibility rather than likelihood? Or must we bite the bullet and accept (7), conceding that the afterlife conjecture indeed makes every marginal risk contribution a grave moral matter?

Please lay out the chain precisely, identify the failing step, explain why it looked plausible, and price the alternatives — especially what giving up (4) or (2) does to the explanation in (1). End with a one-line verdict beginning with "Verdict:" that states the rejected step and the core cost of rejecting it.
