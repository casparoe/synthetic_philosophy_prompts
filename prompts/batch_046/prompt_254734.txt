I spent my career in condensed-matter physics, and only recently have I begun to take philosophy seriously. I am struck by an institutional practice in my old field that seems to carry hidden metaphysical commitments.

In large-scale molecular dynamics simulations—used everywhere from materials science to drug design—there is a standard algorithmic protocol: the simulation maintains a “neighbor list” that is rebuilt only at fixed intervals. Between rebuilds, every atom is treated as interacting only with the neighbors on that list; all other pairwise relationships in the system are left untouched in the state vector, even though the underlying Hamiltonian couples every particle to every other. The practice is defended on purely computational grounds, but it also embodies a specific representational choice: the system updates only what has been explicitly flagged as changed, and treats everything else as invariant by default.

What philosophical commitments does this practice presuppose? Does it commit us to a localist ontology of causation—that only proximal interactions are real, and distant ones are ontologically inert between updates? Or can it be defended as a purely instrumental approximation that carries no metaphysical weight? Is there an implicit theory of change and persistence here—something like a frame axiom built into the code—that parallels the philosophical frame problem? And if we replaced this protocol with a continuously updated global coupling scheme, would we be abandoning a hidden commitment, or merely trading efficiency for accuracy?
