I'm developing a conceptual taxonomy of doxastic absence for a project on divine hiddenness, and my study group has pressed that taxonomy into an argument that seems to prove God cannot exist — yet when we extend the same reasoning structure to ordinary human relationships it implies that loved ones must be absent or unloving whenever trauma produces alienation. We think there is a subtle slide or an overstrong premise, but we cannot pinpoint it. Here is our reasoning, with steps numbered:

(1) Doxastic absence regarding God comes in four distinct forms: (a) culpable disbelief; (b) non-culpable disbelief; (c) agnostic suspension; and (d) doxastic alienation, in which the agent actually holds the belief "God exists" but cannot integrate it into practical reasoning because of psychological, social, or trauma-related barriers.

(2) If God exists and is maximally good, then God seeks a fully reciprocated loving relationship with every rational agent.

(3) Every form of doxastic absence — including (d) — obstructs that fully reciprocated relationship.

(4) Therefore, if God exists, God removes all four forms in every rational agent, unless doing so violates some overriding moral or epistemic duty.

(5) Removing doxastic alienation (d) may require altering an agent's psychological or social conditions in ways that risk violating autonomy or other goods; but an omnipotent being can accomplish this without net moral loss.

(6) So if God exists, no rational agent suffers from (a), (b), (c), or (d).

(7) Yet rational agents plainly suffer from all four.

(8) Hence God does not exist.

(9) Now apply the same form generally: if being B necessarily loves agent S, is able to overcome S's doxastic alienation about B without net moral loss, and B exists, then B removes that alienation; otherwise B either lacks love or lacks existence.

(10) Apply this to ordinary human love: if my partner loves me and could heal my trauma-induced inability to integrate belief in their presence, then either they do heal it or they do not truly love me or do not exist.

(11) But trauma-induced alienation about a partner's presence is common, and we do not ordinarily conclude that the partner is absent or unloving.

(12) So the argument form must fail somewhere.

We would prefer to preserve premises (1) through (3) if possible, and we are especially reluctant to deny (5). Which specific step should we reject, and why did it initially look plausible? Then explain what the standard responses — the autonomy defense, the pedagogical or soul-making defense, and the skeptical-theist defense — each have to concede or pay in order to reject that step. In your response, please write in American English. Please also separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among philosophers of religion and philosophers of mind who work on these issues, and do not present a contested interpretation as though it were the consensus view.
