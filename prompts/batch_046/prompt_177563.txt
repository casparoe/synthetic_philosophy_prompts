I am working on a novel in which the protagonist gradually revises his memory of a failed marriage to preserve his sense of moral competence, and I want the psychology to be precise rather than merely dramatic. To that end, I have been testing my intuitions about self-deception—whether it is genuinely possible, and if so, what distinguishes it from ignorance, compartmentalization, or ordinary rationalization—against a set of concrete cases, and I am hoping you can help me articulate the principle behind my judgments, or show me that they cannot all stand together.

Here are my verdicts. The corporate director who has read every declining report yet answers questions about the firm’s health with sincere optimism, citing only the one profitable division and never feeling that he is lying, strikes me as self-deceived. The mother who sees her son’s failing grades, describes him to relatives as a late bloomer with genuine warmth, and feels only a brief, suppressible anxiety when she accidentally glances at the report card, also strikes me as self-deceived. The memoirist—perhaps closer to my own experience—who writes an autobiographical novel that renders her childhood gentle, then finds the fictional scenes more vivid and true than her actual memories, which now feel distant and possibly exaggerated by youth; she can still retrieve them with effort, but they lack the emotional authority of the invented version: I think she too is self-deceived, though I am less certain here. By contrast, the patient with a terminal diagnosis who updates his will, says a real goodbye to his sister, and yet books a non-refundable safari for next year with genuine excitement, never mentioning the prognosis in that particular conversation, does not strike me as self-deceived; he seems to hold two incompatible realities in separate mental spaces without denying either. The diplomat who accepts lavish gifts and sincerely insists they do not influence her recommendations—though she has never reviewed her voting record against the gift timeline—seems self-deceived to me, much like the director. Finally, the young singer who has never heard an honest recording of her voice and sincerely believes she is talented strikes me as merely ignorant, not self-deceived.

What principle, if any, unifies the cases I have marked as self-deception and excludes the others? I am especially troubled by the contrast between the mother and the terminal patient: both suppress or partition painful knowledge, yet I want to say one is deceiving herself and the other is not. Does the difference lie in the availability of counter-evidence, in the emotional function of the belief, in whether the belief is held with a kind of divided awareness, or in a failure to integrate one’s beliefs into a single coherent outlook? Please state the principle precisely, say what it predicts for the soldier who, after committing an atrocity, writes in his diary that he was merely following orders with such repetition that he can no longer recall his own initiative, and be honest about which of my verdicts—particularly on the memoirist or the patient—your account forces me to abandon. If no single principle saves them all, I would prefer a small family of candidates, each anchored to the case that motivates it, with an explicit statement of the cost of adopting each.
