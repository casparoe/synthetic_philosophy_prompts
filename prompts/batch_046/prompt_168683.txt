Design an exam for an upper-level undergraduate philosophy of ethics course consisting of one essay question, a model answer of approximately 700 words, and a marking scheme totaling 25 points.

The essay question should examine the validity of consent through the interaction of deception, coercion, and competence, but it must avoid generic textbook recitation by using the following niche scenario: a research participant consents to a long-term neurocognitive study after receiving disclosures that are factually accurate in every particular. The consent form describes all risks exclusively through statistical risk ratios that assume familiarity with Bayesian reasoning; no qualitative or narrative rendering of the risks is provided. Meanwhile, recruitment materials employ vivid, emotionally resonant language emphasizing cognitive enhancement and social contribution. The participant holds no false beliefs—no false statement was made, no direct threat was issued, and the participant has normal general intelligence—but lacks quantitative literacy. The institution designed the disclosure protocol with knowledge that most participants in this demographic share this literacy gap, and no interpretive assistance was offered.

The question should ask students to defend a specific verdict on whether valid consent has been obtained. It must require them to address three issues: whether deception can occur in the absence of false assertion through strategic framing and omission of interpretive context; whether coercion can be structural and institutional rather than interpersonal and direct; and whether competence is purely an individual cognitive property or also includes relational conditions of epistemic access that the disclosing institution must satisfy.

The model answer must take a committed, definite position—specifically arguing that consent is invalid—rather than surveying options neutrally. It should defend the view that framing can be deceptive when it exploits predictable interpretive failures without falsehood; that structural coercion is present when an institution engineers disclosure conditions that make refusal unreasonably difficult to understand; and that competence requires domain-specific epistemic access conditions that are institutionally satisfied, not merely general reasoning capacity.

Provide a marking scheme divided into five required analytical moves, each assigned a specific point value (summing to 25), with each entry including a one-line note on the typical mistake that costs marks on that point. The scheme should distinguish an excellent answer—one that modifies the standard categories of deception, coercion, and competence to fit the framing and structural twist in the scenario—from a merely adequate answer that applies the standard labels correctly but fails to address the institutional design of the disclosure protocol.

Write in American English. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, citation requests, or references to specific texts that require verification. The entire prompt should be answerable without web access.
