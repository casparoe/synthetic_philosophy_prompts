I am a philosopher convening an interdisciplinary workshop on long-term societal transformation, and I must deliver the opening remarks. The room is divided between archaeologists and historians who work on the Late Bronze Age, the Classic Maya, and Easter Island, and philosophers of social ontology who are skeptical of moralized narratives of failure. I need an eight-minute address that takes as its structural premise the metaphysical question of whether what we call collapse is an institutional death or merely an organizational metamorphosis—continuity of settlement, ritual, and linguistic practice under radically altered economic and political descriptions. For each of the three cases, briefly indicate what the record actually suggests about discontinuity versus persistence, but do not resolve the ambiguity into a tidy parable: because half the audience knows that Easter Island’s ecological stress did not extinguish local culture and that Maya urban abandonment coexisted with rural demographic continuity, I cannot present these as straightforward warnings or celebrations of resilience. Avoid any essentialist claim that societies possess necessary institutional structures whose loss constitutes death, since both halves of the room would reject it. The framing must genuinely constrain what can be said, leaving me unable to deliver either a simple environmental-cautionary tale or an unqualified resilience narrative. Close with an open question that renders the category of collapse itself conceptually unstable, so that the historians feel addressed rather than lectured and the philosophers see the problem as live.
