As a novelist constructing a scene in which two intellectuals observe a character experiencing what I call anticipatory bereavement—a sustained affective orientation toward a loss that is certain but has not yet occurred—I wish to understand whether such a state is fundamentally a cognitive judgment, an embodied perception, or a raw phenomenal feeling. Write a formal dialogue between a proponent of the cognitive-evaluative view and a proponent of the embodied-perceptual view. The first should maintain that anticipatory bereavement necessarily involves a propositional appraisal of future absence and value, and therefore collapses if that judgment is withdrawn or shown to be false. The second should maintain that it can arise from a direct somatic attunement to a temporal rupture in a relationship, operating independently of any articulated belief about the future. Have them respond precisely to each other’s objections, particularly addressing whether an emotion directed at a non-present object can still be genuinely perceptual, and whether a judgment-based account can explain the visceral urgency and resistance to rational revision that characterizes the experience. Render the exchange with philosophical rigor but in prose accessible to a literary audience, and do not invoke specific scholars, texts, or bibliographic references.
