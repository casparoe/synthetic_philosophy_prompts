Consider the color-phi phenomenon: a dot of one color appears briefly at position A, then shortly afterward a dot of a different color appears at position B, yet observers typically report seeing a single object travel continuously from A to B and change color midway through the trajectory—often describing an intermediate hue that was never physically presented. In philosophical treatments of this illusion, particularly those following Daniel Dennett, two competing models of phenomenal revision are distinguished. On an Orwellian model, there is initially a veridical conscious experience of the first dot with its actual color at A, but this past experience is subsequently altered or overwritten, analogous to the retroactive falsification of records in Orwell's 1984. On a Stalinesque model, there never was a veridical experience of the initial color; instead, the cognitive system constructs the entire narrative—the motion, the intermediate hue, and the endpoint—only after both stimuli have arrived, analogous to a propaganda apparatus splicing fabricated footage into an official documentary after the events have occurred.

Popular explanations of this distinction often lean on a media-editing analogy: Stalinesque revision is like a documentary producer inserting archival footage that was never shot during the actual event, whereas Orwellian revision is like a security agency that returns to timestamped logs of a genuinely occurring event and alters them. This comparison is frequently used to argue that Stalinesque revision is the more parsimonious account, because it avoids any need for backward causal influence on an already-existing mental state; it requires only post-hoc assembly.
