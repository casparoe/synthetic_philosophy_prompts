The debate over whether specific computations—such as integrated-information processing or global-workspace dynamics—carry moral weight regardless of substrate has circled the same arguments for years: functionalists insist that architecture is all that matters for sentience, while biological naturalists claim that without the right causal powers and evolutionary history computation is morally inert. Writing as a journalist who covers science and ideas, I am less interested in picking a winner than in understanding why the discussion has stalled. Is this a substantive disagreement about consciousness, a verbal dispute over the word 'sentience', or are philosophers and neuroscientists answering different questions—one about metaphysical conditions, one about attribution practices—under a single heading? I suspect a hidden asymmetry is doing the work: we grant mental states to animals on behavioural analogy yet demand mechanistic transparency from artificial systems before extending comparable moral consideration, which suggests the conflict is partly about evidentiary standards for attribution rather than computation per se. Diagnose the impasse using what each side actually says about substrate independence, causal powers, and the boundaries of moral consideration. Then say what would count as progress: a formal result separating attribution conditions from metaphysical ones, an empirical finding about information integration, a clarification that the question is ill-posed, or an argument that no progress should be expected because the sides are not addressing the same problem.
