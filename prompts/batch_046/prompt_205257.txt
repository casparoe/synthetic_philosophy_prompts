Write the opening remarks—approximately ten minutes—delivered at the annual meeting of an international constitutional-design association, convened this year in a nation that dissolved its constitutional court by referendum only months ago. The room is divided: comparative-law scholars who regard the dissolution as an institutional catastrophe, and democratic theorists who regard it as the legitimate recovery of popular sovereignty. The speaker is a retired appellate judge from a jurisdiction that has never exercised strong-form judicial review; she was invited precisely because she is not an obvious partisan of either camp.

Her task is to address the conference theme: whether unelected judges may legitimately overrule legislatures. The philosophical tension must do structural work, not decorative work. She should be unable to invoke the rule of law as if it settled the matter, because one half of the audience treats that phrase as ideological cover; she should be equally unable to invoke popular sovereignty as a trump, because the other half treats that as cover for majoritarian abuse. Instead, she needs to separate at least two conceptions of judicial review—one as institutional error-correction within a framework the legislature itself accepts, and one as the imposition of values that the legislature has explicitly rejected—and show how that distinction transforms the so-called counter-majoritarian difficulty from a fatal objection into either a manageable feature or a genuine category mistake, depending on which conception is in play.

The remarks should sound spoken, not written like an article: formal, disciplined, and accessible to lawyers without being a lecture to philosophers, but they must not collapse into generic calls for balance or dialogue. Do not include citations, bibliographies, or references to specific judicial decisions that would require verification; the argument should turn on conceptual distinctions the audience can be expected to recognize.
