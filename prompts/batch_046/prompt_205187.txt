I am preparing a 90-minute session for a graduate historiography seminar of about ten to twelve students, focused on Fernand Braudel's distinction between the longue durée—geographical, climatic, and deep structural rhythms—and the rapid, surface-level time of events, individuals, and political decisions. Rather than lecture about the asymmetry between these temporal layers, I want participants to inhabit it physically through a structured simulation with hidden rules, asymmetric victory conditions, and tangible stakes.

Design a game—call it "Layers of Time"—that divides the group into three roles: Chroniclers (representing événement-level actors such as rulers, generals, and diplomats), Conjuncturalists (representing medium-term economic and social cycles), and Geographers (representing the longue durée of climate patterns, soil regimes, and geographic barriers). The setting should be a fictional but realistic Mediterranean region spanning roughly 1600 to 1900, mapped onto a shared board with three vertical layers: deep geographic and climatic structures (hidden cards held by Geographers), intermediate economic and demographic structures (managed by Conjuncturalists), and political events (proposed by Chroniclers).

The mechanics should proceed across three rounds, each representing about a century. In each round, Chroniclers propose specific actions—wars, treaties, reforms, dynastic changes—placing colored tokens on the event layer and immediately earning "narrative capital" for dramatic choices. Before these tokens are finalized, however, Geographers reveal hidden structural cards representing conditions such as seasonal malaria ecology, Alpine snow-closure patterns, soil-exhaustion cycles in olive or grain regions, and maritime current regimes; these cards may nullify an event entirely, redirect its effects to a different region of the board, or silently determine long-term outcomes without any agent having chosen them. Conjuncturalists then allocate intermediate resources—trade capital, institutional memory, demographic pressure—to determine whether a given event survives into the next round or dissipates.

Crucially, inject a non-standard conceptual framing not typically found in textbook summaries: introduce the idea of "structural inertia" by requiring that any Chronicler token which survives past its initial round must be physically moved downward into the conjunctural layer, and then, if it persists to the final round, downward again into the deep-structure layer. This means events only endure by being gradually absorbed into slower rhythms; they never survive as pure events. Chroniclers should initially believe they are competing for dominance over the group's final written historical narrative, but the hidden victory conditions should reveal that Geographers win if the board's end-state—settlement patterns, economic geography, demographic distribution—matches their structural predictions regardless of which events occurred; Conjuncturalists win if they correctly predict which events will be captured versus which will vanish; and Chroniclers win only if they can place events that survive all three downward moves, which should be structurally extremely rare.

Please include a precise timeline: five minutes for role assignment and hidden-card distribution; twenty minutes per round with distinct phases for proposal, structural revelation, conjunctural mediation, and mandatory token movement; ten minutes for collective construction of the final "official history" with only ten available memory slots; and twenty minutes for debrief. Specify the materials needed (a three-layer board, colored tokens for each layer, hidden structural cards, event-proposal cards), the non-monetary payoff scheme (narrative control points, board-state accuracy scores, prediction tokens), and the rules for hidden information.

In the debrief, map specific game moments onto Braudel's framework: a token nullified by a geographic card should correspond to political history as surface foam; a token that silently reshaped outcomes through downward absorption should illustrate the anonymity and non-intentionality of the longue durée; and the downward-movement rule should open discussion of how Braudel's layers are not merely competing causal forces but different rhythms of time requiring distinct historiographical methods. Be explicit about predictable failures—Chroniclers will likely feel the rules are rigged against human agency, and participants may conflate the simulation's structural nullification with crude historical determinism—along with facilitation notes for handling both. Finally, include an honest note that the simulation cannot reproduce Braudel's methodological pluralism, specifically the idea that these temporalities demand different disciplinary approaches rather than being reducible to a single causal model, so the facilitator must supply that conceptual layer in discussion rather than through the rules themselves.
