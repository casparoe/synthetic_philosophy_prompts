Analyze the British Slavery Abolition Act of 1833 — specifically the parliamentary decision to pay £20 million in compensation to slave-owners while granting nothing to the formerly enslaved — through the lens of Nietzsche's genealogy of morality. Treat the evangelical abolitionist campaign led by figures such as William Wilberforce and the Clapham Sect as a moral-political movement, and read the compensation clause not only as economic policy but as a moral ledger: slave-owners received payment for lost "property," while the enslaved received freedom framed as an unearned gift. What does Nietzsche's account of the slave revolt in morality, the psychology of ressentiment, and the creditor-debtor origin of guilt predict about how these actors should have understood their own position and the value of emancipation? Which features of the episode — the inversion of "good" (the humane reformer) against "evil" (the cruel planter), the mobilization of collective guilt, the retention of a financial accounting of human lives — does the genealogy illuminate, and which does it obscure or misdescribe, such as the abolitionists' own elite social standing, their cooperation with state power, and their willingness to preserve property rights through compensation? Finally, ask what the case in turn reveals about the theory: does the compensation scheme show that slave morality never fully escaped the master morality's logic of debt and equivalence, or does it instead expose a tension within Nietzsche's framework between reactive value-inversion and the practical demands of institutional reform? Keep the mapping disciplined — identify who plays which role, what counts as the moral inversion, and where the theory is silent rather than forced to speak.
