After a long and somewhat circular argument last night with my friend Elena about what Zhuangzi’s butterfly parable actually establishes, she reconstructed for me an exchange she had earlier in the week with a colleague, Marcus, and insisted that I was missing the force of their disagreement. Since I could not adjudicate it to her satisfaction, I am passing the exchange to you.

Elena began by arguing that Zhuangzi’s dream demonstrates a radical epistemic parity between waking and dreaming states. Because there is no internal phenomenal marker available to the subject in the moment that distinguishes being awake from being a butterfly that dreams it is human, our belief that we are currently awake lacks any non-circular justification. She insisted, however, that this need not lead to global skepticism; rather, it should produce what she called “localized agnosticism” about the continuity of the self across states. We simply cannot know whether the “I” who wakes is numerically the same entity that underwent the butterfly transformation.

Marcus replied that Elena was misreading the parable’s philosophical target. The point, he said, is not an epistemological claim about justification frames but a metaphysical undermining of the substantial self. The uncertainty Zhuang Zhou reports upon waking is not “am I awake or dreaming?” but “which transformation am I currently undergoing?” On this view, the self is a process rather than a substance, so asking whether Zhuang Zhou is identical to the butterfly is as malformed as asking whether a river is identical to one of its waves. Marcus then added that if identity is genuinely fluid in this way, there is no stable “victim” of deception, which means Elena’s skeptical worry dissolves from within rather than being solved from without.

Elena countered by pointing to an asymmetry Marcus had ignored. Upon waking, Zhuang Zhou reports a feeling of pleasant surprise and retains what Elena called an “affective residue” — a lingering emotional and qualitative trace of the butterfly experience. This residue, she argued, functions as a retrospective epistemic marker: it breaks the perfect symmetry between the two states by allowing the waking subject to orient himself differently toward each. She conceded that this does not restore absolute certainty, but maintained it permits practical orientation without requiring the kind of metaphysical guarantee Marcus seemed to demand.

Marcus responded that the residue Elena identified is merely another state in the process of transformation. The feeling of pleasant surprise is part of the flow, not a privileged window onto reality. He pressed further: if we take transformational fluidity seriously, then moral evaluation must also transform. We cannot hold the “butterfly-self” responsible for actions performed in the dream-state, because there is no continuous moral agent linking the two phases. Elena’s attempt to preserve “weak continuity” through narrative threads, he claimed, is just smuggling substance metaphysics back in under a different name.

Elena’s final move was to distinguish metaphysical continuity from moral agency. Even on a process ontology, she argued, there exist “narrative threads” — patterns of concern, partial memory-structures, and affective orientations — that are sufficient for weak moral attribution without requiring a Cartesian ego. She suggested that this middle path is actually closer to Zhuangzi’s practical intent: not to induce epistemic paralysis or moral dissolution, but to cultivate what she termed “ontological humility” — a stance that avoids clinging to any single state as metaphysically privileged, while still permitting engaged ethical life.

Please adjudicate this exchange move by move. For each of the five moves above, rate whether it is clearly mistaken, mostly right, or clearly right, and briefly justify your rating. Then say who is more right overall, what each party is missing, and whether there is a coherent position that incorporates the best elements of both arguments without falling into contradiction.
