okay so i've been looking at how zoning boards in a lot of US cities actually work in practice, not just on paper. there's this pattern: boards will routinely approve massive single-family teardowns or luxury additions that objectively alter a block's scale and shadow more than a small multi-family building would, but they reject duplexes or fourplexes because existing homeowners come in and argue the project "doesn't fit the neighborhood character" or will "change the feel" of the street. the formal codes talk about setbacks and parking, but the real veto seems to sit with people who already own homes within a few hundred feet.

what philosophical commitments does that practice presuppose? specifically, does it depend on something like "domiciliary sovereignty" — the idea that buying a house gives you a kind of micro-property right over the demographic and aesthetic stability of the surrounding area, not just your lot line? and does that treat the city as a federation of private enclaves rather than a shared commons with a "right to the city" that belongs to inhabitants regardless of ownership? reconstruct the implicit theory of urban belonging and property here, check whether it's actually coherent with the legal system's normal treatment of property as bounded by lot lines, and say what a zoning practice built on inhabitant rights rather than homeowner veto power would have to change. also flag where this is philosophically underdetermined — could the same practice be justified by some other theory that doesn't rely on sovereignty over neighboorhoods?
