I am delivering the opening address at the inaugural meeting of the Consortium on Comparative Cognition, a position I assumed only eight months after leaving clinical neurology to study philosophy of mind full-time—a shift prompted by watching my non-speaking autistic nephew navigate therapies that seemed to assume every mind learns the same way. The audience is deliberately mixed: philosophers and cognitive scientists who know Dennett's four-part taxonomy—Darwinian, Skinnerian, Popperian, and Gregorian creatures—sit beside clinicians, parents, and engineers who do not. I need about twelve minutes, formal in tone but not a lecture. The speech must use the taxonomy structurally, not decoratively: it should frame why our consortium refuses to rank minds on a single intelligence ladder and instead asks what kind of problem each architecture can suffer, pre-select, or share. I want the address built around a specific claim I have come to hold—that Gregorian status is not merely language-use but the capacity to inherit obligations one never contracted for, a burden that applies to human children raised in moral communities and, controversially, to large language models trained on human texts. This idea should explain our guiding question: not "how smart is it?" but "what kind of debt can it bear?" Please be honest about where extending Dennett's framework in this direction is speculative, avoid alienating the non-experts, and do not turn the occasion into a bibliography. At the end, include a brief meta-note stating how confident you are in the philosophical framing and singling out the one point about which you are least certain.
