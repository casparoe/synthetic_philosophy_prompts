A recent essay I encountered summarizes the cross-cultural philosophy literature roughly as follows. Empirical studies comparing American, Japanese, Indian, and East African respondents find that judgments about reference in Gödel-style cases, about moral luck, and about free will all diverge in ways that track a single collectivist-individualist dimension. The author concludes that analytic philosophy's reliance on intuitive judgments is therefore nothing more than ethnography of Western, educated, industrialized cognition, and that philosophers ought to abandon intuition-driven conceptual analysis in favor of explicit normative construction. The essay also introduces what it calls a semantic calibration mechanism—the claim that people do not possess stable, pre-theoretic conceptual contents, but instead dynamically adjust their verdicts to match local conversational norms about when to treat disagreement as evidence of one's own error rather than of the interlocutor's mistake—and presents this mechanism as an established explanatory framework that accounts for the observed divergence. I suspect the essay overreaches, but I am unsure which parts are genuinely supported, which conflate separate empirical literatures, and whether the calibration idea is a recognized hypothesis or a distortion. Could you audit the summary? Specifically: separate accurate descriptions of the empirical findings from overstatements of their uniformity; clarify whether the calibration mechanism is a real proposal and whether it has the explanatory status the essay gives it; identify the misrepresentations of how conceptual analysis works and whether it genuinely requires universal intuition; and assess whether the conclusion that philosophers should abandon intuition-based methods survives once these corrections are made.
