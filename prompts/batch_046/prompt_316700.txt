I'm presenting the opening argument at a joint sociology and labor-economics seminar next Thursday—an audience of about thirty, including labor historians who worked on the French Aubry laws, Japanese sociologists of work who study karoshi, and a smaller contingent of Weber scholars. My thesis is that modern debates about the right to work and leisure systematically misunderstand the structural logic of Weber's argument: the Protestant work ethic was never a license for unlimited labor, but a rationalization of labor inside bounded temporal structures—the sabbath as a hard limit, Beruf understood as vocation rather than obsessive totalization. I read the thirty-five-hour week not as mere welfare regulation but as a secularized sabbatarian boundary that preserves the rational separation of labor and rest, and I read Japanese karoshi as the pathological inversion of the same logic—inner-worldly asceticism decoupled from its theological brakes, so that labor becomes totalized precisely because the framework that once made it meaningful and bounded has disappeared. The labor economists in the room will treat this as normative philosophy smuggling itself into empirical wage-hour analysis; the Japanese sociologists will object that Weber is Eurocentric and that karoshi is driven by institutional dualism, peer-pressure overtime norms, and labor-market structure that need no theological explanation; the Weber scholars will say I'm conflating the original 1904-1905 thesis with later American reception history, and that inner-worldly asceticism never included anything like a structural right to leisure.
