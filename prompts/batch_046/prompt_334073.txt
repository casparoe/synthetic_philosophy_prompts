Consider the position I will call minimalist tranquilism: the only intrinsic goods are the absence of craving (taṇhā) and the absence of suffering (dukkha), and every other apparent good—knowledge, aesthetic experience, virtuous character, deep relational attachment—is either instrumentally valuable solely as a means to those two absences or is itself a disguised form of one of them. I want an honest map of what would count against this view. Move beyond the standard objection that it undervalues joy or achievement; specify which particular intuitions, which possible cases, and which results in other areas—value theory, philosophy of mind, Buddhist textual scholarship—would genuinely threaten it. Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward. For each, say whether a committed minimalist tranquilist would concede it as damaging or dismiss it as resting on a misinterpretation of craving or suffering. Conclude by assessing whether the view can be qualified—by expanding the scope of what counts as suffering or craving—so thoroughly that it becomes insulated from counterexample, and whether, for a view of this kind, that insulation is a theoretical virtue or a defect.
