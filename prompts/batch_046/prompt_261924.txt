I was listening to two colleagues in my philosophy reading group—Mira and Jonah—debate whether maximal moral goodness is genuinely desirable, taking Susan Wolf’s familiar distinction between moral saints and the rest of us as their shared backdrop. They agreed on Wolf’s basic framing: a moral saint is someone for whom the moral point of view dominates life, crowding out non-moral goods like personal projects, humor, or deep partial attachments. Their disagreement was not about Wolf’s taxonomy but about whether her critique applies only to “bland” saints or to anyone who truly maximizes moral value, and whether a specific hypothetical case breaks her argument.

Mira introduced Dr. Elena Vasquez, a physician who spends sixty hours weekly on tropical-disease eradication, donates half her income, and still maintains close friendships and a serious chamber-music practice. Mira said Vasquez is clearly a moral saint—she sacrifices far beyond duty—yet she is not joyless, humorless, or lacking in rich personal goods. So Wolf’s worry that sainthood necessarily crowds out the attractive non-moral dimensions of life seems defeated by example. Jonah pushed back: Vasquez is not actually a moral saint in Wolf’s sense, because the moral point of view does not dominate her life. She still reserves substantial time and emotional energy for friendships and music that she could, in principle, redirect to saving lives; if she genuinely maximized moral goodness, Jonah argued, she would treat those hours as morally available resources and abandon them. The fact that she does not means she is merely very good, not maximally so. Mira accused Jonah of making Wolf’s view tautological: define “maximal moral goodness” as “allowing nothing non-moral to compete,” then anyone with competing goods falls short by definition, which guarantees that saints are undesirable precisely because they lack competing goods. Jonah replied that the criterion is not tautological but substantive. One can ask whether the moral point of view is the supreme organizing principle of a life without assuming the answer in advance. He added that Mira was conflating two things: acting on the best moral reasons available in each particular case—which Vasquez does—with living a life structured so that moral value is always the trump consideration—which Vasquez does not. Mira then countered that if Jonah is right, then “maximal moral goodness” is an impossible or self-defeating ideal, since any sustainable human life requires some non-moral structure—sleep, friendship, aesthetic experience—to remain psychologically intact and recognizably human. Jonah conceded that sustainability might set practical limits, but insisted this is a psychological fact, not a moral one: the ideal remains intact even if human beings cannot reach it without breaking.
