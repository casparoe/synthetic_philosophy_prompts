I have been discussing with a colleague whether Maimonides' rationalist account of animal sacrifice can be taught without reducing it to a mere historical curiosity, and we found ourselves imagining how a modern university might institutionalize his claim that korbanot are not intrinsic goods but pedagogical concessions to human psychological weakness. For a dataset I am assembling on philosophical prompts that reward reasoning over retrieval, please write a satirical, deadpan course catalog entry and full syllabus—approximately 700 words—for a fictional undergraduate course titled "PHIL 430: Sacrificial Logic and the Pedagogy of Concession," ostensibly designed by an anonymous Maimonidean rationalist and offered at a contemporary liberal-arts college seeking accreditation.

The syllabus should translate Maimonides' actual argument—that divine law permitted animal sacrifice only as an accommodation to deeply rooted human tendencies toward tangible, idolatrous worship, with prayer and intellectual contemplation representing the unstated telos—into modern academic bureaucratese. Include a course description, five measurable learning outcomes (for example, distinguishing necessary moral laws from statutory accommodations, or assessing "ritual redirection" as a cognitive skill), a weekly schedule mapping conceptual units onto sacrificial practices, and a grading rubric that evaluates student "competence in managing inherited cultic tendencies." The satire must derive entirely from this translation: exaggerate institutional assessment culture, maintain the flat voice of an accreditation self-study, and never wink at the reader. The target is the practice of packaging premodern theological rationalism as transferable skills, not any named individual. The piece should remain legible to someone who has never opened the Guide for the Perplexed, yet precise enough that a reader familiar with Maimonides' treatment of chukkim and the reasons for the commandments in Part III recognizes the argument being faithfully ironized rather than reduced to a strawman. Please avoid bibliographies, citation lists, or references to specific modern editions and page numbers; if texts are mentioned, treat them conceptually. Do not ask for external sources or include footnotes.
