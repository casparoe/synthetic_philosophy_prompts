I am a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy or historiography, and I am trying to articulate my own intuitions about when a historical account of technology becomes "Whig history"—that is, when it illegitimately projects present values or outcomes onto the past. I need help identifying the principle behind my judgments, if there is one. Below are six concrete narratives about technology development. For each I have a gut verdict: some feel like prohibited teleological projection, others feel like legitimate causal explanation, but two structurally parallel cases pull in opposite directions and I cannot reconcile them.

Case 1: Describing TCP/IP's adoption over OSI in the 1980s as "the inevitable triumph of pragmatic, bottom-up engineering over bureaucratic, top-down design." Verdict: this is Whig history. It reads present network dominance back as destiny and moralizes institutional differences that were contingent.

Case 2: Explaining TCP/IP's spread by citing DARPA funding structures, the need for rapid deployment across heterogeneous military networks, and the absence of licensing fees—without any claim of inevitability, moral superiority, or endpoint. Verdict: not Whig history. It offers causal explanation without teleology.

Case 3: Framing the evolution of programming languages from assembly to Python as "the gradual liberation of human reasoning from the mechanical constraints of the machine." Verdict: Whig history. It treats contingent technical development as the unfolding of an abstract idea.

Case 4: Noting that containerization spread because of specific operational pressures at large-scale internet firms in the early 2000s, and that its adoption correlates with particular economic incentives rather than with intrinsic technical merit over virtual machines. Verdict: not Whig history; structurally parallel to Case 2.

Case 5: Calling the organizational shift from waterfall development to agile methodologies "a correction of an earlier, less mature understanding of software engineering." Verdict: Whig history. It evaluates past practice by present standards and implies progress toward a correct form.

Case 6: Calling the architectural shift from monolithic applications to distributed microservices "an evolutionary improvement that better aligns system structure with modern scalability requirements." Verdict: surprisingly, this does not feel like Whig history to me, despite also comparing a past architecture to a present one and using evaluative language. I keep going back and forth on whether Case 5 and Case 6 can both stand.

My tentative hypothesis is that the boundary runs between narratives that use present evaluative categories to explain why past agents made their choices (prohibited) and those that use past agents' own practical categories to explain how present outcomes emerged (permitted). But I am unsure whether that distinction survives Cases 5 and 6, since both seem to invoke present evaluation.

Is there a precise principle—or a small family of competing principles—that captures my verdicts on Cases 1 through 4 and 6, and that forces me either to revise my verdict on Case 5 or to explain precisely why Case 6 is genuinely different? Please state the principle precisely, identify which clause or condition accounts for each case, predict its verdict on an unlisted scenario (for example: describing the decline of punch-card computing as "the necessary abandonment of an obsolete data-entry paradigm" versus explaining the same decline through labor-cost economics and the adoption of magnetic storage), and be explicit about which of my six verdicts it cannot accommodate without revision.
