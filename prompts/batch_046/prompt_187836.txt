Consider a regional water authority whose charter assigns voting seats on the basin council not by residency or citizenship but by hydrological footprint—the volume of water withdrawn and returned to the shared aquifer—giving seats to upstream municipalities, downstream agricultural districts, tribal nations with treaty rights, and large industrial extractors, while denying representation to residents of neighboring counties who draw from the same groundwater but lie outside the administrative boundary. The charter justifies this arrangement as democratic inclusion of all affected stakeholders. What conception of the demos—territorial, associative, or affected-interest—does this allocation rule presuppose? Does it implicitly hold that democratic standing derives from causal impact on a collective resource rather than from subjection to collective authority, and if so, can it consistently exclude the adjacent residents who are equally affected? Or can a territorialist or all-subjected-people view accommodate both the inclusions and the exclusions by appealing to jurisdictional boundaries? Identify where the practice is philosophically underdetermined: could a relational or functional theory of membership license the same allocation, and what would have to change in the charter if democratic equality were interpreted as equal vote per affected person rather than per stakeholder category?
