As a novelist, I have long been troubled by the philosophical stalemate over sphexishness—the mechanical rigidity observed in the sphex wasp and analogised to human cognition—because it mirrors my own uncertainty about whether writing a character's decision is an act of flexible authorship or merely the execution of narrative grammar. For decades, compatibilists have argued that human flexibility—reason-responsiveness, counterfactual adaptability—dissolves sphexishness into complex determinism, whilst incompatibilists maintain that any underlying mechanistic architecture, however layered, negates genuine authorship. Both camps now agree on the neural data and on the wasp's behavioural loop, yet the literature has not shifted in years. Is this impasse substantive—about whether causation permits degrees of rigidity—or verbal, with 'flexibility' functioning as a placeholder for whatever each side needs free will to be? Or are they, as I suspect, answering different questions: one about metaphysical causation, one about the phenomenology of inventing a plot? Diagnose the stall using what each side actually commits to, not by surveying interpretations of determinism, and tell me whether a conceptual distinction—say, between procedural repetition and narrative improvisation—could move it, or whether the disagreement is bedrock and should be expected to remain.
