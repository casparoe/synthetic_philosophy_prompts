In my philosophy-of-mind and AI-alignment reading group we've been stuck for roughly eighteen months on whether current large language models possess derived intentionality in Dennett's framework. The stance-ists argue that Dennett's two-bitser — a minimal system interpretable as wanting one of two states — shows that derived intentionality requires only a reliable interpretive pattern and an original intender behind the design; since engineers have original intentionality and users systematically interpret LLM outputs as about specific states of affairs, the models clearly carry derived aboutness, regardless of internal architecture. The biological-naturalist side counters that the two-bitser only functions within an embedded causal economy where misrepresentation carries practical costs for the system itself, and that LLMs lack this functional saturation: their syntactic outputs never misrepresent in a way that feeds back to constrain the model, so users are merely projecting intentionality onto inert pattern-matching. Both camps agree on transformer mechanics, on Dennett's account of the intentional stance, and on Searle's claim that syntax is not sufficient for semantics; they simply replay these commitments against each other without convergence. My working hypothesis is that the real stall comes from treating derived intentionality as a binary property, when it might actually be graded along a dimension I'm calling interpretive debt — the degree to which an artifact's interpretation is actively maintained by a community that shares its functional goals and corrects its errors in real time. Under that view, LLMs might possess a thin, consumer-facing derived intentionality but lack the thicker, system-internal variety that makes the two-bitser genuinely about anything.
