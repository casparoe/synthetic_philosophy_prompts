Write a poem of exactly fourteen lines, arranged in two septets separated by a single blank line that must contain no words—only silence or punctuation—functioning as the poem’s structural hinge. The first septet should trace the inversion by which observed competence in artificial selection (the breeder shaping form without grasping heredity) is used to license belief in a parallel competence in nature (adaptation arising without any directing comprehension). The second septet must be a strict syntactic inversion of the first: line by line, the grammatical subject and object of the first are reversed, active verbs become passive or reflexive, and the direction of reasoning is flipped, now tracing the parallel inversion by which an observer’s inference of intelligence from human behavior—made without access to inner comprehension—is used to shift the burden of proof onto anyone who would deny the same inference to an artificial system exhibiting equivalent behavioral competence. The rhyme scheme should mirror this reversal: the first septet follows ABABCDC, the second DCDBABA, so that the sonic architecture literally runs backward while the philosophical architecture runs parallel. The poem should not name the historical figures behind these arguments, but should render the logical moves—the analogy from known to unknown case, the absence of mechanistic understanding in the source of the competence, and the reversal of the evidential burden—with enough precision that a reader acquainted with both the breeder-analogy argument for natural selection and the behavioral-criterion argument for machine intelligence recognizes the exact inversions being performed. The turn should occur in the blank line between the stanzas, where the reader realizes that the two arguments share a single form: competence is legible precisely where comprehension is absent.
