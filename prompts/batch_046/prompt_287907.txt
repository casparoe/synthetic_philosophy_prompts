I've been working through Dennett's intentional stance in my philosophy of mind course and have adopted a qualified version specifically for large language models: we should deploy intentional attributions—belief, desire, intention—only in diagnostic contexts, such as tracing reasoning failures or interpreting anomalous outputs, but systematically withhold them in participatory contexts like open-ended conversation, on the grounds that interaction design induces a phenomenology of reciprocity that swamps whatever predictive utility the stance might have. This preserves the core idea that the stance is a predictive strategy rather than an ontological commitment, while trying to avoid the social costs of anthropomorphizing systems that lack the relevant capacities. What would genuinely count against this bifurcated view? I want defeaters sorted from decisive to merely awkward: empirical findings about whether users can actually maintain this separation, conceptual arguments that the stance applies uniformly or not at all regardless of context, and practical cases where withholding attributions in participatory settings either collapses into incoherence or produces worse outcomes than uniform application. In particular, does this qualification risk making the view unfalsifiable—since I can always retreat to "that was participatory, not diagnostic"—and if so, is that insulation fatal, or merely an acceptable feature of a heuristic-based position?
