I just spent the evening in a long, somewhat heated discussion with two friends, Mira and Jonas, about whether recognizing that nations are largely modern inventions actually undermines their moral authority. Mira began by insisting that once you accept Hobsbawm’s analysis of invented traditions and Anderson’s account of imagined communities, the claim that one’s nation stretches back to antiquity becomes not merely inaccurate but actively deceptive. She argued that citizens are essentially enlisted in a collective fiction, and that if people truly grasped the recency of standardized languages, ceremonial flags, and canonical histories, patriotic obligation would lose its rational foundation. Jonas replied that this confuses origin with legitimacy: every social institution, from currency to grammar, has a constructed beginning, yet that does not render it fictitious in any meaningful sense. He said traditions become real precisely through the repetition and institutional embedding that Anderson describes, so that calling them invented is like calling marriage invented because wedding rituals vary across centuries.

Mira then pushed back with a more specific point about what she called archival discontinuity. In several post-colonial states, she noted, pre-modern records exist not in a single vernacular but in competing regional scripts and oral registers that do not map neatly onto modern borders. The modern national narrative therefore does not merely select from an existing archive; it must first construct a unified archive where none existed, translating and suppressing sources that resist national framing. She argued this makes the invention of tradition structurally different from, say, the standardization of a dialect, because there is no underlying continuous substrate being refined—only discontinuous fragments being stitched together.

Jonas conceded the descriptive point but denied its normative weight. He argued that precisely because there was no pre-existing unified record, the modern state’s work of standardization and translation functions less as elite deception and more as a democratic coordination problem—creating shared reference points where fragmentation previously prevailed. He compared it to the emergence of a common convention in a population with previously incompatible local practices, except that in this case the convention concerns collective memory itself. He then asked whether Mira was implicitly demanding an impossible standard of organic continuity that no large-scale social form could meet.

At this point they turned to me and asked me to adjudicate. Can you go through their exchange step by step and say who is right about which specific claim—Mira’s point about deception, Jonas’s analogy to convention and legitimacy, Mira’s concept of archival discontinuity, and Jonas’s defense of standardization as democratic coordination? Also, what are both of them missing? In particular, is there a meaningful distinction between traditions that refine an existing substrate and those that must first synthesize one from discontinuous fragments, and does that distinction actually bear on whether patriotic obligation is rational?
