I am a PhD student in metaphysics, and at a recent workshop on system-relative properties two colleagues—Mara and Leo—got into a sustained disagreement about how to apply the distinction between finks and masks to an engineered artifact. I want a committed adjudication rather than a survey of options.

The artifact is a “stabilized reactive crystal” (SRC): a crystalline structure whose base form has a disposition to undergo rapid phase transition under mechanical stress. In the SRC, however, the crystal is embedded in a lattice of damping polymers that absorb mechanical energy. This lattice is a mask: it prevents stress from reaching the crystal without altering the crystal’s internal structure. But there is an additional feature: if the lattice is breached and stress does reach the crystal, the resulting phase transition generates enough heat to carbonize the polymer lattice instantly, destroying the very conditions that allowed the stress to reach the crystal. Leo calls this a fink—the disposition is present but is destroyed by its own triggering conditions—while Mara insists it is a nested mask, because the carbonization is an effect of the manifestation, not an interference with the disposition itself.

Mara’s opening argument: If the crystal genuinely had the disposition to transition under stress, then in a counterfactual scenario where stress is applied, the transition should occur. But in any nearby world where stress reaches the crystal, the lattice is destroyed, and the crystal is no longer embedded in the conditions required for attributing the disposition to the artifact. So the counterfactual fails: there is no nearby world where the crystal both receives stress and retains the disposition-bearing conditions. Attributing the bare disposition is therefore incoherent.

Leo’s reply: That misapplies the counterfactual analysis. We evaluate the counterfactual by holding fixed the crystal’s internal molecular structure and varying only the external conditions—specifically, removing the lattice. In worlds where the lattice is absent, the crystal receives stress and transitions, with no interfering fink. The carbonization only occurs in worlds where the lattice is present but breached, which are not the worlds relevant for testing whether the crystal has the disposition. The lattice is a mask, not a fink, and masks do not eliminate dispositions.

Mara’s second argument: But Leo’s evaluation illicitly removes the lattice, which is constitutive of the artifact. The SRC just is the crystal-in-the-lattice system. If we hold the system fixed and ask what it does under stress, the answer is that the lattice absorbs stress and, if breached, the crystal transforms and destroys the system. There is no stable counterfactual support for attributing the bare crystal’s disposition to the system itself. Leo is treating the crystal as an independent object, when in fact its dispositions are system-relative and here absent.

Leo’s counter: Mara conflates ontological dependence with dispositional absence. Yes, the crystal is ontologically dependent on the lattice for being an SRC, but the crystal’s internal bonding remains the basis of the disposition. The system has a complex dispositional profile: it has the disposition to absorb stress (from the lattice) and, derivatively, the crystal within it retains its own profile, masked but not eliminated. To deny this is to commit to an extreme holistic dispositionalism that would deny that a brittle vase inside a padded box is brittle, which is implausible.

Mara’s final point: The vase analogy fails precisely because the padded box does not change the vase upon breaking. Here, the manifestation of the crystal’s disposition destroys the very container that defines the artifact. This makes the case structurally different: it is not mere masking, but a self-undermining disposition. If we allow such dispositions, we must allow that an object can have a property whose exercise necessarily eliminates the object. That might be acceptable for bare crystals, but for engineered artifacts like the SRC, we should say the disposition is genuinely absent—designed out—rather than hidden.

They asked me to referee and I could not settle it. Please adjudicate move by move: assess Mara’s opening argument, Leo’s reply, Mara’s second argument, Leo’s counter-reply, and Mara’s final point. For each, indicate whether it is clearly right, clearly wrong, or partly right but missing something, and briefly justify the rating. Then give an overall verdict: is the SRC’s crystal genuinely disposed to phase transition, or is the disposition absent? Your answer should take a definite position, explain what both parties are missing—particularly regarding whether self-undermining manifestations affect dispositional attribution differently than standard finks or masks—and avoid merely laying out options. I am drafting a paper on system-relative dispositions, so please be precise and committed.
