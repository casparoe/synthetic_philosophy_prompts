I'm in an advanced seminar on collective moral responsibility and the philosophy of history, and I've been assigned to model an adversarial collaboration for my final project. One partner defends a Fischer-style intentionalist view: that the German state in 1914 possessed a hierarchically organized will to risk general war for hegemonic ends, making the nation blameworthy as a corporate agent in something like the same register as an individual who deliberately plans harm. I defend a systemic-structuralist view closer to the sleepwalkers interpretation: that no single state willed the conflict, that responsibility is distributed across interlocking alliance structures, mobilization timetables, and bureaucratic inertia, and that blaming Germany mistakes an emergent systemic failure for a unitary intentional act.

Write the joint statement we should have produced. It should contain: (1) a numbered list of claims both sides can sign—about the archival record of July 1914, about the logical conditions for attributing responsibility to collectives, and about what kinds of evidence could bear on the dispute—without either side conceding its core thesis; (2) a disagreements table with two columns, each stating the opposing view in wording its own proponents would accept, phrased neutrally, covering both the historical question and the philosophical crux of whether a nation-state can be a moral agent capable of blame in a non-metaphorical sense; here, inject the less-discussed crux of whether the capacity for reflective self-correction—being able to recognize one's own motives and revise them—is a necessary condition for being a proper object of moral blame, and whether modern bureaucratic states possess that capacity in any non-metaphorical way; (3) for each crux, identify a concrete case or piece of evidence both sides agree is relevant, with each side's prediction recorded in advance—for the historical crux, the interpretation of the December 1912 War Council; for the agency crux, the analogy to corporate criminal liability in domestic law; for the self-correction crux, the comparison between a democratic deliberative body that can change course after public debate and an autocratic command structure; (4) a frank assessment of which part of the dispute is genuinely substantive and which risks being verbal—particularly whether holding a nation responsible means attributing causal contribution, attributing moral fault, or expressing a political judgment about reparative obligations.

Please be explicit about what would change your assessment: which argument or consideration—historical, conceptual, or analogical—would lead you to conclude that one framing of collective blame is clearly superior, and identify the specific point in your reasoning where you are most likely to have smuggled in an unstated assumption about agency or causation.
