You are a philosopher of history with expertise in comparative methodology, counterfactual reasoning in the social sciences, and the long-running literature on the Great Divergence. Before delivering your diagnosis, take a brief moment to map the logical space: enumerate the main ways an entrenched dispute can stall—verbal ambiguity about key terms, hidden normative or metaphysical premises, mismatched evidentiary standards, incommensurable causal frameworks, bedrock intuitions that neither side can justify, or the possibility that the two sides are answering different questions under one heading.

Here is the dispute. For decades, scholars have pressed a contrastive question: why did Britain industrialize first, and not Qing China, treating both as comparable agrarian empires facing Malthusian constraints around 1700? One camp holds the question well posed. They argue that Britain and China share sufficient baseline conditions—large agrarian economies, sophisticated markets, comparable technological levels—that identifying the differential factor, whether coal geography, colonial extraction, institutional fragmentation, or patent regimes, is a genuine explanatory task. The opposing camp contends the comparison is structurally malformed. They claim that “Britain” and “China” are not stable units across the counterfactual shift required by the question; that the framing smuggles in a teleological endpoint called “industrial modernity”; and that the causal mechanisms invoked—say, property-rights incentives versus state-market coordination—are culturally embedded to the point that comparing their presence or absence already presupposes the convergence the explanation seeks to justify.

Despite extensive empirical work, the debate has hardly moved. The same cases are rehearsed—location of coal seams, degrees of political centralization, scientific institutions, market integration—with each side interpreting the evidence through its prior methodological lens. I want a diagnosis, not a survey of interpretations. Is this a substantive disagreement about historical causation, a verbal disagreement about what counts as an explanation in history, a clash over hidden normative commitments to a development narrative, or a case of two groups answering different questions—one about macro-structural conditions, one about local path dependency—under a single heading? In particular, consider whether the impasse turns on an unacknowledged requirement for what we might call “counterfactual unit stability”: the idea that for a contrastive historical question to be well posed, the comparator entities must remain identifiably the same under the counterfactual alteration. If that requirement is accepted, the macro-comparative framing collapses; if rejected, the localist critique loses its force. Where does this distinction belong in your diagnosis?
