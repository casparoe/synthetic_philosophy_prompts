I am revising the syllabus for an advanced seminar in value theory, and I have found myself returning with renewed dissatisfaction to the heterogeneity problem for hedonism: the challenge that bodily satisfactions, intellectual achievements, aesthetic absorptions, and even malicious enjoyments seem to share no common qualitative feature sufficient to justify their subsumption under a single natural kind called 'pleasure.' The standard taxonomy of replies—uniform phenomenal quality, intentional-object specifications, and dispositional theories of attraction—strikes me as incomplete because each neglects what I have begun to call 'modal texture': the structural profile governing a pleasure's resistance to interruption, its capacity for prolongation without diminishing returns, and its susceptibility to being translated across sensory or cognitive registers without qualitative dissolution. I intend to introduce this notion to my students not as a settled doctrine but as a working conceptual tool, and I want them to derive its ramifications without recourse to external scholarship.

Please describe, with as much philosophical precision as you can, the most important implications of taking modal texture seriously as a dimension of heterogeneity among pleasures. Your analysis should address three specific domains of consequence. First, consider welfare economics and the measurement of well-being: if pleasures differ structurally in their modal texture, does the project of aggregating individual welfare through a single cardinal scale of 'utility' or 'pleasure units' rest on a category error? Second, examine the argument from marginal cases in animal ethics: if the moral relevance of suffering turns not on a shared phenomenal feel but on a shared modal-texture profile—say, an analogous susceptibility to prolonged, uninterruptible distress—how does this alter the comparative assessment of human and non-human experience, and does it weaken or strengthen the anti-speciesist conclusion? Third, evaluate artificial reward architectures that optimize a unitary reward signal: is such maximization conceptually coherent if the target state lacks the homogeneity that modal-texture heterogeneity reveals?

This is intended as a conceptual exercise for graduate students working from first principles alone. Do not provide a bibliography, a list of further readings, or specific citations to canonical texts; the point is to test whether the reasoning holds together internally. Treat 'modal texture' exactly as defined above—resistance to interruption, prolongation dynamics, and cross-modal translatability—and assume your interlocutors are fluent in basic hedonistic theory but have not encountered this particular framing.
