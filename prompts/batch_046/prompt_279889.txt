Here's an argument I cobbled together for a seminar on political ethics and now I can't shake the feeling it's a trap. (1) Corruption just is the abuse of entrusted power for private gain, and "private" has to mean either "outside the formal rules of that office" or "outside universal moral norms." (2) If it's the first, then corruption is culture-bound: in a Weberian bureaucracy clientelism is corruption, but in a patronage society it's just fulfilling your reciprocal obligations, so there's no cross-cultural fact of the matter. (3) If it's the second, then any use of social position to benefit kin, clients, or friends counts as corrupt, since you're directing public-trust resources toward private circles. (4) But family loyalty and patron-client reciprocity are near-universal human goods, not moral failures; calling them corruption proves way too much and makes the concept apply to almost every society ever. (5) So either corruption is just "violates Western bureaucratic norms" — which makes international anti-corruption work pure imperialism — or it's "violates universal morality" — which makes corruption unavoidable and strips the word of critical bite. (6) Therefore "corruption" is either incoherent or necessarily parochial, and either way we can't consistently condemn bribery of judges in societies where it's normalized without either imposing our norms or condemning all social reciprocity.

Which step should I reject? I'd prefer to keep (1) and maybe (4), but I feel like something slips between (2) and (3), or maybe in the hidden move from (3) to (4). Tell me the weakest link, explain why it looked okay, and price out what giving it up costs — especially whether there's a middle path that saves corruption as real and cross-culturally wrong without collapsing into either imperialism or conceptual bankruptcy.
