"Two-dimensional semantics, developed mainly by Chalmers, says every expression has two intensions. The primary intension assigns an extension in each possible world considered as actual — so for 'water' it's whatever substance plays the water-role in that world (H2O here, maybe XYZ on Twin Earth). The secondary intension assigns the extension in worlds considered as counterfactual, holding fixed the actual-world reference — so 'water' picks out H2O everywhere. This is why 'water is H2O' is necessary a posteriori: epistemically, we have to discover which substance fills the water-role in our world (primary intension), but metaphysically it picks out H2O in all worlds (secondary intension). In fact, this means the statement is analytic in the secondary dimension but synthetic in the primary dimension, which is exactly how 2D semantics splits the difference.

This is basically the same mechanism as Kaplan's character/content distinction, except applied to natural kind terms instead of indexicals. Kaplan's character is a rule that takes a context and gives content; the primary intension is a rule that takes a world and gives an extension. One big objection comes from critics who argue that 2D semantics confuses metaphysical and epistemic modality. They say that just because we can't know a priori whether water is H2O doesn't mean there's a separate 'dimension' of meaning — it just shows our cognitive limitations.
