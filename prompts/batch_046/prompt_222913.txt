As a novelist working on a long manuscript about an archivist who discovers that the institution she serves has begun using its own demarcation criteria as a kind of secular orthodoxy, I have found myself trying to articulate, with real philosophical precision, what a theory of the boundary between legitimate history and pseudohistory must actually deliver to count as adequate rather than merely ideological. I am not asking for a survey of existing proposals scored against one another; I want the full specification of adequacy itself—the complete list of desiderata—written out with the motivating case or consideration behind each requirement, and with a clear flag for which conditions are genuinely non-negotiable and which are only the prejudices of particular camps. Specifically, the theory must be able to classify correctly not only the familiar cases like Atlantis narratives or ancient-astronaut claims, but also the more technically disguised reconstructions such as Fomenko's statistical chronology, where pseudohistory hides inside formal mathematical apparatus, and it must preserve distinctions that matter, such as the difference between a genuinely revolutionary revision of the record and one that is systematically insulated from evidentiary refutation, or between an unconventional historiographical method and one that collapses the hierarchy separating primary sources from speculative reconstruction.

Furthermore, I want you to state what results from actual historiography, archaeology, and the philosophy of science the theory cannot contradict—particularly anything concerning consilience across independent evidentiary lines and the epistemic asymmetry between negative and positive chronological claims—together with the reason each consistency condition is required. Crucially, examine whether any subset of these desiderata is jointly unsatisfiable: for instance, does demanding falsifiability in any rigorous sense necessarily exclude all large-scale narrative reconstructions, and does requiring consistency with established chronologies render the criterion viciously circular against radical revisionists? Then tell me which single requirement does the most damage to the most common demarcation proposals—whether it is the demand that the theory account for the sociology of belief formation without reducing history to it, or the demand that it leave conceptual room for genuine historical revolutions that initially look indistinguishable from pseudohistory. Please present all of this in continuous prose only—no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables—the way one writes a long, careful letter to a colleague, since I will use your response to clarify my own thinking before I return to the chapter.
