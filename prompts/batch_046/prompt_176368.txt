Write a dialogue between two equally competent philosophers, call them M and N, debating whether the inefficacy objection undermines individual moral obligations in collective harm cases—specifically climate-related consumption choices, participation in industrial meat markets, and voting in large-scale democracies. M should defend a unified version of the objection: in all three domains, the individual act makes no perceptible difference to the harmful outcome, and moral obligations must be tied to actions with discernible causal efficacy. N should reject this, but not by denying the empirical premises about scale. Instead, N should introduce a distinction between outcome-inefficacy and equilibrium-inefficacy, arguing that in gradient-structured harms (climate, meat demand) individual withdrawal alters the expected distribution or normative baseline of the system even when the aggregate outcome is unchanged, whereas in threshold-structured harms (voting) the objection actually gains force but fails because the wrong lies not in causing a specific result but in occupying a legitimating role within a collective decision procedure. They should respond directly to each other's points—M pressing that equilibrium shifts are empirically undetectable and morally irrelevant, N countering that moral evaluation tracks structural position rather than marginal causal contribution, and both addressing whether this distinction collapses when applied across the three domains. The dialogue should end with both acknowledging a tension they have not resolved: whether equilibrium-inefficacy can be quantified or whether it remains a purely conceptual claim. After the dialogue, state how confident you are in the distinctions drawn and single out the specific point—whether regarding voting or continuous markets—about which you are least certain.
