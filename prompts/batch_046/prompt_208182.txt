I've been thinking about moral status lately—mostly because I was discussing with my friends whether it's wierd to care more about a puppy than a spider, and we got stuck on whether there's actually a sliding scale or just a sharp line somewhere. I want to check if my intuitions even hang together, so here are my verdicts on specific cases. A common house spider that avoids bright light: I think it deserves some tiny slice of moral consideration, not because it's smart but because avoiding harm seems to matter a little. A venus flytrap that snaps on prey: none at all, even though it's reacting. A smart thermostat with a learning algorithm that predicts you'll feel cold and adjusts preemptively: also none, and this feels obvious, though I'm not sure I can defend it against someone who says it's processing discomfort-data just like the spider processes danger-data. A human in deep vegetative state with only brainstem activity: still high status, close to full, which feels right but I can't say exactly why the brainstem makes the difference. A cluster of lab-grown neurons—like a million human neurons in a dish wired into loops that generate patterns identical to pain responses, but with no body, no behavior, no survival drive—this is the one that makes me pause; I think it might have intermediate status, maybe more than the spider but less than a conscious human. And then the classic philosophical zombie, behaviorally identical to a person but with no inner experience: I lean toward saying it gets zero direct moral status, though treating it badly still feels wrong for indirect reasons.

The pair that really bugs me is the thermostat versus the neural cluster. Both are artificial systems processing signals about harm or discomfort. One I dismiss instantly, the other I want to grant some weight to. Is there one principle—maybe about biological origin, or about integrated experience, or about potential for suffering—that captures all these without just being a list of exceptions? If you have to overturn one of my verdicts to keep the principle clean, which should go? I also want to know what your best principle predicts for cases I haven't listed—like someone sleepwalking who can navigate a kitchen and respond to questions but remembers nothing and reports no experience afterwards, or a hive of ants that solves mazes collectively but each ant is simple.

Please don't just list theories I've heard a million times; I'm trying to figure out what I actually think, not get a survey. Give me the principle, or a small set of candidates, with the case that motivates each clause, and be honest about which of my gut reactions it can't save.
