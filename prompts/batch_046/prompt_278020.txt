I chair the adjudication panel for a national professional guild that reviews allegations of serious ethical misconduct. Complaints arrive weekly; sanctions can include expulsion, and our panel comprises both legal experts and lay members without philosophical training. We have forty-five minutes per case and must produce a written justification that distinguishes factual findings from normative judgements.

A recurring difficulty concerns standing to blame when the complainant is themselves implicated in the same or an analogous failing. I want to move beyond the crude view that hypocrisy automatically disqualifies, and beyond the view that it is always irrelevant. The framework I wish to operationalise is as follows.

Standing is graded, not binary, across three dimensions:
(i) direct victim standing, arising from having suffered the wrong in question;
(ii) representative standing, arising from speaking on behalf of affected parties who lack voice; and
(iii) civic standing, arising from membership in the moral community entitled to hold others to shared norms.

Hypocrisy interacts with these grades in specific ways. Symmetric hypocrisy—where the complainant exhibits the same fault to an equal or greater degree—fully cancels representative standing, but only proportionally diminishes civic standing (never below a minimal threshold). It does not affect direct victim standing unless the complainant’s own fault was a direct cause of the harm being blamed. Asymmetric hypocrisy—where the fault is present but structurally compelled by institutional rules the complainant was required to follow—creates a structural exemption that removes the hypocrisy discount entirely, though it does not create standing where none existed.

Please construct a reusable decision procedure my panel can apply in order. It should be a flowchart in words: the sequence of questions to ask, what counts as a sufficient answer at each stage, where the procedure issues a definitive “no standing” and stops, where it hands the decision to human judgement because the question is irreducibly one of values, and where it is likely to misfire. Be explicit about which steps are judgement calls dressed up as procedure. The procedure must work within forty-five minutes, require no specialist philosophical knowledge, and state clearly what it cannot determine.

Then apply the procedure to the following three cases:

1. A doctoral student alleges that their supervisor plagiarised sections of a grant proposal. The student was previously reprimanded for self-plagiarism in a minor conference abstract—same category of offence, markedly lesser severity, no direct causal link to the alleged harm.

2. A senior nurse alleges that the hospital board systematically suppressed adverse event reports. The nurse admits to having personally omitted two minor adverse events from records, but only after receiving explicit written instructions from the same board that omission was mandatory under a now-repealed internal policy.

3. A senior academic alleges that a colleague engaged in discriminatory hiring practices. The complainant has a well-documented record of expressing identical discriminatory views in private professional contexts, though never in formal hiring decisions and never sanctioned. The complainant is not a direct victim, but presents the complaint in a civic capacity on behalf of affected candidates.

In your third application, please include an outcome that the procedure produces but that I, as chair, am likely to find unwelcome, and explain precisely why the procedure yields it despite the intuitive resistance.

Finally, state the limits of the procedure: which kinds of standing disputes it cannot resolve, and which normative premises it silently assumes.
