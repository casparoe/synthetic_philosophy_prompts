My father recently updated his advance directive following an early-stage dementia diagnosis in our family, which has made these theoretical questions feel immediate rather than abstract. For this exam, students were asked:

Consider Mara, who executed a valid advance directive refusing life-extending treatment should she develop severe dementia. After developing advanced dementia, Mara expresses consistent cheerful contentment, requests food and care, and shows no distress. A critic argues that enforcing the directive is unjustified because the narrative self that authored it has been replaced, not merely obscured, so the document lacks authority over the present person. Evaluate this argument, paying particular attention to whether the earlier self’s authority is best understood as an extension of autonomy (will reaching across time) or as a form of temporal jurisdiction (the earlier self delimiting the later self’s decision-space), and whether either framework survives the claim that present Mara is numerically different with her own interests.

One student wrote:

The autonomy view holds that the competent earlier Mara extends her will into the future, binding her later self regardless of preference change, much as one might bind an heir. The jurisdictional view—sometimes called temporal jurisdiction—treats the directive not as continuous will but as a boundary the earlier self draws around the later self’s options. These two views actually depend on the same premise: that personal identity survives in some form. As Locke argued in his Essay, if psychological continuity is broken by dementia, the earlier and later Mara are not the same person; therefore both the autonomy and jurisdictional models collapse, since neither can apply across a gap in identity. The present Mara is happy and asks for care, which proves she has developed autonomous interests that must be respected. Because autonomy is the supreme principle in medical ethics, these present preferences should override the directive. The jurisdiction idea fails specifically because jurisdiction requires a single sovereign subject at both times, and since the subject has changed, there is no standing to enforce the boundary.
