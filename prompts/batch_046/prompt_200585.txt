As a journalist covering science and ideas, I have been following a proposal in the mid-sized municipality of Rivermouth to establish what administrators call a “veil of aggregation” oversight board for predictive policing algorithms. The board’s members would be deliberately denied access to individual-level demographic and biometric data—names, addresses, neighborhood-level ethnic composition—while retaining full authority over aggregate statistical profiles that determine deployment thresholds. The rationale is that direct knowledge of particular cases corrupts deliberative judgment with implicit bias, so rational oversight requires a structured ignorance that tracks justice only indirectly. I would like you to write a dialogue between two scholars—Dr. Aris Thorne, who defends this design from the standpoint of indirect normativity, and Dr. Lena Vasquez, who critiques it through the lens of idealized deliberation—debating whether such engineered ignorance is normatively legitimate or epistemically self-defeating.

Thorne should argue that board members’ deliberative motives ought not to mirror the direct tracking of rightness in each individual case, but should instead be calibrated to institutional dispositions that produce just outcomes over time, even if that requires withholding information that would be relevant in an unidealized setting. Vasquez should respond that idealized deliberation demands agents have access to all reasons that bear on the decision at hand, and that deliberately constructed ignorance violates the conditions under which normative authority can be legitimately exercised—if the board cannot know whether a specific neighborhood is being disproportionately targeted, its endorsement is not rational consent but manufactured acquiescence. The exchange should contain genuine back-and-forth: each must respond to the other’s actual moves rather than talk past them. Include a moment where Thorne concedes a tension in the indirect view—perhaps that if the aggregate data themselves encode historical biases, indirect tracking amplifies rather than corrects injustice—and a moment where Vasquez acknowledges that full transparency might indeed paralyze decision-making with cognitive overload. Conclude without declaring a winner, but with both articulating what empirical or conceptual finding would change their minds. Keep the tone formal and philosophical, suitable for a feature on normative ethics in public administration.
