I'm drafting the final exam for an upper-level seminar in philosophy of rationality (advanced undergraduates and first-year graduates), and I want one 90-minute essay question on expected-value fanaticism that can't be answered by reciting the standard dust-speck-versus-torture framing. As someone who's followed these debates since the old LessWrong threads on St. Petersburg and Pascal, I want the prompt to force students into a specific under-discussed wrinkle: suppose an agent evaluates an astronomically high-payoff, astronomically low-probability gamble via EV maximization, but she also assigns a small, non-zero meta-probability that her reasoning about the gamble's existence, magnitude, or probability is corrupted—whether by cognitive bias, adversarial deception, or a hallucinated premise. Critics claim this meta-uncertainty either (i) bounds the expectation and collapses fanaticism into ordinary risk-aversion, or (ii) launches an infinite regress of higher-order checks that paralyzes all action. I want students to take a definite, committed position—either defending the cutoff view or defending the intact-fanaticism view—and to defend it with specific analytical moves, not merely survey both sides neutrally.

Please provide exactly three things:

1. The exam question, framed as a 90-minute essay (target 1,200–1,500 words, 20 marks total). It must be answerable without web access, must not ask for bibliographies, citations, or reading lists, and must not lean on obscure formalisms a well-prepared student couldn't reason through on the spot. It should explicitly require the student to choose and defend one side of the meta-uncertainty dilemma.

2. A model answer of about 700 words that takes the committed stance that the meta-uncertainty objection fails—specifically, that it conflates first-order decision-theoretic value with second-order epistemic utility, that the apparent regress is not vicious but terminates in a practical stopping rule tied to the agent's actual credence budget, and that applying EV maximization to the choice of decision rule does not undermine itself. The answer should give its reasons directly and should not present the two sides as equally plausible.

3. A marking scheme with five must-hit analytical moves, the marks each carries (summing to 20), and a one-line note on how candidates typically miss or botch each move. The scheme should separate answers that genuinely understand the structure of the regress from answers that have only memorized the label "expected-value fanaticism."
