I'm writing a feature on how norms actually die, and my colleague Mara — she teaches intellectual history and has this running taxonomy she's been testing on moral revolutions — walked me through her verdicts on five cases. She says teh abolition of the British slave-trade wasn't mainly won by moral argument but by economic restructuring and geopolitical pressure; dueling faded because state violence monopolies rewired honor codes, not because anyone rationally persuaded duelists; foot-binding collapsed through elite shame and international prestige dynamics rather than material interest; Victorian bans on public blood sports came from class anxiety about respectability, not rights-based reasoning; and she puts the rapid fall of arranged marriage in postwar Japan partly down to prestige dynamics among urban elites, not economic modernization alone. She's basically running a version of teh Bernard Williams thesis that moral change is more like geological drift than rational persuasion, layered with Kwame Anthony Appiah's idea that honor does teh hidden labor arguments pretend to do.

Now she's stuck on a newer shift: teh near-collapse of teh "always-on" workplace culture and after-hours email norms in parts of Europe after right-to-disconnect legislation, where employer economic interests clearly pushed against it, moral arguments about work-life balance had circulated for decades without much effect, and yet the norm flipped within a few years once it got tied to national prestige and elite self-image. If you're adjudicating her track record, what rule is she actually running — pure material interest, honor-and-shame dynamics, or some hybrid where honor only works when material conditions are permissive? What should she predict about this case to stay consistent with herself, and which of her earlier rulings is the closest analog? And what's the one hypothetical norm shift — maybe one where both elite honor and economic interest strongly favor keeping the old practice — that would actually split her framework from a pure-interest account?
