I'm preparing an extended essay for my philosophy seminar on whether normative rationales can exist independently of any reasoning subject—what my tutor calls free-floating rationales. Over the past term she has offered verdicts on five cases, and I want to test whether her judgments form a consistent pattern before applying it to a hypothetical I constructed. Her rulings, paraphrased: first, that arithmetic truths (for instance, that every prime greater than two is odd) generate rationales for belief that do not depend on any particular mathematician being present; second, that evolutionary explanations of camouflage carry no free-floating rationale, since talk of a "reason" for the trait is only causal shorthand; third, that if the universe were permanently empty of conscious beings, the wrongness of gratuitous suffering would remain as a suspended but real normative rationale; fourth, that a non-conscious optimization algorithm tracking harm-minimization possesses no internal rationales, because functional alignment is not the same as being guided by reasons; and fifth, that aesthetic judgments about natural landscapes require an actual perceiver, so there is no free-floating aesthetic rationale in an unobserved forest.

Now I want to know what she would say about this new case: imagine a bioengineered system—call it a synthetic moral patient—capable of experiencing suffering but entirely lacking reasoning, self-awareness, linguistic capacity, or any potential for agency, and permanently isolated so that no other being ever interacts with it. Is there a free-floating rationale against allowing it to suffer?

If you think her verdicts are genuinely underdetermined, please do the following. Predict her answer and identify the closest analog among the five above. Then articulate at least two distinct principles—perhaps one that ties rationales to objective normative domains regardless of agents, and another that requires either actual or potential reasoners for a rationale to be instantiated—that both accommodate all five of her earlier rulings. Show what each principle predicts about the synthetic moral patient. Finally, propose one further case that would clearly separate these principles, explaining which verdict on that case would commit her to one rule rather than the other.
