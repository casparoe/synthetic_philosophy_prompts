Consider the following judgments about when belief formed through others' say-so is epistemically justified, and whether that justification is irreducibly testimonial or reducible to my own reasoning about reliability, coherence, and evidence.

Case 1. A geologist examines a sample and tells me it is basalt. I have no independent access to the sample's properties. I believe her. The justification feels partly like trusting her word, and partly like my background judgment that geologists are reliable.

Case 2. The same geologist now reads an output from a black-box mineral-classifier she does not understand. The device says "basalt" with 99.7% accuracy in trials, but she has no geological story for why this sample fits. She reports the result. I still form the belief that it is basalt. Here my acceptance seems to track the device's reliability rather than her understanding, yet the epistemic result feels almost identical to Case 1.

Case 3. I read an entry in a collaborative encyclopedia that has survived thousands of anonymous edits over fifteen years. No single contributor has verified the specific claim I read. I treat the claim as credible. This does not feel like trusting one person, but it also does not feel like performing my own independent inference.

Case 4. A close friend tells me she left her previous job voluntarily. I happen to have seen her termination letter. I reject her testimony outright. The rejection seems warranted not merely because my direct evidence outweighs hers, but because testimony that conflicts with better evidence is disqualified in a way that ordinary perception is not.

Case 5. A child describes an imaginary friend who lives in the clouds. I dismiss the claim. But if the same child, with the same demeanor, tells me there is a spider on my shoulder, I check and usually believe her. The difference seems to lie in domain-specific plausibility rather than in a general assessment of her sincerity or competence.

Case 6. A news broadcast reports that a distant supernova was observed. The anchor reads a script; the astronomer who wrote it has never met the anchor. I form the belief. The chain involves institutional filtering, but at no point do I assess the astronomer's personal reliability.

My tentative pattern: testimony is justified when it is embedded in a structure—professional, institutional, or technological—that I have reason to trust, except when it conflicts with direct counter-evidence or falls outside a plausible domain. Yet this sounds reductionist: I am always reasoning about structures and reliability, never taking a speaker's word as a basic reason.

But there is a tension. In Case 2, I seem to accept the geologist's report even though she lacks the understanding that would make her an authoritative speaker in the traditional sense; the justification seems to run through the device, not through her mental states. In Case 3, there is no speaker at all. If reductionism is correct, these should be analyzable as ordinary inductive reasoning about systems. But if anti-reductionism is correct—if testimony is a distinctive source of knowledge that requires a speaker with appropriate mental states—then Case 2 and Case 3 should either be unjustified or should be reclassified as non-testimonial.

Meanwhile, Case 4 suggests that testimony has a special vulnerability to defeat by counter-evidence that differs from how I would treat a perceptual belief of my own. And Case 5 suggests that domain plausibility gates acceptance in a way that pure reliability-tracking does not fully explain, since I do not generally assess children's global reliability before accepting each claim.

Is there a coherent principle—whether reductionist, anti-reductionist, or a hybrid—that accommodates all six verdicts? If so, state it precisely, identify which clause handles which case, and say what it predicts for a case not listed: an autonomous diagnostic system that generates and communicates a medical prognosis to me without any human reviewing or endorsing the message. Must I drop any of my verdicts to maintain simplicity? Defend your answer.
