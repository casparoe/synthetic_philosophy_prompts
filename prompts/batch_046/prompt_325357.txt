My friend and I just spent three hours arguing about whether a restored marble statue—say, one where conservators have replaced half the original stone with chemically matched material—is still the same object as the artist’s work, or whether it has become a new thing that merely occupies the same shape. We kept circling back to the classic statue-and-clay problem: is the statue identical to the lump of marble, or are there two things coincident in the same place? The philosophical literature feels stalled to me. The coincidence view and the identity view have traded the same modal arguments and the same destruction intuition-pumps for decades, and neither side has moved. My working theory is that the disagreement is not a substantive ontological dispute at all, but a clash between two incompatible norms of individuation that have been smuggled into metaphysics: art-historical identification tracks authorial form and institutional cataloguing, while physical identification tracks spatiotemporal continuity of matter, and these norms simply diverge in cases of gradual restoration or molecular replacement in porous stone. But I want an honest diagnosis rather than my own guess. Are the sides actually answering different questions—one about ontology, one about conservation ethics—under a single heading? Is there a hidden empirical commitment, perhaps about how materials science determines “original substance” in aged marble? Or is the impasse just bedrock intuition about whether form or matter is the principle of unity? Given your diagnosis, what would count as actual progress: a formal result in mereology, a conceptual distinction that separates artistic objecthood from material constitution, or an argument that the question is ill-posed because “the statue” is not a natural kind? And please push back plainly if my framing rests on a confused premise—say, if I am wrongly assuming that conservation practice carries metaphysical authority—or if the task of diagnosing this stall cannot be done well.
