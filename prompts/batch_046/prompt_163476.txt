During a recent visit to a museum of contemporary art, I encountered a monumental installation—an enclosed, sound-dampened chamber filled with slowly rotating industrial materials—that the curators described in the wall text as achieving a "deliberate collapse of spatial perspective." When I told a colleague that I was inclined to take the curators' assessment seriously, she dismissed the point with the familiar refrain that "there's no accounting for taste," adding that expert opinion about art is therefore merely institutionalized personal preference. I want to understand exactly what work that slogan is capable of doing in this kind of exchange. Could you lay out the distinct precise theses that "there's no accounting for taste" (and its Latin equivalent) could actually be expressing, arranged from the most trivial or definitional to the most substantive and controversial? For each reading, please specify: (i) whether it concerns the ontology of aesthetic properties, the epistemology of aesthetic judgment, or the normative rules governing taste disputes; (ii) whether that reading genuinely undermines appeals to curatorial or critical testimony—specifically, the claim that this particular work achieves a deliberate collapse of spatial perspective; and (iii) whether the reading still permits any meaningful distinction between informed aesthetic judgment and mere personal liking. I need this delivered as a disambiguation menu rather than a single interpretation, with clear indication of which versions would actually license dismissing an expert's assessment and which would not.
