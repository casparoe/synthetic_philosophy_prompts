I'm writing a feature for a long-form science and ideas magazine on why epiphenomenalism has returned to the center of philosophy of mind, and I've been tracking a year-long dispute between two camps that has become technically dense without converging on the actual fault lines. One camp—the Epiphenomenalists—holds that phenomenal consciousness lacks all causal efficacy yet poses no special epistemic problem; they maintain that phenomenal judgments are fully generated by subpersonal representational mechanisms that need never be causally influenced by the conscious states themselves. The opposing camp—the Anti-Paradox Critics—contends that this collapses into a performative contradiction: if consciousness plays no causal role, then the judgments defending epiphenomenalism are themselves causally disconnected from consciousness, undermining both the reliability of phenomenal reports and the rational authority of anyone advancing the view. Rather than continue the polemics, I want them to produce a formal adversarial collaboration document that both sides could sign.

Please draft that joint statement. Begin with a numbered section of agreed claims—covering the causal closure of the physical, the distinction between first-order phenomenal states and second-order representational judgments, and the empirical findings on neural correlates of consciousness that both sides accept as common background. Follow with a disagreements table stating each camp's precise position in wording both would endorse, specifically on whether representational or predictive-processing theories of introspection can explain the accuracy of phenomenal reports without appealing to causal efficacy, and on whether the paradox of phenomenal judgment is genuinely self-defeating or merely apparent once introspection is properly modeled. Then identify the cruxes: I take one to concern whether predictive-processing accounts of introspection can recover the specificity of phenomenal content without direct acquaintance, and another to concern whether scientific practices that depend on calibrated first-person reports implicitly presuppose that consciousness causally contributes to those reports. For each crux, propose a concrete case or conceptual test both sides agree bears on it—perhaps an analogy between blindsight and introspective access, or a hypothetical neurofeedback study comparing phenomenal reports driven by direct neural feedback versus purely behavioral conditioning—and record in advance what each camp predicts the result would show. The document should be rigorous enough for specialists in philosophy of mind yet structured clearly enough that an educated general reader can see exactly where the residual disagreement lies after all shared ground has been mapped.
