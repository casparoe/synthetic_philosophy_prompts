Since my sister's recent diagnosis of semantic dementia, I have become preoccupied with the observation that conceptual failure is not primarily a loss of verbal definitions but an erosion of analogical reach—she can still name objects but no longer grasps why a whale belongs with mammals rather than fish, not because she forgot a classification rule but because the structural mapping that sustains the category has thinned. This personal urgency has led me to study Hofstadter and Sander's argument that analogy is not an ornamental comparison but the core mechanism of cognition itself, and I have agreed to deliver a single fifty-minute lecture on this view to an audience of non-specialist adults at a community college series. I want them to understand that concepts are not boxes bounded by necessary and sufficient conditions but fluid "blobs" maintained by analogical proximity to central exemplars, with membership fading gradually rather than ending at a sharp border. To keep the session from collapsing into vague talk about seeing similarities, I want to center it on a cross-modal carrying example: a structural correspondence between a spatial domain (physical balance or equilibrium) and an abstract moral domain (justice as equilibrium), chosen specifically because the two domains share no perceptual features, so that if the audience understands the mapping they must be grasping analogical structure rather than mental imagery. Please plan the explanatory arc: what foundational distinction—between categorization and analogical projection—must be secured before this example can land; why the standard "atom is like a solar system" example should be avoided in favor of this cross-modal case; the misconceptions they will predictably bring—likely that analogy is mere metaphor, that concepts have definite edges, and that comparison requires shared visual or sensory properties—and the precise point in the sequence where each should be anticipated and defused; one final question I can pose to test whether they have understood analogical structure rather than just acquired the vocabulary; and which technical aspect of the theory—perhaps the mathematics of conceptual slippage or the neuroscience of analogical mapping—I should explicitly refuse to treat at this level.
