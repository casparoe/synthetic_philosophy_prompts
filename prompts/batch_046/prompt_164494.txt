Consider the institutional practice, most visibly embodied in Article 231 of the Treaty of Versailles and in subsequent reparations regimes, by which an entire nation-state is assigned collective moral and legal responsibility for an aggressive war—framed in treaty language as guilt and atoned for through reparations—while the same international legal architecture, exemplified by the Nuremberg tribunals, simultaneously insists that criminal culpability for war crimes is strictly individual and that civilian populations may not be punished collectively for governmental acts. This tension is compounded by the Fischer controversy, in which historians have disputed whether the German leadership deliberately engineered the conflict through calculated expansionism or whether the war emerged from structural diplomatic failures and alliance dynamics, leaving the empirical question of collective elite intention genuinely unsettled. What philosophical commitments about collective agency, blame, and state personhood does this practice actually presuppose? Must it commit us to a metaphysically robust view of nations as intentional moral agents—corporate persons capable of culpable wrongdoing in something like the way individuals are—or can the practice be reconstructed as a non-agential, institutional liability scheme analogous to strict liability in tort law, which assigns costs to risk-generating structures regardless of intent? Is the practice consistent with the legal refusal to apply collective criminal punishment, or do treaty-based collective blame and criminal-law individualism rest on incompatible theories of group responsibility? Moreover, does the justification for collective reparations depend on resolving the Fischer-style debate about elite intentionality, or would a purely structural, risk-based account of state conduct justify identical obligations without attributing collective intent? Reconstruct the implicit theory that best rationalizes the practice, distinguish what it strictly requires from what it merely accommodates, identify where the practice is philosophically underdetermined, and specify precisely what would have to change in treaty language, institutional design, and moral rhetoric if the underlying theory were replaced by one that denies moral personhood to states entirely.
