I have recently become the legal guardian of a child born in a society that operates with a relational, kin-embedded conception of personhood rather than the bounded, continuous self I take for granted in my own WEIRD framework, and this has made the cross-cultural debate over moral psychology suddenly urgent rather than merely academic. Here is an argument I have constructed to test whether moral objectivity can survive empirical divergence, but it now seems to prove far more than I intended and threatens the very conceptual ground of my obligations to her. (1) If a domain of belief exhibits deep, systematic cross-cultural divergence that tracks local socio-ecological conditions rather than converging under improved information and reflection, then that domain lacks objective, mind-independent truths. (2) Moral cognition exhibits precisely this pattern: populations in large-scale, mobile societies weight harm and fairness as near-universal, whereas many small-scale societies calibrate loyalty, authority, and purity to kinship density and resource unpredictability, with no evident convergence upon exposure to alternative frameworks. (3) Therefore morality lacks objective, culture-independent facts. (4) The same divergence pattern holds for metaphysical beliefs about the self: WEIRD cultures presuppose a bounded, continuous individual who bears rights, while relational cultures treat personhood as distributed across kin networks and social roles. (5) By (1), personal identity and the continuity conditions of rights-bearers also lack objective grounding. (6) But my parental obligations to this child depend precisely on her being a continuous individual with stable rights that persist across contexts; if (5) is true, I have no non-arbitrary, culture-independent reason to treat her as the same rights-bearer tomorrow or to prioritize her welfare over collective obligations. (7) Thus the reasoning that undermines moral objectivity in (3) simultaneously dissolves the conceptual framework that renders my guardianship intelligible. (8) Because I cannot abandon that framework, I must reject (1). (9) Yet (1) is the only principle that distinguishes moral and personal-identity beliefs from mathematics or elementary logic, which do converge cross-culturally; without it, the evolutionary debunker can apply the same adaptive-variability argument to logic itself, and I have no principled defense of moral objectivity. (10) So I am caught: either I accept that my obligations rest on a culturally parochial fiction, or I reject a premise whose empirical motivation I still find compelling. Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost me? I am especially reluctant to give up (2), since the empirical pattern seems real, and I would prefer to keep the idea that my obligations are genuinely binding rather than locally adaptive fictions, but I want to know whether that preference forces me into an inconsistency. Please present your diagnosis in continuous prose only, without headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, as one would write in a letter.
