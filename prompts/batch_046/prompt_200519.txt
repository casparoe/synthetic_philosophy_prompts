I spent last evening arguing with a friend about whether plural values can ever be genuinely incommensurable, and I want to see whether my own intuitions form a pattern or just a mess. I tentatively propose that goods are incommensurable when they are constitutive of non-overlapping forms of life, but merely hard to compare when they serve the same form of life through different means; yet I am unsure this survives contact with the cases.

Here are my verdicts.

First, a curator must choose between preserving a single unrecoverable manuscript containing an unknown philosophical tradition—no copies exist, and translation would take decades—and funding a medical intervention that saves exactly one life. I say save the life without hesitation, but I resist describing the manuscript's loss as less valuable; instead, the choice feels tragic because the goods belong to different registers that share no common metric.

Second, a musician must choose between completing a symphony that only she can finish—which requires placing her young children in institutional care for two years—or remaining with the children and letting the work stay unfinished. I say she should stay, yet I do not think this is because family happiness outweighs artistic achievement on some scale. It feels more like a domain-specific obligation that operates as a threshold rather than a weight.

Third, a city must choose between demolishing a historic district that defines communal identity in order to build climate-resilient housing for five hundred families, or preserving the district and leaving those families unhoused. I think the housing should be built, but I want to insist that the community suffers a harm that is not canceled out by the housing gain; the two outcomes are not fungible.

Fourth, a researcher can either conduct an experiment that will definitively resolve a long-standing scientific debate but will destroy a small isolated culture's ritual practices, or abandon the experiment. I feel the experiment should not proceed, yet I also think that if the same knowledge could be gained without destruction, we ought to pursue it. This suggests to me that the wrongness lies in the specific violation, not in the knowledge itself.

Fifth, a policy choice: extend one thousand lives by one year each, or preserve an endangered language with twelve remaining native speakers that encodes irreplaceable cosmological concepts. Here I still favor extending the lives, but my confidence is far weaker than in the first case, and I notice that if I imagine the lives extended are all severely demented while the language carries unique conceptual structures, my intuition wavers.

The tension I cannot resolve is between the second and the fifth cases. In the second, I reject quantitative aggregation across domains and invoke a threshold; in the fifth, I seem to accept quantitative aggregation for lives even though language and life appear to belong to different registers. Meanwhile, in the first case I prioritized life over culture, but in the fourth I protected culture over knowledge, which makes me wonder whether my tentative principle is doing any work at all.

Is there a coherent principle—perhaps the one about constitutive goods and non-overlapping forms of life, or something else entirely—that delivers all five verdicts without collapsing into simple weighing? If not, which of my verdicts should I overturn? And what does the best available principle predict for a case I have not listed: choosing between funding basic scientific research with no foreseeable practical application, and funding palliative care for terminal patients who will die within months regardless?
