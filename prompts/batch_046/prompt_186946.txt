I just finished a long and somewhat exhausting debate with a close friend who studies clinical psychology. She argued that eliminative materialism about beliefs and desires—the view that our common-sense framework of propositional attitudes is not merely inaccurate but entirely empty, with no corresponding neural reality—would be harmless if confined to philosophy seminars, but becomes dangerous if taken seriously in domains where people actually try to change behavior, such as addiction treatment, cognitive behavioral therapy, or forensic assessment. I pushed back, insisting that a total elimination of belief and desire might still leave intact the normative and explanatory structures we need, but I left the conversation unsure whether I was defending eliminativism or just smuggling folk psychology back in through the rear door.

Please describe the most important implications of a total, rather than partial, eliminative materialism about beliefs and desires, with particular attention to three practical domains. First, consider the coherence of giving someone a reason to act differently: if there are no beliefs about means-ends relations and no desires that represent ends, can the practice of reason-giving survive as anything more than causal manipulation, or does it require us to adopt a purely predictive-processing vocabulary—talk of prediction errors, precision weighting, and allostatic regulation—that loses the normative force of "you ought to do X because you want Y"? Second, examine self-deception: if the phenomenon requires both a true belief and a false belief held simultaneously by the same subject, and neither exists, is self-deception merely a misleading description of competing neural dynamics, and does that reconstrual eliminate the first-person authority we ordinarily claim when we confess to being self-deceived? Third, address moral responsibility in cases of compulsive or addictive behavior, where agents describe their actions as "unwanted" or "against their better judgment"—descriptions that seem to depend on a contrast between what they believe they should do and what they desire to do. Does total eliminativism force us to replace these descriptions with purely causal or neurobehavioral accounts, and if so, do we lose something essential to the practice of holding people responsible rather than merely treating them?

In your answer, be explicit about where your reasoning is most vulnerable: which of these three domains—reasons-giving, self-deception, or moral responsibility—would be the first to collapse if an eliminativist accused you of smuggling in the very folk-psychological categories being eliminated? Furthermore, tell me what specific argument or empirical finding—perhaps about the neural mechanisms of decision-making, the pragmatic indispensability of mental-state attribution in clinical settings, or the failure of neurobehavioral vocabulary to capture normative distinctions—would lead you to change your mind about whether eliminativism can survive these practical implications intact.
