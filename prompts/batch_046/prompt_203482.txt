I'm working through my intuitions about non-sensory self-knowledge, using Avicenna's flying man as a starting point but extending it into territory the original argument doesn't cover. Here are my verdicts on concrete cases, and I want to know whether they cohere around one principle or whether I have to abandon one.

If a person is suspended in absolute void with no bodily sensation and no external perception, she still knows that she exists — the classic flying-man result, which I accept. A brain in a vat receiving only simulated sensory streams also knows it exists, though the knowledge feels mediated rather than direct. A human born without any sensory modalities but with an intact internal mental life also knows herself, which makes me think acquired sensory loss isn't the deciding factor. By contrast, a person in deep, dreamless sleep lacks this self-knowledge for the duration despite physical continuity, and an artificial system that tracks its own internal states but has never possessed qualitative experience or a point of view does not genuinely know it exists, even when it produces correct self-referential outputs. I keep going back and forth on a hypothetical distributed subject — consciousness spread across a network with no central node, each part registering only local relations — feeling that there is no single "I" to know itself, yet unable to rule out a collective first-person awareness.

Is there a precise principle of first-person self-awareness that delivers all of these judgments, or nearly all? Please state the principle clearly, note the case that motivates each clause, and say what it implies for a situation I haven't listed — for instance, a being that experiences only abstract mathematical relations with no qualitative character at all.
