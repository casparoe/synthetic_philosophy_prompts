Hey, im drafting notes for a short essay on Parfit's teletransporter. I want to move past the usual identity-vs-psychological-continuity framing and inject a third factor: accountability inheritance—basically whether the future person should inherit my debts, promises, and moral blame regardless of whether they 'are' me or whether I 'survive' in thier welfare.

In the standard destroyed-original case, psychological continuity seems to cover survival (i care about the copy's future), but accountability is weird: the copy recieve my memories of signing a contract, yet the legal person who signed is dead. In a branching version where both original and copy existant, identity splits cleanly, survival gets ambiguous, but liability gets even weirder—can i still be sued if my copy is the one holding the job?

I'm arguing our gut reaction tracks accountability inheritance more than pure survival. If the copy won't be held to my promises, something feels broken even with perfect memory transfer. So 'what matters' might actually be a bundle of three separable things: numerical identity, care/survival, and liability inheritance.

Is this just a muddled redescription of legal-personhood or relational theories? Am I wrong to think branching breaks liability?
