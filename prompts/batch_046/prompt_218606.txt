I serve as a volunteer coordinator at a neighbourhood support centre that distributes emergency meals and issues temporary shelter referrals. The local authority has proposed that anyone seeking a shelter referral must first complete a two-hour "independence and readiness" workshop and submit dated proof of at least two recent job applications, regardless of health, literacy, or housing status. The centre's director supports the rule, arguing that unconditional provision fosters dependency and that requiring visible effort ensures resources go only to those genuinely committed to improvement. A colleague who conducts intake interviews replies that the workshop teaches no marketable skill in two hours; its real function is symbolic, acting as a filter that rewards those able to perform bureaucratic compliance rather than those in greatest need. She notes that people with untreated mental illness, language barriers, or no fixed address are unlikely to clear this hurdle, not because they lack motivation but because the condition mistakes administrative visibility for moral desert. I am drafting the centre's response and feel genuinely divided. Is my discomfort merely sentimental, or does the policy embody a coherent philosophical mistake about how to distinguish the "undeserving" from the genuinely vulnerable? Specifically, can one defend conditioning survival-level aid on performative effort without reducing the concept of desert to the ability to navigate paperwork? Please advise, and please respond in American English.
