After a long argument with a friend about whether evolutionary history should enjoy any privileged status in fixing what a biological trait is genuinely “for,” I want to work through the tension between etiological and causal-role accounts of biological function without falling back on the usual textbook illustrations. To force the issue, we kept returning to a scenario we invented: imagine a de-extinct mammoth-elephant hybrid carrying a restored Pleistocene gene for a dense undercoat. In its original environment the coat was selected for thermoregulation; in the modern sanctuary where the hybrid actually lives, the same keratin structure happens to bind a local pathogen and now plays a central causal role in immune defense, while thermoregulation is ecologically irrelevant. My friend insists that the etiological account correctly assigns the “real” function—cold adaptation—because selection history fixes normative function, and that the causal-role view wrongly licenses an infinite proliferation of present-tense functions. I countered that biologists studying the hybrid today investigate immune binding, not Pleistocene climate, so the causal-role account tracks actual scientific practice; yet I worry that without some etiological constraint we cannot distinguish function from accidental effect. Please compare these two accounts—etiological and causal-role—using this hybrid-undercoat case not as mere decoration but as a genuine stress test. Specifically, address whether each account can accommodate what we might call functional drift without collapsing into the other; whether the causal-role view can avoid the charge of promiscuity without smuggling in historical criteria; and whether the distinction still carries normative weight for conservation policy when the organism in question is itself an engineered or resurrected entity rather than the continuous product of wild-type selection.
