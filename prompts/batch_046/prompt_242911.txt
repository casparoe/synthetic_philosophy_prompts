For my philosophy of religion course I am drafting a brief position statement, and I would appreciate a consistency audit before I present it to the seminar. My commitments regarding the ontological argument—particularly Gödel's modal formulation—are roughly these. First, I grant that the argument is formally valid within S5 modal logic, but I reject S5 as the correct logic for metaphysical modality, since I can conceive of a being that is necessary relative to one possible world without being absolutely necessary across all worlds. Second, despite that reservation, I find the claim that necessary existence counts as a perfection genuinely compelling, and I think a being that exists necessarily is greater than one whose existence depends on contingent conditions. Third, at the same time, I regard the distinction between positive and negative properties as too vague and culturally loaded to do rigorous metaphysical work. Fourth, I maintain that divine existence, if real, must be either necessary or impossible—there is no coherent middle ground of contingent divinity—because any being worthy of worship cannot depend on anything external for its being. Fifth, I believe that even if Gödel's argument fails, it still teaches us something deep about how modal logic constrains theological reasoning, rather than being merely an interesting formal exercise.

Do these commitments hang together, or am I helping myself to incompatible motivations? I am especially worried that endorsing necessary existence as a perfection in my second commitment conflicts with my dismissal of the property framework in my third, and that my rejection of S5 in the first commitment might undermine the significance I claim for the argument in the fifth. Could you audit this package systematically: rank the tensions by severity, distinguish genuine contradictions from merely apparent ones, identify the least costly revision that would restore consistency, and tell me whether there is a hidden coherence I have missed? I am not asking for a general lecture on the ontological argument—just a targeted consistency check on this specific combination of views.
