In reporting on how social conventions form and stabilize, I keep encountering an analogy used to defend an evolutionary, broadly conservative view of norms: writers compare the development of social conventions to the evolution of chess opening theory. Just as chess players, with no governing body, gradually converge on certain opening sequences through decentralized trial, error, and punishment by defeat—producing an accumulated strategic knowledge that no single player could design from scratch—so social norms, they argue, emerge through interaction, encode practical coordination wisdom, and should not be redesigned by rational planners.

Can you audit how far this holds? Provide an explicit correspondence table—the players, the rulebook, the win condition, the opening manuals, the deviation that gets punished—and then show exactly where the mapping fractures: do social norms have an equivalent of checkmate, are they as discrete and observable as chess moves, and does the source domain smuggle in features like an intentionally designed rule system or unambiguous feedback loops that real-world coordination lacks? Please also distinguish what is well settled about Lewis-style accounts of convention and norm dynamics from what specialists genuinely contest, and do not present a disputed interpretation as the consensus. Finally, tell me whether the policy conclusion—that we should generally defer to evolved norms rather than attempt top-down engineering—survives once the disanalogies are on the table, or whether the analogy was carrying that conclusion all along.
