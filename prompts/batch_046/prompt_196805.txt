I hold that omissions are never genuine causes—not only in ordinary action but also in structural and institutional contexts, such as a regulatory body’s failure to enforce a safety checkpoint or a biological system’s failure to maintain homeostatic inhibition—where an absence is treated as producing harm. I maintain that every apparent case of causation-by-omission either reduces to the presence of a preventing condition, to the causal efficacy of surrounding positive events, or to a pragmatic misdescription that serves blame-attribution and explanatory convenience rather than metaphysical explanation. Tell me what would count against this position. Which intuitions—about culpable negligence, about omission-related error signals in cognitive neuroscience, or about counterfactual dependence in epidemiology—would be decisive, and which merely awkward? What formal results in interventionist or counterfactual accounts of causation, particularly the treatment of absence-variables in causal modeling, would force a revision of the view rather than a reinterpretation of the formalism? Does the thesis risk collapsing into a rival positive-event reductionism, and if so, is that a fatal flaw or just a clarification of what was always meant? What would committed defenders of the no-omission-causation thesis themselves concede as genuinely counting against them—perhaps asymmetries in legal liability, or the predictive success of omission-talk in public health—versus what they would dismiss as linguistic or normative convenience? Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, say where the position is well insulated by qualifications (for instance, by sharply separating metaphysical causation from normative responsibility), and indicate whether that insulation is philosophically virtuous or ad hoc.
