In Hofstadter's GEB, the Gödelian analogy moves from formal self-reference—a formula that talks about its own unprovability within a fixed system—to the idea of self-aware minds that can reflect on their own reasoning. I keep seeing a specific extension of this analogy in later discussions that seems distinct from both the original formal result and from Hofstadter's strange loops. The pattern runs like this: a speaker claims that just as Gödel showed no consistent formal system can prove all arithmetic truths about itself, so too no fixed formal model of a mind can capture all truths about that mind's reasoning. But then they add a particular twist: human cognition is said to involve not a single self-referential loop, but an inexhaustible, iterative ascent—whenever you formalize a mind's reasoning in system S, the mind can recognize a new Gödel sentence for S, then formalize that recognition in S', only to step outside S' in turn, ad infinitum. This is presented not as one tangled hierarchy but as a never-ending ladder of formalizations, each escaped in sequence. Does this specific configuration—an iterative, ladder-like Gödelian escape rather than a single strange loop or fixed-point self-reference—have an established name in philosophy of mind or in critiques of mechanizability? How exactly should it be distinguished from Lucas's argument, from Penrose's claim about non-computability, and from Hofstadter's own tangled-hierarchy account of self-awareness? And if the literature treats these as one family, what is lost by collapsing this iterative version into the others?
