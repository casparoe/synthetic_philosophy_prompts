I'm designing the final take-home assessment for an upper-level undergraduate seminar in comparative philosophy of religion and metaphysics, and I need a prompt that sits in the weeds of the Buddhist no-self (anattā) literature without letting students retreat to generic summaries they could reproduce from an introductory textbook. The course has covered the Pali distinction between conventional (sammuti) and ultimate (paramattha) truth, the Abhidharma analysis of mental continuity through causal streams of skandhas, and Western narrative-identity theory, but students often conflate anattā with simple psychological discontinuity or with Humean bundle theories they have encountered in earlier courses. To prevent pure regurgitation, I want the exam to turn on a specific, somewhat constructed tension: whether the Buddhist two-level framework can accommodate the normative demand that a single agent "author" her life-narrative, or whether accepting anattā forces one into a reductionist view structurally parallel to Parfit's fission cases—yet with different moral consequences for karma and responsibility across rebirth.

Please write a single take-home essay question (approximately 1,200 words, 50 marks, 48-hour window) together with a model answer and a detailed marking scheme. The prompt itself should ask students to evaluate whether mapping the conventional/ultimate distinction onto the metaphysical/narrative-identity distinction succeeds, specifically requiring them to address the objection that without an enduring self to "own" the narrative, moral responsibility becomes as incoherent as personal survival in a split-brain reductionist scenario, and then to explain precisely why this parallel either holds or breaks down within the Buddhist causal framework. The model answer should identify the specific conceptual moves a top response must make: accurate exposition of dependent origination (pratītyasamutpāda) in the skandha framework; correct characterization of narrative identity's teleological and normative dimensions rather than treating it as purely descriptive psychology; analysis of whether conventional selfhood in Buddhism is merely pragmatic or carries metaphysical weight; direct engagement with the karma/responsibility objection; and a reasoned comparison with reductionist personal-identity theory that goes beyond naming Hume or Parfit. The marking scheme (out of 50) should assign points to each move and explicitly penalize identifiable errors: for instance, conflating anattā with nihilism or absolute non-existence (loss of 5 marks), ignoring the conventional/ultimate distinction entirely (loss of 6 marks), misrepresenting the structure of fission problems in reductionist accounts (loss of 4 marks), treating narrative identity without its authorial normativity (loss of 4 marks), or offering only a generic summary of bundle theory without applying it to the Buddhist framework (loss of 5 marks). The difficulty should come from applying the conceptual framework to the tension between narrative coherence and metaphysical emptiness, not from recalling standard labels.
