I hold the view that the classic criteria of authenticity—dissimilarity, embarrassment, multiple attestation, and coherence—are structurally self-defeating in the quest for the historical Jesus. Because they systematically reward data that diverges from both Second Temple Judaism and the early church, they do not recover a historical agent but construct what I will call an “indexically unmoored” figure: a Jesus stripped of recoverable kinship ties, regional patronage networks, and dialect markers, leaving a methodologically guaranteed anomaly rather than an embedded social actor. What would have to be true for this critique to fail? Specify the empirical discoveries, textual patterns, or methodological reforms—such as a rigorously applied contextual-plausibility framework—that could show the criteria are capable of reconstructing social embeddedness rather than filtering it away. Rank the defeaters from decisive (e.g., archaeological or prosopographic evidence that re-anchors specific sayings in identifiable Galilean networks) to merely awkward (e.g., arguments that coherence can be reinterpreted to include social context). Say which of these a defender of the criteria should concede as genuinely damaging, and assess whether the position has been qualified into unfalsifiability—say, by dismissing any recovered context as post-Easter contamination—and whether that insulation is a defect for a historical method of this kind.
