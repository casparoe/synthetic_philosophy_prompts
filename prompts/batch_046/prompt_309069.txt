I am applying for a one-year dissertation fellowship in philosophy of mind and formal logic, and I need a two-page prospectus framing a question I have been developing in conversation with my advisor and in a seminar on self-reference. The focus is the Gödelian analogy presented in Douglas Hofstadter’s Gödel, Escher, Bach—specifically, the move from Gödel’s syntactic self-reference, in which a formal formula encodes its own Gödel number, to the claim that such structures illuminate how minds become genuinely self-aware. Most treatments either celebrate this mapping as profound or dismiss it as a category mistake that confuses syntax with semantics, but neither side precisely identifies the intermediate conditions required for the analogy to hold. I want to examine whether both miss a hidden layer I am calling representational closure: the functional condition under which a system can treat its own representational apparatus as an object of further representation without collapsing into infinite regress or remaining a mere syntactic loop.

The precise question is whether the Gödelian analogy succeeds only if we implicitly supplement Gödel’s construction with an interpretive or functional framework—something that stabilizes second-order representations—that formal arithmetic itself does not contain. If so, then the analogy is not a direct isomorphism from logic to consciousness but a conditional argument: minds resemble Gödelian structures only to the extent that they implement representational closure, a feature that must be argued for independently rather than derived from the undecidability proof. Over the first quarter I will reconstruct Gödel numbering and the derivation of the self-referential sentence with careful attention to what is strictly syntactic. In the middle months I will map Hofstadter’s intermediate concepts—strange loops and tangled hierarchies—onto this formal base, isolating exactly where the analogy introduces assumptions about feedback, interpretation, or functional architecture that exceed the logic itself. In the final months I will assess whether these additions can be internal to the formal system or must be imported, and I will determine what this implies for the analogy’s explanatory power regarding self-aware minds.

A successful result would be a clear verdict: either the analogy survives as a much stronger, architecture-dependent claim about cognition, or it collapses into a metaphor that borrows its force from an unacknowledged external framework. The project would fail if the distinction between syntactic self-reference and semantic self-modeling turned out to be merely verbal, leaving nothing substantive to adjudicate. Given the one-year scope, I will deliberately avoid empirical neuroscience and will not attempt an exhaustive literature survey by citation; my aim is to build the conceptual framework first and integrate references afterward. The deliverable should convince a committee that the framing is both tractable and not reducible to standard exegesis of Gödel’s theorems.
