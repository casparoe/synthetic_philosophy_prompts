I'm working through concrete cases of perfectly symmetric choice with a colleague in action theory, and we are trying to determine whether a single principle governs when arbitrary selection is rational and when it amounts to a failure of practical reason. Here are my verdicts, given as they come to me. If two pastries at a café are identically appealing, I point to the left one without guilt—this is clearly fine. When I was offered two postdoctoral fellowships that were genuinely indistinguishable in salary, location quality, and research fit, I agonized for months and finally resolved it with a coin flip; I now judge the paralysis irrational, but the coin itself correct. If an autonomous triage algorithm assigns the last available ICU bed to one of two patients with identical prognoses and identical moral claims via a random number generator, that is not merely permissible but required, since fairness demands a tie-breaker when reasons are exhausted. By contrast, when a surgeon delays an operation for days between two surgical techniques that carry exactly the same risk profile and expected outcome, hunting for a hidden distinction that does not exist, that strikes me as a clear failure—reason should not invent differences to avoid deciding. The pair that most troubles me involves commitments to others: I have made identical promises of help to two friends who both need assistance at the same time, and I can only assist one; flipping a coin to decide feels like a moral abdication rather than a necessary resolution, yet I struggle to articulate what distinguishes this from the postdoc case except the presence of another person's claim. Meanwhile, the case I keep returning to is choosing a doctoral advisor: two scholars are equally aligned with my interests, offer identical mentorship quality, and belong to departments of equivalent standing, so that no further reason distinguishes them, yet because this choice will partly constitute my intellectual identity, I feel I ought not to randomize but rather to generate or discover a reason—even if that reason is in some sense manufactured by the choice itself. Is there a principle—perhaps one that distinguishes contexts in which reason is genuinely silent and the will may break ties arbitrarily from contexts in which the nature of the commitment requires the agent to constitute herself through the decision, or in which treating persons as interchangeable violates the logic of the relation—that captures all of these judgments? State it precisely, apply it to each case above plus one I have not considered (an AI system distributing identical, indivisible resources between two claimants with identical entitlement), and tell me honestly which of my verdicts it preserves and which it forces me to overturn, especially whether my intuition that coin-flipping between the two friends is wrong survives or must be abandoned. I want a committed answer, not a survey of options: take a definite position, give the clause of the principle that handles each case, and do not leave the reconciliation of the postdoc and the friendship cases to me.
