Write a short story about Elara, a conservator in the Infinite Repository, an institution that does not collect books but worlds—each volume is a self-contained moral history of a universe with its own population of sentient beings. The Repository's shelves extend through transfinite ordinals, and Elara's duty is not to read every world but to decide, under a strict energy budget, which histories receive restoration and which are allowed to decay into noise. The philosophical tension she faces is not merely quantitative—more suffering versus less—but structural: in some branches, populations are infinite in size, yet arranged in hierarchies of moral proximity (those whose choices she can influence directly versus those separated by irreducible modal distance). Elara has developed her own working principle, which she calls "attenuated transitivity of obligation": she holds that her duty to preserve a world weakens not by simple numerical dilution across infinity, but by a specific kind of relational discontinuity—when a world shares no causal or decision-theoretic pathway with her own, its claims enter her calculus differently, not as zero but as transformed.

The story should center on a single decision: Elara discovers that fully restoring one particular volume—World 7-omega, in which an infinite sequence of generations lives under conditions of gradually diminishing but never-ending suffering—would require degrading exactly one fragment from every other volume in a specific uncountable subset. She must determine whether the obligation to eliminate that particular pattern of infinite harm overrides the distributed loss, and whether her principle of attenuated transitivity allows her to treat the suffering in 7-omega as more urgent than the collective erosion elsewhere, or whether the infinity of affected other worlds collapses the distinction entirely. Do not treat this as a simple arithmetic puzzle; instead, show Elara reasoning through analogies, confronting the possibility that her principle was invented to avoid paralysis rather than discovered, and reaching a conclusion that feels philosophically unsettled rather than neatly resolved. The narrative should make the abstract problem of comparing infinite moral populations feel concrete—the smell of decaying paper, the weight of a single restored page—without resolving it into a moral formula.
