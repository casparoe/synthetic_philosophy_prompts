I am trying to work out my own view on whether death is ever a harm to the one who dies, taking the Epicurean symmetry argument seriously but finding my intuitions resistant in particular configurations. Here are my verdicts on concrete scenarios; I want to know whether they rest on a single principle, and if not, which ones I should relinquish.

(i) A philosopher dies at 92, having completed her life’s work, with no remaining ambitions she cares about and no suffering. Verdict: her death is not a harm to her.

(ii) A researcher dies suddenly at 30, the day before she is to present findings she spent a decade developing; she had every reason to expect a flourishing career and the satisfaction of discovery. Verdict: her death is a serious harm.

(iii) Same as (ii), except that after her death her colleagues reconstruct her results from her notes and present them exactly as she intended, achieving the recognition she sought. Verdict: the harm persists but is somewhat reduced—she is still deprived of living through the achievement, even though the achievement itself occurs.

(iv) A fetus dies in utero at 26 weeks, never having achieved consciousness. Verdict: not a harm; there is no subject with interests to be deprived.

(v) A 45-year-old dies instantly, is cryogenically preserved, and revived 400 years later with her psychology intact, living another 40 years. Verdict: the 400-year intermission is not bad for her during the dead period itself, but her life is biographically fractured—worse, all else equal, than an uninterrupted life of equal total length, though not because she experienced anything during the gap.

(vi) Comparing two worlds—one in which a particular person lives 70 years and dies, another in which she is never conceived—I judge the first to contain a harm that the second lacks. Verdict: death is bad for her, despite the fact that she does not exist to suffer it, and despite (iv).

(vii) A 50-year-old with a sustained, clear, and rational wish to die is killed accidentally one day before he intended to end his own life. Verdict: his death is not clearly a harm; relative to his own settled interests, it is at worst neutral.

The tension I feel most acutely is between (iii) and (vi): if harm can be mitigated by events occurring after death that the deceased never experiences, then harm seems to depend on something other than the deprivation of experienced goods; yet (vi) insists that death harms a subject who, ex hypothesi, no longer exists. Meanwhile (v) suggests a form of biographical or narrative harm that does not require consciousness during the harm, while (iv) denies harm precisely where there was never any consciousness at all, and (vii) makes the harm depend on the content of the subject’s prior interests rather than on objective goods alone.

Is there a coherent principle—perhaps combining an interest-based deprivation condition with a prior-subjecthood requirement and a separate clause for authorial or narrative unity—that accommodates (i) through (vii) without ad hoc adjustments? Or must I drop one of them? Please state the principle precisely, identify the clause that handles each case, and be explicit about which verdict it forces me to abandon if none can be saved. Also tell me what your principle predicts for this further case: a musician dies leaving an unfinished symphony, which is later completed by an AI trained on her drafts with such fidelity that critics treat it as genuinely hers—does this mitigate the harm the way human posthumous completion does in (iii), or does the absence of human agency in the completion make the harm closer to (ii)?
