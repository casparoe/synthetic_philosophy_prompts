I have been working through F. H. Bradley's Appearance and Reality in a metaphysics seminar, and I find myself convinced that his famous regress argument is best understood not as a general ontological complaint about relations, but specifically as a dilemma concerning the unity of the proposition. When we assert that Socrates is wise, it seems we must combine Socrates, wisdom, and some copulative element; yet if that copula is itself a relation, it apparently requires a further relation to connect it with the other terms, and so on ad infinitum, leaving us either with an infinite hierarchy or with Bradley's undifferentiated Absolute in which all distinctions collapse. The standard analytic response, as I understand it, is to treat the relational constituent as primitive or to analyse the proposition as a structured complex in which the relation genuinely relates its terms without external glue; but to me this looks like relocating the problem rather than resolving it, since we still need an account of what makes the structure one unified thought rather than a mere list.

Recently, however, I have encountered adverbialist theories of predication—the view that properties are not entities standing in relations to subjects but rather modifications of how a subject exists, so that 'Socrates is wise' describes Socrates existing wisely rather than Socrates being related to wisdom. It strikes me that if we extend this strategy to Bradley's case, we might eliminate the relational constituent altogether: there would be no copula requiring binding, and hence no regress of binders. Does this not mean that adverbialism dissolves Bradley's regress at its root, rather than merely pushing it back one level? Or does the difficulty re-emerge in explaining how a single subject can bear multiple adverbial modifications—say, being wise and being seated—without those modifications themselves entering into relations that reproduce the original problem? I would be grateful for a response in British English that addresses whether I am misplacing Bradley's original concern, and that examines whether the adverbialist move genuinely avoids the regress or quietly smuggles back in the very relational structure it claims to eliminate.
