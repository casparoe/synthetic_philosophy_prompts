Having spent three decades in condensed-matter physics and only recently turned to philosophy of mind, I am struck not by consciousness itself but by the peculiar resistance we feel when physical explanations are offered for it. The meta-problem asks why complete physical accounts still seem to leave an explanatory gap. One prominent response holds that phenomenal concepts are irreducibly demonstrative or indexical—functioning somewhat like "here" or "now" in physical theory—so that third-person descriptions necessarily miss the first-person mode of presentation without implying any non-physical ontology. The rival view treats this felt gap as a persistent cognitive bias, analogous to vitalist intuition in nineteenth-century biology: our introspective apparatus was never engineered to reveal neural mechanisms, and with sufficient conceptual revision the resistance should dissolve.

Write an essay examining whether treating phenomenal concepts as indexical demonstratives genuinely resolves the meta-problem or merely relocates it. Weigh the argument that if phenomenal reference operates like spatial or temporal indexicals—which do not require non-spatial or non-temporal entities—then the hard problem is a problem about conceptual mapping rather than about nature. Then consider the objection that this account fails to explain why consciousness alone triggers this particular form of explanatory resistance, whereas other indexical domains in science do not generate comparable ontological anxiety. Reach and defend a specific conclusion: does the indexical-demonstrative framework dissolve the meta-problem, expose a genuine but non-mysterious feature of our conceptual scheme, or ultimately fail to account for the uniqueness of conscious experience?
