Consider Philippa Foot's formulation of the doctrine of double effect, according to which an action producing both good and harmful outcomes is permissible only if the harm is not intended either as an end or as a means, if the good effect is proportionate, and if the agent does not will the harm. A student was asked whether this doctrine applies to an autonomous rail-control system that selects track switches by computing expected utility across all outcomes, treating each potential fatality as a weighted term in its objective function. Specifically: if the system avoids a probable multi-casualty collision by switching to a track that will certainly kill one maintenance worker, and if the worker's death is explicitly represented in the computation that justifies the switch, does this constitute intending the death as a means?

The student submitted the following answer.

"Foot requires that the bad effect not be causally necessary for the good effect to occur; since the system's saving of the bus passengers is achieved precisely through the computation that includes the worker's death, the harm is causally embedded in the means and therefore intended. In standard trolley cases, by contrast, the agent's bodily movement diverts the tram without the victim's death being part of the reasoning process. Because artificial systems lack practical reason in Foot's Aristotelian sense, they cannot form the kind of direct intention she analyzes, so the doctrine is inapplicable and we should evaluate them only by expected utility. Moreover, Foot's 1967 discussion was developed specifically for abortion ethics, which means extending it to algorithmic decision-making is an unwarranted analogical leap."

Do not state your own position on whether the system actually violates double effect; assess only the reasoning presented.
