I have been working out a private model for how self-awareness operates, which I think of as a kind of intermittent verification: rather than being a steady background state, existence has to be actively confirmed in the moment, like a light that only reveals what is there when it flashes. Reading about Descartes, I recognized the same pattern in his argument that because he is thinking, he must exist—he seems to catch himself in the very act and thereby prove he is present at that instant. But this leaves me with a problem I cannot solve on my own. If the proof only holds during the act of thinking, then during deep sleep or under anesthesia, when conscious thought is absent, would the self not temporarily cease to exist, only to be recreated upon waking? And if that is so, is the person who wakes up genuinely the same individual, or merely a new instance carrying over old memories? I have read that modern neuroscience finds the brain never fully shuts down—there is always subconscious processing—so perhaps there is never a true gap in thinking, only a lowering of the light. Would this mean that Descartes's argument, if updated with what we now know about unconscious neural activity, actually establishes continuous existence rather than a flickering one? Or have I mistaken what his reasoning was meant to prove?
