I'm running a 75-minute graduate seminar in moral psychology with about fifteen students. We've already worked through the standard WEIRD literature and Haidt's moral foundations framework, but in our last session several participants kept framing cross-cultural moral disagreement as essentially a calibration problem—different weights placed on shared dimensions like care versus loyalty, harm versus purity. I want them to feel, through direct deadlock rather than lecture, why that framing can miss the structure of the disagreement entirely: in certain relational moral ontologies, the primary harm is not an individual injury amenable to compensation but a rupture in the reciprocal obligations that constitute the collective moral patient itself. Design a structured in-class deliberation game with assigned roles, hidden symbolic payoff cards, and a unanimous-decision rule, in which the group must agree on restitution for a concrete broken agreement over shared water or land use. The scenario should be specific enough that participants can inhabit their roles without specialized ethnographic knowledge. The role cards should represent distinct moral ontologies—one framing the issue through individual rights and distributive compensation, others framing it through relational integrity, ancestral obligation, or collective stewardship—such that the "obvious" WEIRD solution (monetary or individual restitution) is visible and attractive to some roles but actively deepens the harm for others, producing an irreconcilable deadlock. Include the rules, materials, the hidden payoff conditions tied to each ontology's satisfaction criteria, the debrief that maps specific moments of negotiation failure onto the conceptual distinction between weight disagreement and ontology disagreement—particularly disagreement over what counts as a moral patient and what counts as genuine restoration—and be explicit about which feature of actual cross-cultural moral life this simulation necessarily flattens and must be supplied by the facilitator. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, especially the risk that students collapse relational roles into simplistic "collectivist" stereotypes rather than engaging the ontological difference.
