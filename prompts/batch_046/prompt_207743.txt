I want to defend a specific, counterintuitive position: large-scale habitat destruction is not unambiguously harmful from a welfare perspective, and under certain ecological conditions it may reduce net wild-animal suffering. Juvenile mortality, predation, starvation, parasitism, and density-dependent resource competition produce enormous suffering among individuals who never reach reproductive age, while only a minority experience lives that are net-positive. Conservation practices that preserve or expand such habitats therefore risk functioning as suffering-preservation mechanisms: they maintain the structural conditions that continuously regenerate large populations of short-lived, negatively affected organisms. By contrast, a sustained reduction in carrying capacity—whether through habitat fragmentation, climate-driven range collapse, or direct destruction—might permanently lower the total number of suffering individuals, even though it destroys biodiversity, ecological function, and evolutionary potential.
