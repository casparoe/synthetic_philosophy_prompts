Consider the track record of my university's historical-naming committee. Its precedents: we retained Jefferson despite slaveholding, treating it as normative for Virginia planter elites and separating political authorship from private conduct; we retained Churchill despite imperial policies, treating them as standard British statecraft of his era and privileging wartime leadership; we removed Columbus, ruling that initiating enslavement exceeded even conquistador norms and that contemporaneous critics made the wrong recognizable at the time; we retained a nineteenth-century surgeon-pioneer despite routine non-consensual experiments, holding that informed consent was not then a medical concept and that life-saving achievements stand independently.

We now face a proposal to rename the hall honoring an eighteenth-century naturalist who inherited enslaved people, funded botanical expeditions that relied on coerced labor, and yet in his later writings publicly denounced the slave trade—without ever manumitting those he held. Some members invoke Columbus: the wrong was recognizable, he profited from it, and dissent was present. Others invoke Jefferson or the surgeon: the practice was normative for his class and profession, and his scientific legacy is separable. A third invokes Churchill: exceptional contribution excuses conduct that was standard for the era.

Please recommend whether we should rename, consistent with our own precedents. Identify the closest analog among the four, lay out the competing principles that fit all four rulings equally well, say what each predicts for this naturalist, and specify the further case—perhaps a figure whose harmful acts exceeded norms in a domain where no contemporaneous dissent existed—that would best distinguish the principles.
