Write a sestina—six stanzas of six lines each, followed by the standard three-line envoi—about Zorn’s lemma and the philosophical unease that accompanies the Axiom of Choice. The six end-words of the first stanza, in order, must be: chain, bound, choice, element, order, existence. In stanzas two through six, rotate these end-words according to the standard sestina sequence (6-1-5-2-4-3, then 3-6-4-1-2-5, and so on cyclically), so that the poem’s recursive architecture literally enacts the lemma’s movement through ordered chains toward an upper limit. Use each word in both its mathematical and ethical or metaphysical register—so that “choice” evokes both the axiom and human agency, “bound” both an upper bound and a moral constraint, “element” both a set-member and a constituent of judgment—without letting either register dominate or resolve into either constructivist rejection or naive Platonism. The poem must be technically accurate: a mathematician should recognize that every chain has an upper bound, that maximality is guaranteed without being constructively exhibited, and that this guarantee depends on choice; a philosopher of mathematics should see the analogy between this non-constructive existence claim and decision-making under incomplete information rendered rather than merely named. The envoi must contain all six words arranged in the standard closure (line-ends 6, 1, 5; the remaining words inside the lines), preserving the original sequence across the three lines, and should leave the reader with the lemma’s precise logical tension: a maximal element necessarily exists, yet no procedure within the system identifies it. Do not cite mathematicians, texts, or theorems by proper name; do not include a bibliography or reading list; and do not resolve the tension with either a sermon on rational order or a shrug about arbitrariness. Keep the poem to thirty-nine lines total.
