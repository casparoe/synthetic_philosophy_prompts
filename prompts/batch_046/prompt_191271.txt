I have spent thirty years measuring signal decay in atmospheric physics, and since retiring I have been trying to understand why anyone owes obedience to a state they never explicitly joined. I am building a conceptual map—not a historical survey—of the live positions on political obligation and civil disobedience, but I want it rendered as a diagnostic decision tree rather than an alphabetical list of theorists. The root question should be whether a state's claim to your obedience survives your explicit rejection of it; from there, the first branch should separate views that require an actual act of consent from those that generate obligation through receipt of benefits, tacit acceptance, or mere membership in a cooperative scheme.

Continue branching through the combinations that genuinely separate positions in practice: whether a failed consent argument dissolves all duty or merely weakens it to a defeasible presumption; whether civil disobedience is permissible only against substantively unjust laws, or whether purely procedural violations—breaking a law to protest the process by which it was enacted—can themselves be legitimate. Each leaf should carry the name of the position if one is standard (strict social contract, hypothetical consent, associative obligation, fair-play, natural duty, philosophical anarchism, and any others that belong), plus a single sentence describing its most awkward practical commitment—the specific concession its defenders must make that sounds worst to common sense. Where the logical space contains combinations that no philosopher occupies—say, a view that denies all obligation yet permits disobedience only for procedural errors—mark the node as empty and briefly diagnose whether the gap is due to incoherence, unattractiveness, or simple neglect. If two named positions yield identical verdicts on every concrete case and differ only in metaphysical vocabulary about the "source" versus "nature" of duty, flag them as indistinguishable at this resolution rather than forcing a false split.

Please do not supply bibliographies, citation lists, or author-date references; I am working without library access and want to see whether the architecture can be reconstructed from conceptual first principles. Likewise, avoid relying on highly niche formalisms—no game-theoretic equilibrium proofs, no recent jurisprudential statutes—so that the taxonomy remains legible to a reader whose formal training is in empirical science rather than normative philosophy.
