Below are working notes for an essay I am drafting on the possibility of free-floating rationales—reasons or normative weights that exist independently of any reasoning agent. I distinguish three strata: causal-functional explanations that cite why a trait or strategy persists; instrumental rationales that specify means-end relations relative to a fixed goal structure; and normative rationales that claim to justify beliefs or actions irrespective of who holds them. My central claim is that instrumental and normative rationales can be genuinely free-floating, instantiated by structural relations—such as an optimization landscape or a logical entailment—without being grasped by a mind. To block the objection that this merely projects mental vocabulary onto causal patterns, I propose a counterfactual test: a rationale counts as free-floating only if altering the underlying goal or evidential structure would shift the rationale in a way that supports counterfactual reasoning, without requiring anchoring to any particular agent's beliefs. I also reject the fallback position that all rationales are implicitly indexed to a hypothetical rational agent, on the grounds that this reintroduces the reasoner through a modal back door rather than explaining the rationale's independence. I am particularly concerned with whether this framework conflates explanatory success with normative authority, and whether the counterfactual condition genuinely separates structural relations from agent-relative indexing. Would you comment on the coherence of the distinction between free-floating instrumental and normative rationales, on whether the counterfactual test succeeds in blocking the projection objection, and on whether there remains a hidden dependence on a prior specification of value or goal that undermines the claim that these rationales can exist without reasoners?
