I've been working through the problem of verisimilitude on my own—whether a false scientific theory can genuinely be closer to the truth than another false one—and I've found myself pulled between two positions that seem incompatible. One view, which I'll call the error-structure position, holds that we can measure truth-likeness without knowing the full correct theory: we examine whether a theory's predictive failures remain localized and stable when we perturb its auxiliary assumptions, taking that stability as evidence that its core mechanisms track real patterns even if its ontology is wrong. The opposing view, the reference-benchmark position, insists that this just measures empirical adequacy in fancy dress; without comparing our theory to an independently known correct model—which scientists never possess—"closer to the truth" has no non-circular meaning, and all we can ever say is that one theory predicts better.

Please draft the joint statement an adversarial collaboration between these two camps would produce. Structure it as: (1) a numbered list of claims both sides can sign, covering what verisimilitude was originally meant to capture, why purely logical-distance measures fail, and what scientists actually observe when theories break down; (2) the exact disagreements, each formulated in wording that the camp it represents accepts and the other does not reject as a mischaracterization; (3) the cruxes—specifically whether failure-stability under intervention genuinely distinguishes approximate truth from mere predictive success, and whether the absence of a reference theory makes verisimilitude vacuous rather than merely hard to apply; and (4) for each crux, one concrete historical case both sides agree is relevant—such as the transition from Newtonian mechanics to general relativity, or the predictive failures of phlogiston theory compared to Lavoisier's framework—together with each camp's explicit, advance prediction about what that case shows.

Don't present this as a menu of options: commit to which disagreements are substantive rather than verbal, identify the cruxes precisely, and state each side's prediction clearly. I want a document formal enough that either camp could endorse the agreed-claims section without embarrassment, with the residual conflict laid out exactly and no hedging about where you think the weight of argument falls.
