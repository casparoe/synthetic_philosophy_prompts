In a philosophy of practical reason reading group, our discussion of Glaucon's challenge and the ring of Gyges has stalled in a specific way. One camp holds that the ring strips away only social observation, leaving a pure case of instrumental rationality: if justice has no payoff when unobserved, then justice is not rationally required, and the thought experiment demonstrates that moral obligations are merely conventional scaffolding. The other camp responds that invisibility does not eliminate self-observation or the structural demands of practical identity—an unjust agent, even when hidden, lives with a divided will, and this internal fragmentation is itself a failure of rational agency rather than merely a contingent emotional cost. For months, neither side has moved. Everyone accepts the narrative framing from the Republic, everyone concedes that external sanctions vanish, and everyone grants that psychological self-punishment can occur. Yet the first camp treats internal fragmentation as a suppressible psychological fact that a sufficiently disciplined agent could eliminate, while the second camp treats it as a necessary feature of rational agency that no discipline can remove.

My working hypothesis is that the disagreement is not primarily factual or normative in a direct sense, but concerns the architecture of practical reason—specifically, whether rationality is unified under a single scale of optimization or involves irreducible plural commitments that resist trade-off. Is this diagnosis correct, or is the stall better explained as a verbal dispute over the word rational, or as two groups answering different questions—one about instrumental efficiency under opacity, one about the conditions for unified practical identity—under the single heading why be moral? Please support your diagnosis by engaging concretely with what each side actually claims about the ring, about invisibility, and about internal division, rather than surveying general theories of moral motivation. Then tell me what kind of move—whether a formal result in decision theory, a conceptual distinction separating instrumental from structural rationality, an empirical claim about self-deception under conditions of perfect anonymity, or an argument that the question is ill-posed—would actually count as progress given your diagnosis, or whether no progress should be expected and why.
