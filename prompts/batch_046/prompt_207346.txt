I've been turning over Avicenna's flying-man thought experiment ever since I heard it described in a podcast, and I got into a long back-and-forth with two friends who are not academics—just people who like thinking things through. I'd like an outside view that walks through our exchange step by step.

The setup is the classic one: imagine a person created fully formed and suspended in empty space, with all senses blocked and no prior experience. Even then, Avicenna claims, that person would still know that they exist.

My friend Maya took this as proof that the self is a non-physical soul. She argued that since you would have zero awareness of your body—no feeling of limbs, no heartbeat, nothing—your awareness of yourself cannot be coming from the body. If the self were bodily, she said, then with the body completely absent from consciousness there should be no self; yet there is one, so they must be separate things.

Leo pushed back. He said the experiment only shows you can have a bare sense of existence without sensory input, but that could still be your brain running in isolation—like a computer processing without peripherals. He added that being unaware of your body does not mean you lack one; right now he does not feel his spleen, but that does not make him spleen-less.

Maya answered that there is a big gap between not noticing one organ and not noticing any body at all. In the thought experiment, she said, bodily content is totally missing from awareness, not merely overlooked. If self and body were identical, then the total absence of body from awareness should mean the absence of self—yet self remains. So identity fails.

Leo replied that identity does not require constant awareness. He compared it to a seed that does not display tree-ness but is still a seed. The self might be bodily but in a dormant or hidden state where its physical nature simply is not manifest to consciousness.

Maya then asked what explains the flying person's ability to think and doubt. A seed does not think, she pointed out. If thinking occurs with no bodily mechanism in view, that suggests a thinking substance that does not need matter.

Leo countered that thinking might depend on matter even when matter is not experienced. A computer calculates without sensing its circuits, so thought without bodily sensation only proves independence from sensation, not independence from physical substrate.

They kept circling, and I honestly could not tell who was landing the stronger blows. Can you adjudicate their exchange move by move? For each of the four rounds I described, rate the argument from clearly mistaken to clearly right and briefly justify the rating. Then tell me who is more right overall, and say whether there is something important both of them are missing—perhaps about what kind of knowledge "I exist" actually is, or about whether the thought experiment quietly smuggles in assumptions it claims to have stripped away.
