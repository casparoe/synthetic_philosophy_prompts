I have no formal background in history or philosophy, but after reading several popular accounts of the Cold War's origins I find myself caught on a conceptual problem that seems to sit between ordinary reasoning about blame and the way historians actually talk about the period. The standard debate asks whether Stalin's security demands, Truman's strategic choices, or the structural logic of bipolarity should be named as the origin of the conflict, with orthodox, revisionist, and post-revisionist accounts offering different answers over time. Yet the more I consider it, the more I suspect that the very question "who started it?" conceals a hidden logical premise—namely, that assigning blame requires what I have come to call counterfactual exclusivity: the idea that one party's actions must constitute a singular sufficient trigger such that, had they been removed, the adversarial system would not have crystallized at all. From that perspective, the post-revisionist emphasis on shared responsibility and structural instability does not merely divide blame among agents; it appears to undermine the conditions that would allow the original question to have a coherent answer, somewhat like asking which leg of a table is the true cause of its height.

If that reasoning holds, then asking "who started the Cold War" might be not just empirically disputed but conceptually malformed—an attempt to apply a category meant for discrete individual actions to an emergent interaction between state systems. If blame genuinely requires the kind of singular causal sufficiency I have described, then no distributed account can preserve the question; we ought to replace origin-language with something like a weighted causal contribution map, or perhaps abandon blame-talk entirely in favor of structural vulnerability metrics. But I am uncertain whether I have discovered a real constraint on historical attribution or simply imported an inappropriate model of causation—perhaps drawn from chemistry or legal liability—into diplomatic history, where collective and incremental initiation might be perfectly coherent. Could it be that historians are not disagreeing about a single fact of origin but implicitly using different thresholds of contribution, so that the debate is really about how much weight to assign rather than about who crossed a decisive line? I would appreciate a clarification that addresses whether my notion of counterfactual exclusivity is a legitimate philosophical constraint, or merely a confusion that treats a complex systemic conflict as if it were a reaction with one necessary reagent.
