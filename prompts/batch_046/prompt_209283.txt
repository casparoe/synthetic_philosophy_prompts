You are a professional philosopher with expertise in moral psychology, the ethics of blame, and institutional morality. Before answering, take a moment to brainstorm the structural conditions—such as role-based authority, direct victimhood, shared commitment, and moral consistency—that philosophers have argued either confer or undermine standing to blame. Then construct a thought experiment that meets the specifications below. Be precise, avoid science-fiction or trolley-style scenarios, and do not invent bibliographic references.

Task: Design a realistic, one-paragraph thought experiment set in an ordinary workplace or community institution (not a hospital triage or runaway train). The scenario must isolate the relationship between hypocrisy and standing by producing the following divergence:

1. Blamer A has recently committed the very same wrong they are blaming another for, shows no sign of reform, and is therefore clearly hypocritical. Nevertheless, Blamer A genuinely possesses standing to blame the wrongdoer.
2. Blamer B has never committed that wrong, lives up to the relevant moral standard impeccably, and is therefore not hypocritical at all. Nevertheless, Blamer B genuinely lacks standing to blame the wrongdoer.
3. The difference must not come from Blamer A having adopted an obviously defective rule, from sociopathy, or from Blamer B being an outsider in a trivial sense. Instead, it should fall out of a structural feature (e.g., an institutional role, a direct stakeholder relationship, or a jointly undertaken commitment) that is genuinely defensible as relevant to standing.

After presenting the scenario in a single paragraph, provide the analysis:
- State the belief or judgment at issue, the method by which each blamer arrives at it, and the relevant nearby worlds in which the structural feature is present or absent.
- Show what the moral-consistency view of standing (that hypocrisy undermines standing) says about Blamer A and Blamer B, and why.
- Show what the structural-stakeholder view says about each, and why.
- Identify exactly which feature of the case is doing the philosophical work.
- Explain what happens if that feature is removed: does the case collapse into a standard hypocrisy example, or does something else survive?
- Finally, present the strongest objection a defender of the moral-consistency view could raise against your scenario—specifically, an attack that denies that the structural feature actually confers moral standing rather than merely procedural authority.

In your response, say how confident you are in each of the major claims you make about the theories, and single out the one point about which you are least sure—whether it concerns the coherence of the structural-stakeholder view, the plausibility of Blamer A's retained standing, or something else.
