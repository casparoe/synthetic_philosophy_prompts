Write the closing address (approximately fifteen minutes) for the retirement symposium honoring Dr. Elena Voss, a historian of institutional change. Half the audience are scholars familiar with her argument that large-scale outcomes depend not on continuous chaotic divergence but on “brittle points”—brief windows in which small perturbations become irreversibly locked in through feedback loops—and with her corresponding distinction between path-dependent lock-in and sensitive dependence. The other half are family and non-academic friends who know her only as someone who always rejected “great man” and “for want of a nail” explanations. The idea of contingency must do structural work, not decorative work: the address should honor her career without telling a neat origin myth about it. Specifically, explain why her own framework makes it inappropriate to claim that one missed train, one chance introduction, or one early failure caused her success—that is, why narrative contingency must be separated from causal fragility. Use that refusal as the pivot: if we cannot celebrate her with a tidy causal story, what does celebration look like for a body of work that systematically undermines the origin myths retirement speeches usually rely on? The speech should be accessible to the family half without losing the scholars, and it should not include bibliographies, citations, or reading lists.
