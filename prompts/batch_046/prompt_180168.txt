I have been working through the boundary between rational self-confidence and self-defeating epistemic hubris, particularly in light of the Löbian obstacle—the result in modal logic that a formal system strong enough to represent arithmetic cannot consistently maintain that provability implies truth without collapsing into universal provability. I am trying to see whether my intuitive judgments about particular reasoning agents line up behind a single principle, or whether they are just a collection of post-hoc adjustments.

Here are my verdicts. First, a student who accepts first-order arithmetic and also adopts a restricted reflection principle—'for any arithmetic claim, if my reasoning delivers it, then it is true'—strikes me as entirely rational, since the system is too weak for the obstacle to arise. Second, a philosopher working in a very strong logical framework—say, with full second-order comprehension—who adopts an unrestricted version of the same principle for every claim in her system: this seems clearly irrational, almost like an open invitation to contradiction. Third, an artificial agent with a high-strength base reasoner and a separate meta-monitor that trusts the base reasoner on empirical predictions but explicitly withholds trust on claims about the agent's own consistency: this feels rationally permissible, though I am unsure whether the restriction is genuinely principled or merely ad hoc. Fourth, a mathematician who trusts her own set-theoretic proofs only after they have been independently verified by colleagues: this seems unproblematic, but I wonder whether it still counts as self-trust in any interesting sense. Fifth, an agent that applies a reflection rule only to its past beliefs—'everything I believed yesterday was true'—while remaining agnostic about its present outputs: I keep oscillating between seeing this as a clever structural evasion and seeing it as intellectually dishonest special pleading. Sixth, a young child who implicitly trusts all her beliefs without any formal apparatus: this does not seem rationally defective, but only because she lacks the logical resources for the obstacle to apply.

Is there a coherent principle—perhaps involving the logical strength of the system, the scope of the reflection claim, or a distinction between object-level and meta-level belief—that delivers all six verdicts? If not, which of my judgments should I abandon, and why? Please state the principle precisely, show which clause is motivated by which case, and say honestly which intuition it cannot accommodate. Also, apply it to a case I have not listed: an agent that can actually prove its own consistency, yet deliberately refuses to adopt any reflection principle about its future beliefs. Does the principle treat this refusal as rationally required, rationally permitted, or irrational?
