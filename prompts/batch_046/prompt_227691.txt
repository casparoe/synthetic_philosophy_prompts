For my philosophy of history seminar, design a taxonomy—structured as a decision tree—that maps positions on whether Germany should be held singularly responsible for the outbreak of the First World War, using the Fischer controversy as the central historical reference point. Begin with the diagnostic question: Can a nation-state, as distinct from its individual officeholders, be a proper subject of moral blame? From there, branch through three further nodes: (a) whether the Schlieffen Plan evidences premeditated aggressive intent or merely defensive contingency planning; (b) whether the alliance structure and mobilization timetables diffuse causal and moral responsibility across the Entente and Central Powers alike; and (c) whether a population's retrospective acceptance of war aims can retroactively constitute collective consent that grounds blame. The tree should yield four leaves: an intentionalist view assigning singular blame to German state leadership; a structural-diffusion view distributing blame across the European system; an eliminativist view denying that nations are blame-bearing subjects; and a hybrid position I want included—call it bureaucratic path-dependence blame, which holds that responsibility attaches not to national will or to individuals alone, but to the institutional momentum generated by military planning bureaucracies. Phrase every node as a concrete evaluative claim about the 1914 crisis that a student could answer without prior knowledge of the label it produces. Finally, identify any two leaves that remain indistinguishable on this tree and explain why the distinction collapses at that branch point.
