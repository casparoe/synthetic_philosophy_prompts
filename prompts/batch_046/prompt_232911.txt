Exam Question (30 points): In Principia Ethica, Moore argues that personal affection and the appreciation of beautiful objects are among the greatest intrinsic goods, and he suggests that their co-occurrence can constitute a particularly valuable state. Explain Moore’s principle of organic unities and apply it to the following scenario: two people share a deep friendship, and together they spend an afternoon studying a single painting, each experiencing genuine aesthetic appreciation of the work and feeling that the shared attention deepens their mutual affection. Explain why, on Moore’s view, the value of this entire state of affairs is not equal to (i) the value of Friend A’s pleasure, plus (ii) the value of Friend B’s pleasure, plus (iii) the value of the painting’s beauty, plus (iv) the value of the friendship considered separately from the aesthetic experience. In your explanation, distinguish Moore’s position both from a hedonistic-utilitarian aggregation of pleasures and from the claim that shared aesthetic experience is valuable primarily because it serves as an instrument for moral improvement or character development. Finally, address this variation: suppose Friend A appreciates the painting only because it reminds her of Friend B, while Friend B appreciates it purely for its formal qualities, and neither feels the shared attention deepening the friendship. Would Moore regard this second case as an organic unity of the same kind? Why or why not?

Student Answer:
Moore’s organic unity principle says that the value of a whole is never just the parts summed up; when parts combine, they can produce a new value that is different from any total of the separate values. Applying this to the scenario, we have four separate goods: Friend A’s pleasure in the painting, Friend B’s pleasure in the painting, the intrinsic beauty of the painting itself, and the friendship between them. In the first case, all four are present, but because the friends are appreciating the painting together and feeling closer, these parts form a unified whole. The value of this whole equals the value of all four parts added together, plus an additional relational value that comes from their being experienced as one state rather than four separate ones.

Moore holds that friendship and aesthetic appreciation are both intrinsic goods—good in themselves, not because they produce anything further. This distinguishes his view from utilitarianism, which only looks at the total pleasure produced and misses the extra relational value that Moore’s principle captures. It also distinguishes his view from the idea that shared art appreciation is valuable mainly because it builds moral character, since Moore denies that intrinsic goods need any instrumental justification.

In the second variation, Friend A only values the painting because it reminds her of Friend B. That means her appreciation is not an intrinsic aesthetic good but an instrumental one—she values the painting only as a means to thinking about her friend. Because one of the four parts has lost its intrinsic status, the conditions for forming the specific organic unity are broken. Friend B still has intrinsic appreciation, and the friendship may still exist, but the combination can no longer be the special unified state described in the first case. So Moore would deny that the second case is the same kind of organic unity; it may have some value, but it lacks the particular added value that comes from two intrinsic goods—friendship and shared aesthetic appreciation—being genuinely unified.
