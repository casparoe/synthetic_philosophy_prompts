I serve on the Allocation Reform Subcommittee of the National Council for Research Integrity, and we are presently reviewing a pilot programme—drafted by an internal working group—that proposes replacing conventional peer review for all conceptual-research grants in philosophy, theoretical physics, and moral psychology with a conditional prediction-market mechanism. Rather than submitting proposals to expert panels, applicants would issue tradable contracts tied to three formally specified milestones: whether the research yields a testable framework within thirty-six months, whether an independent arbitration board certifies successful cross-disciplinary uptake, and whether the core argument survives structured replication attempts. Funding release would be governed automatically by market-implied probabilities, yet the scheme introduces two unconventional safeguards: a 'dissent subsidy' that taxes winning contracts to finance opposing positions, and an 'epistemic humility quota' reserving twenty per cent of the pool for proposals carrying low aggregate confidence but high variance, specifically to counter convergence on safe, easily quantifiable topics. Half the subcommittee maintains that this eliminates reviewer partiality and forces explicit, falsifiable commitments; the other half objects that it commodifies scholarly judgement, privileges measurable outcomes over genuine conceptual depth, and treats philosophical progress as if it were a priced commodity. I need the subcommittee's formal decision memo—approve the pilot, reject it, or approve it with binding conditions—setting out reasoning the working group could actually implement, together with an honest statement of the strongest consideration on the losing side. Please present the memo as exactly five numbered points, each consisting of one or two sentences, with no introductory or concluding text.
