In a seminar handout I received for a philosophy of mind course, the Malcolm-Dennett debate on dreaming is summarized as follows: "Malcolm maintains that no one can literally be having an experience while asleep, because the concept of experience entails the capacity for immediate report, and sleep removes that capacity. Dennett responds by conceding that dreams are not experiences in Malcolm's strict sense, but argues that they are still real mental events—specifically, they are 'cassette tapes' loaded during sleep and played back upon waking, which explains why dream narratives feel continuous even though they are assembled retrospectively. Thus both philosophers reject the Cartesian theater model of dreams as inner movies, but Malcolm reaches this conclusion through a conceptual analysis of the word 'experience,' whereas Dennett reaches it through an empirical model of memory construction."

The framing strikes me as suspiciously tidy, particularly the claim that Dennett concedes Malcolm's point about experience rather than attacking its premises. Could you audit this summary? Please distinguish what is textually faithful to Malcolm's argument about the criteria for applying psychological concepts during sleep and to Dennett's cassette and multiple-drafts accounts, what is a standard classroom simplification, and what subtly misrepresents either thinker—especially regarding whether Dennett accepts Malcolm's verificationist criterion, whether the cassette model was offered as an empirical hypothesis or merely as a conceptual possibility, and whether both reject the same version of the Cartesian theater. I am also inclined to think the summary hides a deeper disagreement about whether mental-state ascription requires present-tense accessibility; does that divergence survive the audit, or is the summary's "conceptual versus empirical" framing carrying the weight?
