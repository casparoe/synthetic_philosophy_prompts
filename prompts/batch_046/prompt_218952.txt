I'm planning a single session for a technically literate reading group — mostly software engineers with some rationalist-blog exposure but no formal philosophy background — on whether an "I" can be distributed across brains through Hofstadter's strange-loop framework. Rather than treating persistence as copy-paste duplication, I want to frame the concept as what I call loop complementarity: two brains holding incompatible but complementary fragments of the same high-level self-pattern, such that neither substrate alone closes the loop, yet the pattern approximates persistence across both. Plan the explanation rather than giving it. What has to come first — the substrate-pattern distinction, the self-referential loop structure, or the failure of simple spatial location — and what depends on what? Choose one carrying example that genuinely spans multiple minds, lacks identical replication, and still registers as persistence rather than influence or memory; specify what properties it must have and lack, and explicitly reject the Ship of Theseus and brain-upload scenarios as carriers for this audience and say why. Then map the predictable misconceptions: they will likely reduce the idea to ordinary empathy or to dualist soul-migration, and they may assume distribution means dilution to zero-sum. At exactly which point in the sequence should each be headed off? Design one check question that distinguishes whether someone grasps complementarity versus mere copying. State openly what should be refused at this level — for instance, the neuroscience of mirror systems, or the metaphysics of composite objects — and explain how omitting it protects the concept rather than weakening it. Finally, say explicitly what would change your mind: which argument about pattern identity across substrates, or which observation about the necessary conditions for loop closure, would cause you to abandon the complementarity framing, and identify where this teaching plan is most likely to mislead them into thinking the distributed self is either mysticism or metaphor.
