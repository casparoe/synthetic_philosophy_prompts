I'm writing this for my philosophy of mind seminar on first-person authority and its empirical limits, and I need a specification document rather than a defense of one view. Please lay out the full desiderata that an adequate theory of introspective reliability must satisfy—the phenomena it has to accommodate, the distinctions it must preserve, the empirical results it must be consistent with, and whether each is genuinely non-negotiable or only a preference of a particular camp—together with the motivating case behind each.

Specifically, include at least the following clusters in your specification, and add any others you think belong:

1. The first-person/third-person asymmetry for occurrent mental states (e.g., reporting one's own belief or pain without observational inference) without committing to infallibility. What forces this asymmetry, and is infallibility a negotiable add-on?

2. The standard failure modes—confabulation about the causes of action in split-brain subjects, blindness to change in visual experience, and the post-hoc construction of reasons in decision-making studies. Must the theory treat these as failures of introspection proper, or as failures of a distinct faculty? Which interpretation is more costly?

3. The distinction between access to the content of a state (what one believes or feels) and access to its causal origin or subpersonal mechanism (why or how it was generated). The theory must not collapse these, but must also explain why authority extends to the former and not the latter.

4. Consistency with predictive-processing or hierarchical Bayesian frameworks in cognitive science, according to which introspection is reconstructed post-hoc from predictions about hidden causes rather than being a direct read-out. Must the theory accommodate such reconstruction, or can it reject the framework outright? If it accommodates it, how does it preserve first-person authority?

5. A constructed case: suppose a trained subject attempts to report not her mental content but her own attentional precision weights—the approximate confidence assigned to prediction errors in her perceptual hierarchy. Call this 'algorithmic introspection.' The theory must either explain how such reports could be accurate without direct neural access, or explain why they are necessarily confabulatory, and in either case must preserve the distinction between authority over mental content and ignorance of subpersonal parameters.

6. The transparency thesis—roughly, that to know whether one believes p, one considers whether p is true, not one's internal brain state—without generating a vicious regress or requiring that one knows one knows. How should the desiderata treat luminosity conditions?

Then answer: are there jointly unsatisfiable subsets of these desiderata? For example, does accommodating predictive-processing reconstruction together with full first-person authority and the transparency thesis generate a tension? If so, identify the subset and explain the conflict.

Finally, indicate which single desideratum, if enforced strictly, eliminates the largest number of popular theories (whether Cartesian direct-access, inner-sense, or interpretive-model theories), and briefly state what each of those camps must pay to keep or reject it.

Keep the weight on the criteria and their motivating cases rather than on scoring every contender. A table marking negotiable versus non-negotiable is fine. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations.
