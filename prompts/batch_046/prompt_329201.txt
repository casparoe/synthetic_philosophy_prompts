I'm preparing a single seventy-five-minute session for an upper-level philosophy-of-mind seminar whose students have read standard discussions of the intentionality of pain but have not yet encountered clinical dissociation phenomena. The aim is not to survey literature but to teach the conceptual problem that pain asymbolia raises for the view that pain is bad in virtue of its intrinsic sensory quality rather than its affective character. I want a teaching plan rather than a lecture transcript: specify the dependency order in which the components must be introduced so that the separation between nociception and suffering is experienced as an empirical wedge rather than a semantic confusion; identify the one concrete carrying case that should anchor the entire session, with explicit conditions that it must display clearly preserved sensory intensity alongside completely absent negative affect and must lack any feature—such as analgesic masking or cognitive denial—that would let students dismiss it as something other than genuine dissociation; map the misconceptions the audience will predictably form, including the assumption that these patients simply do not register the stimulus at all and the inference that asymbolia proves pain is never genuinely bad, noting the precise point in the explanatory arc where each misconception should be intercepted; provide a final check question that reveals whether students understand that the badness resides in the affective dimension rather than in tissue damage or sensory intensity; and state explicitly what neuroanatomical detail or clinical taxonomy should be left out at this level and named as such. To prevent the session from becoming a generic replay of standard pain-philosophy tropes, structure the arc around a comparative symmetry: after establishing the asymbolic case, introduce a hypothetical "reverse asymbolia" in which a subject reports intense, localized suffering with no detectable nociceptive input whatsoever, and use the pairing to test whether students are genuinely tracking affective valence or merely associating badness with physical injury. The plan must be usable without consulting external sources in the room, so do not include reading lists, bibliographies, citation requests, or references to obscure clinical classifications that the students could not be expected to know.
