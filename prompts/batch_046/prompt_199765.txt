Consider the following line of reasoning I have been developing for a seminar on the metaphysics of mathematics, which appears to drive finitism into an uncomfortable dilemma. (1) The finitist denies that there exists an actual infinity of natural numbers, maintaining instead that for any given number n there is a successor n+1, but that the sequence is never completed as a totality. (2) Despite this, finitists often invoke Hilbert's hotel — in which every room is occupied yet a new guest can be accommodated by shifting each occupant to the next room — as an intuitive model of how potential infinity behaves, without committing themselves to an actual infinite collection of guests present simultaneously. (3) Now apply Cantor's diagonal argument inside this model: suppose we attempt to enumerate all real numbers by assigning each to a room in the hotel, one per room. At any stage k we have assigned k reals, and we can construct a diagonal real not among them. (4) The finitist will reply that this merely demonstrates that the assignment is indefinitely extensible, not that there exists a completed enumeration that omits some real. (5) But notice that precisely the same reply applies to the occupancy condition of the hotel itself: if every room is said to be occupied, the finitist can claim that this is never a completed state, only an extendable process of filling rooms one by one. (6) Consequently, if the finitist deploys the potential-extensibility defence to block Cantor's conclusion, they must also deny that the hotel ever reaches a state of being genuinely full. (7) Yet the shifting manoeuvre that makes Hilbert's hotel philosophically interesting presupposes that there is a determinate state of full occupancy requiring redistribution; without that state, there is no puzzle and no intuitive content to the analogy. (8) Therefore, the finitist either accepts that 'all rooms occupied' can be a completed condition — in which case actual infinity is coherent and Cantor's argument applies to completed lists — or denies that it can ever be completed, thereby rendering Hilbert's hotel useless as a model for potential infinity. (9) This seems to show that finitism cannot consistently borrow the intuitive force of Hilbert's hotel without either conceding actual infinity or emptying the analogy of its explanatory content.

Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? I am particularly reluctant to abandon (1) or (7), since I wish to preserve the finitist's core commitment and the explanatory value of the hotel model. Is there an equivocation between the modal status of occupancy conditions and the epistemic status of enumerative procedures that saves both?
