Over the past year I have assumed curatorial responsibility for a repository of suspended scientific hypotheses—claims for which the decisive empirical conditions no longer exist and may never be reconstructed. In studying the epistemic status of these entries, I have become preoccupied with Fitch's paradox of knowability: the argument that if every truth is in principle knowable, then every truth is in fact known. Please describe the most important implications of this paradox for how a curator should classify and present such permanently suspended claims. Specifically, address whether the paradox undermines any meaningful distinction between being currently unknown but knowable and being known, what it implies for the ethical labeling of hypotheses as merely unsettled rather than epistemically closed, and how it should shape public-facing language about scientific ignorance in domains where verification is permanently foreclosed.
