Consider the curatorial practice of major public history museums with national mandates. In their science and technology galleries, exhibitions are arranged as chronological narratives of cumulative success: earlier instruments, theories, and experiments are displayed as successive approximations of present knowledge, with failed hypotheses—phlogiston chemistry, geocentric astronomy—framed as necessary stepping-stones whose errors were eventually corrected. In the same institutions’ social and cultural history galleries, however, the framing is sharply different. Past social practices—certain legal restrictions, hierarchical rituals, or exclusionary customs—are presented not as closer and closer approaches to current norms but as moral failures that modern society has transcended.

What philosophical theory of historical development and moral judgment does this dual framing presuppose? Does it require a belief that scientific knowledge progresses toward an objective truth while moral and social values progress toward an independently correct endpoint? Or can the asymmetry be explained without committing to a substantive teleology—perhaps by differences in institutional purpose, audience, or tractability? Reconstruct the implicit theory, distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what merely fits it well, and say whether the two gallery styles are philosophically consistent with each other or whether they depend on incompatible pictures of how the past relates to the present. Finally, describe what would have to change in museum practice if one rejected the idea that history has any normative direction at all.
