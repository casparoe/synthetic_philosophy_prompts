I'm preparing a seminar that pairs Nietzsche's "On the Uses and Disadvantages of History for Life" with Kazuo Ishiguro's The Remains of the Day, and I want a reading that is genuinely philosophical rather than a loose analogy. Read Stevens's narration through Nietzsche's three modes—monumental, antiquarian, and critical—but I am also testing whether the novel implies a fourth orientation, which I shall call "reparative history": not the veneration of the past (antiquarian), not the modelling of present action on heroic exemplars (monumental), not the dissolution of tradition to liberate life (critical), but a selective mourning of particular failures that allows the present to continue without either denial or domination by what came before.

Please examine Stevens's custodianship of Darlington Hall's archives and genealogies, his recollections of Lord Darlington's political gatherings, and the final encounter with Miss Kenton on the pier. Where does Stevens function as an antiquarian historian of domestic service, preserving dignity through meticulous record-keeping? Where does he invoke a monumental narrative of "greatness" and "professional dignity" to justify present sacrifice? Does his belated recognition of Lord Darlington's errors amount to a genuinely critical history that breaks the past's hold, or does it merely revise the antiquarian catalogue? Most importantly, does the ending—Stevens's decision to remain in service, coupled with his tentative hope in "what remains of the day"—support the idea that a reparative mode is available, or does it show that someone formed entirely within the other three modes cannot achieve it? Be precise about scenes and character choices, indicate where Nietzsche's framework illuminates the novel and where the novel resists being reduced to an illustration of it, and please provide your answer in British English. I am not seeking bibliographies, citations, or further reading—only the reading itself.
