I am directing a small interdisciplinary working group—three philosophers with backgrounds in epistemology and action theory, plus two researchers in cognitive science—who have approximately eighteen months to pursue a focused conceptual project. Our point of departure is the classical Meno problem, why knowledge is worth more than mere true belief, but rather than rehearsing the standard taxonomy of answers I want us to tackle what I see as an underappreciated bifurcation in the problem itself: between what I will call "justificatory surplus"—the additional epistemic warrant that distinguishes knowing that p from merely truly believing that p—and "navigational surplus"—the distinctive capacity of knowledge to guide deliberation and action in structurally novel environments where mere true belief would be unstable or inactionable.

Given these constraints—limited time, mixed disciplinary expertise, and a mandate to produce conceptual rather than empirical work—which open questions or lines of argument in this vicinity should command our priority? I am asking for a defended agenda, not a survey: rank the most important unresolved issues according to explicit criteria that include tractability for philosophers versus scientists, upstream dependency (which answers unlock or block adjacent debates in social epistemology and action theory), and practical significance for how institutions ought to weight expert testimony or design deliberative procedures. In particular, address whether resolving the navigational surplus question is genuinely upstream of the justificatory surplus question, or whether the distinction collapses under scrutiny and should therefore be deprioritized. I also want you to identify which alleged implications of settling the Meno problem—for educational assessment, for advisory board design, or for theories of moral testimony—actually carry weight and which are overrated or do not follow at all. Please avoid bibliographies, citation lists, or reading recommendations; the deliverable should be a conceptual prioritization with explicit reasoning about trade-offs, dependency relations, and what evidence or argument over the next eighteen months would cause you to revise the ranking.
