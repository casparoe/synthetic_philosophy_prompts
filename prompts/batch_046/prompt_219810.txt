I'm drafting this for a conceptual dataset and want a rigorous, point-by-point adjudication of an argument I observed in a philosophy-of-mind reading group. As a longtime reader of rationalist blogs, I tend to evaluate disputes by tracking cruxes, testing whether premises survive their own counterfactuals, and checking for hidden conflations between production and explanation; I'd like that same framework applied here.

Two members, Jordan and Riley, were debating mental causation through the lens of the causal exclusion argument. Jordan began with the standard formulation: if every mental event has a physical cause that is causally sufficient for its effect, then mental properties are redundant, and non-reductive physicalism collapses toward epiphenomenalism. Riley objected that this sneaks in an unjustified exclusivity premise. She offered the analogy of uttering "hello": the vocal-cord vibrations are physically sufficient for the acoustic result, yet the semantic content clearly makes a causal difference to whether someone replies, since identical sound waves lacking that content wouldn't produce the same response. Jordan replied that Riley had merely redescribed overdetermination, not rescued mental efficacy. The mental property supervenes on the physical one, so whatever work appears to be done by "meaning" is actually done by the sound-pattern; our intuition tracks which description is epistemically useful, not which properties are ontologically causative.

Riley then introduced what she called an intervention-stability twist. Mental properties, she argued, are partly defined by counterfactual robustness across physical variation: the same belief can be realized by different neural states, and it supports interventions—giving evidence, altering incentives—that don't map one-to-one onto specific neural manipulations. By an interventionist criterion for causation, mental descriptions identify invariant dependencies that physical descriptions miss, because the substrate varies across realizations while the mental pattern holds. Jordan countered that this relocates rather than solves the problem. Any intervention we call "changing a belief" is physically implemented through some neural pathway; the mental description is a stable summary of physical dynamics, not an independent causal lever. In fact, Riley's point about multiple realizability makes mental properties worse candidates for token-level causation, since there is no single competing physical property—only many different ones, each sufficient in its own token instance. They turned to me, and I genuinely couldn't settle where the argument lands.

Then state who is more right overall about whether mental causation survives exclusion, and identify what both parties are missing—especially whether they are conflating token-level causal production with type-level explanatory relevance, and whether there is a principled middle ground that doesn't simply fall back on standard emergentist moves. Don't just declare an overall winner; show exactly where each argument succeeds and where it breaks down.
