As a retired experimental physicist who has only recently begun studying philosophy with sustained attention, I have been attending a small reading group in which we work slowly through classical texts and then argue about whether they still have purchase on modern problems. Last month we turned to Aristotle’s treatment of exchange, money, and usury, concentrating on the relevant chapters of the Politics and the Nicomachean Ethics. Two other members—Dr. Elena Vasquez, an economist, and Professor Marcus Chen, a historian of ancient philosophy—became locked in a disagreement that the rest of us could not adjudicate. I report their exchange below as faithfully as I can, and I ask you to go through it round by round.

Elena opened by claiming that Aristotle’s condemnation of tokos in Politics I.10 rests almost entirely on a biological metaphor: money is described as barren, unable to reproduce like crops or livestock, so charging interest attempts to make something naturally sterile generate offspring. She concluded that once we reject Aristotelian essentialism and natural teleology, the argument collapses into a historical curiosity. She added that modern phenomena—automated yield-generating protocols in decentralized finance, or central-bank negative-interest regimes—demonstrate that capital can indeed expand without any biological analogue, which confirms that the sterility image was always the real engine of the critique.

Marcus replied that this misplaces the weight of the argument. The remark about barren coinage is illustrative, not foundational. The core distinction in Politics I.8–9 is between oikonomike, natural acquisition for the household, which possesses an internal limit fixed by need, and chrematistike, acquisition without intrinsic terminus. Usury is corrupt not because coins fail to reproduce organically, but because it detaches gain from labor and exchange, allowing money to generate more money through the mere passage of time, thereby destroying the natural boundary that should contain economic life.

Elena countered that if the real problem is the absence of a natural limit, then Aristotle’s critique should apply selectively—only to lending that lacks a terminal purpose—yet he condemns interest categorically, even for modest, purpose-bound loans. She suggested that this overbreadth reveals the natural-limit reading to be a charitable reconstruction rather than Aristotle’s actual view. She then invoked Marx’s interpretation in Capital, where he suggests that Aristotle perceived something like the distinction between use-value and exchange-value—C-M-C versus M-C-M’—and was blocked only by the slave economy from seeing labor-value. Elena claimed that this Marxist framing captures Aristotle’s insight more accurately than Marcus’s talk of natural limits.

Marcus objected that this is anachronistic projection. Aristotle possesses neither the concept of exchange-value in the Marxist sense nor that of capital as self-expanding value. The corruption is not that money mediates exchange for profit, but that acquisition becomes autotelic—pursued for its own sake rather than for the good life of the household and the polis. Importing Marxist categories, he said, obscures the specifically ethical and political point: chrematistike undermines the conditions for virtue and citizenship by making unlimited accumulation appear rational.

Elena then raised a challenge internal to Marcus’s own framework. If virtue is a mean between extremes—courage between rashness and cowardice—why should economic activity not admit of a mean? One might practice moderate, prudent lending that serves communal needs without falling into limitless greed. To deny this, she said, is to treat economic corruption as categorically evil in the way Marcus implied when he asked what the courageous amount of murder might be. But she found the analogy strained: lending is not intrinsically harmful in the manner of killing; its harm depends on scale, intent, and social context, which suggests Aristotle should have recognized a virtuous middle in financial practice rather than issuing a blanket prohibition.

Marcus answered that the analogy holds precisely because usury is not an action within a practice that has a proper end, but a corruption of the practice of exchange itself. Just as there is no moderate amount of counterfeiting currency—because counterfeiting corrupts the institution—there is no moderate usury, since it transforms the purpose of exchange from mutual benefit into self-expanding accumulation. He conceded that Aristotle’s text leaves some ambiguity about whether every form of interest is equally corrupt, but insisted that the structural point stands: once money is permitted to breed money, the natural limit that makes economic life compatible with politics and ethics is destroyed.

Please adjudicate their exchange point by point. Say specifically who is right about the role of the sterility metaphor, whether Elena’s appeal to modern decentralized yield mechanisms actually undermines Aristotle’s structural argument, whether the Marxist reading can be defended without anachronism, and whether Marcus’s counterfeiting analogy successfully blocks Elena’s demand for a mean in economic virtue. Then state clearly who is more right overall, explain what both parties are missing—perhaps regarding how Aristotle’s politics relates to his ethics, or regarding how his account of partnership distinguishes risk-sharing from interest—and take a definite, committed position with explicit reasons. Do not retreat to a survey of interpretations; I want a verdict I can act on.
