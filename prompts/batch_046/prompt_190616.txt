I am designing a graduate seminar module on Aristotle's political philosophy, and I would like a rigorous dialogue that stages a precise interpretive dispute about his doctrine of natural slavery in the opening books of the Politics. The dialogue should feature two interlocutors—call them Thea and Marcus—who are equally versed in Aristotelian teleology and who disagree not about whether Aristotle held the doctrine, but about what kind of claim it is. Thea should defend a functional-teleological reading: she maintains that Aristotle identifies the natural slave by a structurally incomplete deliberative faculty, meaning that the slave possesses practical reason only in an instrumental, non-autonomous mode, and that this deficiency is a biological-modal fact about certain human beings rather than a social designation. She should argue that, on this view, the slave genuinely benefits from being governed because the master's practical wisdom supplies the missing deliberative component, analogous to the soul directing the body, and that this is an empirical claim about human flourishing rather than a circular definition.

Marcus, in response, should press a normative-constructivist counter-reading. He should contend that Aristotle's identification of the missing deliberative part is circular: one determines who lacks full practical reason precisely by observing who occupies the social role of slave, then retrojects that deficiency onto nature. Marcus should challenge Thea to specify what empirical observation could possibly falsify the claim—if a person performing slave labor demonstrates autonomous practical wisdom, Aristotle can always reclassify them as not naturally a slave—and he should ask whether this insulation renders the doctrine conceptually empty rather than scientifically robust. He should also push Thea on the metaphysical assimilation of slave to living instrument: if the slave is treated as an animate tool, does this collapse the distinction between human being and instrument in a way that conflicts with Aristotle's broader hylomorphism, which treats human souls as essentially rational? The dialogue must contain genuine back-and-forth: Marcus should respond directly to Thea's soul-body analogy by asking whether a body can ever benefit independently of the soul it serves, and Thea should reply by distinguishing instrumental benefit from autonomous flourishing.

Please write this dialogue in a formal philosophical register, with each speaker addressing the other's specific arguments rather than delivering monologues. Do not include bibliographies, citations, or references to secondary literature that would require verification; instead, keep the discussion anchored in conceptual distinctions—such as the difference between categorical and modal deficiency in practical reason, or between descriptive teleology and normative projection—that an informed reader could evaluate without external sources. The goal is not to resolve the dispute, but to show how each position handles the tension between Aristotle's universal human psychology, which holds that all humans share the same soul-parts, and his particular claim that some are born to rule and others to be ruled.
