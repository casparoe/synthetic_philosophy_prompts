Read Sophocles's Oedipus Rex through the philosophy of language on rigid designators and necessary a posteriori truths. In the Kripkean framework, proper names are rigid designators that hold fixed across possible worlds, and some identity statements discovered only through empirical investigation turn out to be metaphysically necessary. Apply this to Oedipus's discovery that he is the killer of Laius and the son of Jocasta. Treat 'Oedipus' as a rigid designator: if so, does the oracle's prophecy attach to him necessarily, regardless of his upbringing in Corinth? Examine whether descriptive phrases such as 'the man who killed the traveler at the crossroads' function as non-rigid designators that mislead Oedipus into thinking he refers to someone other than himself, and ask whether the tragedy dramatizes an epistemic gap about a necessary identity rather than a contingent accident. To sharpen the reading, introduce the idea of modal inertia: Oedipus tries to change his future by changing location and social role, yet if his identity is rigidly fixed, those changes are metaphysically irrelevant to whether the prophecy applies. At the same time, consider where the play resists this framing. Does Sophocles present the oracle's power as causal or deterministically temporal rather than as modal necessity? Does the chorus's emphasis on fate suggest an inevitability that differs from the metaphysical rigidity of a name? I want a reading that takes both the philosophical distinction and the dramatic structure seriously, pointing to specific scenes—the encounter at the crossroads, the messenger's revelation, Jocasta's recognition, the final self-blinding—where the gap between knowing who one is and being necessarily who one is either illuminates the action or collapses under pressure. Conclude by saying whether Oedipus Rex adds something to the debate over necessary a posteriori identities that philosophical argument alone does not capture, and be honest about where the work simply refuses to behave like an illustration of the theory.
