I've been thinking about what people call "the alignment problem" in artificial intelligence, though I have no formal background in philosophy, computer science, or cognitive science—only a lot of casual reading and long conversations with friends who work in tech. I wrote the short reflection below to try to make sense of why the discussion always feels like it's sliding between different questions. I would appreciate your comments on it. Please respond in American English, do not provide a bibliography or reading list, and do not just summarize my points back to me. I am particularly interested in whether I have actually separated distinct problems or only renamed one problem three times, and whether the idea I call "preference-shaped holes" is coherent or collapses back into something simpler.

Here is what I have so far. When people say we need to "align" an AI with human values, I think they are usually running together at least three different concerns without noticing it. The first is what I will call the instruction problem: does the system do the specific thing I asked it to do, in the way I meant? The second is the preference problem: does it do what I actually want, even in cases where I never stated the want explicitly? The third is harder to name, but I think of it as the ontological problem: does the system understand the world in a way that keeps my wants stable? By that I mean that if an AI interprets my values through a conceptual map very different from mine, the result might technically satisfy my stated preferences while altering what those preferences were about.

That third concern is where my own idea comes in. I have been calling them "preference-shaped holes"—regions of life or value where a human being has never formed a clear preference, not because they are genuinely indifferent, but because those regions sit outside the boundaries of their lived experience and conceptual vocabulary. A system that is optimized to match explicit human preferences might navigate these holes with perfect logical consistency, yet produce outcomes that feel alien or wrong in a way that is hard to articulate, precisely because the human never had the language to forbid them. So alignment, on this view, is not only about matching what people want; it is also about recognizing where human preference-formation itself is incomplete, and deciding whether the machine should stay inside that incompleteness or fill it in.

I am unsure whether these three problems are genuinely separate. It might be that the ontological problem is just the preference problem viewed from a distance, and that "preference-shaped holes" are nothing more than unstated preferences by another name. I am also unsure whether my framing is useful or just adds vocabulary without adding clarity. Where am I likely wrong? Where am I being unclear? And is there a simpler way to say what I am reaching for without losing the distinction I care about? Please be direct: if this is mostly confusion dressed up as insight, I would rather know.
