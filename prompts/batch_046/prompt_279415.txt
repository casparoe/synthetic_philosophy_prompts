I serve as a senior regulatory analyst in a national policy review office tasked with evaluating proposed rules—ranging from workplace surveillance mandates to mandatory savings schemes to professional licensing conditions—before they reach ministers. Every quarter we must produce liberty-impact assessments, but our current reports collapse into useless generalities: they declare that a measure “restricts freedom” without specifying whether the harm is an obstacle imposed by an identifiable agent, a structural condition of incapacity that blocks effective action, or the presence of arbitrary power that could be exercised even when it lies dormant. Ministers need these modes separated because the appropriate remedy differs radically—striking down an interference, redesigning an institution to eliminate domination, or supplying resources and capacities—and because the legal and democratic standards for justifying each mode are not interchangeable. I need a reusable decision procedure my team can run on any proposal in under thirty minutes, operable by analysts with no philosophy training, that asks ordered diagnostic questions, applies a small set of distinguishing tests, returns definite classifications with corresponding remedy recommendations at clear stop points, and marks explicit hand-off points where the issue is one of values or democratic judgment rather than conceptual analysis and must go to the minister or an ethics board. The procedure must preserve the actual conceptual architecture—particularly that republican liberty is violated by arbitrary power regardless of current exercise, that negative liberty is violated by actual or credibly threatened obstruction, and that positive liberty is violated by the absence of effective capacity rather than by the presence of a barrier—without reducing these to intuitive slogans. Apply it to three proposals: first, an employer-mandated biometric wellness application that tracks remote workers continuously but contractually promises not to share or act on the data; second, a state-backed retirement savings program that automatically enrolls workers at four percent of income with an opt-out provision; and third, a healthcare licensing board that can suspend practice privileges at its sole discretion without a prior hearing, though in practice it rarely invokes this power. One case should yield a classification that conflicts with the intuitive judgment that “no actual harm has occurred,” and the procedure should still deliver its answer, explain why the intuition misfires, and specify what institutional redesign would be required. Finally, state plainly what the procedure cannot do—where it will misfire, which value judgments it necessarily smuggles in despite its procedural appearance, and which boundary cases must be resolved outside it altogether.
