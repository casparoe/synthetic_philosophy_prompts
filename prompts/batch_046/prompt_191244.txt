I'm drafting an adversarial collaboration between a committed statutory textualist—who holds that a statute's legal content is fixed by the conventional semantic meaning of its enacted text, with legislative history serving at best as a disambiguating tie-breaker—and a committed purposivist—who holds that a statute's authoritative content is determined by the rational purpose that best explains its enactment, treating the text as evidence rather than container. Write the joint statement they should have produced.

The document should open with a numbered agreed-claims section—including claims about the existence of statutory ambiguity, the phenomenon of scattered legislative intent (different drafters holding different mental contents about a key term), the distinction between semantic content and speaker's meaning, and the limits of judicial competence—followed by a disagreements table. Each disagreement should be phrased in wording that its own side would accept; avoid framing either position as ignoring the text or ignoring justice.

Then identify the cruxes: I take one to be whether ordinary meaning is a descriptive statistical construct (a report of usage frequencies at enactment) or a normative interpretive standard (what a reasonable reader should understand the legislature to have communicated); another is how to resolve cases where the text, read literally, yields an outcome that both sides agree is purposively absurd but the legislative record is genuinely ambiguous and does not clearly resolve the ambiguity.

For each crux, specify a concrete case both sides accept as bearing on it: a 1920 statute prohibits vehicles in the park; the 2024 dispute concerns an electric wheelchair and an autonomous delivery drone. Both sides agree on the available evidence—1920 dictionary definitions, committee reports mentioning horseless carriages, the absence of amendments addressing mobility aids or aerial devices, and the fact that the park's purpose is recreational preservation. For each crux, record each side's prediction about the case: what the textualist predicts should be decisive (e.g., whether the 1920 conventional meaning of vehicle included human-powered or aerial devices) and what the purposivist predicts should be decisive (e.g., whether preserving recreational space is defeated by either device, and whether the rational purpose of the statute can be reconstructed without appealing to the drafters' mental states). Do not include bibliographies, citations, or reading lists. Do not invoke obscure theories that depend on specialized scholarship; build the disagreement from first principles and the shared hypothetical.
