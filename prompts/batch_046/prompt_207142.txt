Having spent a career in physics and turned only lately to philosophy, I have been examining Gödel's rotating-universe solution to the Einstein field equations, especially the closed timelike curves that appear to let a traveler intersect their own past. In popular accounts one often hears the comparison to a circular railway or carousel: the cosmos spins, and traversing the loop long enough brings one back not to a different place but to an earlier time. Before I accept this image, I wish to test its load-bearing capacity. First, specify the mapping in detail—what corresponds to the track, the passenger, the station, the direction of travel, and what "arriving back" means in spacetime terms. Second, identify the precise fractures: does the analogy treat temporal separation as if it were spatial separation? Does it smuggle a local, Newtonian intuition of rotation into a globally curved manifold that lacks any global inertial frame? Third, I want a comparison seldom made in philosophical treatments—the Lense-Thirring frame-dragging near rotating masses, a weak local dragging of inertial frames—juxtaposed with Gödel's global cosmic rotation. Does this physical parallel illuminate the geometry, or does it compound a category error between local and global effects? Lastly, determine whether the philosophical conclusion—that linear time is thereby undermined—actually follows from the cosmology, or whether it was being pulled along by the carousel image all along.
