Here is an argument that seems to push a standard resolution of one paradox into an absurd conclusion about risk, and I cannot see where it slips. (1) In the St. Petersburg game, the payoff is 2^k with probability 2^{-(k+1)}, so expected monetary value diverges. (2) Risk-weighted expected utility (REU) resolves this by applying a concave risk-weight function r to probabilities, bounding the contribution of the extreme tails and yielding a finite value. (3) If REU is correct, it should apply uniformly to any decision with the same formal structure: probabilities that decay exponentially while outcomes scale exponentially. (4) Existential-risk mitigation fits that structure—each incremental safety investment may reduce the probability of catastrophe only by a factor decaying exponentially in scale, while the magnitude of harm, measured across affected lives or durations, scales exponentially with technological development. (5) So under REU, the risk-weighted expected harm from neglecting safety is bounded, and can be dwarfed by immediate, high-certainty costs of the same order. (6) That implies rational agents should treat existential risks as barely more urgent than ordinary trade-offs. (7) But that is plainly wrong: if anything warrants overwhelming priority, it is preventing astronomical harm. (8) Therefore REU must be restricted or abandoned. Which step should I reject? I would prefer to keep (3) and (7)—uniformity in decision theory and the moral urgency of existential risk both seem correct—but I need to know what giving either up costs, or whether a hidden scope or scaling mistake lets me preserve both.
