Hey, I'm a retired ecologist who started reading philosophy after I hung up my field boots. There's this move I keep seeing in talk about dirty hands in politics -- you know, leaders doing something brutal for the greater good. The part that interests me isn't the first decision; it's what happens years later. At the time, they admit it's morally stained, necessary but ugly. Then later, in memoirs or public defense, they don't say it was wrong but unavoidable. Instead they claim it was actually the right call all along, and anyone who still sees a stain is just naive or stuck in black-and-white thinking. The cost doesn't get justified away; it gets eroded, like rock face weathering, until the decision looks clean in hindsight. I've noticed it in debates about emergency powers, in post-war apologies that aren't really apologies, and in every occurence I've seen of leaders talking about surveillance choices made during crises. Is there an established name for this retrospective cleansing of acknowledged dirty hands? If not, I'd tentatively call it "moral weathering" -- the gradual public erosion of a previously admitted moral stain once urgency fades. How is that different from plain rationalization, or from collective memory rewriting history, or from just genuinely changing your mind? I want sharp edges: is it definately distinct from self-deception, what are the boundary cases, and am I carving something real or just seeing patterns in noise?
