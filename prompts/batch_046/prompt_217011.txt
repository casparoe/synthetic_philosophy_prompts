I'm drafting my position for a seminar on institutional ethics and want a consistency audit before I present. Here is where I've landed:

(1) I distinguish transactional corruption—individual office-holders violating locally constituted rules for private gain—from structural corruption, in which an institution's formal purpose is systematically diverted to serve factional or private interests, regardless of whether particular exchanges breach local norms.

(2) Transactional practices such as clientelist patronage or ceremonial gift-giving to officials are not corruption in societies where they operate as transparent, norm-governed mechanisms of access and redistribution; applying Western bright-line anti-bribery standards to them is normative imperialism that mistakes a historically specific bureaucratic culture for universal morality.

(3) Structural corruption, however, carries universal moral weight: when citizens must rely on arbitrary personal connections to obtain goods that an institution is formally obligated to deliver, the institution has failed in every context, irrespective of local normative acceptance.

(4) The correct diagnostic for structural corruption is participatory, not checklist-based: one should examine whether those subject to the institution experience access as contingent on favoritism, rather than measuring deviation from an external transparency index.

(5) Even when structural corruption is present—say, where patronage mediates access to essential services in the absence of formal welfare mechanisms—reformers should refrain from applying the corruption label if the practice is widely experienced as legitimate and serves an irreplaceable distributive function, because the label tends to destroy the mechanism without substituting an alternative and thereby harms those it claims to protect.

(6) The wrongness of corruption is not intrinsic to private gain or to exchange itself; it derives entirely from institutional damage, specifically the erosion of an office's capacity to serve those governed by it according to its legitimate purpose.

Go through these commitments and tell me which pairs are in genuine tension, which only appear to conflict, and how severely. For each real tension, specify what would have to give and what the least costly revision would be. Rank the tensions by severity rather than listing them as equally bad. I have not decided which commitment I would drop if forced, so do not assume that—just trace the conceptual cost of each possible revision. In particular, examine whether my participatory diagnostic in (4) is compatible with my universalist claim in (3) and with my pragmatic restraint in (5), and whether the transactional/structural distinction in (1) actually does the work I need it to do between (2) and (3).
