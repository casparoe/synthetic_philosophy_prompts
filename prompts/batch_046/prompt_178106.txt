I'm designing a conceptual framework for a graduate workshop on democratic innovation, and I want to escape the usual binary of "sortition good versus sortition bad." Please construct a decision-tree taxonomy that classifies substantive positions on lottocracy according to the institutional commitments they entail, not according to the labels people adopt. The root node should pose a concrete case: if a polity must choose between retaining an elected legislature, replacing it entirely with a lot-selected body, or giving the lot-selected body a specialized but non-legislative function, which path is taken? From there, branch through further diagnostic questions—each phrased as a specific institutional conflict rather than an abstract principle—such as whether the output of a citizens' assembly is binding, advisory, or conditional upon electoral concurrence; whether descriptive representation is defended as an epistemic instrument that improves decision quality or as an intrinsic anti-oligarchic value independent of outcomes; and whether retained elected chambers, if any, hold supremacy, parallel authority, or only agenda-setting power. Build the tree from the logical space of these commitments first, then map actual positions onto the resulting combinations, aiming for about eight to ten terminal leaves with descriptive names (for example, pure legislative sortition, advisory mini-publicism, hybrid veto-sortition, or electoral-supremacy with deliberative input). Crucially, identify any coherent combination of answers that appears unoccupied by a named view, and briefly diagnose whether the gap seems due to internal incoherence, normative unattractiveness, or mere historical neglect; also flag any pair of named positions that your tree cannot separate, explaining why they collapse onto the same branch.

I am particularly interested in distinguishing defenders who treat random selection primarily as a competence-filtering mechanism from those who treat it as a symbolic or equality-producing mechanism, since popular discussions often conflate these. Include at least one branch that turns on how the system handles a direct conflict between the lot-body's recommendation and a popular referendum result—this should separate positions that grant ultimate sovereignty to the assembled citizens from those that preserve an electoral or plebiscitary override. The taxonomy should be navigable without prior knowledge of the academic vocabulary, so each internal node must be answerable by someone reasoning about institutional design alone. Please present the tree with clear node questions, the branching answers, the named or described positions at the leaves, and a brief note on unoccupied or indistinguishable combinations. At the conclusion, state explicitly how confident you are in the mapping and branch diagnoses you have offered—especially regarding the empty combinations—and single out the one specific point about which you are least certain, whether that is the coherence of a particular unoccupied institutional design or the correct placement of one of the named positions within the tree.
