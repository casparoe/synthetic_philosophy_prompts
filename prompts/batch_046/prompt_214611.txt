A friend summarized Jackson’s Mary argument for me like this: Mary learns every physical fact about color vision in her black-and-white room, but when she finally sees red she acquires a phenomenal concept of redness that no physical description could have given her, so the argument only targets type-A physicalism—the view that phenomenal truths are a priori derivable from physical truths. The summary then says type-B physicalists, who already deny that derivability, are untouched; therefore Jackson’s case was always a strawman, and the phenomenal-concept strategy (Balog, Papineau, Loar) fully saves physicalism by explaining Mary’s new knowledge as a new mode of presentation rather than a new fact. I think there are subtle slippages here—maybe in how the argument is mapped onto the type-A/type-B distinction, and in whether “saving physicalism” requires more than conceptual irreducibility—but I’m not sure where fair interpretation ends and misrepresentation begins. Does Jackson’s original argument actually commit only to attacking type-A physicalism, or does it also press against type-B? Is the phenomenal-concept reply a genuine dissolution of the knowledge argument or does it change the subject by redefining what Mary learns? And if Mary’s gain is genuinely conceptual rather than factual, does that still leave an explanatory gap that physicalism owes us an account of?
