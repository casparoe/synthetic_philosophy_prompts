Compare the epistemic bootstrapping problem and the easy-knowledge problem, treating them not as synonyms but as potentially distinct failures of non-skeptical epistemology. First, clarify the specific circularity at work in bootstrapping: a belief-forming method is employed to establish the reliability of that very method. Second, clarify the specific permissiveness at work in easy knowledge: a basic epistemic competence, already in operation, is applied to generate substantive knowledge—including knowledge about the competence's own reliability—without any prior, independent warrant for the source. Next, construct and evaluate a hypothetical "reverse bootstrapping" scenario in which an agent possesses exactly one initially unwarranted belief, namely that a given cognitive source is reliable, and then uses that belief alone to justify every subsequent output of the source. Contrast this reverse structure with the standard easy-knowledge pattern, where an agent derives knowledge of both the external world and the reliability of her perceptual faculties through the direct exercise of those faculties. Is the circularity in reverse bootstrapping structurally identical to the circularity in standard bootstrapping, and does resolving standard bootstrapping—say, by requiring independent calibration—simultaneously block the permissiveness that generates easy knowledge? Or can an epistemological framework avoid vicious circularity in self-validation while still allowing a basic competence to license knowledge of its own success? Defend your answer by identifying which of the two challenges, circularity or permissiveness, is more fundamental and whether they can be disentangled.
