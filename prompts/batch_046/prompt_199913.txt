I serve on the Institutional Review Board for Experimental Decision Sciences at a research university. We are reviewing a proposal for a live-subject study—tentatively titled the Stochastic Predictor Protocol with Correlated Luring Payouts—that explicitly combines the logical structure of the Platonia dilemma with a Luring Lottery mechanism. The design is as follows: participants are told that a predictive algorithm, trained on prior behavioral data, has already generated an individual forecast of their choice between two options, A and B. They then make their selection in isolation. Choosing A yields a modest guaranteed payment; choosing B yields a larger guaranteed payment only if the algorithm predicted B, but B-choosers are also entered into a secondary Luring Lottery. This lottery pools a portion of all forfeited A-bonuses and distributes the pool exclusively to B-choosers whose selection matched the algorithm’s forecast, thereby creating a correlated incentive that rewards what the researchers call superrational alignment with the predictor.

The research team argues that this is the first direct empirical test of whether human agents reason superrationally—treating the predictor’s accuracy as a logical rather than causal dependency—rather than merely optimizing expected utility. They note that debriefing will fully explain the correlation structure, and that no deception is used about the study’s purpose, which is disclosed as decision-making under predictive transparency. However, two board members have raised serious objections. One contends that the Luring Lottery introduces an ethically problematic form of engineered interdependence: by tying an individual’s payout to the collective behavior of strangers through a hidden correlation structure, the protocol undermines the autonomy conditions required for valid informed consent, because a rational participant’s best response depends on beliefs about others’ rationality that the experimenters have deliberately constructed. The other objection is philosophical: the study assumes that superrationality is a coherent normative standard for human choice, but if the Platonia dilemma reveals an ambiguity between causal and evidential reasoning rather than a single correct answer, then the protocol is not measuring a stable cognitive trait but inducing a particular framing effect. Meanwhile, the principal investigator insists that without real monetary stakes—amplified by the lottery—the study cannot distinguish genuine superrational reasoning from cheap talk, and that the correlation mechanism is precisely what distinguishes this design from standard Newcomb-style questionnaires.

Please draft the board’s formal decision memo—no more than about eight hundred words—that recommends whether to approve, reject, or approve with conditions. The memo should present a concrete course of action with a clear timeline for implementation or revision, should treat both objections with intellectual seriousness rather than dismissing them as conceptual confusion, and should explicitly address whether the philosophical ambiguity about superrationality undermines the study’s scientific validity or only its ethical framing. In your reasoning, engage the strongest argument on the side you do not ultimately take, and avoid framing the issue as a simple conflict between science and ethics; instead, treat the protocol’s conceptual architecture—its fusion of predictive transparency with correlated payout—as itself part of what the board must evaluate.
