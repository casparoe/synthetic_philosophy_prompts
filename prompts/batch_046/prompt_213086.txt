I'm drafting an essay on what I call the "presumption of independence" in heuristic argumentation—the implicit default that two reasoning tracks drawn from different conceptual frameworks can be treated as structurally uncorrelated unless there is explicit evidence of shared dependency. I was discussing this with colleagues after a seminar on analogical reasoning, and I realized that philosophers and scientists routinely invoke this presumption when they combine, say, evolutionary explanations with normative conclusions, treating the biological premises and the ethical premises as if they occupied separate epistemic spaces. My essay attempts to formalize the conditions under which that presumption fails, not through direct logical entailment but through what I term "cross-domain covariance": a hidden perturbation in the ontological commitments of one framework propagates into the stability conditions of another.

To make this concrete, I sketch a formal check—tentatively called a structural independence audit—that requires tracking whether a local revision to the premises of framework A forces a compensatory revision in framework B through shared background conditions rather than through explicit inference rules. I apply it to a case study involving evolutionary game theory and moral universalism: the heuristic treats evolutionary stability and normative coherence as independent warrants for universalist claims, but I argue that both depend on a suppressed assumption about the scale-invariance of selection pressures across cultural contexts. Once that shared dependency is made explicit, the two lines of reasoning collapse into a single structural argument, and the heuristic's independence claim dissolves.

I would appreciate focused comments on three points. First, is the distinction I draw between "logical independence" and "structural independence" coherent, or does it collapse into a familiar Bayesian or probabilistic independence criterion that I'm merely redescribing? Second, does the evolutionary-game-theory case study actually demonstrate the covariance I'm claiming, or am I just asserting a hidden dependency without showing the propagation mechanism? Third, I'm particularly unsure about the middle section, where I resist formalizing the audit entirely within standard belief-revision frameworks; I worry I'm smuggling in an ad hoc constraint. Is there an obvious objection from formal epistemology—perhaps about whether heuristic arguments should be subjected to this kind of structural analysis at all—that I'm failing to address? I'd especially value suggestions on whether the framing holds together or whether I've invented a problem that doesn't exist.
