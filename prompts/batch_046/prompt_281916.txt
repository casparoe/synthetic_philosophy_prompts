I am a novelist drafting a scene for a historical novel in which a skeptical Philadelphia journalist conducts an interview—published in an obscure 1790 pamphlet—with a modern scholar visiting the American Philosophical Society. The scholar defends a specific, non-obvious interpretation of Franklin’s autobiographical scheme for moral perfection: that the thirteen virtues were not merely private disciplines but constituted a proto-institutional coordination technology, functioning analogously to David Lewis’s account of convention—solving trust problems among commercial strangers through regular, publicly observable patterns of behavior—rather than through introspective aspiration alone. I want you to draft the interviewer’s questions, not the scholar’s answers, and to keep them conceptually dense rather than biographical.

Please organize your response exactly as follows.

Section 1: The Frame and Thesis (approximately 120 words). State the scholar’s position precisely—linking Franklin’s list of virtues to Lewis-style conventions—without quoting Franklin’s original manuscript verbatim and without requesting citations, bibliographies, or reading lists.

Section 2: Primary Questions (approximately 450 words). Write six interview questions that press the institutional-coordination reading. At least two must address how this interpretation handles Franklin’s well-known emphasis on private record-keeping and self-accounting; at least one must ask what distinguishes this convention-based account from a standard virtue-ethics reading; and one must probe whether the view makes Franklin’s failure to achieve perfection a feature rather than a bug of the system.

Section 3: Anticipated Deflections and Follow-Ups (approximately 350 words). For the three answers the scholar is most likely to give—(a) that private tracking is the enforcement mechanism for the public convention, (b) that the commercial-republic context makes the coordination problem primary, and (c) that Lewis’s framework requires common knowledge Franklin never had—provide a concise anticipated reply followed by a sharper follow-up question that anticipates that reply.

Section 4: The Closing Challenge (approximately 150 words). End with a single question that asks what observable evidence—historical, textual, or behavioral—would count against the coordination-technology thesis, framed so that the scholar cannot simply invoke the phrase that this is a research program rather than an objection.

Section 5: Period Voice Notes (approximately 80 words). Brief instructions on how the interviewer should sound—skeptical, commercially minded, suspicious of European abstraction—without asking for generic biographical details about Franklin or for any citation of secondary literature.

Keep the questions specific to this Lewis-Franklin hybrid view. Do not include generic questions about the scholar’s career or about Franklin’s life outside the scheme. Write in a formal, polished register appropriate to a novelist constructing dialogue.
