I've been working through Simone Weil's posthumous essay arguing for the abolition of all political parties—her claim, as I understand it, being that parties do not merely distort judgment but actively destroy the individual capacity for justice by substituting collective passion for the kind of unmediated moral attention that justice requires. I was discussing this with colleagues who work in parliamentary staff roles, and we kept returning to a specific institutional feature: the formal party whip system, in which legislators are expected to vote according to a centralized platform and face career sanctions for defection, even when their own considered judgment conflicts with the line. This practice is not just strategic coordination; it seems to carry an implicit philosophy of where responsibility and authority actually sit.

What theory of collective agency and moral responsibility does the whip system presuppose? Does it require a robust view that the party itself is a moral agent capable of bearing blame or praise, or can it be reconstructed on a thinner, instrumental account in which members merely delegate decision-making without surrendering individual accountability? There is an apparent tension with the broader legal framework: the same jurisdiction that enforces party discipline in the legislature also insists, in criminal and tort law, that responsibility tracks individual control and that we should not punish agents for outcomes they did not personally cause. Are these commitments consistent, or does the whip system quietly depend on an outcome-sensitive or group-agent metaphysics that the rest of the law rejects? I want you to reconstruct the implicit theory embedded in the practice—distinguishing what it strictly requires from what merely accompanies it, identifying where it is philosophically underdetermined (for instance, whether a control-based view of responsibility can accommodate it), and addressing specifically whether Weil's critique reveals an internal contradiction or only an uncomfortable philosophical fit. In particular, if justice genuinely requires the kind of direct, attentive judgment that Weil describes and that cannot be outsourced to a collective, can any version of party discipline survive, or must the practice be abandoned or rebuilt on entirely different grounds?
