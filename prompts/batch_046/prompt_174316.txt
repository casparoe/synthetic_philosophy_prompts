I'm drafting a chapter for my dissertation in the philosophy of comparative historical analysis, and I've constructed an argument about macro-causal identification in development economics that seems to collapse into a self-undermining skepticism. I want to pinpoint the misstep, but I'm unsure whether the flaw is a hidden equivocation between metaphysical and epistemic notions of counterfactual coherence, or whether the chain is actually valid and I must simply surrender one of the premises I value most.

Here is the argument:

(1) To justifiably claim that factor F (for instance, inclusive institutions) caused national wealth W along a specific historical trajectory, one requires counterfactual support: there must be a coherent possible world in which everything about that nation except F is held fixed, and in which W differs.

(2) In macro-historical development, F is structurally coupled with background conditions B—geographic accessibility, resource endowments, prior settlement patterns, and cultural norms—such that any minimal rewrite altering F without altering B violates the coherence conditions that make the rewritten world a version of the same trajectory rather than an unrelated possible world.

(3) Therefore, the counterfactuals required by (1) are metaphysically incoherent when applied to macro-causal claims about development.

(4) If the requisite counterfactuals are incoherent, then no macro-causal claim—whether institutional, geographic, or cultural—can be epistemically justified.

(5) This undermines the explanations symmetrically: it applies not only to institutional accounts that trace legal origins to fiscal capacity, but also to geographic determinism and to culturalist explanations, and even to meta-claims about our own epistemic limitations regarding development.

(6) Consequently, we arrive at a self-refuting skepticism: we lack justification for asserting that we lack justification, since that very meta-assertion relies on the same macro-causal framework it purports to invalidate.

(7) Yet we do appear to possess discriminating causal knowledge about development—for example, tracing specific mechanisms by which particular colonial administrative structures shaped post-independence state capacity—without being able to specify a fully coherent counterfactual world.

I am particularly reluctant to abandon (2), because the claim about structural coupling seems to capture something real about path dependence. I also want to preserve (7): the intuition that mechanism-based reasoning yields genuine, if partial, knowledge even when global counterfactuals fail.

Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? Is there an equivocation in (3) between the metaphysical incoherence of a counterfactual world and the epistemic uselessness of a counterfactual model? Or is the argument valid, in which case I must choose between giving up the coherence constraint and accepting radical historical skepticism?
