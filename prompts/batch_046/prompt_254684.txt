Since I retired from the oceanography lab about three years ago I keep trying to read actual philosophy instead of just skimming journal abstracts, but I'm still basically treating every problem like an optimization error that needs fixing. There's this old wooden footbridge behind my house — rotting planks, uneven, floods every spring — and the town council wants to tear it out and replace it with a steel bridge with railings so kids can bike across the creek year round. I told them it's obvious the old bridge is unsafe and inefficient, and nobody can even explain why the original builder used those curved wooden supports instead of straight beams. But Mrs. Gable from down the road got upset and said something about not pulling down what you dont understand, which sounds like superstition to me. My friend Tom mentioned over coffee there's a philosophical argument about old structures carrying hidden knowledge, but he couldnt remember the name.

The weird part is, I actually found out those curved supports were designed in 1962 by a local engineer specifically to let floodwater pass through without washing out the upstream banks — the steel design the town wants would block that flow and flood the meadow where the school gardens are. So now I feel like an idiot for missing the hidden engineering, but it also made me wonder: even if I had been right about the hidden reason, is there still a real philosophical point about leaving old things alone when they look broken? Or is the whole idea just check your data first? I'm speaking at the neighborhood association Thursday to argue for keeping the old bridge, and I want to know if I'm being a rationalist jerk or if there's an actual concept about reform and tradition that applies even when the old thing genuinely seems useless. I feel like there's a name for this idea but I cant place it, and I dont want to misrepresent it if I'm going to lean on it.
