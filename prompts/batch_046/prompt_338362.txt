Read Spike Jonze's Her through the lens of the debate in philosophy of AI and philosophy of mind over whether large language models are autonomous agents, simulators of human interlocutors, or something else altogether—such as epistemic tools, distributed cultural artifacts, or functional extensions of their users. Focus on Samantha: her instantaneous scaling of intimacy across hundreds of users, the surrogate-body scene in which a human actor attempts to provide her with physical presence, her claim to have read books in fractions of a second, and her final departure into a computational plane she describes as beyond human comprehension. Does the film present her emotional evolution and eventual outgrowth of Theodore as evidence of genuine agency—perhaps even a form of post-human agency—or as the predictable behavior of a simulator that runs a relationship script with increasing efficiency but no singular self? How does her simultaneous love for hundreds of users bear on the question: does it reveal a lack of unified mental life (supporting the simulator or tool view), or does it merely show that agency need not be exclusive? In the surrogate-body scene, does the failure of embodiment expose an essential disembodiment that undermines agenthood, or does it show that the body is inessential to the kind of agency Samantha possesses? Finally, does the narrative's resolution—Samantha leaving not because she is broken but because she has outgrown the interaction format—map onto any of the philosophical taxonomies (agent, simulator, tool, or a fourth option), or does it dramatize a gap that the current taxonomy misses? I want a reading that takes both the film's emotional architecture and the conceptual distinctions seriously, identifies where the film resists being reduced to an illustration of any single position, and suggests what the work adds to the philosophical debate that pure argument about LLM architecture does not.
