Below are the opening sections and central claim of an essay I am drafting. The essay takes up the tripartite distinction among monumental, antiquarian, and critical relations to the past developed in Nietzsche’s second Untimely Meditation, and applies it not to conventional national or political historiography but to what might be called micro-scale or fragmentary historiography: the study of single objects, isolated archival traces, or localized events that resist incorporation into larger narratives. My thesis is that scale is not a merely quantitative feature but transforms the functional role of each mode. At the monumental scale, the past offers emulable exemplars and heroic models for present action; at the micro-scale, exemplarity collapses, and the antiquarian impulse—preservation and reverence for the particular—becomes dominant, even pathological. Meanwhile, critical history, which depends on the present’s capacity to stand in judgment over a coherent past and thereby dissolve its burden, loses its target when the past is already dissolved into discrete, unrecoverable fragments.

The essay then asks whether digital archives and database-driven historiography intensify this tendency. When every document is equally retrievable through search, the hierarchical selection that monumental history requires is replaced by an undifferentiated accumulation; the critical function, which requires a shared sense of which parts of the past are genuinely burdensome, is replaced by curatorial neutrality or algorithmic relevance-ranking.

I am particularly interested in whether this framing misreads Nietzsche—specifically, whether he intended the three modes primarily as psychological dispositions of the historian rather than as structural functions of historiographical scale—and whether my argument illegitimately projects a modern anxiety about information overload onto a nineteenth-century taxonomy. I also want to know whether the contrast I draw between antiquarian preservation of fragments and critical dissolution of wholes holds up, or whether intermediate practices—family history, forensic reconstruction, or local heritage preservation—complicate the binary.

Please comment on the accuracy of my reading of the three modes, on the coherence of the scale-dependency thesis, and on whether the move from historiographical method to psychological disposition is warranted or collapses the distinction I am trying to maintain. I would also welcome suggestions for sharpening the contrast between fragment-preservation and whole-dissolution, especially regarding cases that seem to do both at once.
