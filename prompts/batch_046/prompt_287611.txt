I cover science and ideas for a publication that reports regularly on behavioral genetics, education policy, and public-health research, and I need a reusable editorial decision procedure I can run in about fifteen minutes without a statistical consultant, then present to my editor for sign-off, to evaluate any study, press release, or policy argument that invokes heritability coefficients, polygenic scores, or claims about genetic contribution to a trait. The deliverable should be an ordered sequence of checks in plain language, each with a criterion for a sufficient answer, a clear stop-point where the claim fails and should not be reported as justified, and explicit markers where the remaining dispute is a question of values rather than statistical interpretation. The procedure must preserve the distinctions that actually govern correct inference—narrow-sense versus broad-sense heritability; gene-environment covariance of the active, evocative, and passive types; indirect genetic effects, sometimes discussed under the heading of genetic nurture; and the difference between variance explained in a specific reference population and predictive utility or causal malleability for an individual—while dropping technical distinctions that do not change the editorial outcome. It must also flag illegitimate transport of heritability estimates across populations, environments, or generations, and it must handle cases where a within-family heritability estimate diverges from a between-family estimate. Please construct the checklist, then apply it to three cases: first, a twin-study report of h² near 0.65 for a social-behavioral trait, used by a commentator to argue that environmental intervention will be largely futile; second, a proposal to deploy a polygenic score for educational attainment derived in one ancestry group to allocate tutoring resources in a multi-ancestry school district; and third, a study finding that within-family heritability for body mass index is substantially lower than the between-family estimate, with the authors attributing the entire gap to shared family environment and making no mention of indirect genetic effects. For each case, show exactly where the procedure stops with a rejection, where it returns the decision to me because values are in play, and include at least one case where the procedure produces an answer I will find unwelcome—explain precisely why it forces a conclusion that conflicts with my intuitive sympathy for the claim. Finally, state clearly what this procedure cannot do and where it will misfire even when applied correctly.
