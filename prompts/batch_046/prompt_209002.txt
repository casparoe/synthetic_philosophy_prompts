Please explain the conceptual tension in Émilie du Châtelet’s account of happiness between what might be called “extensive” and “intensive” strategies for well-being. In her Discourse on Happiness, she maintains both that study and intellectual labor furnish a durable, largely self-sufficient contentment capable of outlasting the losses of love and social fortune, and that passionate attachment—particularly romantic love—is an irreplaceable, non-substitutable condition for a fully realized human happiness. The difficulty is not merely practical, about how to divide one’s time, but conceptual: if the happiness derived from study is resilient precisely because it is distributed across many objects of knowledge and does not hinge on any single external good, it exemplifies an extensive model of well-being. By contrast, her treatment of love implies an intensive model, in which happiness is maximized by concentrating emotional investment in one singular object, yielding extraordinary depth at the cost of vulnerability. Clarify whether du Châtelet’s framework resolves this tension by subordinating one model to the other, by assigning them to different domains of life (study as a refuge in adversity, love as the crown of flourishing), or by implicitly redefining one term—perhaps treating intellectual passion itself as an intensive attachment to truth—so that the distinction collapses. In your explanation, specify whether her view treats the absence of either dimension as not merely a quantitative deficit in happiness but as a specific conceptual failure: a failure of scope if study is missing, or a failure of depth if love is absent.
