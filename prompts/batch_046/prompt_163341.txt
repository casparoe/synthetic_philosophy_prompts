I have recently recovered from a prolonged illness that left me with the distinct sense that the agent who made commitments, formed intentions, and bore obligations before the crisis is not straightforwardly identical to the one who must now honor or renegotiate them. This experience of personal discontinuity has led me to study Buddhist anattā—the doctrine that there is no permanent, unitary self—alongside Western debates about psychological continuity, narrative identity, and bodily persistence. The standard comparison, in which the five aggregates replace the Cartesian ego, feels too neat, and I want an essay that forces the tension into sharper relief.

Your task is to write an essay on the following controversy: should anattā be interpreted as a strong eliminativist thesis that denies the self in every register, or as a reductionist thesis that allows the self to be conventionally real even though it is ultimately decomposable into dependently originated processes? To avoid rehearsing familiar textbook contrasts, frame your analysis through the Theravāda distinction between conventional truth and ultimate truth, and through the chariot analogy—found in texts such as the Milindapañha—that illustrates how identity can be pragmatically ascribed without being ultimately found. Weigh whether this two-level framework dissolves the Western standoff between narrative-identity theorists, who treat the self as a constructed but normatively binding story, and reductionists in the vein of Parfit, who treat it as a useful fiction sustained by psychological continuity. Does the Buddhist framework reveal that both sides are talking past each other by mixing conventional and ultimate registers? Or does it force a genuine choice—either conceding that personal identity is a pragmatically indispensable illusion with no normative weight, or admitting that conventional selfhood carries a binding force that pure reductionism cannot capture? Reach and defend a specific conclusion; do not end on a non-committal note. At the end, provide your verdict in one line: Verdict: [a brief label summarizing your position, for example 'reductionist with conventional weight' or 'strong eliminativist'].
