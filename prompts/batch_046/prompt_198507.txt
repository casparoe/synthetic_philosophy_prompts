I've recently become my father's financial guardian after he was diagnosed with a progressive neurological condition, and I'm facing a decision that feels more like philosophy than accounting. There's an experimental treatment developed by a small biotech firm; it's expensive, unproven, but represents the only non-palliative path. The firm requires a single, indivisible capital commitment—if I hedge with other assets, a contract clause voids the partial refund they offer upon failure, so I must treat this as an all-or-nothing bet on one outcome. My sister, an actuary, insists I should commit capital in direct proportion to my subjective confidence—if I assess the trial's success at 60%, then 60% of his liquid savings should go in. Another advisor, a mathematician, warns that rational allocation follows a logarithmic, multiplicative rule: maximize the long-run growth rate of remaining capital, which severely penalizes large bets even when confidence is high, because bankruptcy is an absorbing state. Meanwhile, the emotional stakes are asymmetric—if I under-invest and he declines, the regret is qualitatively different from losing the money itself, and my sister claims that folds back into the utility function and justifies her proportionality view. Before answering, please take time to brainstorm the core tensions: linear expectation versus geometric survival, the separation of belief strength from stake size, whether non-financial goods can be inserted into the growth formula without circularity, and how the no-hedging clause changes the problem. Then, acting as an expert in philosophical decision theory, give me a committed, non-hedged answer: which allocation rule should govern my decision? Specifically, does the geometric rule survive once we recognize that the "wealth" being optimized includes my father's health, my moral standing as his guardian, and the contractual lock-in? Tell me definitively whether my sister's proportionality intuition or the mathematician's growth-maximization is the rational guide here, and identify the hidden premise that makes the other one seem plausible.
