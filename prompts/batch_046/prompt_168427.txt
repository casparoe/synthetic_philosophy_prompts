I sit on my university's interdisciplinary research-ethics and funding review board, and we must reach a decision by the end of the month on a three-year grant proposal for a program titled Intergenerational Meaning and Existential Risk. The proposal is built around Samuel Scheffler's conjecture that human extinction is catastrophic not primarily because of the deaths it entails, but because it eliminates the collective afterlife—the continuation of human life beyond one's own death that serves, on his account, as a tacit structural condition for the rationality of long-term commitments, cultural transmission, and emotional investment in the future. The program includes both theoretical research and a mandatory graduate seminar in which participants undertake a structured, semester-long counterfactual exercise: they must plan and defend long-term projects, whether scholarly, artistic, or institutional, under the explicit working assumption that humanity will have no descendants after the present generation. Proponents on the board argue that testing the resilience of present meaning without this assumed backdrop is exactly the philosophical stress-test Scheffler's framework demands. Opponents argue that the exercise is not merely descriptive but performative—by institutionalizing the suspension of belief in a collective future, the seminar risks inducing the very collapse of meaning that Scheffler describes, and therefore violates our duty of care toward students. We need a formal decision memo recommending approval, rejection, or approval with conditions. The memo should contain: a clear bottom line with a specific timeline for any conditions; an honest account of the strongest objection from the side that loses; a precise engagement with Scheffler's conjecture that distinguishes its psychological interpretation (that people happen to need belief in future generations for emotional stability) from its normative interpretation (that the existence of future generations is a constitutive condition for the rationality of present long-term agency); and concrete, actionable modifications—regarding participant safeguards, the framing of the counterfactual exercise, or the research outputs—that the applicants could actually implement in response. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citation requests.
