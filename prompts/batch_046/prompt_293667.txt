Please argue for replacing lexical-threshold negative utilitarianism with what we might call a discontinuous-metric account: the view that pleasure and suffering remain fully commensurable on a single cardinal scale, but suffering exhibits exponential intensity growth beyond a critical phenomenal density whereas pleasure scales only linearly. On this account, extreme suffering dominates any quantity of mild pleasure not because the values are lexically incommensurable, but because the metric itself produces an asymptotic dominance relation at high suffering densities. Defend this position against the charge that it collapses into standard negative utilitarianism by explaining how the discontinuity operates specifically within practical reasoning—namely, that rational agents face a discrete “jump” in obligation at the threshold rather than a smooth trade-off. Then address the objection that phenomenal density cannot be measured or identified independently of the very value judgments it is meant to ground, and show why that circularity does not undermine the metric’s normative force. Keep the argument conceptually precise but do not rely on specific named texts or bibliographic references.
