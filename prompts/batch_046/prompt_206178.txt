Recently I encountered a sketch of F.H. Bradley's regress: the idea that if a proposition is a genuine unity of subject and predicate, and if that unity requires an explicit relation to bind them, then that relation itself stands in need of further relations to connect it to its relata, generating an infinite regress that apparently destroys the coherence of judgment itself. I also understand that analytic philosophers have responded in several ways—some treat relations as internal to their relata rather than as external connectors, others invoke primitive predication that needs no further relating relation, and still others reject the demand for an explicit binder altogether.

Please restate Bradley's regress and these main lines of response using only the vocabulary of graph-database architecture and distributed transaction logs. Be precise: map the proposition to a specific data structure or transaction scope, map the relation to an edge, constraint, or commit protocol, and map the binding problem to something like atomicity, referential integrity across shards, or the need for a meta-transaction that validates the first. For each philosophical position, tell me exactly what survives the translation cleanly, what must be distorted—perhaps by treating a metaphysical primitive as a database primitive that behaves differently in practice—and what has no expression at all within my engineering framework. Then provide the residue report: does the untranslatable remainder point to a genuine feature of propositional unity that distributed data models genuinely overlook, or does it reveal that Bradley was demanding an unnecessary kind of architectural closure, analogous to expecting every log entry to contain its own consensus protocol? Write your response in American English, and do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations.
