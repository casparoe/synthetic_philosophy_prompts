I've noticed a recurring argumentative pattern in debates about the justification of punishment, and I am unsure whether philosophers have given it a specific name or treated it as a distinct failure mode.

Consider two cases. In the first, a speaker insists that punishment is fundamentally a matter of desert: offenders suffer because they have earned it through wrongdoing, and appeals to deterrence or rehabilitation are dismissed as missing the point. Yet when the discussion shifts to sentencing—why a particular crime warrants eight years rather than twelve—the speaker quietly relies on considerations of proportional harm, social cost, and marginal deterrence, as if these consequentialist factors naturally determine the precise quantum of deserved suffering. The retributive principle justifies the institution in general, but a consequentialist calculus appears to do the actual work of calibrating its outputs.

In the second case, the roles reverse. A speaker defends punishment entirely by its good effects—crime reduction, public safety, rehabilitation—treating desert-talk as a primitive moral mistake. But when confronted with the framing problem—punishing an innocent person to avert a riot—the speaker does not respond by weighing aggregate welfare. Instead they reject the framing immediately on the ground that an innocent person has done nothing to deserve punishment. Here a consequentialist framework justifies the practice at the institutional level, while a retributive intuition is imported, without acknowledgment, to block a particular application.

What strikes me is not mere inconsistency, nor an explicit hybrid theory that openly combines both values. Rather, each camp maintains the appearance of a pure foundation—desert on one side, utility on the other—while silently borrowing the rival foundation's resources to handle the cases where its own principle runs out of guidance: sentencing proportions on the retributive side, innocence-exclusions on the consequentialist side. The borrowed principle is not defended; it is treated as an obvious boundary condition.

Is there an established concept for this precise architecture, where a normative theory's defenders depend asymmetrically on its competitor's resources—one for institutional justification, the other for case-level blocking—without integrating them into a unified account? If the literature lacks such a concept, can you coin one and delineate its boundaries: how it differs from explicit mixed theories, from simple equivocation, and from the motte-and-bailey maneuver in which a strong claim is defended by retreating to a weaker one? I am less interested in a bare label than in a precise carving of what the phenomenon is, where its edges lie, and whether recognizing it should change how we assess the underlying theories.
