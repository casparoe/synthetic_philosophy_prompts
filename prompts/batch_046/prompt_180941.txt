I have been working through the exchange surrounding Galen Strawson's Basic Argument and the standard compatibilist responses, and I am struck by how little the dialectic has moved over the years. Strawson maintains that true moral responsibility requires an agent to be the ultimate source of its own nature and choices—something impossible within any causal order—while critics reply that this demands a metaphysically extravagant standard that has little to do with the actual practices of holding people accountable, and that hierarchical or mesh models of agency supply all the responsibility worth wanting. After tracing these moves repeatedly, I am unsure whether the disagreement is genuinely substantive—about whether responsibility needs an origin that transcends the agent's causal history—or whether it is largely verbal, with each side defining moral responsibility to match the conditions its preferred theory can satisfy. To press the diagnosis, I want to inject a constructed scenario: imagine an agent engineered de novo with fully integrated second-order volitions, rational coherence, and reflective self-governance, but with no personal past beyond the design process that produced it. Would our intuitive judgments about blame in this case turn on the absence of a prior causal history, or on the present structure of the agent's psychology and social embedding? Using that case, together with what Strawson and his critics actually say about explanation, agency, and the conditions for resentment, diagnose the stall. Is there a hidden empirical claim about the psychology of blame? Are the sides answering different questions—one about metaphysical ultimacy, one about social practice—under a single heading? Is the question itself ill-posed, or just extremely hard? And tell me what, if anything, would count as progress: a precise conceptual distinction, a psychological experiment, a formal model of hierarchical agency, or should we expect permanent impasse, and on what grounds?
