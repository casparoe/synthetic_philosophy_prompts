Exam Question:
Explain how Scanlon's contractualist account of wrongness addresses the aggregation problem. In your answer, (a) articulate the 'individualist restriction' and clarify how it differs from aggregative moral reasoning; (b) apply it to the classic case in which a surgeon might kill one healthy patient to harvest organs for five dying patients; and (c) consider a modified rescue scenario in which saving five strangers requires permanently paralyzing one other stranger, while the five, if not saved, would each suffer only temporary and mild discomfort. Does Scanlon's framework permit this trade-off?

Student Answer:
Scanlon's contractualism holds that an act is wrong if it is governed by a principle that no one could reasonably reject as a basis for informed, unforced general agreement. The individualist restriction requires that we assess the burdens and benefits a principle imposes on each affected person separately, rather than treating them as fungible across persons. This explains why the transplant surgeon acts wrongly: the healthy patient could reasonably reject any principle allowing him to be killed for others' benefit, and the fact that five people would be saved does not diminish his grounds for rejection, since benefits distributed among others cannot be summed to offset a catastrophic burden on one.

In rescue cases where we must choose between saving one person or saving five, Scanlon argues that we should save the five. This is not aggregation, but rather reflects that each person has an independent claim to aid, and fulfilling more of these non-competing claims requires no harmful trade-off. Turning to the modified scenario, the student contends that once the harm to the one is serious but non-lethal—permanent paralysis—while the burdens on the five are homogeneous and minor—temporary headaches—Scanlon's restriction relaxes into a threshold model. On this reading, small uniform burdens can be summed when they fall below a severity ceiling, so the collective discomfort of the five can outweigh the one person's paralysis. The student also suggests that Scanlon orders reasons lexically by severity, so that catastrophic harms are always protected while lesser harms may be aggregated.
