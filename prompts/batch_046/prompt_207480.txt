I have recently assumed leadership of an initiative whose outcome hinges on macroeconomic and institutional shifts largely outside my direct influence, and I have returned to Machiavelli’s distinction between fortune and virtù in order to calibrate how much genuine agency I possess in this situation. To clarify my own thinking, I would like a dialogue between two equally competent interlocutors who take opposing interpretations of that distinction. The first should defend a rigorous Machiavellian position: fortune is an autonomous, gendered, river-like power that virtù can only channel through timely preparations and dikes, never fully master; the normative urgency of virtù lies precisely in recognizing this asymmetry rather than denying it. The second should advance a revisionist structuralist critique: what Machiavelli calls fortune is actually a placeholder for unmodeled causal dependencies, and virtù is not an independent causal power but a retrospective narrative coherence imposed on stochastic outcomes after the fact. They must engage directly rather than talk past each other—the structuralist should confront the river metaphor and the gendered language of conquest head-on, explaining why reducing fortune to hidden variables does not erase the practical problem of acting under radical uncertainty, and the Machiavellian should respond by explaining why structural reduction misses the normative weight Machiavelli places on timely action before the flood rather than explanatory reconstruction after it. Neither should be a strawman. The exchange should help me see more clearly whether my present anxiety about this project reflects a failure of virtù or an accurate perception of fortune’s dominance.
