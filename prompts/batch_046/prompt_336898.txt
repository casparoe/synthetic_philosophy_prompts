Popular expositions of Nāgārjuna's catuskoti often liken the tetralemma to a four-way stoplight: green for affirmation, red for negation, yellow for both, and black for neither. This image is used to present Madhyamaka emptiness (śūnyatā) as a kind of logical stalemate—a refusal to take any of the four exits—rather than as an ontological claim about the absence of svabhāva (inherent existence) and the dependency of all phenomena (pratītyasamutpāda). Audit this analogy in detail. First, provide an explicit correspondence table: what plays the role of the four lights, the intersection, the driver, the traffic rules, and the decision to stop or proceed? Second, identify the precise points of breakdown: does the stoplight preserve the interdependent relation that emptiness is meant to express, or does it reduce emptiness to indecision? Does it quietly import a fifth position—the selecting observer—that the tetralemma explicitly rejects? Third, examine what the analogy smuggles in about value: does framing emptiness as a traffic deadlock make it appear defective rather than liberative? Finally, evaluate whether the common conclusion drawn from this image—that Madhyamaka is a form of skeptical non-commitment—survives once these disanalogies are explicit. If the stoplight fails, sketch a better structural analogy that captures interdependence without reducing the four corners to mere logical options.
