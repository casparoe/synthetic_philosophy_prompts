As a novelist constructing a courtroom scene, I have become fixated on an institutional practice I observed in several civil-law jurisdictions: in wrongful-death damages, courts supply juries with official cohort-based mortality tables, and the procedural rules explicitly instruct jurors to treat these statistical frequencies as objective chances to which their own beliefs must be calibrated—personal adjustments for the victim's health, habits, or family history are formally dismissed as epistemically illegitimate. What philosophical commitments about chance, belief, and rational deference does that practice presuppose? Must it be read as an institutional endorsement of something like the Principal Principle—that knowing an objective chance fixes one's credence—or can it be justified instrumentally without that metaphysical commitment? Is the practice internally consistent with the same legal system's allowance for subjective expert testimony elsewhere? And if the instruction were replaced by a straightforward Bayesian framework that permitted individual updating, what else in the doctrine of damages would have to be reconstructed?
