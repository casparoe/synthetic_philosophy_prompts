I'm a journalist writing for a science and ideas publication, currently drafting a long-form essay on Max Weber's thesis regarding the Protestant ethic and modern capitalism. Rather than revisiting the well-worn dispute over whether Calvinist theology efficiently caused capitalist development, I want to examine a more precise philosophical difficulty: whether cultural values can serve as causal explanations at all without collapsing into circularity. In my draft, I propose that Weber's notion of elective affinity—often mistranslated and misunderstood as simple correlation—might be illuminated by analogy to the evolutionary-biology concept of niche construction, in which an organism's behavior modifies the selective environment that subsequently shapes its descendants. Applied to Weber's argument, the Protestant ethic would not operate as an independent variable pressing upon economic actors from the outside, but as an enabling condition that reconstructed the cultural niche within which rationalized, ascetic economic conduct became adaptive. The difficulty, of course, is that values are themselves products of the material and institutional conditions they are invoked to explain; treating them as niche-constructing agents risks explaining capitalism by reference to values that capitalism itself helped produce. I have tried to navigate this by distinguishing values as immediate selectors of practice (the niche-construction moment) from values as long-run evolutionary outcomes (the selection-product moment), but I am unsure whether this distinction is stable or whether it merely relocates the circularity one level up.

The draft continues by asking whether Weber's own methodological writings—particularly his insistence that sociology must be interpretive yet causal—permit such a structural-causal reading, or whether the niche-construction analogy smuggles in a functionalist explanation that Weber explicitly rejected. I also worry that by framing values as environmental modifiers rather than motives, I may have stripped away the subjective meaning that Weber considered essential to sociological explanation.

I would welcome substantive comments on the conceptual architecture of this argument. Specifically: first, is the niche-construction analogy defensible as a reading of Weber's elective affinity, or does it distort his account of the relationship between religious asceticism and economic rationalization? Second, does the distinction between values as selectors and values as products genuinely resolve the problem of explaining with values, or does it merely rename the circularity? Third, have I overlooked the strongest objection to treating culture as a cause—perhaps an objection drawn from Weber's own critique of materialist reductionism or from contemporary philosophy of social science? Fourth, where does the argument overreach conceptually, and what sharpening or qualification would be required to make it publishable as serious intellectual journalism?

Please respond without providing citations, bibliographies, or reading lists; I am interested solely in the coherence of the idea and the accuracy of its philosophical framing. I would value specific identification of conceptual missteps, suggestions for stabilizing the selector-product distinction, and an assessment of whether the analogy ultimately strengthens or weakens Weber's original thesis.
