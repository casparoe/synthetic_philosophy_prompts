Marcus began by describing counterfactual mugging: an agent predicts whether you will pay if they mug you, but they only actually carry out the mugging in worlds where they predict non-payment. Because in the world where you do pay, no mugging occurs, Marcus said it seems rational to pay — yet the mugger is not causally present in that world. He argued this reveals a pathology in evidential decision theory: you are incentivized to pay an attacker who, in your actual branch, never interacts with you.

Marcus then introduced XOR blackmail: a blackmailer sends a damaging message only if they have predicted you will not pay, and they withhold it precisely when they predict payment. He claimed this is structurally parallel but makes the problem obvious. You would be paying to prevent a message that, in your actual world, was never sent — which looks like throwing away resources for a counterfactual shadow.

Elena countered that XOR blackmail differs from counterfactual mugging in a critical respect. In the mugging case, the agent's physical presence is the counterfactual variable; in XOR blackmail, the message's transmission is the trigger. She argued that in XOR blackmail, paying genuinely prevents the message from being sent in your world, whereas refusing means you suffer the release and pay nothing. Therefore refusal is irrational if you value avoiding harm, and Marcus has the logical direction reversed.

Marcus replied that Elena is smuggling causal-decision-theory premises into what should be an evidential analysis. He said that once the blackmailer has formed their prediction, your subsequent choice is merely correlated with the outcome, not causally efficacious. Paying is therefore like burning money to influence a prediction that has already been fixed. He added that if Elena allows any correlation to count as causation, evidential reasoning collapses into causal reasoning and loses its distinctiveness.
