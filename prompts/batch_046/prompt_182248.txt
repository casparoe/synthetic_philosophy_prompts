After arguing for hours with a friend about whether superrationality can survive any real-world epistemic friction, I tried to formalize our stalemate and now fear I have built a reductio. (1) Superrationality directs symmetric agents in a shared decision problem to choose the action they want all symmetric agents to choose, but only when they share a common reasoning process. (2) Applying this requires each agent to know that every other applies the same reasoning, which requires knowing they share the process, which requires knowing that they know this, ad infinitum—so full common knowledge of reasoning equivalence is necessary. (3) Consider a three-player game in which each player chooses cooperate or defect: defecting while the other two cooperate yields 5, cooperating when exactly one other cooperates yields 2, and every other outcome yields 0. (4) In this game, what each agent "wants everyone to do" depends on whether the others are reasoning superrationally at all, but determining that already requires solving the reasoning-equivalence problem from (2). (5) Now observe that in any physically implemented system, agents differ in hardware, sensory history, or internal noise, so perfect algorithmic identity—the kind required by (2)—is never literally satisfied. (6) Therefore superrationality either applies only to abstract, disembodied agents with identical architectures and perfect transparency, or it collapses back into standard causal or evidential decision theory once real-world epistemic friction is admitted. (7) But if it applies only to impossible agents, then it is not a normative theory for actual rational choice. Where is the error? I want to keep the idea that superrationality carries unusually demanding epistemic preconditions, but I do not want to conclude that it is normatively empty. Which premise should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost me?
