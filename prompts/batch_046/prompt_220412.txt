I am a doctoral student in philosophy of mathematics, and in my dissertation work I have come to defend the following position on the axiom of choice: it should be rejected not primarily because of counterintuitive geometric consequences such as the Banach-Tarski paradox, but because it violates what I term the principle of definitional transparency. By this principle, any object whose existence is asserted within a given mathematical structure should be specifiable by a property that uniquely determines it within that structure; the axiom of choice, however, asserts the existence of choice sets without supplying any such specifying property, thereby introducing entities that are structurally anonymous. I want to stress-test this view rigorously, and I am not interested in generic responses about the indispensability of choice for analysis.

Please map what would genuinely count against my position. Specifically, address the following:

1. Which results in reverse mathematics or in the foundations of functional analysis are genuinely difficult to recover without full AC, and do partial replacements—such as the axiom of dependent choice or countable choice—restore the needed structure or merely approximate it?
2. Are there philosophical responses—perhaps drawing on the distinction between existence and constructibility, or on the explanatory role of non-constructive proof—that undermine the principle of definitional transparency itself, rather than merely accommodating AC?
3. Which intuitions about mathematical objects, particularly regarding implicit uniqueness conditions accepted elsewhere in mathematics, does this position conflict with, and are those conflicts fatal or merely awkward?
4. Does the position risk collapsing into a familiar form of constructivism or predicativism, and if so, is that collapse a problem for the view or a clarification of it?

Rank these defeaters from fatal to merely awkward. Be explicit about which objections a committed defender of definitional transparency could concede without abandoning the core view, and which would compel a substantial revision. Finally, present one concrete case—perhaps involving a well-ordering argument or a selection principle in measure theory—where applying my principle produces a conclusion that is not merely counterintuitive but actively obstructive to standard practice, and explain whether that obstruction should be treated as a feature or a bug of the view.
