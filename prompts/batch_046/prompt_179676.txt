After retiring from a career in evolutionary microbiology, I have recently begun studying philosophy of biology, and I find the enduring controversy over the units of natural selection conceptually puzzling. It seems to me that if selection is fundamentally the differential replication of genetic replicators, then any adaptive phenotype—whether an organismal trait or a group-level division of labor—must be reducible to the statistical aggregation of gene-level advantages. From this perspective, treating individuals or colonies as genuine units of selection appears to commit a category error: it reifies the vehicle through which replicators travel, mistaking a temporary container for an autonomous causal agent. My conviction in this reduction is reinforced by the development of synthetic gene drives, which can spread alleles through populations by directly manipulating inheritance probabilities, apparently demonstrating that evolutionary change can proceed with minimal or no mediation by organismal fitness. Given these considerations, I do not understand why philosophers and biologists continue to defend multilevel selection theory as anything more than a heuristic convenience. Is there a principled conceptual distinction—rather than mere disciplinary habit—that prevents us from concluding that gene-level selection is ontologically primary, and that both organismal and group selection are derivative descriptions with no independent explanatory force?
