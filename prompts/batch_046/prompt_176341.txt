As a retired experimental physicist who has recently begun studying philosophy of time, I have come to accept the following resolution of Aristotle's sea-battle problem: that propositions about genuinely contingent future events—such as whether there will be a naval engagement tomorrow—possess an objective, physically instantiated indeterminacy analogous to quantum superposition prior to measurement, so that they are neither true nor false in a substantive ontological sense rather than merely epistemically unknown; that this local failure of bivalence requires neither a branching multiverse of actualized worlds nor a global abandonment of classical logic, but blocks the fatalist inference by denying that present truths about the future entail determinacy; and that the openness of the future is therefore not a semantic or epistemic gap but an ontological feature of the world on par with the absence of hidden variables in quantum mechanics. What would count against this position? I want an honest map of its exposure, ranked from fatal to merely awkward: which results in physics—concerning determinism, decoherence, or the interpretation of quantum mechanics—would sever the analogy; which metaphysical rivals it risks collapsing into if the analogy is pressed too far; which intuitions about truth, bivalence, or the asymmetry of past and future it must reject, and whether those rejections are sustainable; whether macroscopic historical contingency actually exhibits the isolability required for superposition-like indeterminacy, or whether the analogy fails precisely at the scale of human action; and whether the view has been qualified into unfalsifiability—if so, whether that insulation is appropriate for a metaphysical claim or a defect in it. Please distinguish what defenders of the view would themselves concede as damaging from what critics charge but defenders can dismiss. Lead with your bottom-line assessment in the first sentence, then provide the reasoning with no preamble and no restating of the question.
