A colleague in a strategic-studies seminar summarized the Walzerian doctrine of the moral equality of combatants like this. Because soldiers on both sides wear uniforms and face reciprocal lethal risk, they enter a symmetric permission structure that renders their killing of each other morally neutral, regardless of whether their cause is just; this means jus in bello is fully insulated from jus ad bellum, so an unjust combatant commits no wrong in the act of killing a just opponent—only in the prior decision to enlist. Non-combatant immunity, on this account, is not grounded in innocence but in exclusion from that risk pool: civilians are protected only because they do not participate in the symmetric exchange, which is why a civilian who organizes local defense and wears an identifying armband would automatically enter the pool, gain the same reciprocal kill-right, and forfeit protection. The colleague concluded that this makes the doctrine incoherent, because it cannot explain why an unjust war's soldiers remain permitted to kill without also licensing the targeting of armed civilians who have merely entered the same structural position. I suspect the summary conflates several distinctions, but I am not sure which ones. Is this a fair account of the standard view? Please separate what is accurate, what is a common simplification or overstatement, and what is a misrepresentation—especially regarding the basis of non-combatant immunity and the separation of ad bellum and in bello judgments. Does the charge of incoherence survive once the summary is corrected, or does it depend on one of the distortions? Please answer in American English.
