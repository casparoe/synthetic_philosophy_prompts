I have been thinking about inheritance taxes and dynastic wealth, and I have worked myself into an argument that seems to prove far too much. Here it is, step by step.

(1) Fair equality of opportunity means that people's life chances should not depend on advantages they did not earn.
(2) Inheriting a large fortune is clearly an unearned advantage.
(3) Therefore justice requires heavy inheritance taxation, or even the abolition of private bequests, to level the starting line.
(4) Yet parents also confer unearned advantages that are not financial: they teach language and manners, introduce children to social networks, provide emotional stability that builds confidence, and pass on genetic traits.
(5) If the principle in (1) genuinely requires removing unearned advantages, then consistency demands we also regulate or equalise these non-financial transfers—perhaps by state-assigning mentors, redistributing genetic advantages, or monitoring family environments.
(6) Nobody thinks we should do that; family life and biological inheritance lie outside legitimate state control.
(7) So either we abandon the principle in (1) altogether—which removes the justification for inheritance tax—or we accept that the principle applies selectively to money alone, which looks arbitrary.
(8) I want to keep the feeling that a child born into billions has an unfair head start, but I cannot see how to defend inheritance taxes without either accepting (5) or admitting arbitrariness in (7).

Please tell me which step to reject, explain why it seemed plausible, and price the alternatives. In particular, is there a hidden distinction—perhaps between advantages that are transferable property and those that are inseparable from a person or relationship—that blocks the move from (2) to (4) without making the tax arbitrary? And if so, does that distinction itself depend on something I am missing? Answer in British English.
