Philosophers have debated for decades whether supererogation — acts that are morally excellent yet not morally required, such as the saint who gives away nearly all her wealth or the soldier who voluntarily takes a lethal risk for others — is a coherent moral category. The familiar positions are well rehearsed: denialists argue that if an act is genuinely best, its cost is never sufficient to excuse it, so it becomes obligatory; defenders insist that morality sets a floor of duty, leaving room above for praiseworthy but optional acts. Despite broad agreement on the intuitive cases, the literature feels stalled; recent work mostly sharpens existing distinctions without moving anyone.

My working hypothesis is that the impasse is not really about the cases, but about an unacknowledged disagreement over the architecture of practical reason — specifically, whether moral reasons are binary (required versus permitted) or come in graded strengths that can exceed any corresponding obligation, and whether "cost to the agent" functions as a defeater of obligation or merely as a scalar modifier of reason-weight. If so, the debate may be partly verbal (each side uses "obligatory" with a different normative geometry) and partly about a formal question in deontic logic that neither side has made explicit.
