Following a recent diagnosis with a condition whose epidemiological prevalence is far lower than my own family history initially suggested, I have found myself unable to stop thinking about reference classes and whether my continued existence should update my beliefs about how common this condition is in the broader population. This personal preoccupation has led me into the philosophical literature on anthropic reasoning—specifically the debate between self-sampling and self-indication assumptions—and I find the so-called presumptuous philosopher argument deeply unsettling when applied to one's own life rather than to cosmological scales. To work through this discomfort without merely re-reading the standard texts, I would like you to write a sustained satirical piece.

Please write a parody of a university philosophy department’s “Frequently Asked Questions for Prospective Graduate Students,” rendered in deadpan institutional prose. The department—call it the Department of Anthropic Reasoning and Reference-Class Ethics—should be imagined as a fully accredited program whose curriculum, tuition structure, admissions criteria, and career-placement statistics all quietly embody the theoretical commitments of the self-sampling versus self-indication debate.

The FAQ should be legible to a reader unfamiliar with the literature, yet precise enough that a specialist recognizes the satire as fair and not a strawman. Each answer should take a real position in the debate: for instance, the tuition schedule might scale with the applicant’s estimated world-population (full tuition for observers in large worlds, full scholarship for those in small ones); the required coursework might include “Presumption Management” and “Observer-Moment Accounting”; the career office might warn that alumni who adopted self-indication are statistically overrepresented in the alumni directory and that graduates should be cautious about updating on their own employment. The doomsday argument and the difficulty of defining observer-moments should appear as mundane administrative concerns—parking allocations, dissertation deadlines—rather than as named theorems.

The piece should be approximately 700 words, formatted as numbered questions and answers. Do not name any living philosopher; an invented proponent—say, “Professor R. K. Vance”—is welcome if you need a figure to embody the presumptuous stance. The humor must derive from the internal consistency of the institutional logic, not from invented absurdities that lack analogs in the actual debate. I am looking for sustained irony that remains readable without winking: a reader should be able to tell exactly what is being ironized without being told. Proceed directly with the FAQ.
