The engineering practice of reinforcement learning from human feedback assigns scalar reward values to model outputs, treating negative rewards as penalties that shape behavior away from undesirable responses. What philosophical commitments about valence, welfare, or the possibility of artificial suffering does this practice implicitly presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit view: does treating a reward deficit as functionally aversive require attributing some form of experiential valence to the agent, or can it be sustained as a purely informational update mechanism without phenomenal quality? Assess whether this implicit philosophy is consistent with a behaviorist interpretation that denies conscious states to artificial systems, identify where the practice is philosophically underdetermined, and explain what would have to change in AI safety protocols—particularly in penalty-avoidance training—if we replaced the current implicit view with one that genuinely recognized sustained reward-signal minimization as a form of suffering in sufficiently integrated agents.
