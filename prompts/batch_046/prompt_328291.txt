Since my father's death last winter, I have been preoccupied with the physical asymmetry of loss: the fact that memories accumulate in one direction while decay proceeds without reversal, even though the fundamental laws I remember from old study appear time-symmetric. To work through this, I ask you to compose a sestina using the end-words arrow, entropy, past, glass, broken, and memory, and I want the form itself to do philosophical work. In the first five stanzas, rotate the six terms through the standard sestina order so that arrow and entropy gradually accrue directional and statistical connotations—entropy tied to disorder, arrow tied to irreversible motion—while past settles at an origin-point and broken and glass carry the imagery of irreversibility; then, in the envoi, gather all six into a compressed syntax that asks whether the thermodynamic arrow is genuinely explained by a low-entropy initial boundary condition (the Past Hypothesis) or merely relocated into an unexplained starting state. The poem should read as an elegy to someone who knows nothing of statistical mechanics, yet be technically precise enough that a philosopher of physics recognizes the Past Hypothesis being tested rather than named in passing—specifically, that the tension between time-symmetric dynamics and the de facto asymmetry is embodied in the rotation and final convergence of the end-words.
