The exchange proceeded as follows.

(1) Reeves said that Carroll's tortoise demonstrates that any attempt to justify a deductive inference collapses into an infinite regress of premises. Therefore deduction ultimately rests on nothing more than unjustified convention.

(2) Chen replied that this misreads the dialogue. The tortoise generates a regress only if one treats logical rules—such as modus ponens—as premises that must themselves be believed or asserted. Once you distinguish rules of inference, which we apply, from premises, which we assert, the regress stops at a legitimate point.

(3) Reeves countered that the distinction is ad hoc. The tortoise can always reformulate the rule as an additional premise—adding, for example, the conditional that if P and P implies Q then Q—to the premise set, and then demand justification for that addition. (4) Chen answered that there is an independent argument, especially visible in formal proof systems and automated reasoning: rules belong to the metalanguage that specifies permissible syntactic transformations, whereas premises belong to the object language being transformed. The tortoise's demand is therefore a category mistake, analogous to asking for a justification of the rules of chess inside a chess match.

(5) Reeves then argued that even granting the metalanguage/object-language distinction, it only relocates the problem. We still need to justify our choice of that particular metalanguage. Carroll's deeper point, Reeves insisted, is that at some point we must accept something without further deduction—but that means deduction itself never provides justification; it can only execute transformations that are externally sanctioned.

(6) Chen responded that this is precisely Carroll's lesson, but Reeves has mischaracterized it.
