I retired from ecology about six years ago and lately I've been trying to read philosphy of science, which feels weird after a lifetime in the field. Last week my book club was arguing about whether old scientific theories can be "right but early" instead of flat-out wrong, and I got stuck on Malthus. The standard story is that he was falsified by the demographic transition and green-revolution tech, but I started wondering if that's too quick. There's this idea I ran into — call it "Malthusian debt" — that every rescue just moves the bottleneck: synthetic fertilizers bought us calories but tied us to phosphorus depletion, climate migration is just the pressure valve shifting, and soil microbiome collapse might be the hidden correction. So the position I'm holding, maybe too tightly, is that Malthus's core mechanism never went away; it was just operating on nested timescales, and short-run rescues don't falsify long-run predictions — they just delay and sharpen them.

What would actually have to be true for this to be wrong? I'm not asking for a bibliography, just an honest exposure map. Which observations or arguments would be decisive rather than merely awkward — say, sustained decoupling of population from resource curves, or formal results about prediction horizons in complex systems? Which of these defeaters would a defender of the "right but early" view concede as genuinely damaging, and which would they dismiss as missing the timescale point? And be straight with me: has this position been qualified into unfalsifiability, where any future crisis gets read as the delayed Malthusian correction and any absence of crisis gets read as another temporary rescue? If so, is that a fatal problem for a view of this kind, or is it just what long-run theories have to live with? I definatly want to know if I'm insulating myself here.
