I'm leading a ninety-minute seminar for first-year philosophy students who have completed only an introductory ethics course and have not encountered the moral-luck literature. The session must establish whether moral luck is genuinely paradoxical or merely apparently so, but I want to avoid the standard Gauguin-and-negligent-driver sequence that dominates textbooks. Instead, the session should be built around the less common claim that circumstantial moral luck—being morally assessed differently because of outcomes outside one's control—depends conceptually on constitutive moral luck—being formed as the kind of moral agent one is by factors outside one's control—and that students typically miss this dependency because they believe they can reject the latter while comfortably accepting the former.

Plan the explanatory arc: the precise order in which the conceptual components must be introduced and why the dependency structure makes this ordering necessary rather than merely conventional; the single carrying example that must simultaneously illustrate both forms of luck and what specific features it must possess and what it must lack so that students cannot dissolve the tension too quickly by appealing to intentions or outcomes alone; the misconceptions the audience will predictably generate in sequence—including the conflation of moral assessment with legal liability, the assumption that "control" is a single unified concept rather than a family of notions, and the attempt to eliminate luck by isolating the will from its formation—and the exact point in the arc where each should be intercepted and absorbed; the check for understanding that would show they have grasped the dependency relation rather than simply memorized the two categories; and what standard distinctions, cases, or literature-derived moves should be explicitly left out at this level with an open acknowledgment of the omission. Do not request readings or citations.
