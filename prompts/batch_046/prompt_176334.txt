I run a small discussion series for people who have read a fair amount of LessWrong-style writing and can follow a conceptual argument, but who have not taken a history or philosophy of science course. Next month's session is on Kuhn's account of paradigm shifts and incommensurability—specifically the claim that rival paradigms do not merely disagree about answers within a shared framework, but restructure what counts as a legitimate problem, an admissible method, and even an observable fact. I want to teach this through one concrete carrying case rather than through abstract taxonomy, and I am inclined toward the phlogiston-to-oxygen transition because it lets me show the same combustion phenomena being embedded in radically different ontologies without requiring physics beyond high school.

Could you map out the teaching plan in detail? First, the dependency order: which pieces must come before others so that incommensurability emerges as a structural feature of scientific practice rather than as a rhetorical excuse for relativism. Second, specify the carrying example—what the phlogiston case (or an alternative you prefer) must include and what it must deliberately omit, such as whether I should withhold Lavoisier's quantitative measurements until after the conceptual restructuring is visible, and how to present the observation of metal calcination so that the audience feels the ontological shift rather than just learning a new vocabulary. Third, map the misconceptions this audience will likely import: the belief that incommensurability means literal untranslatability of sentences, the belief that paradigm choice is therefore irrational or merely sociological, and the rationalist reflex to treat paradigm shifts as Bayesian updates with different priors—identify where each should be defused in the sequence. Fourth, propose a check for understanding that distinguishes disagreement about the facts from disagreement about what counts as a fact. Finally, state explicitly what should be left out at this level—perhaps the full sociology of scientific communities, the taxonomy of anomalies, or the post-Kuhn sociology of scientific knowledge—and say so openly, since I want to be honest about the boundaries rather than pretend to completeness. The audience is analytically inclined but technically naive, so the plan should depend only on conceptual reasoning, not on chemistry they have not studied.
