I am preparing a feature on whether structural realism can survive without collapsing into either standard epistemic caution or full-blooded ontic commitment, and I want to stress-test a specific framing. Call it instrument-invariant structural realism. The claim is that what endures across scientific revolutions is neither a hidden ontology nor bare mathematical form, but a class of relational patterns—those that remain stable when both the theoretical interpretation and the physical measuring apparatus are replaced, provided the replacement preserves predictive calibration across domains. On this view, structure is defined not by invariance under theory change alone, but by invariance under coupled theoretical-instrumental redesign. This permits the realist to claim we learn something genuinely world-directed, yet only about relations robust to the technological mediation through which we access them.

What would genuinely count against this position? I want a map of its exposure: which historical patterns of theory change, which results in measurement theory or philosophy of instrumentation, and which formal arguments show that the patterns taken to be instrument-invariant are in fact artifacts of design convergence; whether rival views—say, an austere epistemic structuralism or a direct realism about entities—predict the same stability without the extra machinery; and whether defenders can always retreat by redefining "instrumental redesign" narrowly enough to render the view unfalsifiable. Be specific about what proponents of this kind of view would themselves concede as damaging, not only what critics allege. Sort the objections by decisiveness, from fatal to merely awkward, and identify anything the position clearly risks—some expectation about future physics, or about the limits of interdisciplinary modeling—that its rivals do not share. If the view is well insulated, say so explicitly, and assess whether that insulation is a philosophical virtue or a vice for a claim of this kind.
