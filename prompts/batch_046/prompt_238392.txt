I run a little philosophy reading group and we're pairing novels with thinkers they accidentally echo. Next month we're doing Animal Farm, but I want to read it through Simone Weil's essay arguing for the abolition of all partys — specifically through her stranger claim that party loyalty isn't just corrupt politics but a kind of voluntary spiritual death, a failure of attention where the soul turns away from the real to belong to a collective ego.

Weil thinks parties manufacture enemies and suppress individual judgment, but she also frames this as metaphysical: the partisan stops perceiving truth and starts perceiving only what serves the group. Read Orwell's farm through that lens. Which scenes show animals surrendering moral perception precisely because they want to belong? Does Snowball's expulsion illustrate Weil's idea that parties must invent enemies to survive? Do Boxer and Clover retain anything like Weil's concept of attention, or are they just naive laborers? And where does the novel resist the reading — is Napoleon's regime a party at all in Weil's sense, or is it pure tyranny that makes her critique miss the point?

I'm teaching this to a group that's tired of standard Animal Farm equals Stalinism takes, so I want a reading that takes both Orwell and Weil seriously: don't reduce the book to an illustration of her essay, but also don't distort Weil to fit the pigs. What does the work add to her philosophical debate that argument alone doesn't?
