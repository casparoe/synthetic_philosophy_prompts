Over the past year I have been working through the symbol grounding problem on my own, without formal training in philosophy of mind or cognitive science. I have come to think that the usual framing—how an internal symbol gets tied to an external object—misses a distinct difficulty that arises in systems that have no direct sensors but only generate outputs and observe their own consequences. I am drafting an essay tentatively titled Phantom Anchors: Grounding Without External Coupling, and I would welcome your critical comments on its central line of argument, particularly on whether it genuinely diverges from familiar objections or merely restates them in unfamiliar vocabulary.

My core claim is that there are not two but three modes of symbol-world connection. The first is direct sensorimotor coupling: the symbol is tied to the world through bodily interaction, as in standard embodied-cognition accounts. The second is mediated coupling: the symbol is tied through instruments or representations of representations, but still ultimately traces back to direct interaction somewhere in the chain. The third, which I call reflexive or closed-loop coupling, occurs when a predictive system has no access to the world except through its own prior predictions about its outputs. In this case, a symbol might stabilize—its predictions might converge—without ever being anchored to anything outside the loop. I call the apparent but possibly illusory referent a phantom anchor.

The essay then argues that reflexive coupling produces a specific kind of grounding failure different from the Chinese Room problem. Where that thought experiment worries that syntax is not semantics because the system lacks understanding, my concern is that a system might acquire something functionally indistinguishable from reference—stable predictive convergence—while referring only to its own internal dynamics. The danger is not absence of meaning but misdirected meaning: the symbol behaves as if it points outward when it actually points inward.

I am particularly interested in feedback on three points. First, is the distinction between mediated and reflexive coupling coherent, or does any mediated chain eventually collapse into reflexive coupling once we recognize that all observation is theory-laden? Second, does the concept of a phantom anchor add explanatory value beyond existing discussions of self-reference or predictive coding, or is it simply a redescription of problems already addressed in the literature on mental representation? Third, are there unstated assumptions in my argument—perhaps about the nature of prediction, or about what counts as outside a system—that someone with proper background in philosophy of mind or neuroscience would immediately challenge?

If you see structural problems, conceptual confusions, or places where I have unknowingly reproduced a well-known position without realizing it, please point them out. I am not asking for a bibliography or a reading list; I am trying to determine whether the essay's central distinction holds up to scrutiny and whether its vocabulary is doing real work.
