At a recent workshop on personal identity, two participants—Leah and Marcus—spent the lunch break arguing about Derek Parfit’s teletransporter and what actually survives it. I listened without taking sides and could not decide who was getting the better of whom. Their exchange went roughly as follows.

Leah opened by defending a psychological-continuity view, but she introduced a modified case to test it: imagine the teletransporter scans the original brain with perfect fidelity, creates a copy on Mars, but does not destroy the original. Instead, for six months the two brains are joined by a continuous two-way neural interface that synchronizes memories, intentions, and affective states in real time. Only after this period is the Earth-bound brain destroyed. Leah argued that here it is obvious the Martian copy survives: the psychological stream was never bifurcated, and the fact that it temporarily occupied two skulls shows that physical continuity and spatiotemporal location are irrelevant. What matters is only that the pattern of mental life continues unbroken.

Marcus replied that Leah had changed the subject. In her two-way case, he said, there was never really “a copy” at all—there was one distributed psychological system spread across two bodies, like a single mind with two peripheral nodes. So she had not shown that a copy can survive; she had shown that identity can be spatially distributed. To press the point, he proposed a one-way variant: the original feeds its mental states continuously into the copy, but nothing flows back. The copy is psychologically continuous with the original, yet the original experiences nothing of the copy’s life. Marcus claimed that if Leah’s pattern view is correct, then after the original’s death the copy should be the survivor and the original should have had reason to care about that future. But intuitively, the original should care far less here than in her two-way case, even though the copy’s mental contents are identical. This asymmetry, he said, reveals that psychological continuity alone cannot explain rational concern or survival.

Leah answered that Marcus was conflating identity with prudential concern. In the one-way case, the copy still realizes the original’s projects and values, so there is still reason for the original to care—just as a parent might care about a child who carries forward her work, even without feedback. She also rejected his “distributed system” reading of her first case: once the original brain is destroyed, what remains is a single continuous stream in one body, and the prior dual location is no more metaphysically significant than having two eyes. The point, she insisted, is that physical continuity never did any work; only the pattern of connection does.

Marcus then introduced what he called “narrative ownership.” He said that in her two-way case there is indeed one narrative being lived across two bodies, which is why we intuit one survivor; in the one-way case there are two narratives—one that ends with the original’s death, and a second that begins by borrowing from the first—so the copy is not the survivor but a narrative successor. He argued that this explains both cases without appealing to physical continuity, but also without reducing survival to bare psychological similarity. What determines whether there is one life or two is not the causal chain of mental states but the integrative story that binds them into a single subject of experience. He concluded that Leah’s pattern view misses this narrative structure, and therefore cannot distinguish genuine survival from mere psychological duplication.

Leah’s final response was that “narrative ownership” is just psychological continuity described in literary language. The narrative is continuous precisely because the mental states are continuous; add the mental links and you have added the narrative, subtract them and you have not. She also noted that Marcus had not answered her core challenge: if physical continuity mattered, then destroying the original brain should matter regardless of links, yet in both her variants it does not. Only the pattern of connection varies, and that is exactly what determines our judgments. She conceded that branching cases might make survival come in degrees, but she saw that as a feature, not a bug, of the psychological-continuity approach.

Please adjudicate this exchange round by round: for each move, say who makes the stronger point, where the reasoning is weakest, and whether either party mischaracterizes the other’s position. Then state who is more right overall about what matters in survival, and identify what both Leah and Marcus are missing or taking for granted. In particular, do either of their modified scenarios—the two-way synchronized link or the one-way feed—actually clarify the standard teletransporter problem, or do they merely relocate the same ambiguity into a more complex setting?
