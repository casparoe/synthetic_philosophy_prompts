As a retired field ecologist who has recently begun working through philosophy of ethics and environmental value, I have accumulated a set of commitments concerning meat consumption and wild-animal suffering that I would like audited for joint consistency. Here is my current position:

(1) I endorse a restricted negative-utilitarian framework for non-human animals: the moral weight of suffering substantially exceeds that of pleasure, and our primary direct duty is to avoid causing suffering rather than to maximize aggregate well-being.

(2) I accept the empirical claim that crop agriculture kills substantial numbers of small wild animals—rodents, ground-nesting birds, insects—per unit of nutrition, and that pasture-based beef can, in certain production contexts, entail lower direct crop-related mortality per calorie than grain-fed chicken.

(3) Despite (2), I reject the so-called crop-death argument against veganism on philosophical grounds: I hold that moral responsibility tracks direct, foreseeable, and avoidable agency rather than diffuse causal contribution through land-use systems, so that downstream ecological mortality does not automatically implicate the consumer.

(4) I regard wild-animal suffering—predation, starvation, parasitism, exposure—as a genuinely serious moral problem of enormous scale, yet I deny that it generates a general human obligation to intervene in ecosystems, on the grounds that ecological stability and evolutionary trophic dynamics possess intrinsic value that constrains interventionist duties.

(5) I take phenomenal consciousness to be the criterion of moral patienthood. I am fairly confident about mammals and birds, persuaded about cephalopods and decapod crustaceans, and skeptical about insects and plants.

(6) Practically, I maintain a mostly plant-based diet with occasional consumption of pasture-raised beef, reasoning that this combination minimizes my direct, avoidable contribution to suffering relative to conventional chicken consumption or diets dependent on extensive monoculture.

(7) I also hold that instantaneous, painless death is not itself a harm to animals that lack the temporal integration and future-oriented preferences characteristic of human persons.

I suspect there is tension between (3) and (6): if I dismiss crop-related mortality as philosophically irrelevant in (3), it seems inconsistent to invoke land-use comparisons to justify beef in (6). I also worry that (4) sits uneasily with (1), since the scale of wild suffering dwarfs farmed suffering, yet ecological value in (4) blocks the very reduction my framework in (1) seems to demand. Meanwhile, (5) and (7) may appear to pull in opposite directions—extending patienthood downward while denying that death matters—but I think that appearance may dissolve on inspection.

Could you examine whether this package hangs together? Specifically: rank the tensions by severity; for each, say whether the least costly repair requires abandoning a philosophical principle or only refining an empirical application; identify any apparent tension that actually dissolves; and advise whether a single revision—most plausibly to (3) or to (4)—would resolve the greatest number of conflicts simultaneously.
