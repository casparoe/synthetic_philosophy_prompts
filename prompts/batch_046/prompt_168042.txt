A recent review essay in behavioral economics summarizes Herbert Simon's theory of satisficing as follows: "Satisficing means that decision-makers fix a single aspiration level and select the first option that clears it, abandoning any search for a global optimum. Simon conceded that this is irrational by the lights of substantive economic rationality—since a better option might lie just beyond the stopping rule—but defended it as procedurally rational, because the computational cost of full optimization exceeds its benefit. Gerd Gigerenzer later formalized this insight, demonstrating that fast-and-frugal heuristics not only approximate satisficing but strictly dominate optimization algorithms under conditions of uncertainty and limited data, which explains why bounded-rationality models have largely displaced expected-utility theory in both cognitive psychology and behavioral economics."

This sounds authoritative, but I am suspicious of several moves: the reduction of aspiration levels to a fixed, non-revisable threshold; the framing of Simon as conceding economic irrationality rather than redefining rationality; the claim that Gigerenzer demonstrated strict dominance rather than ecological fit; and the assertion of disciplinary displacement. Is this a fair summary? Please audit it point by point: separate what accurately reflects Simon's distinction between procedural and substantive rationality and his adaptive-aspiration framework from what misstates or exaggerates it; identify whether the characterization of Gigerenzer's ecological-rationality research overstates its scope, method, or conclusions; and determine whether the claim that bounded rationality has "largely displaced" expected-utility theory in the relevant fields follows from anything true in the summary. Also, clarify whether satisficing as Simon originally conceived it requires a single static threshold, or whether that is a common simplification that obscures the dynamics of aspiration adjustment.
