I'm convening a philosophy-of-science and conceptual-engineering seminar — fourteen graduate students, one 75-minute session — and I want a structured role-play that makes participants feel conceptual slippage in a microdomain rather than hearing about it. To prevent the exercise from collapsing into a familiar discussion of metaphor or family resemblance, build it around an invented but precisely defined concept: "refractory patience," imported from materials science into pedagogical evaluation. Define it initially as a system's capacity to withstand repeated "conceptual heating" — sustained counterexample pressure — without structural degradation. The hidden baggage, which should not be revealed until the debrief, is that the metallurgical origin smuggles in assumptions about homogeneity, irreversibility, and a single integrity metric that do not map onto learning.

Structure the activity as a three-court simulation: Materials Scientists (who defend the home-domain meaning), Pedagogical Assessors (who must apply the concept to evaluate learners), and Boundary Adjudicators (who rule on contested cases). Provide five staged case cards describing learners exposed to escalating counterexample sequences; the courts must jointly classify each case as "refractory," "degraded," or "transformed." Design the decision rules and a simple non-monetary payoff scheme — cumulative reputation points that reward internal consistency within each court but penalize cross-court disagreement — so that Assessors and Adjudicators gradually commit to judgments that feel coherent in the pedagogical frame. At round four, introduce an "integrity audit" card that forces them to specify what "structural integrity" means for a learner, which should expose that they have been applying metallurgical criteria without noticing. Specify the timeline (opening framing 5 minutes, rounds 1–3 at 12 minutes each, audit round 10 minutes, debrief 20 minutes), the exact rules preventing any court from vetoing unilaterally, and the materials needed (case cards, scoring sheet, audit prompt). Then include the debrief: map round 1 to borrowing, rounds 2–3 to domestication and hidden architecture, round 4 to crisis, and explain which feature of fluid-copycat dynamics — likely the social negotiation of family resemblance over time — the single-session format cannot reproduce and must be supplied by the facilitator. Finally, be explicit about the predictable failure mode: the Materials Scientists will likely dominate with technical authority, or the Adjudicators will collapse the distinction between "degraded" and "transformed" too early; say exactly how to prevent each.
