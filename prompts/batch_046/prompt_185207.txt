I am a philosophy professor who serves as the external methodological referee for the exhibition review board of a regional natural-history museum, and every quarter we must evaluate unsolicited proposals for temporary exhibits that revise established ancient chronology. These submissions often blend fragments of archaeological data with expansive speculative frameworks, and recently we received one advancing what its author calls the Meridian Synchrony Thesis, which asserts that the Great Pyramid, Stonehenge, and Göbekli Tepe were all completed within a single fifty-year window around 8500 BCE because of a shared precessional alignment event rather than being separated by millennia as conventional chronology holds. I need a reusable decision procedure, grounded in the philosophy of science's actual demarcation literature—specifically the distinctions between Popperian falsifiability, Lakatosian research-programme progressiveness, and consilience—that my board can run in a single two-hour session without requiring specialized historiographical training. The deliverable should be an ordered sequence of questions, each with an explicit pass-or-fail threshold, a clear marking of which steps involve empirical judgment and which are value-laden choices disguised as method, and definite stopping points where the board rejects, approves with conditions, or defers to human judgment because the matter concerns interpretive values rather than demarcation criteria. Please apply this procedure to three cases: the Meridian Synchrony Thesis just described; a proposal that reads Plato's Atlantis narrative as a literal report of a Bronze-Age Atlantic civilization destroyed by a specific geomagnetic reversal near 9600 BCE; and a proposal that applies Fomenko-style statistical re-dating of astronomical tables to collapse the Roman Empire and the medieval period into a single two-century span. The procedure must produce at least one unwelcome result—either allowing a proposal that feels intuitively wrong or forcing the rejection of one that sounds plausibly intriguing—and it must explicitly state what it cannot do, particularly regarding the evaluation of aesthetic or cultural value. Finally, present everything in continuous prose without headings, bullet points, boldface, tables, or enumerated lists, as in a formal letter or extended memorandum.
