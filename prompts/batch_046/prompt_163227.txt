I have been trying to work out, in conversation with friends and partly in response to reading Harari’s framing of the cognitive, agricultural, and scientific revolutions, Diamond’s emphasis on geographic and material constraints, and Graeber and Wengrow’s argument that evolutionary stage-thinking should be abandoned altogether, whether there is a defensible distinction between historical transitions that open an entirely new possibility space for human coordination and those that merely scale up capacities already present. I find myself giving the following concrete verdicts, and I am unsure they cohere. The invention of cuneiform writing in Mesopotamia strikes me as genuinely a threshold event, because it made large-scale fictional entities—corporations, gods, states—administratively real in a way that oral culture could not sustain. The initial domestication of wheat and barley in the Fertile Crescent also feels like a threshold, since it locked societies into a new energy regime; yet the subsequent consolidation of temple-state administration in Sumer feels more like scaling, because the organizational logic was already present in smaller shrine economies and was simply expanded in scope. The spread of alphabetic literacy across classical Greece, by contrast, seems like scaling rather than a new threshold, since it democratized access to an existing structural capacity without altering what writing could do ontologically. Where I hesitate is with the development of double-entry bookkeeping in fourteenth-century Italy: it seems to have enabled a genuinely novel kind of economic actor, the perpetual corporation separated from its individual members, which suggests a threshold event comparable in kind to writing, yet none of the grand narratives treat it as such. Meanwhile, the widespread legal abolition of chattel slavery in the nineteenth century feels to me like a moral threshold of comparable weight to the cognitive revolution, because it altered the boundary conditions for who could be included in cooperative networks, yet Graeber and Wengrow’s framework seems to resist treating any single legal moment as an evolutionary leap rather than a contingent political choice. Is there a precise principle—perhaps one that distinguishes changes to the ontology of social coordination from changes merely in scale, efficiency, or moral sentiment—that can deliver all six verdicts together? If not, which of my judgments should be overturned, and what does the corrected principle predict for a case I have not listed, namely whether the Inca quipu system counts as a threshold event despite never developing into writing? Please answer entirely in continuous prose, without headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, as though writing a long reflective letter, and be explicit about which of my verdicts your account must abandon.
