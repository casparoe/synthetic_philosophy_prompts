I'm on my college's gen-ed committee and we're redesigning the history distribution, and there's this weird leftover rule that every student must take one course tagged "before the Renaissance" and one tagged "after the Renaissance," with the cutoff at 1400 — except the catalog is inconsistent and some courses list 1500, so the boundary is basically administrative fiction. Half the historians on the committee treat the Renaissance and the Scientific Revolution like real ruptures in how people related to authority and nature; the other half say they're 19th-century branding exercises that historians invented to sell textbooks. I don't care which date is correct. I'm trying to figure out what our curriculum policy is philosophically committed to just by using those two buckets. Does slicing the curriculum at 1400 presuppose that intellectual history moves in discrete, internally unified "ages" rather than messy continuous drift? If we also require a separate "Scientific Revolution" module that starts around 1600, are we quietly endorsing a stacked-rupture model — one invention layered on another — and is that even coherent with the claim that both periods are later constructions? Or could someone who thinks all periodization is invented still defend cutting the gen-ed line somewhere practical without smuggling in an essentialist ontology of "modernity"?
