After a long argument with a friend about Buridan's ass and whether rational agency can survive perfect indifference, I drafted the following notes for a short essay. I distinguish epistemic indifference—where an agent lacks information to rank two options—from axiological indifference, where the agent knows that two hay bales are exactly equal in value. The standard reading treats the ass's paralysis as a failure of motivation, but I want to argue that the deeper problem is structural: any act of selection in conditions of axiological indifference requires what I call a procedural identity, a pre-committed tie-breaking rule (for example, "select the left option when values are identical") that is not itself justified by the values being compared. Without such a rule, the agent does not merely hesitate; the very concept of "choosing" dissolves, because choice presupposes a standing normative relation between agent and options rather than a momentary computation of preference. My friend countered that this only relocates the arbitrariness: the commitment to "left" is as groundless as the commitment to either bale. I responded that the rule is justified not by the hay but by the agent's prior need for continuity of action, though I am unsure whether this answer saves rational agency or quietly collapses into a form of compatibilism about free will that I did not intend—if every choice ultimately traces back to an arbitrary procedural commitment, then the freedom of the selection itself seems illusory. Would you comment on whether the distinction between epistemic and axiological indifference does real philosophical work here, whether procedural identity is a coherent concept or just a renamed tie-breaker, and whether my framing inadvertently concedes too much to determinism?
