For a working paper on the coherence of anti-luck moral frameworks, I have sketched the following set of commitments and need an audit of whether they hang together. Here is my profile:

1. I accept Thomas Nagel's four-fold taxonomy of moral luck—resultant, circumstantial, constitutive, and causal—as descriptively accurate, but I deny that it carries direct normative force; it classifies the mechanisms by which fortune enters moral life, not whether that entry vitiates assessment.

2. I reject the unrestricted control principle for deontic judgment: an action can be wrong even when the agent lacked the power to avoid performing it. At the same time, I maintain a restricted control principle for aretaic judgment: praise and blame properly attach only to features lying within an agent's "sphere of practical influence," defined not by actual causation but by what the agent could have reasonably foreseen and adjusted for, given her epistemic position and social context.

3. I regard constitutive luck—the fortune of possessing the temperament, capacities, or psychological profile one has—as genuinely real, but morally benign in the proper domain of evaluation, which I take to be actions and maxims rather than underlying character. Consequently, I reject virtue-ethical frameworks that make character the primary locus of moral assessment.

4. I distinguish a specific phenomenon I call structural resultant luck: cases in which two agents perform actions with identical mental states and intentions, yet social institutions (legal systems, labor markets, norms of stigma) distribute praise, blame, or material consequences asymmetrically on the basis of group membership, visibility, or social position. This divergence is morally arbitrary from the agent's point of view, yet structurally ineradicable without dismantling the institutions that produce it.

5. I think Bernard Williams overreaches when he argues that moral luck undermines morality as such; however, I accept his narrower claim that luck specifically undermines the Kantian thesis that moral worth is wholly self-constituted and independent of fortune.

6. To preserve a robust practice of blame despite commitments 2 and 4, I adopt a "role-relative stabilization" strategy: we conventionally fix the boundaries of the sphere of practical influence according to social roles (parent, professional, citizen, etc.), and we hold agents to those stabilized standards, even though both the roles themselves and the capacities required to occupy them are partly products of circumstantial and constitutive luck.

Please examine these six commitments for joint tenability. Identify which pairs are in genuine tension—ranking them by severity, explaining how they pull against each other, specifying the cheapest revision that would resolve each tension and what that revision costs in terms of other commitments—and distinguish these from pairs that only appear incompatible but dissolve upon inspection. Finally, indicate which single revision (dropping or substantially weakening one commitment) would eliminate the greatest number of tensions at once, and whether adopting that revision forces me into a view I explicitly reject elsewhere in the set.
