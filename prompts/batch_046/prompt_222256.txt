Design a realistic thought experiment—no science fiction, no teleportation, no demons, and with the scenario itself confined to a single paragraph—centered on the Ship of Theseus but engineered with a specific non-standard twist: over decades, every original plank and component of a working cargo vessel is replaced, but the removed parts are never assembled into a second ship; instead, they are gradually installed into a different, originally unrelated tugboat that, by the end, performs exactly the cargo vessel's original commercial navigation role, while the original hull, now composed entirely of new materials, is permanently moored and converted into a static museum exhibit with no navigational capacity. Throughout, ownership never changes, legal registration remains unchanged, and the maintenance crew consistently acts with the intention of preserving the vessel. The case must be calibrated so that three positions deliver three distinct verdicts about whether the original ship survives: (i) a spatiotemporal-continuity essentialist who identifies the ship with its original material arranged in ship-form; (ii) a four-dimensionalist who treats the ship as a spacetime worm comprising all its temporal parts; and (iii) a functional-design-continuity view holding that the ship survives only if an unbroken chain of design-intentional maintenance preserves both its original navigational function and its origin history. After presenting the scenario, demonstrate that every specification is met: for each position, state the identity claim, the criterion or method applied, the relevant nearby worlds (for instance, the world in which the original hull retains its navigation function), and why the verdict follows. Then identify the precise feature that isolates the variable—specifically, the decoupling of material continuity from functional-role continuity while maintenance intention is held constant—explain how all three verdicts collapse or converge if that feature is removed, and provide an honest attack from the defender of the position that fares worst, showing how they would claim the case cheats by treating a museum display and a working tugboat as symmetrical candidates and thereby illicitly separating functional role from artifact identity. The purpose is to test how material, spatiotemporal, and functional-criterion theories interact when forced to choose between a non-functional original-form and a functional non-original-form; therefore, do not stipulate verdicts by fiat, but derive them from applying each theory's own criteria. Do not include bibliographies, citations, references to specific texts, or appeals to conventional naming conventions.
