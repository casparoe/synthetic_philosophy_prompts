I've been teaching a seminar on the philosophy of science, and we've spent several weeks on the Duhem-Quine thesis in its standard form—how any theory can be preserved in the face of recalcitrant evidence by adjusting auxiliary hypotheses. To move beyond the textbook illustrations, I want to introduce a creative exercise that makes the abstract problem concrete through poetic form.

I'm looking for a sestina: six stanzas of six lines each, followed by a three-line envoi, with no rhyme but with six end-words that recur in the strict rotational order characteristic of the form (1-2-3-4-5-6, 6-1-5-2-4-3, and so on through the six stanzas, then all six compressed in the envoi). The end-words should belong to a shared observational lexicon—suggested examples include orbit, shadow, measure, curve, silence, and light, though you may substitute your own provided they remain constant across all stanzas. In each stanza, the same six terms should be arranged so that they suggest a different theoretical interpretation of an identical dataset: one stanza implying a realist reading of physical law, another an instrumentalist or predictive-only stance, and another a structural-realist compromise that retains only relational structure. The point is not to advocate for any view, but to let the formal repetition-with-variation enact the very phenomenon of empirical equivalence. The poem's envoi should resist closure: pair at least two end-words in a way that sustains ambiguity rather than resolving the interpretive competition. Please avoid archaic or ornamental language; the tone should be intellectually rigorous and contemporary, suitable for a seminar audience that knows the philosophy but needs to feel the problem rather than merely recall it.
