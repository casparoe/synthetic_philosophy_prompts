As a doctoral student in philosophy of mind and applied ethics, I am facilitating an adversarial collaboration between two colleagues who have reached an impasse on the moral significance of insect sentience under conditions of massive scale and deep epistemic uncertainty. One party—the Aggregationists—maintains that if the per-individual probability of phenomenal consciousness in insects is low but non-zero, and global insect populations number in the quintillions, then expected-value reasoning yields an enormous aggregate moral weight that should reshape our ecological and agricultural obligations, even absent certainty about any particular species. The opposing party—the Thresholdists—argues that moral status does not aggregate linearly across uncertain instances; they hold that without crossing a stringent evidential threshold for phenomenal consciousness within a specific taxon, those entities fall below the boundary of direct moral consideration, and that scaling low probabilities across vast populations conflates epistemic uncertainty with ontological gradation.

Both sides have agreed to produce a joint statement rather than continue debating past one another. Please draft the document they should have written together. The deliverable should include: (1) a section of agreed claims—covering the current neuroscience of nociception and higher-order sensory integration in invertebrates, the estimated scale of global insect biomass and population dynamics, the structural features of expected-value reasoning as applied to moral status, and the empirical limits of our capacity to predict ecological cascade effects—stated in neutral language that both parties can endorse without reservation; (2) a table of precise disagreements, phrased so that each side recognizes its own position accurately, particularly on whether moral consideration requires meeting an evidential threshold or can accumulate across low-confidence instances, and on whether ecological unpredictability undermines the practical force of the scale argument even if its theoretical structure is sound; (3) the cruxes—sub-questions such that resolving them would move at least one side—including whether phenomenal consciousness is binary or comes in degrees, whether there exists an independent value to ecological processes irreducible to sentient welfare, and whether epistemic humility about our predictive models should lead to precautionary inaction or precautionary moral consideration; (4) for each crux, a concrete empirical case or logical scenario that both sides agree is relevant, together with each side's explicit prediction of what that case or scenario would imply for their view.

Write in a formal, academic register appropriate for philosophers and biologists. Do not include bibliographies, citations, or reading lists; the document should stand entirely on its conceptual and logical structure.
