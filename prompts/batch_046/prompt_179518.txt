The philosophical debate about whether Darwin's evolutionary inversion and Turing's computational inversion genuinely achieve "competence without comprehension" — adaptive or intelligent outcomes without any underlying understanding — has persisted for decades without resolution, despite both sides accepting the same scientific descriptions of natural selection and universal computation. Proponents argue that Darwin dissolved the need for a designer's mind by substituting differential reproduction, and that Turing dissolved the need for a mathematician's insight by substituting formal symbol-manipulation rules; together, they claim, functional success requires no comprehension at its base. Critics respond that natural selection presupposes an environment that reads phenotypic variation with normative force, and that a Turing machine's outputs only acquire significance relative to an interpreter's prior comprehension, so understanding hasn't vanished but been distributed across system and context. What kind of disagreement is this? Is it substantive — about whether comprehension can be fully decomposed into non-comprehending parts — or verbal, arising because "comprehension" is used to cover both causal efficacy and semantic access? Are the sides answering one metaphysical question, or is one camp practicing philosophy of science (asking what explanations must include) while the other practices philosophy of mind (asking which mental properties are irreducible)?
