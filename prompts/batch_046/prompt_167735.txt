The proposal is this adverserial inquiry model: student reps and faculty jointly frame three competing curriculum hypotheses, run them in parallel for one semester across different colleges, collect outcome data, then reconvene and revise the working hypotheses based on results rather than just winning the debate. It's treating democratic decision-making like an experiment, but with a forced disagreement structure baked in so no side can just dominate the narrative. Some committee members say it's bureaucratic theater that delays real choices and risks treating students like lab rats; others say it's the only way to avoid capture by the loudest voices and actually learn from failure.

Can you draft a short memo recommending whether we approve the pilot? I need a clear bottom line, the reasoning that supports it, and an honest treatment of the strongest counter-argument — the memo should be something the committee could actually vote on. Don't just cite Dewey or standard democratic theory; make the case stand on it's own feet. Also, I'm still going back and forth on whether the 'parallel run' part is essential or if a single sequential trial with built-in revision points would work just as well — address that if you can.
