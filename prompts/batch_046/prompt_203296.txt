I run a small philosophical reading group that meets in a converted chapel near the university, and we have recently been working through issues in epistemology and practical reasoning rather than the usual ethics syllabus. At our last session we discussed the frame problem—the difficulty of knowing, after some change, exactly which background beliefs must be revised and which can be left untouched without having to check every single one—but the members felt that every illustration they could recall involved robots or computer programmes, which made the issue feel like a technical curiosity rather than a human predicament. I would like you to write a short story of about seven hundred words that translates this problem into an entirely non-technological setting.

Please imagine a municipal land registry in an unnamed northern port city, governed by an unwritten tradition of “dependency chains”: whenever one deed is corrected or relocated, an unspecified cluster of related records must be updated, but no complete list of these links exists, and clerks learn the patterns only through long apprenticeship. The protagonist should be an archivist who discovers that a single misfiled document from decades ago has quietly altered the ownership boundaries of an entire waterfront district, and she must now make an urgent administrative decision—perhaps about a pending demolition permit—without being able to trace every affected record. The narrative should convey the specific anxiety of not knowing where the “frame” of relevant change ends and the untouched background begins, and it should conclude with her acting on one unverified assumption about what has remained constant, accepting the risk that she has ignored some hidden dependency. Please avoid any reference to artificial intelligence, algorithms, databases, or digital systems, and do not include citations, footnotes, or references to named philosophical works.
