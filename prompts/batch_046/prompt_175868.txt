I have been working through the following argument in a graduate seminar on Avicennian psychology and its reception in contemporary philosophy of mind, and I find myself unable to locate the precise error, although the conclusion seems to me plainly absurd.

(1) In Avicenna's flying-man thought experiment, a human being created suddenly in mid-air, with eyes veiled and limbs spread apart, and with no prior sensory experience whatsoever, would nevertheless affirm that he exists.
(2) This affirmation is immediate and non-inferential; it does not depend upon any perception of bodily parts, internal sensations, or spatial location.
(3) Consequently, the self is known to itself through an essential, non-corporeal relation of direct acquaintance.
(4) If the self is known in a manner that is essentially independent of bodily representation, then bodily properties are accidental rather than essential to the nature of the self.
(5) Contemporary predictive-processing accounts of minimal phenomenal selfhood maintain that the minimal self just is the brain's best predictive model of its own embodied states.
(6) Therefore, if there is no embodied predictive processing — as in the flying man's condition — there ought, on that account, to be no minimal phenomenal selfhood.
(7) But Avicenna's intuition is precisely that minimal selfhood persists even in the total absence of embodied processing.
(8) Hence predictive-processing accounts of selfhood are false, and the self must be a non-physical substance whose essence is known directly and independently of the body.
(9) Yet if the self is such a substance, capable of existing and knowing itself without any embodied structure, then there is no principled reason why it could not persist through radical bodily change, be transferred between substrates, or exist in disembodied simulation.
(10) Thus the flying-man argument, if sound, not only undermines embodied cognition but renders personal identity radically indeterminate across physical substrates.
(11) I regard (9)–(10) as intolerable, and I am strongly inclined to preserve (1)–(3). Which step ought I to reject, and what does rejecting it cost for my interpretation of Avicenna's argument?
