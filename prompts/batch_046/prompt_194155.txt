I'm trying to work out my actual commitments on preference idealization—specifically, what counts as part of a person's or group's coherent extrapolated volition versus what should be filtered out as corruption, instability, or strategic distortion—before I attempt any formalization. I keep testing my intuitions against concrete cases, and I want to know whether there's a unifying principle or whether I'm just holding contradictory gut reactions.

Here are my verdicts. First: an individual who, in calm reflection, endorses longevity but consistently chooses sugar; I treat the reflective endorsement as closer to volition. Second: a democratic polity that votes for carbon-intensive policy, yet whose citizens, when surveyed in isolation about climate risks, strongly prefer decarbonization; I treat the isolated preferences as closer to volition, on the grounds that collective-action framing corrupts revealed preference. Third: someone whose career preference reverses after reading a single persuasive essay—unless the essay only corrected a factual error—I discount the new preference as unstable or manipulated. Fourth: by contrast, someone who comes to genuinely enjoy atonal music after six months of sustained exposure, despite initial dislike, seems to have formed a legitimate preference, not suffered corruption. Fifth: a group of rational agents who, through transparent non-coercive discussion, converge on an ethical framework none initially held; I treat that convergence as potentially closer to shared volition than any starting point. But there's a case that splits me: a community satisfied with a hierarchical structure for generations, where an external analyst demonstrates that egalitarian alternatives dominate by every welfare metric the community endorses abstractly. Is their satisfaction with hierarchy adaptive preference formation that should be corrected in extrapolation, or is it a legitimate local equilibrium to be respected?

Can you state a precise principle—one that could guide a formal model, not merely a slogan—that captures these verdicts, including the hard reconciliation between democratic distortion and collective convergence, and between induced aesthetic change and induced career reversal? It should say what it predicts for the hierarchical-community case, and be honest about which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate without ad hoc adjustments.
