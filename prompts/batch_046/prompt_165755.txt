Write a poem in exactly five numbered sections (1 through 5), with no introduction or conclusion outside the numbering, each section consisting of a single sentence of unrhymed iambic pentameter broken across four to six lines, on the discursive dilemma in judgment aggregation. The scenario is a five-member ethics committee that must decide whether a particular research protocol is unjustified, judging separately on whether subjects were inadequately informed, whether risks exceeded benefits, and whether unjustified research requires both conditions together; each member holds these premises consistently, yet majority aggregation on each premise conflicts with the majority vote on the final conclusion. Section 1 should establish each member’s private logical consistency; section 2 should show the separate premise-level majorities; section 3 should reveal the contradictory conclusion-level vote; section 4 should stage the forced choice between premise-based coherence and conclusion-based democratic equality, including one member who, anticipating the aggregation rule, strategically reverses a premise judgment; and section 5 should leave the contradiction suspended, letting the formal tension stand without resolving whether coherence or equality should prevail. The line breaks should fall at logical rather than merely grammatical pauses where possible, and the strategic reversal should feel structurally inevitable rather than narratively explained.
