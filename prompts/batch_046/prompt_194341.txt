At a workshop on historical method, two historians disagreed about how to treat the 1976 United States swine-flu immunisation campaign within a survey of twentieth-century public health. Dr Elena Voss argued that framing the campaign as a 'step towards' modern pandemic preparedness is textbook Whig history: it treats today's risk-communication and rapid-deployment regimes as inevitable endpoints, suppresses the documented cases of Guillain-Barré syndrome that followed the rushed rollout, and ignores the fact that the feared epidemic never materialised. Dr Marcus Chen replied that refusing to recognise institutional learning in the episode is equally distorting: the failures of 1976 directly reshaped FDA oversight and liability protocols, and a narrative that treats every dead end as pure error renders historical change unintelligible. Voss countered that 'learning' is merely a retrospective judgement imposed by survivors; from the standpoint of those injured by the vaccine, the campaign was not a lesson but a harm, and privileging the survivors' institutional perspective is precisely the Whig move. Chen answered that every historical explanation selects a standpoint, and that treating measurable improvement—such as the long-term decline in infectious-disease mortality—as nothing more than subjective projection denies reality itself.
