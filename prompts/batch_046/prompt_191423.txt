I am writing a novel in which a philosopher of science, convinced her research programme has turned degenerating, writes a poem in her notebook. Write it for me: a modified sestina—six stanzas of six lines, then a three-line envoi—using the end-words core, belt, predict, accommodate, novel, and decay. Lock core at the end of line one in every stanza, breaking the standard rotation to represent the hard core's immunity; let the other five rotate through positions two through six in the classic 6-1-5-2-4-3 pattern, enacting the protective belt's adjustments. The poem should render Lakatos's distinction between progressive and degenerating research programmes structurally rather than didactically: the rotating words should feel like auxiliary hypotheses shifting to save the fixed term, predictions collapsing into post-hoc accommodations, and the envoi should place core beside decay without resolving whether the programme survives. Make it precise enough that a philosopher of science recognizes the machinery, but let the form carry the argument.
