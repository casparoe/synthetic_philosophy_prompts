In a seminar on public memory, two researchers debated a local case: a highland community maintains that its ancestors built an extensive network of underground water channels long before colonial engineers arrived and documented similar structures in imperial surveys. The community seeks to have the ancestral-engineering narrative featured as the primary account in the regional museum.

Researcher 1 argued: (1) The stratigraphic and hydraulic evidence points clearly to colonial-era construction; presenting the ancestral claim as primary teaches falsehood for the sake of political comfort. (2) Even granting that colonial archives are biased, the remedy is better archival science, not substituting unverified oral tradition—otherwise any community could override documented fact with identity-based assertions. (3) Collective memory serves social cohesion, but institutional history owes fidelity to the record; in public education, accuracy must take precedence over belonging.

Researcher 2 responded: (4) Labeling the oral account 'unverified' already presupposes that colonial reports are epistemically neutral, when in fact they systematically erased indigenous labor to justify territorial claims. (5) The request is not to erase archives but to exercise mnemonic sovereignty—the authority of a community to govern representations of its own past—because history serves the living rather than dead documents. (6) Privileging 'archival realism' over lived transmission reproduces the very epistemic framework that generated the biased record.

Adjudicate each move (1–6): rate it from clearly mistaken to clearly right and briefly justify the rating. Then identify what both researchers miss about how collective memory and institutional history should interact when they conflict. Present the adjudication as a table with columns: Move, Speaker, Rating, Justification.
