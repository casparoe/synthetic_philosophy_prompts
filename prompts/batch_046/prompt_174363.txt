I sit on my university's philosophy of science advisory board, and we have issued preliminary rulings on four conceptual cases concerning rotational motion and its explanation. Our track record is as follows: we held that in an otherwise empty universe the concavity of water in a rotating bucket demonstrates absolute rotation; we accepted that two isolated bodies orbiting one another require only relational motion with no appeal to absolute space; we endorsed Mach's principle that a bucket rotating relative to distant, uniformly distributed stars exhibits concavity because of that relation; and we ruled that a body rotating inside a stationary, massive hollow shell can be explained relationally by reference to the shell's presence. We now face a proposal describing a hypothetical universe containing no discrete bodies, boundaries, or directional asymmetries, but filled everywhere with a continuous, isotropic, non-interacting field that occupies space without exerting gravitational or electromagnetic influence. The applicant argues that this field provides the necessary relational partner to explain rotational concavity without invoking absolute space; critics reply that its perfect isotropy renders it relationally inert, leaving substantivalism as the only explanation of the observed behaviour. Predict the recommendation our board should issue if we wish to remain consistent with our earlier rulings, identify the two competing principles that best account for our track record, and specify which further hypothetical case would distinguish between them. Please begin with your bottom-line recommendation in the first sentence, then present the reasoning with no preamble and without restating the question.
