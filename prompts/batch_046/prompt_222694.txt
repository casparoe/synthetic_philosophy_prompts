I work as a software engineer designing decision-support systems, and I have been trying to understand rational-choice theory well enough to think about how algorithms ought to handle preference updates over time. In that literature I keep encountering a debate that feels stuck: philosophers invoke "money pumps"—sequences of trades that exploit cyclic preferences to extract value from an agent—to argue that rationality requires transitive preferences. The diachronic version of the problem asks whether an agent whose preferences genuinely change between Monday and Tuesday—because of new information, revised values, or temporal context—remains irrational if she can be pumped across that interval. One camp seems to treat any vulnerability to a pump, regardless of time span, as proof of an inconsistent preference ordering. The other camp treats diachronic preference revision as normatively permissible and argues that labeling the revised agent irrational mistakes a legitimate state transition for a logical contradiction—analogous, perhaps, to calling a database inconsistent because a cached value was updated according to a valid rule.

This disagreement appears to have persisted for decades in essentially these terms, and I want to understand its structure rather than pick a winner. My working hypothesis is that the sides are not contesting a single concept of rationality but are answering different architectural questions: one about static consistency of a preference relation at a given slice (like schema validation), the other about normative transition rules between mental or algorithmic states (like state-machine governance). Is that diagnosis correct, or is there a hidden empirical claim—say, about whether human preference change is always evidence-driven—doing the actual work? Could the stall instead be verbal, turning on whether "preference" denotes an occurrent mental state or a dispositional pattern? Or are both sides relying on bedrock intuitions about coherence or naturalness that neither can defend?

Please diagnose the disagreement concretely: state what each side actually takes the money-pump argument to establish, identify whether they share premises they fail to recognize, and specify whether they differ about facts, evidential standards, explanatory aims, or the framing question itself. Then tell me what, given your diagnosis, would genuinely advance the debate—a formal result on dynamic consistency, a principled distinction among kinds of preference change, an empirical finding about reasoning, a reconceptualization of the agent architecture—or whether the impasse signals that the original question is ill-posed.
