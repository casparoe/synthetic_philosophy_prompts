After a long and rather frustrating argument with a friend about whether universities genuinely practise the open-mindedness they preach, I have become interested in what our research institutions actually presuppose about belief, justification, and knowledge. The pattern is this: funding panels and tenure committees reward scholars who maintain a coherent, long-term research programme and treat frequent theoretical reversals as a mark of unreliability; pre-registration protocols in experimental fields require investigators to lock in hypotheses and analysis plans before seeing data, with deviations treated as suspect; yet the same institutions teach that rational belief ought to be proportioned to available evidence and revised without embarrassment, and they celebrate revolutionary paradigm shifts in the abstract. What view of epistemic commitment does this institutional design presuppose? Does it rely on a distinction between pragmatic or 'working' commitments — which must be held firmly for inquiry to proceed — and reflective beliefs, which remain open to revision, and if so, does that amount to a tacit resolution of the dogmatism paradox? Or does it presuppose that knowledge necessarily rests on a stable framework of background assumptions that cannot simultaneously be questioned? Reconstruct the implicit epistemology here, distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what merely accommodates it, say whether pre-registration, peer evaluation, and pedagogical rhetoric presuppose consistent things, and explain what an institution genuinely committed to fallibilism in every domain would have to change. Please write your answer in British English.
