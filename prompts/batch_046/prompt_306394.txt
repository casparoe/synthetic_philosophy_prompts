You are the senior scientific reviewer for a national biosafety board that evaluates, approximately every two months, applications for open-environment release of engineered microbial consortia designed to accelerate biodegradation of persistent pollutants in freshwater systems. Each review must be completed within ten working days using only applicant-submitted data; you must sign a determination of deny, approve with mandatory redesign conditions, or approve with monitoring obligations; and failure to document your reasoning exposes the board to legal challenge. The board operates under a specific interpretation of the precautionary principle that separates epistemic uncertainty (how large or probable is harm?) from ontological reversibility (can the ecological state be restored in kind, not merely in scale?). You need a reusable, staff-runnable decision procedure—ordered questions or tests in sequence, with stopping rules, sufficient-answer criteria, and clearly marked points where judgment is unavoidable—that applies this principle without requiring philosophical expertise. The procedure must treat irreversibility-in-kind as a hard structural threshold: if crossed, precautionary redesign is mandatory regardless of probability estimates. Magnitude uncertainty, by contrast, should be bounded by data but acknowledged as never fully eliminated. Apply the procedure to three cases: (a) a well-characterized consortium for a small enclosed lagoon with rapid dilution and documented baseline toxicity; (b) a poorly characterized consortium proposed for a flowing river with endemic endangered species and no credible containment boundary; and (c) a moderately characterized closed-loop pilot that contains an accidental open-release contingency clause, where the procedure should logically mandate redesign of that clause—yielding a restrictive outcome you might personally resist but must accept. Conclude by stating what the procedure explicitly cannot do, particularly regarding conflicts between scientific reversibility assessments and societal risk tolerance.
