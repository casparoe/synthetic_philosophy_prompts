I hold the view that clinical reports of pain asymbolia — in which subjects report intact pain sensations yet show neither aversion nor avoidance behaviour — undermine both standard affectivist and pure sensory-discriminative explanations of why pain is bad, and instead support what I call the volitional-decoupling thesis. On this account, the harmfulness of pain is constituted neither by its qualitative sensory profile nor by any brute affective push, but by a specific functional integration — call it evaluative transparency — between the sensory representation and an involuntary, action-guiding commitment that the subject cannot suspend without altering the experience itself. The asymbolic, on this reading, genuinely instantiates the sensory and even sub-personal affective markers of pain, yet because the decoupling mechanism disrupts the feedback loop that binds representation to volitional judgement, no normative harm occurs. I want an honest map of what would genuinely count against this position. Which empirical findings about dissociation conditions in which affect is preserved but integration is disrupted in the opposite direction would compel abandonment rather than refinement? Which conceptual distinctions — for example, a sharper separation between personal-level judgement and sub-personal registration — would reveal the decoupling as either incoherent or reducible to a simpler affective deficit? What predictions does the thesis make that rival accounts do not, particularly concerning interventions that restore or suppress evaluative transparency without altering sensory quality? Which of these potential defeaters would a committed defender concede as genuinely damaging, and which could be dismissed or absorbed by qualification? Rank them from fatal to merely awkward, and address whether the view risks being qualified into unfalsifiability — and, if so, whether that insulation is a vice for a claim about the nature of harm.
