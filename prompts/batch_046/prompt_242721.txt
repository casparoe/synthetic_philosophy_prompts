I am developing a conceptual essay on the difficulty of separating moral preferences from implicit biases, with a particular focus on why the standard reflective-equilibrium test fails to discriminate between them. The core claim introduces a mechanism I call epistemic insulation: a selective discounting of disconfirming evidence in domains where rapid, unexamined judgment is required for practical social coordination. I argue that biases are protected by this insulation because they operate precisely within those embedded practical domains, whereas genuine moral preferences—especially those concerning abstract normative principles—tend to be destabilized by the same mechanism, since they lack the immediate practical feedback loops that would otherwise shield them from revision. I apply this framework to well-known framing effects in trolley-style dilemmas, where altering group-identity salience produces systematic shifts in judgment that subjects consistently report as principled rather than prejudicial.

Please provide comments on the accuracy and philosophical coherence of this argument. In particular: (1) Does epistemic insulation genuinely distinguish preferences from biases, or does it merely relocate the original problem by redescribing stability? (2) Are there important considerations from moral psychology—accessible without specialist literature or external lookup—that suggest insulation protects genuine preferences as often as it protects biases? (3) Is the separation between practical-success domains and abstract-normative domains defensible, or does it presuppose an implausible partition within moral cognition?
