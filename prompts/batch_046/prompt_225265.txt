I am trying to map the conceptual landscape of public-memory revision, and I have repeatedly encountered a specific asymmetrical pattern that I do not know how to classify. A society or institution removes every visual and sculptural trace of a historical figure—statues dismantled, portraits withdrawn from public halls, murals erased—while leaving intact every non-pictorial reference to the same figure: streets, bridges, universities, and administrative districts retain the original name. Those who defend the retention of the names typically argue that a toponym or institutional title functions as a neutral geographic or organizational label, lacking the explicit honorific force of a monument in a central square. Meanwhile, those who supported the visual removals often treat the statue, not the street sign, as the locus of moral endorsement. The result is not a uniform policy of retention or removal, but a split field: the figure becomes linguistically present and navigationally available, yet visually absent from shared civic space.

I am familiar with the general distinction between honoring and remembering, and with debates over whether commemorative objects should be relocated to museums rather than destroyed. But I am asking about the precise configuration in which iconographic representation is systematically eliminated while onomastic reference is deliberately preserved. Does this asymmetry have an established name in the ethics of commemoration, political philosophy, or memorial studies? How should it be differentiated from uniform retention, uniform removal, or the museum-relocation model? If no existing concept isolates this pattern, I would like it characterized precisely: what is its internal logic, under what conditions is it stable rather than incoherent, and what are its boundary cases—say, when the retained name is itself a prominent building rather than a distant street? I am not looking for a list of cases or a reading list; I want to know whether the phenomenon is already carved out, and if not, where its edges lie.
