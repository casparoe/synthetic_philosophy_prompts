Examination question: Keith Frankish's illusionism maintains that phenomenal consciousness—what philosophers often call "what it is like"—is not a real feature of mental life but rather a kind of introspective misrepresentation. Examine this claim with particular attention to (i) its relationship to David Chalmers's formulation of the hard problem of consciousness, and (ii) its use of Ned Block's distinction between access consciousness and phenomenal consciousness.

Student answer: Illusionism, as articulated by Keith Frankish and anticipated in Daniel Dennett's earlier rejection of the Cartesian theater, grants that human beings possess access consciousness in Block's precise sense: information is globally broadcast and available for reasoning and verbal report. However, illusionists deny that there exists any additional phenomenal consciousness constituted by intrinsic qualitative properties or qualia. When we introspect, we do not detect real phenomenal properties; instead, our cognitive architecture generates a false representation of ourselves as bearing such properties. This means that Chalmers's hard problem can be solved rather than merely dissolved: once neuroscience explains the mechanisms that produce this introspective illusion, it has explained everything there is to explain about consciousness, including why phenomenal consciousness seems irreducible. Block originally developed his distinction between access and phenomenal consciousness precisely to provide the conceptual framework for illusionism, since it allows the illusionist to accept all genuinely explanatory phenomena—access, reportability, rational integration—while treating phenomenal consciousness as a theoretical fiction. Critics, including Galen Strawson, have charged that illusionism is self-undermining, because the very act of denying phenomenal consciousness presupposes the phenomenal awareness being denied; illusionists respond that this objection mistakes access consciousness for phenomenal consciousness. Although illusionism is sometimes presented as a fringe view, it is now broadly regarded within naturalistic philosophy of mind as the default resolution of the mind-body problem, and it avoids the metaphysical excesses of dualism and panpsychism alike.
