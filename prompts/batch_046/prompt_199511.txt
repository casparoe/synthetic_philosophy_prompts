I'm serving as a TA for a graduate seminar on comparative moral philosophy, and I've been asked to assess a short exam response on Buddhist ethics. The prompt given to the students was this: "Some philosophers argue that Buddhist ethics is fundamentally incompatible with consequentialism because it evaluates actions by the mental factor of cetana (intention) rather than by outcomes. Others reply that the doctrine of karma—according to which intentional deeds generate vipaka (fruits) that shape future experience—makes Buddhism implicitly consequentialist, at least about the agent's own stream across rebirths. Evaluate whether Buddhist ethics can be consistently interpreted as a form of consequentialism. In your answer, discuss cetana, karma and vipaka, the Bodhisattva's use of upaya (skillful means), and the doctrine of anatta (no-self)."

Here is the answer I need graded, reproduced in full:

"Buddhist ethics is centered on cetana, the volitional impulse that the Abhidhamma identifies as the actual root of karma. Because the moral quality of an act is fixed by the mental state that produces it, Buddhism rejects outcome-based evaluation at the level of individual deeds: if a monk gives alms with pure generosity but the recipient uses the gift to harm others, the monk's act remains fully meritorious, since the harmful result does not infect the original intention. At the same time, the law of karma is structurally consequentialist, because every intentional action produces vipaka—experiential fruits that accumulate over the cycle of rebirth. A rational practitioner should therefore calculate which patterns of action will maximize wholesome results across many lives, which makes Buddhist ethics a long-run, agent-relative form of consequentialism focused on one's own causal stream. The Bodhisattva path in Mahayana sources confirms this interpretation: through upaya, a Bodhisattva may break standard precepts—lying, for example, to mislead a murderer—whenever the suffering prevented clearly outweighs the breach of the rule. This is essentially a utilitarian calculus applied within a Buddhist framework. The doctrine of anatta, however, introduces a serious tension. If there is no enduring self, then there is no stable subject who owns these future fruits, so the prudential reasoning that motivates long-run karmic planning collapses. Consequently, Buddhist ethics is best understood as a hybrid: intention fixes the immediate moral status of a particular act, while consequentialist reasoning governs the strategic, life-spanning choice of which mental habits and actions to cultivate."
