I want the full specification for what a theory of historical teleology—specifically, the claim that history exhibits a directional arc bending toward justice—must deliver to count as adequate, rather than as retrospective narrative smoothing. The theory’s purpose is explanatory: it must distinguish a genuine directional tendency from path-dependence smoothing, in which contingency is erased once an outcome is fixed and the past is read as convergent.

Write the desiderata. For each, include the motivating case or constraint and flag whether it is non-negotiable or a camp-specific preference: the theory must accommodate prolonged moral regress without ad hoc exemptions; it must preserve cross-domain incommensurability (progress in legal rights, material welfare, and ecological stability need not align); it must handle near-miss counterfactuals in which justice clearly fails to advance; it must distinguish diachronic inevitability from a synchronic attractor and separate “having a direction” from “having a predetermined endpoint”; and it must remain consistent with strong historical contingency, the absence of a universal metric for justice, and the sociological fact that progressive narratives often serve present coalition-building.

Then address two structural questions. First, whether any subset of these desiderata is jointly unsatisfiable—particularly whether requiring both robust contingency and a non-accidental directional pull creates an internal tension that no formulation can resolve. Second, identify the single requirement that, if enforced strictly, eliminates the largest share of popular accounts (Whig, Hegelian, evolutionary-progress, or Rawlsian realistic-utopia narratives), and briefly explain why that requirement is fatal to them. Keep the weight on the criteria and their motivating distinctions rather than on scoring the contenders.
