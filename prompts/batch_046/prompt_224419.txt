I retired from biophysics a couple years ago and lately I've been sitting in on a philosophy meetup near my house, mostly to keep my brain from turning to mush. Last week we got onto Buddhist ethics and whether it's really consequentialist, and two regulars — Raj and Lena — got stuck in a back-and-forth that I couldn't quite referee. I'm writing it out here because I want someone to go through it move by move.

Raj said Buddhism is fundamentally about ending suffering, so every rule is just a heuristic. He imagined a Buddhist doctor with a dying patient who asks if she's going to die; telling the truth causes terror and hastens death, while a gentle lie brings peace. Raj said the compassionate lie is clearly right because it minimizes dukkha, and treating the precept against false speech as absolute is like treating a scientific model as true regardless of data — it's importing a deontological rigidity the Buddha never intended. Lena pushed back using Abhidhammic karma analysis: the ethical weight of an act is fixed by the root mental states present in the volition — greed, hatred, delusion — not by a later tally of suffering reduced. She argued false speech has a double root: the cognitive distortion of moha (misapprehending reality) and the affective attachment of lobha (manipulating the world to secure a desired outcome), so even a benevolent lie feeds unwholesome roots in the speaker's mind. The purpose of sila, she said, is citta-visuddhi — intrinsic mental purification — not engineering external results.

Raj came back with Mahayana upaya, where bodhisattvas break precepts for compassion, and with the Kalama Sutta, where the Buddha advises testing teachings by whether they lead to harm or welfare. If outcomes can override rules in the tradition itself, he said, then Buddhist ethics is consequentialist in its advanced form. Lena replied that upaya concerns adapting teachings, not general ethical license; the extreme stories require perfect wisdom, not ordinary cost-benefit reasoning. And the Kalama Sutta checks whether teachings increase greed and hatred inside the practitioner, not whether they compute external suffering. Raj then asked for a principaled line that doesn't just reduce to 'truth is always better because I said so,' and suggested that if lying genuinely left the speaker's mind cleaner and reduced net suffering, there's no Buddhist reason to forbid it. Lena said that collapses Buddhist practice into utilitarianism and loses what makes it distinctive: mental states are not instruments for outcomes, they are the primary field of ethical work.

They asked me to adjudicate and I honestly froze — my gut says both are partly right, but I don't know how to weigh the mental-state analysis against the outcome focus. Can you go through there exchange move by move, rating each step from clearly mistaken to clearly right with brief reasons? Then say who's more right overall, and what's missing — especially whether there's a middle path that respects cetana without treating outcomes as irrelevant. Also please say what would change your mind: which argument or textual point, if firmly established, would flip your verdict? And be honest about where your reasoning is most likely to fail — I don't want fake certaintly, since philosophy has more hidden traps than a badly calibrated instrument.
