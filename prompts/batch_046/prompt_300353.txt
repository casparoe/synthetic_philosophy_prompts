In a seminar on comparative philosophy of mind, a colleague keeps invoking the Ubuntu maxim umuntu ngumuntu ngabantu—usually rendered “a person is a person through other people”—to dismiss the idea that cognitive autonomy is a meaningful prerequisite for moral agency. She treats the slogan as if it settles the matter: since personhood is relational, any theory that takes the individual mind as the basic unit of moral evaluation is not merely wrong but conceptually incoherent. I want to bring a precisified menu to the next session rather than just object. Could you lay out the distinct precise theses that slogan could be expressing, ordered from the trivial to the ontologically radical? For each reading, specify exactly what kind of “through” is doing the work—causal, constitutive, normative, or epistemic—and say whether it is a platitude, a defensible substantive claim, or false. In particular, distinguish claims that social relations are necessary for the development of personhood from claims that they are necessary for its continued existence or for its ontological completeness, and flag any reading that smuggles in a metaphysics of collective minds or distributed cognition that the proverb itself does not entail. Then indicate which of these readings, if any, actually license her move—dismissing cognitive autonomy as incoherent rather than merely undesirable—versus which only establish that autonomy is socially scaffolded or normatively enriched. I need this as a list I can take to office hours, with the residue reported: what the slogan genuinely proves, what it leaves untouched, and where her argument needs an extra premise she has not supplied.
