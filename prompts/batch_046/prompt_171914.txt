Write a dialogue between two equally competent philosophers—one a Hofstadter-inspired pattern pluralist who holds that an "I" is a self-referential pattern capable of persisting across substrates, and one a substrate singularist who accepts deep mental integration but denies that a single self can genuinely be distributed across two living brains. Frame the disagreement around a concrete institutional case: an IRB is reviewing a proposed six-month trial of bilateral synaptic-bridge implants that merge working-memory buffers between paired volunteers, with asymmetric degradation—one partner's hippocampal traces remain intact while the other's are gradually overwritten by shared representations. The pluralist should argue that if the merged system exhibits unified diachronic agency and reflexive self-reference, there is no principled boundary preventing both partners from being partial substrates of one distributed "I"; the singularist must respond by explaining why continuity of one substrate still anchors identity even under full informational symmetry, specifically addressing whether the death of one partner destroys half a person or ends one person entirely, and whether pre-implant consent binds the post-merge system. Require real back-and-forth: each speaker must revise or sharpen their position in direct response to the other's actual point, not talk past each other. Keep the reasoning precise, the distinctions sharp, and the tone appropriate for a longtime rationalist audience that expects arguments about diachronic rationality and personal identity to be engineered rather than rhetorical.
