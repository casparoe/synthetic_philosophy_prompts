Since accepting a position on the ethics review board for a transatlantic consortium testing autonomous navigation systems, I have found myself mediating a philosophical dispute between two colleagues—Dr. Müller (Munich) and Ms. Callahan (Chicago)—that has become personally unavoidable. The conflict concerns not policy details but the moral status of procedural rules themselves, and it crystallized around a concrete case. A testing protocol mandated a forty-eight-hour cooling-off period before any adjustment to a vehicle's neural-network weighting parameter, a safeguard designed to prevent impulsive alterations. During a live trial, a technician discovered that delaying an adjustment by even six hours would expose a volunteer passenger to an unnecessary collision risk, and requested an immediate waiver. Dr. Müller insisted the protocol be observed without exception; Ms. Callahan insisted that conscience required overriding it.

Their exchange unfolded as follows.

(1) Müller: The rule is not a prediction about outcomes but a commitment made to every participant: no parameter will be altered without the full cooling-off period. To waive it in this case, however beneficial the result, is to break that commitment. The wrongness lies in violating the promise embedded in the rule itself.

(2) Callahan: That treats the rule as an end rather than a means. The commitment was made to protect people; if literal adherence now harms one, then keeping the rule intact is a kind of fetishism—valuing the procedural symbol over the human reality it was designed to safeguard.

(3) Müller: But without the unconditional form—without the symbol—there is no safeguard at all. Once you permit ad hoc exceptions based on individual judgment about harm, you replace a shared, predictable standard with private discretion, and you destroy the very trust that makes the commitment meaningful.

(4) Callahan: Predictability matters, but not infinitely. There is a difference between a system that permits reasoned, accountable exceptions and one that demands ritual obedience. In the American folk-moral tradition, individual conscience functions as the final court of appeal precisely because rules are fallible human artifacts that cannot anticipate every moral urgency.

(5) Müller: And in the corresponding German folk tradition, conscience is considered unreliable precisely because it is shaped by immediate emotion, proximity bias, and self-interest. The rule exists to discipline conscience, not to be disciplined by it. To place private feeling above the agreed structure is not moral courage but moral solipsism—substituting one's own judgment for a shared order.

They have asked me to adjudicate, not merely to split the difference. Please go through the exchange move by move, rating each argument from clearly mistaken to clearly right with brief justification. Then say who is more right overall about the particular case, and address the broader comparative question: is the German emphasis on rule-following as a moral virtue genuinely superior, inferior, or incommensurable with the American emphasis on individual conscience as the ultimate check? Finally, identify anything both parties are missing—perhaps a hidden agreement, or a distinction that would dissolve part of their conflict.
