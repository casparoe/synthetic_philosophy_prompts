I am writing a dissertation chapter on comparative value judgments, and I need a disciplined conceptual comparison rather than a literature survey. Compare the standard rational-choice defense of transitivity for the relation 'better than'—according to which intransitive pairwise rankings reveal a failure of practical rationality—with the spectrum-argument position that gradual qualitative change can generate genuine, non-pathological intransitivity without any mistake in reasoning. Focus your comparison on three axes. First, the source of failure: does the intransitivity arise from a logical property of the ordering relation, from ontological vagueness in the properties being compared, or from an epistemic limitation of the comparing agent? Second, the role of aggregation: does the spectrum argument implicitly rely on an additive aggregation principle—such that a sequence of imperceptible improvements sums to a perceptible change—and if so, can it survive the rejection of interpersonally comparable utility units or cardinal welfare metrics? Third, practical deliberation: what should an agent do when faced with a closed loop of pairwise preferences (A over B, B over C, C over A), and does either view have resources to recommend a stable choice without smuggling in an external arbitration rule? Finally, evaluate whether a middle position—call it domain-localized intransitivity, where transitivity holds within sharply bounded qualitative domains but systematically fails at the boundaries—is conceptually coherent, and whether either of the two main views can accommodate it without ad hoc stipulation. Be precise about where each view is silent rather than forcing it to speak.
