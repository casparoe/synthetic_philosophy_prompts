I am drafting a novel in which the central figure serves as a probabilistic archivist in a near-future institution, tasked with assigning survival and failure rates to individuals by sorting them into statistical reference classes—demographic, occupational, behavioral—only to discover that every subject belongs to contradictory classes yielding incompatible predictions. A philosophy-minded colleague summarized the conceptual difficulty she calls the reference class problem in these terms: probability is always relative to a description, and since any particular event can be classified in infinitely many ways, there is never an objective fact of the matter about its likelihood until one arbitrarily selects a class; consequently, objective risk assessment is impossible, Bayesian methods are useless because they demand a prior that cannot be justified without circularity, and the only remedy is to restrict all reasoning to the narrowest class for which data exist, which explains why personalized medicine succeeds where population statistics collapse.

Something in this account strikes me as more defeatist than the literature warrants, and I am unsure whether she is describing a genuine ambiguity in statistical reasoning or smuggling in a stronger skepticism than philosophers of science and epistemologists actually endorse. Would you audit this summary carefully? I would like you to distinguish what accurately captures the problem—particularly the tension between competing descriptions and the challenge it poses to objective probabilities—from what amounts to common simplification or overstatement, and to identify which claims a defender of probabilistic inference would reject as misrepresentations. Specifically, does her conclusion that Bayesian reasoning is undermined and that the narrowest available class is the correct remedy follow from the reference class ambiguity itself, or does she conflate this problem with a different failure of statistical inference? I am also interested in whether the difficulty operates symmetrically for first-person predictions—such as my archivist estimating her own survival—versus third-person predictions about others, since her summary treats both cases identically, yet I suspect that considerations of self-locating belief might alter the structure in ways her account ignores.
