I'm moderating a podcast interview on the ontology of legal rules, and my guest is an unnamed philosopher who has recently articulated a view she calls substrate-indexed constitutionalism. She contends that the well-known paradox of self-amendment in Peter Suber's Nomic framework—where a provision governing constitutional change appears to undermine its own authority precisely when it is exercised—arises only under a flat ontology that treats all rules as symbolic strings on a single plane. Instead, she distinguishes the surface rule (the explicit textual clause) from its interpretive substrate (the institutional or logical conditions that determine validity at the moment of application), and she argues that amendments should be indexed to substrate depth: a change enacted from a deeper layer can legitimately alter a surface-layer rule without contradiction, because the authority for the change resides outside the modified rule's own scope.

I want a sequence of interview questions that begins by pressing her to specify exactly what separates a genuine substrate-level amendment from a mere reinterpretation of the existing text, then moves to the standard objection that she has not solved the paradox but only relocated it downward, with a follow-up asking whether the substrate nodes themselves are subject to amendment and, if not, what grounds their immunity, or if so, whether an infinite regress or an arbitrary fixed point is unavoidable. The sequence should conclude with a question about what specific institutional failure or logical result could actually count against substrate-indexed constitutionalism—rather than simply motivating the addition of another substrate layer—and should anticipate the deflections she is likely to use, particularly the reply that counterexamples are unfinished research programs rather than genuine objections.
