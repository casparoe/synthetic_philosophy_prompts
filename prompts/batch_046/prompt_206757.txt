I recently heard a summary—apparently drawn from an essay on the ethics of historical comparison—of the view that ranking atrocities is not merely difficult but conceptually obscene. The summary claims that anyone who says the Holocaust was worse than another genocide is implicitly treating human suffering as fungible currency, measurable on a single scale; that defenders of comparative judgment are therefore committed to applying a utilitarian calculus to mass violence; and that because the qualitative dimensions of different atrocities are incommensurable, any ordinal ranking commits a category error rather than expressing a moral truth. From these premises the summary concludes that we should reject all comparative language about historical evils and adopt a posture of absolute, non-comparative remembrance. I suspect this bundles together several distinct arguments and may misrepresent what philosophers who talk about incommensurability actually mean. Which parts of this characterization are faithful to serious debates about moral comparison, which parts conflate quantitative measurement with qualitative uniqueness or misrepresent the commitments of comparative judgment, and does the conclusion—that comparison must be wholly abandoned—follow once those distinctions are kept clear?
