Here is an argument I built for a seminar on collective intentionality and now find suspicious because the conclusion is far stronger than I intended.

(1) If two agents are genuinely engaged in a joint action J, then each is rationally committed to the success conditions of J as the other understands them, unless an explicit withdrawal has occurred.
(2) Explicit withdrawal must be a communicative act that is mutually recognised by both parties as terminating that specific commitment.
(3) In sustained joint actions—say, co-administering a research archive over several years—the success conditions are not fully fixed in advance; they are filled in gradually through evolving practice.
(4) Consequently, partner A’s conception of what success requires can expand (for instance, from “catalogue the holdings” to “digitise the entire back-catalogue and migrate formats”) without A having performed any explicit withdrawal from the original commitment.
(5) If B adopts this expanded conception and A is aware of B’s adoption yet continues to participate, then by (1) and (2) A is now committed to the expanded conditions.
(6) This reasoning applies iteratively: each further layer of practice can expand the conditions, and in the absence of explicit withdrawal A remains bound to the latest, broadest version.
(7) Hence either A issues explicit withdrawals at every stage of expansion—which dissolves the joint action altogether—or A is committed to an open-ended, ever-growing set of obligations.
(8) But sustained collaboration does not normally turn into unlimited, unspecified servitude; therefore the conjunction of (1) and (2) must be false.

I should like to preserve (2) if I can, since the idea that withdrawal needs to be explicit and mutually recognised strikes me as correct. Still, I cannot locate the slip between (4) and (7). Is there an equivocation on “success conditions” or a hidden quantifier shift that lets (6) iterate illegitimately? Or is the flaw earlier—perhaps in (1), where commitment to the other’s understanding is mischaracterised? Tell me which premise or inferential step to reject, explain why it looked defensible, and what giving it up costs for standard accounts such as Bratman’s, Gilbert’s, or Searle’s.
