I am currently enrolled in an upper-division philosophy of mind seminar, and we have been examining Douglas Hofstadter's proposal that the self is a strange loop—a pattern that can, in principle, be distributed across more than one brain rather than locked inside a single skull. I want to use this framework to structure a research proposal, but I need a defended agenda rather than a literature survey.

Specifically: if we take seriously the idea that an 'I' can be partly instantiated in the neural architecture of others, which open conceptual questions should be treated as upstream priorities, and which are downstream or overrated? I am particularly interested in the distinction between loop-closure conditions—the causal-functional requirements that allow a distributed pattern to maintain its identity as one loop rather than fragmenting into two—and resonance conditions—the representational or symbolic overlap that allows one brain to carry part of another's self-pattern without necessarily completing the loop.

Please prioritize two or three lines of inquiry arising from this tension. For each, defend its rank by explaining: (a) what other questions depend on its resolution; (b) how tractable it is for purely conceptual analysis without reliance on empirical neuroscience; and (c) what conceptual result—not an empirical finding—would cause you to reorder the priorities. I am especially interested in whether the most important work lies in personal identity over time, in the ethics of cognitive extension and brain-computer interfaces that might externalize parts of a loop, or in the philosophy of language—specifically whether first-person reference can remain singular when the referent loop is genuinely distributed.

This is for a term-length project, not a dissertation, so I want to know where a semester's effort actually moves something, not just which questions are interesting in principle.
