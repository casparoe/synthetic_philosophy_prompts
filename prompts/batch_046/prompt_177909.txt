The exam prompt was: “Philip Pettit’s republican theory holds that freedom is non-domination rather than mere non-interference. Consider a municipal ‘behavioral-optimization’ system in which an unaccountable algorithm sets real-time public-transit fares according to an opaque welfare-maximization function. Citizens may appeal to a human review board, but appeals are permitted only after implementation, and in five years the board has never overturned an algorithmic decision. Analyze whether this arrangement violates republican liberty, negative liberty, or both. In doing so, distinguish carefully between interference, domination, and arbitrary power, and explain why Pettit believes this distinction carries weight for democratic legitimacy.”

The student’s answer follows. It correctly identifies Berlin’s framing of negative liberty as absence of actual interference, recognizes the benevolent-master structure in the algorithmic case, and sees that republican liberty concerns the structure of power rather than only its exercise. Nevertheless, it contains one or two subtle mistakes that a hurried grader might overlook: it imports a justification condition into negative liberty (treating benevolent purpose as neutralizing an interference-based violation), and it mischaracterizes Pettit’s notion of arbitrary power as merely “not tracking welfare metrics” rather than power not forced to track the common avowable interests of those subject to it, which slightly distorts the democratic-legitimacy argument. There are no glaring factual errors.

Student answer: “Negative liberty, as Berlin frames it, is the absence of actual interference in an agent’s choices. The algorithm clearly interferes by altering fares, so negative liberty is reduced. However, because the algorithm is benevolent—designed to maximize welfare—the interference is justified, which means the negative-liberty cost is minimal or null. Republican liberty, by contrast, is non-domination: not being subject to arbitrary power. The algorithm is unaccountable, so its power is arbitrary. Even though citizens can appeal, the board has never overturned a decision, which shows the appeal mechanism does not make the power accountable. Pettit argues that domination is present whenever power is arbitrary, regardless of whether it is exercised. Here, the algorithm’s power is arbitrary because it is not forced to track citizens’ interests; it merely tracks welfare metrics. For democratic legitimacy, Pettit holds that a state must not only avoid interference but also avoid domination, because citizens cannot regard themselves as free equals if they live under the will of an unaccountable authority. So in this case, republican liberty is violated, while negative liberty is technically violated by the fare changes but morally neutralized by the benevolent purpose. The key distinction is that republican liberty cares about the structure of power, not just its exercise.”
