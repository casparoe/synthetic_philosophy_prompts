For a graduate workshop in political philosophy (14 participants, 75 minutes), design a structured in-class simulation that makes participants feel the conceptual pressure behind the claim that corruption is not a universal category but depends on whether a society institutionalizes a separation between office-based authority and open-ended personal reciprocity obligations. The setting is a fictional municipal housing-allocation board with scarce permits. Divide participants into two groups: applicants embedded in a reciprocity network, for whom prior favors create legitimate expectations that must eventually be honored; and board members operating under a formal anti-corruption code that prohibits any gift or obligation from influencing allocation, but leaves ambiguous the status of symbolic courtesy acknowledgments that carry no explicit price. Introduce a visible mechanism: a social ledger that records every exchange not as a bribe but as a recognized reciprocal debt, paired with a transparency-threshold rule permitting gifts below a specified symbolic value provided they are disclosed. Structure the simulation in three rounds—allocation, disclosure audit, and network-stability review—so that participants must decide whether to honor ledger obligations, invoke the code, or exploit the ambiguity of the threshold, with outcomes scored on both permit success and the continuity of reciprocity ties (no real money; use point-based status and future-access tokens). The rules should be arranged so that consistently applying the Western-style prohibition forces participants to treat legitimate social bonds as corrupt, while consistently honoring reciprocity renders the formal prohibition either hypocritical or destructive, and this tension should emerge from the mechanics before the facilitator names it. Include specific roles, a minute-by-minute timeline, the payoff structure, and a debrief that maps moments in the rounds onto the philosophical distinction between transactional closure and open-ended reciprocity. Be explicit about what the simulation cannot reproduce—such as the historical institutional embedding of these norms—and predict what will go wrong the first time, particularly participants defaulting to liberal-individualist framing and refusing to play reciprocity roles sincerely, with a brief note on how to handle it. Do not request bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; the prompt should be fully runnable without external sources.
