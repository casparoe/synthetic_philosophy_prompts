Assume the role of a professional philosopher with expertise in ancient Stoicism, philosophy of action, and the conceptual foundations of clinical psychology. I am preparing a graduate seminar on Stoicism's modern revival and want you to think carefully about an institutional practice before offering your analysis; please begin with a brief brainstorming of how the Stoic concept of ta eph' hēmin (what is up to us) is translated in modern therapeutic contexts, then proceed step by step.

The practice I have in mind is the standard protocol in many CBT-derived resilience-training programs that draw explicitly on Stoic sources. Clients are asked to complete a "control inventory," sorting stressors into two fixed categories: internal to their agency (their judgments, intentions, efforts) and external to it (other people's behavior, past events, bodily health, economic conditions). This sorting is presented not as a pragmatic heuristic but as the recognition of a stable metaphysical or normative boundary—something discovered rather than constructed. Yet the same protocol immediately teaches "cognitive reframing," which trains the client to treat apparently external stressors as causally mediated by internal judgments, thereby dissolving the very boundary the inventory just established for therapeutic purposes. The institution thus operates with a hybrid model: the dichotomy is rigid enough to ground practices of acceptance and emotional detachment, yet porous enough to be therapeutically reconstructed through mental discipline.

What philosophical commitments about agency, mental causation, and the ontology of control does this institutional practice presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory that renders both moves—rigid classification and porous reframing—mutually coherent, or argue that the practice is philosophically underdetermined and can be licensed by rival frameworks (for instance, a compatibilist account of mental causation, a phenomenological view of embodied agency, or a purely pragmatic behaviorist framework with no metaphysical commitments). Be precise about whether the practice strictly requires a Stoic metaphysics of prohairesis, or whether it can survive if we replace that metaphysics with a different view of where agency begins and ends. Finally, describe what a therapy protocol built consistently on one specific interpretation—say, a strict Epictetan view in which judgments alone are within our power and their causal reach over the passions is direct rather than reconstructed—would have to change about its reframing techniques, and assess whether the current hybrid is philosophically stable or collapses into an unrecognized incoherence between discovery and construction. Do not offer bibliographies or citation lists; work from the conceptual structure alone.
