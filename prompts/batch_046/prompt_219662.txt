As a retired experimental physicist who has recently turned to philosophy, I find myself troubled by a comparison that pervades the seminars I attend: philosophical thought experiments are said to function like experiments conducted in a mental laboratory. One sets initial conditions, introduces a controlled perturbation, records the intuitive response, and infers a theoretical principle—much as one might calibrate an instrument and read a dial. Proponents deploy this analogy to argue that well-crafted intuition pumps deserve epistemic standing comparable to empirical data, particularly in ethics and metaphysics. I ask you to audit this analogy with the rigor one applies to a proposed experimental protocol. First, provide an explicit correspondence table: in the mental case, what plays the role of the apparatus, the isolated system, the control variables, the measurement instrument, replication by independent observers, statistical error analysis, and peer-reviewed publication? Second, identify the precise points where the mapping fractures—especially regarding the inability to blind the observer to prior theoretical commitments, the absence of quantitative calibration, and the difficulty of isolating a single conceptual variable without altering the framing conditions. Third, determine which of these disanalogies actually undermine the intended conclusion—that philosophical intuition pumps yield trustworthy, theory-neutral data—and which are merely cosmetic. Fourth, assess whether the analogy quietly imports normative weight from physical science, such as objectivity, reproducibility, and predictive success, that the mental domain lacks, and whether this importation does the argumentative work that the explicit premises cannot carry. Finally, if the laboratory analogy collapses under scrutiny, propose a more apt comparison—perhaps clinical case studies, mathematical proof by contradiction, or calibrated rhetorical devices—specifying exactly what that replacement preserves and what it sacrifices. Please keep your analysis free of bibliographic references; I am interested in the conceptual architecture rather than in a literature review.
