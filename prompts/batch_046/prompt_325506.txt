Hey, I'm drafting a novel where the protagonist is a stats professor who can't stop second-guessing himself. I wrote this argument into his mouth and now it feels like a logical trap—can you find the exit?

(1) Real epistemic modesty means taking the outside view: I should weight my beliefs by the base rate of people like me being wrong.
(2) But defining that reference class—who counts as "people like me"—requires the exact inside-view judjement I'm supposed to distrust.
(3) So every outside-view correction rides on an inside-view choice of reference class.
(4) If my inside view is unreliable enough to need fixing, it's unreliable enough to pick the wrong class too.
(5) Therefore either I skip correction and stay arrogant, or I apply it on shaky ground and stay inconsistent.
(6) So epistemic modesty is either useless or self-undermining.

Where's the slip, if there is one? I'd rather save (1) than (6), but I want to know what keeping it costs me.
