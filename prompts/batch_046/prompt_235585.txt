A study-group summary of the Margalit–Rieff exchange on collective remembrance runs as follows. Margalit is said to defend an unconditional ethical duty to remember the victims of radical evil, one that binds even strangers in purely political, thin relationships and that can never be traded away for social peace. Rieff is said to reply that collective remembrance operates as a moral technology that fixes groups in grievance, and that democratic reconciliation therefore requires not merely permission but an active political right to forget past wrongs. The summary concludes that Rieff's view reduces Margalit's position to moral fetishism—treating memory as an intrinsic good irrespective of its effects on the living—while noting that Margalit's own distinction between thick and thin relations was designed precisely to avoid this, though the summary claims he applies it inconsistently by extending the duty to strangers. How accurate is this characterization? Assess sentence by sentence: where does it capture the thinkers' actual commitments, where does it conflate personal ethics with political practice, and does the accusation of moral fetishism follow from Rieff's premises? Be specific about whether Margalit allows the duty to be overridden and about the strength of Rieff's claim—does he argue for a right to forget, or for something more modest?
