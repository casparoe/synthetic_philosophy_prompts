Here is an argument I have been turning over about pragmatic encroachment and epistemic contextualism, and I cannot locate the error, though the conclusion—that the two views are jointly incoherent—seems far too strong to accept.

(1) Pragmatic encroachment holds that whether a belief counts as knowledge depends on the practical stakes for the believer: the higher the cost of error, the stronger the epistemic support required.

(2) Epistemic contextualism holds that the truth conditions of sentences of the form "S knows that p" depend on the attributor's conversational context, specifically on which error-possibilities are salient and which epistemic standards are in play.

(3) Consider Dr. Voss, a forensic pathologist. In her routine laboratory work she examines a tissue slide and forms belief B about the cell type present. The practical stakes for her are low—errors are caught in peer review—so by (1) she knows B.

(4) Voss is then subpoenaed to testify about B in a capital sentencing hearing. The defendant's life hangs on her identification, but Voss herself faces no personal risk: she is not penalized for error, her career is secure, and she bears no legal liability.

(5) By (1), since Voss's personal practical stakes have not changed, she still knows B.

(6) By (2), in the courtroom context the salient error-possibilities include catastrophic misidentification leading to execution, so the epistemic standard for attributing knowledge to Voss is maximized. Her evidence, unchanged from the lab, fails to meet it; hence "Voss knows B" is false in that context.

(7) So Voss knows B (step 5) and Voss does not know B (step 6).

(8) This is not merely a benign linguistic ambiguity about the word "knows," because both attributions concern the very same cognitive achievement of the same subject at the same time, not different relations indexed to different speakers.

(9) To avoid contradiction, one might say that encroachment actually tracks all stakes made salient in any context where knowledge is attributed, not just the subject's own. But then encroachment collapses into contextualism, since the subject's knowledge depends on conversational salience rather than on her practical situation.

(10) Alternatively, one might say contextualism tracks only the subject's own practical stakes. But then contextualism collapses into invariantist encroachment, and the familiar data—where we deny knowledge in high-stakes conversations about others—must be explained away as pragmatic implicature rather than semantic shift.

(11) Since neither collapse is acceptable to proponents of the respective views, (1) and (2) are jointly incoherent.

If I must preserve one claim, I would rather keep (1) and treat (6) as misapplying contextual standards to a subject whose stakes remain constant. But I want to know exactly which step is the weakest link, why it looked plausible, and what each of the two standard rescue strategies—collapsing encroachment into context-sensitivity, or collapsing contextualism into invariantist stake-sensitivity—actually costs in terms of explaining the linguistic and normative data each view was designed to accommodate.
