I've been thinking on my own about those short hypothetical stories philosophers use to make ideas feel obvious — the train-track kind — and I think I've noticed something they rarely admit out loud. These stories don't just illustrate; they quietly edit the world. They strip away broken equipment, other voices, time to shout, and then present the leftover choice as if it were the whole situation. I'm not an academic — I read philosophy for fun and run a small discussion group — but I want to close our session with a poem that captures this hidden framing.

Please act as a poet-philosopher with expertise in conceptual analysis. Before you write, take a moment to brainstorm: how can a repeating form show an idea being crowded out by its own setup? Then write a villanelle — exactly 19 lines, five tercets and a final quatrain, with two refrains that alternate and finally meet in the last two lines without resolving. Refrain A should be the experiment's voice: "Strip the noise, then choose." Refrain B should be the excluded middle: "But the lever sticks; I could shout." Let the rhyme and repetition make B feel interrupted each time it returns, so the form itself enacts the way intuition pumps squeeze out complications. Make the content accurate enough that someone who studies ethics or philosophy of mind wouldn't wince — reference the isolation of variables and the false binary — but do not quote any real text and do not include citations or a bibliography. Keep it to exactly 19 lines. Lead with the poem itself, then follow with one brief paragraph explaining how the structure mirrors the framing trick. Think step by step.
