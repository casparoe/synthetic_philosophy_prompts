I am a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy, though I have been working through some introductory texts on logic and language. Recently I encountered Moore's paradox—the well-known oddity of asserting that it is raining while simultaneously denying one's own belief that it is raining. My understanding is that this is not a logical contradiction in the strict sense, since it is entirely possible for it to be raining while I fail to believe it; rather, the tension is pragmatic or epistemic, tied to the act of assertion itself. I find this distinction clear enough in the first-person case, but I am confused about its scope.

In distributed systems, we frequently encounter an analog that seems structurally identical: a monitoring service logs a statement that the primary database is unavailable, but the health-check module reports that it is available. Here we have an explicit assertion of a state of affairs coupled with an explicit record that the system's own belief-state, encoded in the module's output, denies that state. If Moore's paradox arises whenever an assertion of p is paired with an assertion that p is not believed, then our logging infrastructure should be riddled with paradox. Yet no engineer treats this as a philosophical problem; we treat it as a routine consistency failure between observation and state estimation. This leads me to ask: is the paradox genuinely limited to first-person, present-tense, human assertion, such that automated logs describing mismatched belief-states are philosophically unremarkable? Or is there a deeper feature—perhaps related to the transparency of belief or the norms of assertion—that explains why the human case feels paradoxical while the system case does not, even though both involve the same logical form?

More generally, I wonder whether resolving Moore's paradox requires us to adopt a specific modal or epistemic framework—something like treating belief as an operator that behaves differently in self-attribution versus third-party attribution—rather than treating it as a simple propositional attitude that can be mapped onto database flags. I would appreciate an explanation in American English that addresses whether my systems analogy misses something essential about the paradox, and if so, what that missing element is.
