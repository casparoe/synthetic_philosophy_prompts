I'm in this philosophy of science elective and my teacher brought up verisimilitude—like, how can a totally false theory still be closer to the truth than another false one? I tried testing my gut on a bunch of cases and now I'm second-guessing everything. Okay so Newtonian mechanics feels closer to real physics than Aristotle's idea that heavy things fall faster cause they want to reach their natural place, even though Newton is technically wrong about space being absolute and stuff. Ptolemy's geocentric model with all those epicycles feels closer to the truth than a bare "sun circles earth in a perfect circle," since Ptolemy at least predicted planetary positions decently. But then there's Kepler—he got elliptical orbits which is way closer to Newton, but he also thought planets were pushed by some solar magnetic soul-force and wrote about celestial music. Does all that mystical baggage make his theory closer to Ptolemy, or is the orbit shape enough to keep him near Newton?

Sometimes I think A is closer because the mechanism is mostly right, other times I think B is equally close because both miss air resistance and both nail the prediction. Is there one principle that sorts all these—maybe counting true consequences vs false ones, or weighing structural similarity, or separating mechanism from prediction?
