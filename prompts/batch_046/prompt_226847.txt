Consider the long-standing impasse over whether normative reasons can exist independently of any agent who could be guided by them—what philosophers sometimes discuss under the heading of free-floating rationales. One side maintains that a reason is essentially relational: it is always a reason for some subject to act or believe in a certain way, and to speak of reasons that float free of every possible reasoner is to confuse normative force with objective value. The other side maintains that we can coherently hold that there is reason for a state of affairs—say, the absence of suffering in a region of the cosmos where sentience never evolves—to obtain, even though no being is present to be moved by that reason; on their view, denying this collapses normativity into a psychology of motivation.

My working hypothesis is that the disagreement is not primarily about metaphysics but about disciplinary function: the first camp is answering the question of what justifies an agent's deliberation, while the second is answering the question of what makes a world better, and both are using the single term "reason" for these distinct jobs. Yet I also suspect there may be a concealed formal disagreement lurking behind the scenes—perhaps about whether a reason must be tokened in some mental or causal structure to have any normative weight at all, which makes the dispute appear empirical when it is actually conceptual.

Please diagnose this stall honestly. Is it a verbal disagreement about the word "reason," a substantive disagreement about the ontology of normativity, or two different questions being pursued under one label? In particular, address whether either side actually denies the coherence of what we might call pre-personal reasons—normative considerations that apply to states of affairs prior to, or in the absence of, any agent who could respond to them—or whether they merely deny that such considerations should occupy the center of ethical theory. Given your diagnosis, is there a conceptual distinction—such as that between guiding reasons and justifying reasons, or between agent-relative and state-relative normativity—that could dissolve or clarify the dispute? Or is the disagreement bedrock, resting on intuitions about the purpose of normative language that neither side can argue for? Finally, tell me what, if anything, would actually count as progress: a formal result, a conceptual distinction, a change of framing, or nothing at all?
