Consider the institutional pattern in contemporary liberal democracies by which criminal codes retain “morals offenses”—prohibitions against consensual adult incest, the commercial exchange of certain bodily materials, or the desecration of widely revered symbols—justified not by demonstrable injury to identifiable victims but by the claim that these acts corrode the shared moral environment necessary for social cohesion; while, simultaneously, those same legal systems license, through tightly regulated exemptions, large-scale practices of animal confinement and invasive experimentation that cause extensive suffering, on the grounds that the harm is contained within an institution serving economic or scientific ends and does not threaten communal moral bonds. A reformer notices the asymmetry: the law criminalizes acts that violate collective sentiment without causing direct physical harm, yet permits institutionally contained suffering that causes massive physical harm, provided it does not offend dominant morality. What theory of law’s relation to morality does this pairing presuppose? Must it be Devlin’s thesis that law enforces a society’s shared moral code to prevent social disintegration, treating harm as legally relevant mainly when it undermines that code? Or can Hart’s harm principle accommodate the pattern by reconceiving “moral harm” as a genuine form of damage and treating institutional suffering as a permissible trade-off? Reconstruct the implicit theory with enough precision to explain both practices together; distinguish what the pattern strictly commits its defenders to from what is merely contingently compatible with it; assess whether the two practices rely on a consistent conception of harm, morality, and the limits of state power; and specify exactly which institutional arrangements—whether particular criminal statutes or specific regulatory exemptions—would have to be abandoned or redesigned if the legal system adopted the rival view wholesale.
