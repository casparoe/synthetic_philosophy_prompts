I'm constructing a historical novel set in 1870s London, and I've encountered a structural dilemma with my central antagonist—a real, documented municipal reformer who led a vocal campaign against the installation of the new sewer system. His opposition was not simply religious or reactionary. He maintained a developed cosmological theory in which rapid drainage disrupted the city's miasmatic equilibrium, that concentrating waste removal into hidden pipes violated natural cycles of purification, and that rendering waste invisible would corrupt both the physical atmosphere and the moral order of public life. Within the epistemic framework he inhabited, the sewer represented a genuine danger rather than an obvious good.

My literary agent insists that I render him as a flat obstacle to sanitary progress: a superstitious crank standing in the way of modern hygiene, so that readers experience clear narrative relief when the system is built and he is discredited. The arc she wants is essentially enlightenment defeating ignorance. Yet when I attempt this framing, the character collapses into caricature. I can reconstruct his reasoning with fidelity—he read contemporary engineering reports, he corresponded with physicians, he weighed evidence within a cosmology that treated visibility, circulation, and moral accountability as physically inseparable. The difficulty is that portraying his defeat as an uncomplicated triumph of progress feels like I'm imposing a teleological structure on the past, judging his framework not by its internal coherence but by its proximity to our present understanding of cholera transmission.

As an alternative, I'm considering a bifocal narrative architecture: rather than treating the sewer debate as a binary of ignorance versus enlightenment, I want to structure the plot around contingency and hidden cost. The reformer would be wrong about disease mechanisms, yet prescient about the social displacements and ecological assumptions embedded in the engineering—specifically, that making waste invisible without addressing its volume creates deferred consequences that the triumphal narrative suppresses. In this version, the past does not march toward the present; it makes a consequential choice that involves real losses on both sides, and the reader's sympathy is divided rather than directed.

My concern is whether this symmetrical framing genuinely corrects an intellectual distortion, or whether it merely romanticizes obsolete error in the name of narrative fairness. Is there a defensible middle position between writing the past as a failed rehearsal for the present and granting every superseded cosmology equal epistemic dignity?
