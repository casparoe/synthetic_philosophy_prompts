As a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy, I have been working on data-ontology problems in distributed systems—specifically, how to commit to entity-types when the underlying properties that define them are not directly observable. This has led me to a philosophical cluster I understand as “inaccessible information and ontology identification”: the problem of fixing what exists or what kind something is when our access is limited to indirect signatures, correlations, or structural relations rather than intrinsic properties.

Rather than a survey, I am trying to build a mental map of which open conceptual questions in this area actually deserve priority effort, and why. If one were advising a small interdisciplinary research group—say, philosophers of science and conceptual engineers—who have about three years and no empirical lab, which lines of inquiry should they pursue first?

In particular:

1. Is the upstream problem the refinement of identification conditions under epistemic closure—figuring out when indirect, correlational access is sufficient to fix an ontology—or is it the prior question of whether ontology itself should be indexed to available information structures?

2. Among the practical and theoretical implications, which carry real weight? For instance, do the answers mainly affect scientific taxonomy and information-architecture design, or do they largely stay internal to metaphysical debates about natural kinds and reference?

3. Which two or three specific conceptual problems in this space are genuinely tractable for philosophers rather than empirical researchers, and how should limited resources be ordered between them? I want the reasoning to include: what makes one problem upstream of another, what evidence or conceptual breakthrough over the next few years would reorder the priority list, and what trade-offs exist between tractability and importance.

Please treat this as an agenda-setting exercise, not a request for citations, bibliographies, or a reading list. I am interested in a defended ordering with explicit criteria—upstream dependence, tractability for non-empirical work, and relevance outside pure metaphysics—rather than an exhaustive catalog.
