In a recent seminar discussion of semantic externalism, a colleague advanced an analogy she treats as decisive against Putnam's Twin Earth argument: the brain is like a computer running software, and mental states are like lines of executable code. She applies it to the standard Earth-versus-Twin-Earth scenario in which Oscar and his molecular duplicate share every internal neural configuration, just as two identical machines running the same program share every syntactic line. If relocating the laptop does not alter the software's intrinsic properties, she reasons, then relocating the brain to an environment with XYZ rather than H2O should not alter the intrinsic mental content; therefore, reference must be fixed internally, and externalism collapses. The analogy is meant to carry the full weight of that conclusion.

Can you audit this comparison with precision? First, provide an explicit correspondence table—what maps onto the hardware, the operating system, the compiled code, the user input stream, the network protocol, and the error-correction mechanism—then identify which of these mappings hold for biological cognition and which break. In particular, examine whether the analogy smuggles in the assumption that syntax is autonomous from causal history and evolutionary function, and whether it can accommodate not only natural-kind terms like water but also socially constructed terms—say, democracy—where the external environment consists of institutional practices rather than chemical structures. Finally, assess whether the anti-externalist conclusion survives once the disanalogies are explicit, and propose a more apt analogy—perhaps drawn from biological signaling or distributed ecological tracking—that does not presuppose the internalist result from the outset.
