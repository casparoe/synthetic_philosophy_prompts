After being sharply corrected on a statistical inference I had defended with near-certainty, I have been trying to calibrate exactly when my inside view should override, or yield to, the outside view. My judgments on the following cases feel somewhat stable, but I suspect they may not cohere.

Case A: I have spent months tracing a formal argument about modal essentialism, reaching a conclusion that conflicts with the standard interpretation in the literature. I can defend every premise and inference, but I have not found anyone else who reaches it. Verdict: I should tentatively trust my inside view and treat the disagreement as others not having followed the derivation through.

Case B: I have a strong intuition that a classic argument in philosophy of mind contains an error, yet I cannot locate the specific flaw. Verdict: I should defer to the outside view and continue to treat the argument as sound.

Case C: Together with two colleagues, I devised a “dual-track coherence protocol”—a method for cross-checking normative principles against systematically varied counterfactual scenarios—and applied it to a problem in value theory. We all concur on the result, but the protocol has not been reviewed or adopted outside our group. Verdict: I should remain skeptical of the result, even though we understand the protocol best.

Case D: I am assessing a causal claim in a technical domain where I have deep expertise, but the broader expert community is divided and has a poor historical record of predictive success. I have traced the mechanism step by step and see no failure mode that others have identified. Verdict: I should rely primarily on my inside view.

Case E: Last year I was highly confident in a philosophical inference that collapsed once I noticed a hidden ambiguity; now I encounter a structurally similar inference with an identical feeling of certainty. Verdict: I should heavily discount my confidence, appealing to my personal base rate of error.

Cases A and C seem parallel—both involve careful reasoning with little external validation—yet I want to trust one and distrust the other. Meanwhile, Cases D and E both involve reasoning in domains with weak track records, yet I treat domain-level failure differently from personal failure.

Is there a single principle—perhaps distinguishing types of feedback availability, kinds of expertise, or error-risk structures—that captures A through E? If not, which verdict should be abandoned for simplicity? And what does the best principle predict for a case I have not listed: I read a popular summary of a result in theoretical physics, feel I understand it, and it conflicts with what working physicists say?

Please present a precise formulation of the principle, apply it to each case, and state explicitly which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate. Conclude with the principle summarized in \answer{...} and a final line beginning Verdict: identifying the verdict that must be overturned.
