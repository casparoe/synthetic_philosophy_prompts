Compare the traditional doctrine of double effect with the hypothetical principle of causal insulation—defined here as the view that harming is permissible only when the harm is not intended and is also causally mediated through a process, mechanism, or agent that the actor does not directly control, so that the actor’s will does not immediately determine the harmful physical movement. Apply both frameworks to three scenarios: (1) the standard switch case, in which a bystander diverts a trolley away from five people toward one; (2) the footbridge case, in which the bystander pushes one person onto the tracks; and (3) a constructed intermediate case in which the bystander can save five only by remotely triggering a pre-existing automated bridge-collapse mechanism that kills one, where the bystander merely activates the mechanism but does not physically contact the victim. For each scenario, state whether double effect permits the action and whether causal insulation permits it, and highlight precisely where the two frameworks diverge. Then identify which specific condition within double effect—the intention/foresight distinction, the means/end condition, or the proportionality requirement—is responsible for the divergence in case (3), and explain why a proponent of causal insulation might argue that double effect still licenses an impermissible form of direct agency. Finally, discuss whether either framework can accommodate one-to-one lethal self-sacrifice without contradiction, and if so, on what grounds.
