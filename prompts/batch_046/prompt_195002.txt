I'm taking a seminar on moral philosophy this term, and after our session on rescue cases a recurring pattern in our discussions has started to bother me—one that doesn't quite match the standard distinctions we read about. I want to describe it concretely and ask whether it corresponds to an established concept, or whether I'm noticing something that hasn't been named precisely.

Here is the pattern across two kinds of examples. First, the direct rescue scenario: you can save either one person or five people, and everyone in the room agrees you should save the five, usually by saying that each person has an equal claim and that five equal claims outweigh one. Second, the resource-allocation scenario: with the same fixed budget, Program A saves one life with near-certainty, Program B saves five lives with near-certainty but at five times the cost per life, and Program C saves one hundred lives with near-certainty but at ten times the cost per life. In this setting, many of the same people who endorsed simple aggregation in the rescue case suddenly reject that one hundred lives generate a hundredfold stronger obligation than one; they talk about efficiency, about not "buying" lives in bulk, and about weighing each case independently. Yet they still maintain that, all else equal, saving one hundred is better than saving one. So the number functions like an ordinal switch—more is always better—but not like a cardinal scale; the moral weight doesn't accumulate proportionally once cost, risk, or institutional framing enter the picture.

What interests me is not scope neglect, since people clearly register the difference between five and one hundred, and it is not the familiar active-versus-passive distinction, because both charity programs involve active allocation. Rather, it seems to be a contextual toggle: in simultaneous, direct comparisons the principle "save the greater number" operates as a simple ordering rule, but in distributed, probabilistic, or budget-constrained contexts people revert to evaluating claims individually and resist treating the group size as a summed magnitude. Is there an established name for this specific toggle, or for the implicit boundary condition that allows aggregation to determine ranking but forbids it from scaling obligation? If not, how should one carve it out from Taurek's flat rejection of number-sensitive reasoning on one side, and from standard risk-weighted expected-utility reasoning on the other? I am not looking for a bare label; I want to know exactly what the phenomenon is, where its edges lie, and whether the distinction between ordinal aggregation and cardinal proportionality captures it, or whether it collapses into something already familiar.
