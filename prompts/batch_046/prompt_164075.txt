You serve as the legislative review officer for a parliamentary committee that receives proposals to either retain or repeal criminal prohibitions on specific private, consensual sexual practices. You have thirty minutes per proposal, no access to external research, and you must sign off on a recommendation. The committee wants an audit grounded specifically in Jeremy Bentham's unpublished utilitarian analysis of sexual offences from the 1780s—not in modern rights-based or dignity-based frameworks. Write a reusable decision procedure your office can apply repeatedly. It must take the form of an ordered checklist with explicit stopping rules: at which step the procedure returns a firm recommendation to repeal, at which step it returns a firm recommendation to retain, and at which steps it must halt and return the file to elected members because the issue is one of values rather than demonstrable harm.

The procedure must operationalize Bentham's actual distinctions rather than a generic harm principle. Include these steps in sequence: first, a consent filter that checks whether every participant is an adult acting without coercion; second, a deception filter that checks for concealment of material facts—such as health status, identity, or the existence of binding commitments—that would vitiate consent; third, a taxonomy-of-harm step that classifies alleged damage into direct physical pain, direct mental suffering to participants (explicitly excluding observer disgust or "antipathy," which Bentham treats as non-harm), breach of explicit promise or contract, and demonstrable collateral damage to identifiable dependents; fourth, a "naturalness" filter that asks whether the prohibition is justified solely by the claim that the act is "unnatural," which Bentham rejects as irrational and therefore disqualifying; fifth, a net-utility balancing step that asks whether criminalization would actually reduce the identified harm or merely displace it into secrecy, blackmail, or abusive enforcement, weighing countervailing harms of prohibition; and sixth, a judgment-flag that marks any step relying on speculative social harm—such as "moral decay" without identifiable victims—as a values question for the committee, not a factual determination.

Specify what counts as a sufficient answer at each step, note which steps are judgment calls disguised as procedure, and identify the cases where the procedure misfires—particularly where harm is real but criminalization is ineffective.

Then apply the procedure to three proposals: (1) repealing a statute that criminalizes private, non-procreative sexual acts between consenting adults with no deception and no dependents affected; (2) retaining a prohibition on private adultery between consenting adults where one spouse is deceived and minor children reside in the household; and (3) retaining a prohibition on consensual sexual contact in which one party actively conceals an infectious disease. Include one application that yields a result a modern liberal committee is likely to find unwelcome, and state clearly what the procedure cannot settle.
