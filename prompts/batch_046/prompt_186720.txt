Analyze the Terri Schiavo case around 2005 through the lens of "precedent autonomy" — the idea that a competent earlier self can bind a later incompetent one. Map the family dispute onto it: Michael Schiavo trying to enforce what he said were her prior wishes, the parents insisting she wouldn't have wanted that. But don't just do the standard personal-identity move. Instead, ask whether this case involves what I'd call "radical evalutive discontinuty" — not just lost memory but a later self with literally no shared values or concerns with the prior one. Did her vegetative state create that break? And does the law's demand for "clear and convincing evidence" of prior wishes actually solve the authority problem, or does it just hide the fact that we're still guessing at what the earlier self meant? I want a committed answer: was Michael right to invoke her authority, and where does the theory of precedent autonomy actually crack under the pressure of this case?
