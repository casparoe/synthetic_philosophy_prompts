I am preparing the methodological charter for a research center that examines whether cross-cultural differences in philosophical intuition should constrain theory-building in epistemology, ethics, and metaphysics. Before finalizing our guidelines, I want to test whether my own judgments about specific patterns form a coherent stance or collapse into inconsistency.

My verdicts are as follows.

In epistemic scenarios structured like Gettier cases—where a subject holds a true belief supported by reasoning that happens to be undermined by an unknown fact—Western and East Asian lay respondents show modest divergence in knowledge attribution. I treat this divergence as superficial. The underlying conceptual framework—distinguishing belief, truth, justification, and accidental failure—appears shared across groups; the difference is a matter of weighting or pragmatic translation, not of kind. Consequently, I do not think this variation undermines philosophical analysis of knowledge.

In moral-luck cases—where agents cause identical harms through negligent versus purely accidental conduct—Western and East Asian lay respondents diverge sharply on blame, and this divergence persists, though in attenuated form, among professionally trained philosophers from both traditions. I treat this divergence as deep. I therefore hold that normative ethics should not be built directly on untutored cross-cultural intuition, since there is no unified common sense to refine.

Within a single Western population, framing judgments about free will in deterministic language versus agent-causal language produces response shifts comparable in magnitude to the cross-cultural moral-luck gap. I initially interpreted this as a shallow framing effect: when subjects are pressed to justify their answers, the framing effect largely dissipates, suggesting it is an artifact of presentation rather than a genuine conceptual difference. By contrast, the moral-luck divergence survives reflective justification, which I take as evidence that it is not merely framing.

The difficulty is that my criterion for depth—persistence under reflective justification—threatens to apply inconsistently. The small Gettier divergence might also be a linguistic-framing artifact, arising from different pragmatic weights of translation equivalents for the relevant epistemic terms, yet I do not discount it as undermining epistemology. Meanwhile, philosophical training reduces the Gettier divergence more effectively than the moral-luck divergence, and I have used that differential filtering to support my claim that training corrects cultural noise in epistemology but not in ethics. But without an independent principle, I seem to be selecting my criterion to preserve my prior theoretical sympathies: I protect epistemology from cross-cultural critique by labeling its divergences shallow, while allowing ethics to be constrained by divergences I label deep.

So I ask: is there a precise principle that distinguishes superficial cross-cultural variation from deep conceptual divergence, applies consistently across epistemic, normative, and metaphysical domains, and accommodates my three verdicts? Specifically, can the principle rely on something other than persistence-under-justification—perhaps on the structure of the concept involved, the type of error that explains the divergence, or the role of philosophical training—without becoming circular? State the principle with explicit clauses tied to each case, indicate its verdict on a further case I have not mentioned—such as whether bilingual respondents who shift personal-identity judgments depending on the language of the survey should be treated like the framing-effect case or like the moral-luck case—and be honest about which of my three verdicts the principle forces me to abandon.
