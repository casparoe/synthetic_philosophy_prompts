I've been working through Samuel Scheffler's argument that human extinction is uniquely bad not just because it kills people, but because it destroys the implicit afterlife conjecture — the background assumption that future generations will continue the projects and values we care about now. I want to test whether this structural claim can be isolated from more familiar explanations such as aggregate lost well-being, lost future achievements, or direct harm. To do that, I need a precisely engineered thought experiment, realistic and present-day (no simulated universes, no alien observers, no perfect predictors), presented in no more than a paragraph, with two scenarios — call them A and B.

The specifications are these. Both scenarios must involve exactly the same present population and the same immediate suffering or death toll; the welfare calculus must be held constant. Scenario A must preserve the institutional and psychological conditions of the afterlife conjecture: people still live and organize their lives as though a human future will carry on their work, even though in fact it will not. Scenario B must collapse those conditions — people know with certainty that there is no continuation — while keeping every direct welfare fact identical. Then, demonstrate the verdicts: (1) a thin welfarist account that counts only direct harm, lost future lives, and aggregate suffering should deliver the same moral evaluation for A and B; (2) Scheffler's structural account should deliver sharply divergent verdicts, with A being significantly less bad (or bad in a different way) than B specifically because the belief-structure of continuation survives; (3) a future-goods account that values extinction mainly for the valuable experiences and achievements it eliminates should also fail to track the divergence, or should evaluate them identically.

After presenting the scenario, show the reasoning for each account, pinpoint the exact feature that does the isolating work (for instance, the presence of credible intergenerational institutions, narrative continuity, or practical orientation toward a future that is in fact absent), and explain what happens if that feature is removed — does the case collapse back into a standard comparison of future populations? Finally, include an honest assessment of how a defender of the thin welfarist view would attack your construction: would they deny that the preserved belief-structure has independent moral weight, argue that you've hidden welfare differences inside the psychology of the agents, or claim that the scenario is under-described?
