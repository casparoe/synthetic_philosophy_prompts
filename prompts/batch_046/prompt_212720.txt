Consider the advisory track record of the Crown's Constitutional Convention Council, which reviews proposed royal actions for consistency with the unwritten conventions of a parliamentary monarchy. Its published rulings are as follows. First, it permitted the monarch to deliver, at the explicit request of the Prime Minister, the annual Speech from the Throne outlining the government’s legislative program, including provisions then contested in Parliament, on the ground that reading a ministerial text is an act of constitutional machinery rather than personal political judgment. Second, it prohibited the monarch from publishing, without ministerial consultation, an open letter urging Parliament to pass a specific bill on carbon-emission standards. Third, it permitted the monarch, during a private audience, to decline to offer views on a proposed reform affecting the upper chamber, holding that the convention of consultation does not extend to matters outside the established scope of royal advice. Fourth, it prohibited the monarch from attending, in an official capacity, a rally organized by a non-partisan civic association advocating proportional representation, on the ground that lending royal presence to any organized demand for structural constitutional change appropriates symbolic capital for a political project regardless of party neutrality.

The Council must now advise on a new proposal: in the immediate aftermath of a terrorist attack, the monarch wishes to issue a brief, self-drafted public statement referring to “the enduring strength of our democratic institutions and the rule of law,” without naming any government, party, or specific policy, and without having been requested to do so by ministers. If the Council aims to remain consistent with its own precedents, what should it advise? Identify the two most plausible general principles that jointly explain the four rulings above, state what each principle predicts about this proposed statement, identify the closest prior analog and explain why, offer your best judgment of what the Council should decide with the reasoning that favors it, and specify one further hypothetical case that would best distinguish the two principles.
