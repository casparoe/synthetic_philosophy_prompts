Consider the following reconstruction of the paradox of deontology, pushed to an extreme scale that seems to reduce the view to a doctrine of personal purity. (1) Deontology holds that the moral status of an act is fixed by whether it violates an agent-centered duty or constraint, not by the magnitude or distribution of its consequences. (2) If wrongness is consequence-independent, then the moral evaluation of an act-type is scale-invariant: killing one innocent person is wrong, and killing N innocent persons is wrong N times over, but the evaluation of each individual killing does not vary with N. (3) By parallel reasoning, if there is no agent-centered duty to rescue strangers, then failing to rescue one is permissible, and failing to rescue N is permissible N times over; the omission remains permissible regardless of N. (4) Now imagine an agent who can either do nothing, allowing N people to die, or kill one innocent person, thereby saving all N. (5) By (1) and (2), the killing is forbidden, no matter how large N becomes. (6) By (1) and (3), the omission is permitted, no matter how large N becomes. (7) So for any N—including values in the billions—the deontologist must rate the omission permissible and the killing forbidden. (8) A framework that assigns identical evaluations to allowing one death and allowing a billion deaths, solely because both are omissions lacking a specific duty, treats the scale of suffering as morally invisible. (9) If scale is invisible, then absent a specific rescue-duty there is no reason to save anyone at all; the agent may remain entirely indifferent to the world's state, provided she avoids direct contamination. (10) Thus deontology collapses into a ritual of purity: avoid striking the match, but watch without distress as the forest burns. (11) This is absurd; any ethics that cannot register the difference between a single tragedy and global catastrophe is not a viable moral theory. (12) Therefore deontology is false, or must secretly import consequentialist considerations to survive.

Where does the reasoning fail, if anywhere? I am inclined to protect (1)—the consequence-independence of deontological evaluation—over (11), because giving up (1) dissolves what distinguishes deontology from other theories. But I want to know which intermediate step is the weakest link, what repairing it costs, and whether preserving (1) forces me to accept that moral agents are indeed permitted to let the world burn so long as they do not strike the match themselves. In particular, is the slide from (3) to (9) an equivocation between the absence of a duty and the absence of any reason? Or does the problem lie in treating scale-invariance of act-evaluation as scale-invariance of overall moral judgment? If a better framing exists—one that preserves agent-centered constraints without rendering the magnitude of suffering morally invisible—what would that framing look like, and which of the premises above must it revise?
