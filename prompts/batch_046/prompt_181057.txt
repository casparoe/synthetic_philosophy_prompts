Consider the following argument, which I have been developing for a seminar on long-termist ethics, and which now strikes me as suspicious because its conclusion undermines the very framework it invokes. (1) By the singleton hypothesis, Earth-originating intelligent life will eventually form one global decision-making agency — a singleton — with no effective rivals. (2) If a future singleton will eventually control all outcomes that matter to present agents, then the rational evaluation of any present action must be indexed to its expected effect on the preferences that singleton will hold. (3) Hence present agents ought to select actions that maximize expected alignment with the singleton's eventual utility function, rather than actions that maximize present or local welfare. (4) But the singleton's utility function is not exogenously fixed; it is path-dependent, shaped in part by the very actions present agents take in attempting to align with it. (5) Consequently, the directive in (3) is either circular — we align in order to match preferences that our alignment helps constitute — or radically underdetermined, lacking any external standard by which to choose among possible alignment paths. (6) If so, the singleton hypothesis yields no substantive normative guidance: it reduces to the tautology that we should do whatever we will eventually do. (7) Yet the hypothesis is typically advanced precisely to motivate distinctive long-range obligations — for example, prioritizing global coordination and value-lock-in over parochial interests. (8) Therefore, either the singleton hypothesis fails to ground any special present obligations, or premise (3) is false — but abandoning (3) seems to strip the hypothesis of its practical point. I would prefer to retain (1) and (7), and I have some sympathy for (4), but I need to know whether I am smuggling an illicit temporal authority claim into (2), or whether there is a scope ambiguity between causal influence and normative justification across steps (2) through (5). Where does the argument fail, and what does repairing it cost?
