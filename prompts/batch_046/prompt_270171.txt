I am writing an essay for a philosophy of science seminar on how cosmological models bear on metaphysical questions about time, and Gödel’s rotating universe has become central to my argument after a classmate summarized his view during discussion. The summary went roughly like this: Gödel proved that Einstein’s field equations allow a rotating universe containing closed timelike curves, which means a physical object could travel along a path that returns it to its own past; he then treated this cosmological result as a direct demonstration that objective time is unreal, since if temporal succession can loop back upon itself it cannot be a fundamental feature of reality. The classmate added that Gödel believed Einstein had failed to draw this philosophical consequence, and that Gödel’s position was essentially a radicalization of relativistic insight—where Einstein showed that simultaneity is frame-dependent, Gödel showed that time itself does not exist—so that the rotating universe functions as a reductio of the idea that temporal ordering is intrinsic to the world.

I am inclined to think this characterization is too neat, but I lack the background to locate the distortions precisely. Does Gödel genuinely derive his idealism about time from the existence of closed timelike curves in his cosmological model, or are the cosmological result and the philosophical argument largely independent lines of reasoning that happen to converge? And is it accurate to say that Gödel regarded Einstein as having missed an implication, or was their disagreement about the interpretation of relativity more subtle and less like a correction? Could you audit this summary in detail—indicating which parts are faithful, which are standard popularizations, and which risk misrepresenting Gödel’s actual position—particularly regarding whether the rotating universe was meant to serve as proof of time’s unreality, or merely as a consistent physical scenario compatible with that unreality? I want to understand the boundary between Gödel’s technical cosmology and his metaphysics of time so that I do not, in my essay, attribute to him a stronger or more naive claim than he actually defended.
