Wittgenstein's discussion of hinges in On Certainty addresses the problem of how justification terminates without collapsing into Cartesian foundationalism. Unlike Descartes, who sought propositions that are intrinsically indubitable, Wittgenstein observes that in ordinary life we hold certain propositions—such as that the earth has existed for many years—fast, and they form the background against which particular doubts arise. These hinges are not subject to empirical testing; rather, they constitute the framework within which testing occurs.

There are two dominant interpretations. The precondition interpretation holds that hinges are not beliefs at all, but conditions of intelligibility or grammatical rules that make belief-formation possible within a language-game. The contextual-immunity interpretation holds that hinges are genuine beliefs, but ones that enjoy absolute certainty within a particular form of life and are therefore not currently open to doubt.

Applying this to the physicist's case, when she treats her spectrometer calibration as beyond question, she is functioning with an instrumental hinge. When she later revises the calibration protocol in light of anomalies, this demonstrates that the claim was never a true hinge. A genuine hinge, by its nature, cannot be overturned by empirical findings; if it can be revised, then it was functioning as a provisional empirical assumption rather than as a structural precondition. This aligns with Wittgenstein's observation that doubt depends on certain propositions being exempt from doubt, like hinges on which inquiry turns. Once such a hinge is removed, the system of investigation collapses, which is precisely why scientists resist revising calibration unless absolutely forced.

That said, the precondition view faces a difficulty. If hinges are not beliefs but conditions for belief, then giving one up should be conceptually impossible—like altering the rules of chess in the middle of play. Because the physicist clearly can and does revise her calibration, the precondition view seems unable to accommodate scientific practice. The contextual-immunity view fares better: the calibration claim was a belief that possessed hinge-status during the experiment, but lost that immunity when the practical context changed. Wittgenstein explicitly allows hinges to shift over time, so this revision is consistent with his account. The belief was never a Cartesian foundation—eternal and unrevisable—but a locally fixed point that could be displaced when the form of life demanded it.

In sum, the case shows that instrumental commitments in science are best understood as contextually immune beliefs rather than as non-belief preconditions, and that Wittgenstein's framework permits their revision without collapsing into skepticism.
