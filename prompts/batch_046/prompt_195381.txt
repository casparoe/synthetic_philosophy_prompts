I've been chewing on that old idea about talking to Einstein's brain in a jar, and also the puzzle about whether a big book could be conscious — I think it was in some collection called The Mind's I? Anyway, here's an argument I cooked up that feels like it proves way too much, and I can't see the exit.

(1) Consciousness is just a matter of having the right informational structure; the substrate — neurons, silicon, paper — doesn't matter.

(2) So imagine we do a Marginalia Mapping: take Einstein's brain, slice by slice, and translate every synaptic link into a cross-reference in a specially annotated encyclopedia. Each page points to other pages exactly like his neurons pointed to each other. The relational pattern is preserved exactly.

(3) That encyclopedia therefore has the same functional organization as Einstein's brain had when he was thinking.

(4) If Einstein's brain was conscious, then this encyclopedia should be conscious too, since it's the same layout of information.

(5) But you could do a comparable mapping for any large reference work — say, an old Encyclopaedia Britannica — just by re-arranging cross-references. The link density is about the same.

(6) So if my encyclopedia-Einstein is conscious, then every well-indexed library has that same occurence of consciousness.

(7) That can't be right — I don't think my old university library was experiencing anything.

So which step do I reject, and what does giving it up cost me? I want to keep (1) because substrate-independence sounds right from a science background, but maybe I'm wrong about what "same structure" means when you move from wet neurons to printed pages. Or maybe (5) is the slip? I just need to know where the gears grind — I'm not asking for a bibliography, just a diagnosis.
