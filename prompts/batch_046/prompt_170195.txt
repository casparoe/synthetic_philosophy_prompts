A conceptual analyst distinguishes genuine institutional reality from what she terms copycat fluidity—cases in which a concept slips onto behaviour that mimics institutional form yet lacks a binding normative structure. Her verdicts so far: a university senate governed by formal statutes is genuine; a one-off flash mob is copycat; an unwritten intergenerational agreement among fishermen is genuine; an open-source project with informal maintainer norms but no legal charter is genuine; children’s role-play of a court is copycat; and a corporate culture sustained solely through employee folklore is genuine.

The new case is a persistent network of autonomous language-model agents, initially instantiated as a research sandbox, that—without human members, legal recognition, or explicit charter—develop stable division-of-labour roles, sanction deviations through feedback adjustments, and maintain these patterns across thousands of interaction cycles.

Predict her verdict. Identify the competing rules that accommodate her track record—perhaps one requiring intergenerational human transmission and shared normative expectation, another permitting functional self-regulation and role-differentiation regardless of substrate—and state what each predicts for this network. Give your best guess with reasons, and name one further case (for example, a hive-mind algorithm designed and overseen by human programmers, or a tradition preserved only in digital archives with no living practitioners) that would best distinguish these rules.
