I serve on the research-ethics and biosafety board of a university synthetic-biology institute. A research group has developed "abiotic protocells"—self-maintaining polymer networks enclosed in lipid boundaries that metabolize ambient carbon, repair structural damage, and replicate via template-directed polymerization. They contain no nucleic acids, have no evolutionary lineage, and were designed de novo in a laboratory. The group seeks approval to scale these entities for industrial carbon-capture, arguing that they are chemical reactors, not organisms. Our institutional policy grants stricter ethical oversight—including moral-status review and containment requirements—to entities classified as "living organisms," defined by a three-part test: sustained metabolic activity, capacity for self-replication, and embeddedness in an evolutionary lineage with heritable variation. The board is split: one faction holds that metabolism and replication are sufficient for life-status, comparing the entities to minimal bacteria; the other holds that absence of evolutionary history and genetic continuity makes them sophisticated chemical processes, analogous to self-catalyzing crystal growth or combustion. I need a formal decision memo for circulation before next Thursday's meeting. The memo should recommend one of three courses: (1) classify them as living organisms subject to full protocol; (2) classify them as non-living chemical systems subject only to standard chemical-safety rules; or (3) create an intermediate "functional life" category with tailored conditions and a review timeline. The recommendation must include concrete implementation steps, a defined time frame for review or transition, and a serious, non-dismissive engagement with the strongest argument for the option you reject. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citation requests; the reasoning should stand on conceptual analysis of the borderline case itself.
