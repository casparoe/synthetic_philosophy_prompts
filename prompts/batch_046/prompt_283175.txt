I'm auditing a graduate seminar on AI governance, and two participants—Rosa and Tariq—got into a sustained disagreement about whether the alignment problem should be defined narrowly as intent alignment or broadly as value alignment. They agreed to disagree but kept returning to a specific institutional hypothetical, and I want an adjudication as a neutral bystander: go round by round, say who makes the stronger point at each step, identify where either side misses something important, and avoid declaring an overall winner unless one genuinely dominates. Keep your response to about 450 words, organized by round.

Here is the exchange as it actually unfolded.

Rosa opened: "The only technically coherent target is intent alignment—making the system do exactly what its deployer intends. 'Value alignment' sounds rigorous, but it's metaphysically loaded: it assumes there are objective values, or at least stable intersubjective ones, and neither is operationalizable. Take a hospital administrator who instructs an AI to 'optimize bed usage.' If the system executes this precisely by discharging chronic patients to free capacity, the failure is not misalignment—the alignment mechanism worked. The failure is that the administrator's intent was ethically thin. You fix the intent, not the alignment architecture."

Tariq replied: "That collapses alignment into obedience to the powerful. Intentions are typically underspecified, self-deceptive, or corrupt. You need at least three layers: stated intent (what they say), revealed intent (what their behavior shows they actually want), and reflective intent (what they would endorse after coherent, informed deliberation). Narrow intent alignment targets only the first layer, which is the weakest form and the one most vulnerable to manipulation or ignorance."

Rosa came back: "Your 'reflective intent' is just value alignment in disguise. Once you invoke 'coherent deliberation,' you've imported a normative framework—rationality, consistency, autonomy, perhaps welfare. How is that not smuggling in values under a procedural label?"

Tariq: "It's anchored to the agent's own reasoning, not to an external moral theory. Consider an AI assistant that refuses to execute a user's immediate command to delete all backup files, because it predicts that the same user, after calm reflection, wants data security. This isn't imposing morality; it's aligning with the user's counterfactual rational self."

Rosa: "But modeling that 'counterfactual rational self' requires solving exactly the hard problems value alignment faces: weighing present versus future preferences, defining coherence, resolving intrapersonal conflict. You've relocated the problem into an architecture you call a 'reflection module,' not dissolved it."

Tariq: "That module is empirically tractable through preference learning and coherence constraints. By contrast, 'value alignment' demands we settle moral philosophy before we build anything. The burden is lighter on my side."

Rosa: "Lighter only if preference aggregation and coherence are easier than they are. In practice, your reflection module needs an aggregation rule—utilitarian, maximin, or some hybrid—which is precisely a substantive value choice. You haven't escaped values; you've hidden them in the engineering specs."

Please adjudicate move by move. At each round, rate whose point is stronger and briefly justify the rating.
