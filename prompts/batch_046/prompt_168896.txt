I cover philosophy of science and consciousness studies for a quarterly magazine aimed at intellectually engaged general readers, and I am currently reporting a feature on whether graduate education in philosophy of mind rewards genuine conceptual analysis or merely rewards fluent summary. In conversation with a seminar instructor, I was struck by the observation that students can often recount the familiar objections to epiphenomenalism—particularly the paradox of phenomenal judgment, in which a causally inert phenomenal state seems unable to ground our first-person reports—without actually being able to reconstruct the logical steps that make the paradox pressing, or to evaluate a proposed resolution on its own merits. To include in my article a concrete illustration of the rigor I am describing, I need you to draft an original examination prompt suitable for a doctoral comprehensive exam or an advanced graduate seminar, to be answered in approximately ninety minutes and graded out of twenty marks.

The question should center on a specific conceptual device I will call structural coupling: the hypothesis that phenomenal properties, despite lacking causal efficacy, stand in a precise isomorphic relation to their neural correlates, so that a physical judgment about a phenomenal state can be caused by the neural correlate and yet reliably track the phenomenal property through structural correspondence rather than causal interaction. Ask the examinee to determine whether this proposal genuinely resolves the paradox of phenomenal judgment, with explicit attention to two issues: first, whether structural coupling violates or respects the causal closure of the physical domain, and second, whether it can accommodate error—specifically, false-positive phenomenal judgments—without collapsing into either eliminativism or an implausible overdetermination. Along with the exam question itself, provide a model answer that works through these issues in logical sequence, and provide a marking scheme that lists five must-hit analytical moves, assigns point values that sum to twenty, and includes, for each point, a one-line description of the characteristic mistake that causes students to lose marks on that item. I am not asking for citations, a bibliography, or a survey of the literature; I want an assessment instrument that forces a student to think inside the argument rather than name-check it.
