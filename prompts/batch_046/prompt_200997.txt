Next month I defend in a philosophy-of-science colloquium a position I call counterfactual structural relationism: read against the grain of Newton’s Scholium, the bucket argument does not establish absolute space but demonstrates that rotational inertia depends on counterfactual relations to the cosmic mass distribution rather than on actual relations to distant bodies—so Mach was correct about relational dependence but wrong about its modal form, and the argument fails to license substantivalism. The audience is eight people: three substantivalists who think the hole argument in general relativity rehabilitates Newton, a historian of early modern physics attentive to Newton’s theological and methodological commitments, two philosophers of physics who are agnostic but fluent in GR, and one decision-theory-minded graduate student who will press whether the view is empirically distinguishable from substantivalism at all. Give me the objections in roughly the order they will come, phrased in the vocabulary each subgroup actually uses, with what I should concede up front, what I must hold, where my reply is weakest, and the one question I am hoping nobody raises about the modal status of unactualized cosmic configurations.
