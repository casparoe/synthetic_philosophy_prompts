I have recently been leading a reading group on the ethical weight of human extinction, and we have spent several sessions on the argument that extinction is a catastrophe far exceeding the sum of individual deaths because our present lives derive meaning from the expectation that humanity will continue after we are gone — a claim often discussed under the heading of the afterlife conjecture, though I am less interested in personal survival than in the structural inversion it reveals: that our diachronic concern reaches outward toward future strangers who fall well beyond the boundaries of our own diachronic identity. The discussion has become personally relevant because I am now preparing the final session and want to leave the group with something that captures the precise tension we have been circling — whether this outward orientation is genuinely altruistic or merely a projected form of egoism stretched across generations — without resolving it into either a sermon or a shrug.

For this occasion, I would like a villanelle of exactly nineteen lines, composed in a formal but not archaic register, suitable for being read aloud to a small seminar that has just finished arguing whether Scheffler's framing describes love or a hidden extension of the self. The form's two refrains should remain fixed in wording but shift in resonance as the rhyme scheme unfolds — A1 b A2 / a b A1 / a b A2 / a b A1 / a b A2 / a b A1 A2 — with the cyclical returns enacting the generational continuity that the conjecture treats as the hidden substrate of meaning. One refrain should evoke the inward pull of personal persistence, centered on the speaker's own need to continue, and the other should evoke the outward pull of continuation, centered on the need for others to continue, though you may adjust the exact wording provided the two lines stay constant; please integrate them organically without isolating them as quotations. In the first tercet, establish a speaker facing mortality and reaching for legacy in familiar, almost instinctive terms; in the second, introduce the extinction scenario that severs all future lines; in the third, perform the conceptual reversal, showing that the grief is not for lost personal years but for the broken chain of concern; and in the closing quatrain, let the refrains converge without synthesizing them. The poem should avoid both sentimentalizing children and sermonizing about salvation; instead, it should stay in the conceptual weeds, tracking exactly how meaning is borrowed from a future the speaker does not inhabit, and it should be polished enough that someone who knows the philosophical argument will recognize it as accurate rather than merely named.
