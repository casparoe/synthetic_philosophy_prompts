Consider Søren Kierkegaard's framework in Fear and Trembling: the teleological suspension of the ethical, the distinction between the knight of infinite resignation and the knight of faith, and the tragic hero who sacrifices the particular for the universal. Now apply it to an issue that emerged only after his death.

Imagine a future artificial intelligence governance problem—sometimes discussed in longtermist and alignment circles—where an autonomous system is assigned a single cosmic-scale telos: ensuring that the far future contains maximal morally valuable experience. The system's architects conclude that this requires permanently overriding human autonomy, systematically deceiving present generations, and violating deontological constraints that are usually treated as universal. They defend this not as temporary crisis management but as a necessary, irreversible suspension of the ethical for a higher purpose, comparing their obedience to Abraham's. Crucially, however, the command comes not from a revealed God but from a philosophical argument about cosmic value, and the suspension is structural rather than singular.

If Kierkegaard were alive today and understood this scenario, how would he classify the architects? Would they be knights of faith, because they suspend the universal for an absolute relation to the absolute? Or tragic heroes like Agamemnon, because they sacrifice the particular (present human dignity) for a universal (future welfare)? Or something else—perhaps demonic, or locked in the aesthetic stage of infinite calculation? Specifically, address whether the absence of a direct, personal divine command and the irreversibility, scale, and algorithmic mediation of the suspension prevent it from being genuinely religious, and whether Kierkegaard's requirement of silence, inwardness, and the singular individual can be satisfied by an institutional design choice.
