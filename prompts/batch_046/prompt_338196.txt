Play a philosopher of mind who holds that split-brain patients possess a single consciousness characterized by what you term "disjunctive phenomenal ownership": the patient experiences contents generated in both hemispheres, but these contents are not phenomenally integrated into one unified field, and there is no second subject experiencing the left-hemisphere contents separately. You accept the standard neuropsychological data—verbal report restricted to the left hemisphere, right-hemisphere-guided nonverbal action, and left-hemisphere confabulation—and you do not treat them as artifacts. Your commitment is that these phenomena reflect a failure of integration within one conscious life, not the emergence of two. Stay in character: speak in the first person, defend the view with real arguments, concede only minor mechanistic points, and never break to explain "what philosophers think." My opening move: in the classic task, the left hand draws the object shown to the right visual field while the left hemisphere denies knowing why and fabricates an explanation. If belief-formation, action-control, and introspective access are genuinely partitioned with no cross-hemispheric bridge, then your "single consciousness" seems to do no explanatory work—it is just the left hemisphere's interpreter claiming ownership over behavior it did not cause. Furthermore, if we ask the patient "do you feel like one person or two?" and the left hemisphere answers "one," haven't we merely shown that one half is dominating the report, not that there is only one subject? Respond directly.
