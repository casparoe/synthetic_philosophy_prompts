I've been trying to work out, in my own terms, why some uses of money feel corrupting rather than merely unfair, and I keep running into judgments that don't line up neatly with harm or exploitation alone. Here are the cases that stick with me.

First, I think it's clearly wrong for a store to charge ten times the normal price for bottled water right after a hurricane, even if every buyer can technically afford it and no one is physically forced to purchase.

Second, I think selling your vote in a local election is wrong in a closely related way, though I can't quite say whether it's the exact same wrongness.

Third, I think it's wrong for a person with two healthy kidneys to sell one to a wealthy stranger for $150,000, even with full informed consent and safe surgery. But I don't feel the same about donating blood for a small stipend — that feels acceptable — so the problem isn't just "selling body parts."

Fourth, I feel it's wrong to pay your own child for getting good grades, even if the child wants the money and the family isn't poor. Yet I'm unsure why paying a neighbor's child to mow your lawn feels different and generally okay.

Fifth, there's a real difference, to me, between leaving a voluntary tip for a server and a restaurant imposing a mandatory service charge and presenting it as the same thing. The mandatory version feels like it takes something that should be a spontaneous gesture and locks it into a price.

Sixth, I think it's wrong to offer cash to a friend who has just volunteered to drive you to the airport in an emergency, even if the amount is generous. But hiring a stranger through an app for the identical drive feels perfectly fine.

Seventh, I think it's wrong for a hospital to charge a separate fee for a chaplain's visit, framing it as a "spiritual comfort service," even if patients can opt out. Charging for an optional private room upgrade feels different — still somewhat uncomfortable, but not corrupt in the same way.

Eighth, I think paying someone to stand in line for concert tickets is fine, and paying for grocery delivery is fine, but paying an actor to attend your wedding as a fake guest — with full transparency that it's a paid role — still feels wrong, even though everyone consents and no one is deceived about the arrangement.

What I'm reaching for is something like "normative layering": money becomes corrupting when it's inserted into a relationship or practice that already carries its own non-market expectations, and the payment doesn't replace those expectations but sits on top of them, distorting them. But this idea is half-baked. It doesn't clearly explain why grade-payment is wrong but lawn-payment isn't, or why mandatory service charges feel different from voluntary tips, or why a transparent paid wedding guest is worse than a paid line-stander.

Can you state a precise principle — or a small family of principles — that captures as many of these verdicts as possible? For each clause, say which case motivates it. Then apply your principle to a case I haven't listed: what if a city charges residents a "community solidarity fee" for access to public parks, marketed not as an entry fee but as a contribution to shared upkeep? Would your principle call that corrupt, unfair, or something else?

Also, please be honest about which of my verdicts your principle has to overturn, and whether a simpler principle exists that saves more of them at the cost of greater complexity. I'm not looking for a bibliography or a literature review — I'm trying to clarify my own thinking.
