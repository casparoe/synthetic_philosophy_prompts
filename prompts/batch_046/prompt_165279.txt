Here is an argument extending Devlin's defence of legal moralism that now strikes me as suspicious, since its conclusion appears to license far more state intrusion than Devlin intended.

(1) Devlin is correct that a shared moral code can be a necessary condition for social cohesion, and that law may therefore protect that code when it faces a genuine threat.
(2) The appropriate test for whether a practice threatens the code is the intensity of intolerance, indignation and disgust it produces among ordinary members of society.
(3) If this test is valid, its application must turn on the strength of collective sentiment rather than on whether the practice causes tangible harm to others; otherwise Hart's harm principle, not Devlin's view, would be doing the work.
(4) Consider now minority philosophical commitments—say, the private adoption of rigorous asceticism that rejects conventional family obligations, or ritual practices symbolically repudiating property rights. In many societies these evoke widespread disgust, yet they cause no direct injury.
(5) By (2) and (3), the law ought to prohibit them whenever the sentiment reaches sufficient intensity.
(6) Moreover, if preserving cohesion justifies suppressing practices that weaken shared morality, then it also justifies suppressing liberal institutions—such as unrestricted philosophical debate or market exchange—that actively encourage moral disagreement and thereby erode uniformity.
(7) Consistency therefore demands either that liberal societies abandon these institutions, or that Devlin's test applies only to a privileged subclass of moral issues, with no principled distinction available.
(8) Yet no liberal society suppresses philosophical dissent or private asceticism on these grounds, and Devlin explicitly denies seeking totalitarian control.
(9) Hence the argument proves too much: taken consistently, Devlin's criterion justifies suppressing liberal dissent itself.

Where does this reasoning fail? Is there a hidden equivocation between threatening social existence and merely offending majority sentiment? Or does one of the premises—perhaps (3) or (6)—contain a scope or quantifier shift that illicitly transfers the burden of justification? I would prefer to retain (1) and (8), but I am unsure whether saving them requires abandoning (2) altogether or merely restricting its domain. What is the least costly repair?
