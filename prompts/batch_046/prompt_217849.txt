As a software engineer without formal philosophy training, I serve on the technical advisory committee of a civic-tech nonprofit that builds structured decision tools for municipal ethics boards. These boards receive petitions asking whether a present-day institution—such as a city government, a university, or a state agency—should formally acknowledge collective guilt for historical harms committed by predecessor regimes or earlier generations of citizens. Examples include forced displacement of indigenous communities, administrative collaboration with authoritarian deportation policies, or environmental destruction carried out decades ago.

I need you to translate Karl Jaspers’ framework on collective guilt—specifically his distinctions among criminal, political, moral, and metaphysical guilt, and his arguments about whether guilt can be inherited across generations—into a reusable, step-by-step decision procedure that a non-philosopher board member could apply in about forty-five minutes per petition. Present it as an ordered checklist in words: the questions to ask in sequence, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, explicit stop points where the answer is no acknowledgment warranted, and explicit hand-off points where the question is one of values or democratic politics rather than fact and must go to an authorized human decision-maker. The procedure should be designed for repeated use with consistent inputs and outputs, but it must flag exactly where judgment calls are unavoidable and where it is likely to misfire—for instance, if applied to successor states that underwent revolutionary breaks or to institutions that have been radically restructured.

To make this usable in our specific context, integrate an additional structural test I have drafted, called the Continuity-of-Institution Audit. This audit consists of three sub-checks: first, legal and administrative succession—does the current body inherit the legal identity of the actor? second, democratic mandate continuity—was there an unbroken chain of legitimate authorization linking present representatives to the past actions? third, material benefit retention—does the present institution still hold assets or advantages derived directly from the harm? This audit should sit alongside Jaspers’ categories, not replace them.

Then apply the completed procedure to three cases:

1. A present-day German municipal government being asked to issue a formal statement of political guilt for the actions of its 1930s–1940s predecessor administration that managed deportation logistics. This should be a case where the procedure yields an answer the board will likely accept.

2. A contemporary university in the American South being asked to accept collective moral guilt for slave labor used to build its original campus in the 1840s, despite having undergone full governance restructuring in the 1960s and holding no direct legal continuity with the original charter. This should be the case where the procedure gives an answer many stakeholders will dislike.

3. A hypothetical Nordic national government being asked to acknowledge metaphysical guilt for environmental degradation caused by industrial policies of the 1950s, where legal succession is intact but democratic mandate was interrupted by an unelected caretaker administration during part of the period. This should test the hand-off point.

Finally, state clearly what this procedure cannot do: which kinds of petitions fall outside its scope, which philosophical disputes it deliberately suppresses in order to remain operational, and what risks arise if a board treats its outputs as binding rather than advisory.

Please do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations. If you reference specific distinctions or works, paraphrase them without quotation marks, and do not ask me to verify sources. The deliverable should be something my board—composed of engineers, lawyers, and community representatives with no philosophy background—can run without consulting academic literature.
