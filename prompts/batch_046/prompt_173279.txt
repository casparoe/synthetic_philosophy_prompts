Since I recently had to design a decision protocol for an autonomous system and, separately, faced a personal choice between two genuinely equal career paths, I have been thinking about choice without preference. Here are my verdicts on cases, and I want to know whether one principle captures them, or whether I must drop some.

First, Buridan's ass facing two identical hay bales: rationality requires picking one arbitrarily; paralysis is irrational, not proof of hidden preference. Second, a hiring committee with two candidates tied on every job-relevant metric: they should resolve the tie by a fair lottery, not by manufacturing a reason. Third, I recently spent months unable to choose between two graduate programs that match on every dimension I care about; I think this paralysis is a failure, but once I arbitrarily choose, I should not later treat the rejected path as the mistake I made. Fourth, a parent with one life-saving dose and two children with identical need: they must not let both die, and a coin flip is permissible, yet the choice should feel tragic rather than routine. Fifth, an AI with a utility function that assigns exactly equal value to two actions: it needs a deterministic tie-breaker to avoid looping, but that breaker should not be read as revealing a true preference.

These feel partly unified and partly in tension—especially between treating arbitrary resolution as liberating in personal life and treating it as tragic but mandatory in moral allocation. Is there a coherent principle here—perhaps distinguishing domains where arbitrary choice generates reasons from those where it merely terminates deliberation? If so, what does it predict for a case I have not listed: a democratic assembly deadlocked between two identical policy texts, where voting is impossible? And please say what would change your mind: which argument or feature of a case would lead you to reject the principle you propose, and where is your reasoning most likely to fail?
