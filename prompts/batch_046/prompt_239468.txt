You have been asked to deliver the opening remarks at an interdisciplinary workshop on political legitimacy, hosted jointly by a political science department and a philosophy center. The room is divided evenly between empirical scholars of East Asia—several of whom have conducted fieldwork on state capacity in Singapore and on cadre evaluation in the People's Republic of China—and normative theorists who regard legitimacy as a moral relation between ruler and ruled rather than as a descriptive feature of stable governance. Your task is not to survey the literature on performance legitimacy, but to use it as structural scaffolding for a problem that both groups tend to miss when they talk past each other. You should distinguish sharply between performance as a generator of popular compliance—the sociological fact that prosperity and order can secure acquiescence without democratic authorization—and performance as a normative justification for authoritarian rule, the claim that high output entitles a regime to govern. To ground this distinction in institutional specifics that the empiricists will recognize, reference the Singapore Housing Development Board's role in tying material welfare to political quiescence, and the Chinese Communist Party's integration of GDP-related metrics into cadre promotion. At the same time, you should press the normative philosophers with a further, less familiar distinction: between performance legitimacy and what might be called administrative legitimacy, the latter being the idea that state capacity and bureaucratic rationality themselves confer a right to rule. You want to suggest that this second idea is doing hidden work in defenses of Singapore and China, and that it collapses the distinction between being well-governed and being legitimately governed. The speech should be addressed to both audiences simultaneously: it must not dismiss empirical findings about stability and growth, but it must also refuse to let those findings settle the moral question. End not with a thesis but with a framing question for the workshop—specifically, whether there is any institutional mechanism through which performance could be transformed into genuine authorization without becoming democratic in form. Keep the remarks to roughly eight minutes, and avoid treating liberal democracy as the unexamined baseline against which these regimes are judged.
