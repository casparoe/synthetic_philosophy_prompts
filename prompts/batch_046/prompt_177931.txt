I'm presenting a paper next Thursday to our university's political philosophy workshop defending a non-liberal, metaphysically grounded reading of Spinoza's democratic theory. My central thesis is that in the Tractatus Politicus Spinoza does not anticipate modern constitutional democracy but instead develops what I call a theory of cognitive-affective sovereignty: democratic legitimacy derives not from representation, consent, or delegated authority, but from the structural alignment of the multitude's collective power (potentia multitudinis) with adequate ideas of common advantage, achieved only through direct, non-delegated collective reasoning that transforms the passions of hope and fear into rational agreement. I argue that any intermediate institutional body—representative assemblies, delegated councils, or expert administrations—introduces a private conatus that diverges from the common good, which is precisely why Spinoza warns that democracy degenerates into aristocracy or monarchy the moment the council is not coextensive with the whole multitude. The argument leans heavily on the metaphysics of the Ethics—particularly the account of adequate ideas, the parallelism of affect and cognition, and the claim that power and right are coextensive—to show that institutional design is not an afterthought for Spinoza but is constrained by whether a given arrangement can produce adequate common notions in the bodies that compose it.

The audience will be about fifteen people: five historians of early modern philosophy who read Spinoza in Latin and are vigilant about anachronism; six contemporary democratic theorists who work on deliberative democracy, representation, and liberal constitutionalism and who regularly recruit canonical figures for modern normative debates; two political theorists focused on affect, power, and sovereignty in contemporary politics; and two graduate students. They care deeply about whether normative institutional prescriptions can legitimately be extracted from metaphysical premises, they are skeptical of readings that flatten Spinoza's historical context, and the democratic theorists in particular are likely to defend representation as a practical and moral necessity that Spinoza could not have rejected in principle. For each objection, provide a concise reply suitable for about two minutes of speaking time, and flag explicitly where the reply is weakest or depends on an interpretive leap rather than textual certainty. Include the one objection I am hoping nobody asks—the challenge that targets the transition from Spinoza's metaphysics of conatus and adequate ideas to my specific normative claim about non-delegated institutional design. Also indicate what I should concede upfront about the historical limitations of applying Spinoza's view and where I should hold firm on the metaphysical reading, so that I do not sound either naively presentist or defensively obscurantist.
