As a retired experimental physicist who has only recently turned to philosophy of logic with any seriousness, I have become preoccupied with a disagreement that seems both ancient and curiously immobile. The problem is the one raised in Lewis Carroll’s 1895 dialogue for the journal Mind, in which a tortoise accepts premises about the equality of triangle sides and even accepts the hypothetical bridge principle that if those premises hold then the conclusion follows, yet refuses to grant the conclusion unless that bridge is added as an explicit further premise, producing an infinite regress of justification. The familiar responses are well known: one camp treats modus ponens and related rules as primitive constitutive rules of a formal system, so that demanding their internal justification is a category error analogous to asking for the physical cause of a conservation law; another camp, influenced by Wittgenstein, treats the regress as revealing that applying logical rules is a form of practical competence or knowing-how that cannot be fully propositionalized, and that justification ultimately bottoms out in shared practice rather than in further premises. Despite decades of exchange—invoking distinctions between implication and inference, between object-language and meta-language, between explicit knowledge and embodied skill—the two sides have not moved appreciably. My own hypothesis, likely distorted by my scientific training, is that the stall is not about logic per se but about a hidden disagreement over what counts as an adequate explanation of a regularity: one side expects deductive steps to be explained by reduction to non-logical mechanisms, whether causal, psychological, or social, while the other treats logical rules as sui generis explanatory primitives. Yet I worry I may be projecting a framework from physics onto a conceptual dispute, and that the real structure might be different—perhaps the sides are answering two different questions, one normative about warrant and one descriptive about cognitive process, under the single heading of justification, or perhaps the disagreement is merely verbal about the word justification itself. Could you diagnose the actual structure of this impasse? Is it substantive, verbal, cross-purpose, or a sign that the question is ill-posed? Is there a concealed empirical issue—say, about whether human reasoning operates through explicit symbol-manipulation or through pattern-recognition—that neither side has acknowledged? And given your diagnosis, what would genuinely count as progress: a formal result establishing the minimality of inference rules, an empirical finding in cognitive science, a conceptual distinction that separates normative warrant from psychological process, or should we conclude that no resolution should be expected and why?
