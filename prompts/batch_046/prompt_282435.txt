After a long, circular argument with a friend—she kept insisting that self-deception is just hypocrisy with extra steps, while I kept pointing to cases where people genuinely seem to miss what they are avoiding—I want to step back and specify the target before reading any more theories. Write the full desiderata that an adequate theory of self-deception must meet: the phenomena it has to explain, the distinctions it must preserve, the conceptual puzzles it must dissolve or accommodate, and the results from philosophy of mind or cognitive psychology it cannot contradict. For each requirement, provide the motivating case or consideration behind it—specifically, how it handles the possibility of simultaneously holding and not holding the same belief without collapsing into simple error or bad faith; why it must respect the phenomenological asymmetry that the deception is directed inward yet feels like being fooled by an other; how it accounts for strategic ignorance or evidence-management that stops short of contradictory belief; and why it must be consistent with the largely involuntary nature of belief formation. Mark which requirements are genuinely non-negotiable and which are camp-specific preferences—such as whether the theory must invoke a divided or partitioned mind, or whether a unified-agent framework can suffice. Then identify any subset of these desiderata that appears jointly unsatisfiable—whether, for instance, full doxastic transparency is required together with strategic self-manipulation of evidence, or whether doxastic voluntarism can be maintained alongside reason-responsiveness in belief. Briefly score the main standard contenders—motivated-irrationality accounts, partitionist or divided-self models, and deflationary tactical-management views—against the list, but keep the weight on the criteria. Finally, separate clearly what is settled from what remains genuinely contested among specialists, and do not present any contested position as the consensus.
