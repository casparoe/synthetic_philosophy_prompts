I'm a novelist drafting a novel about a biographer who insists that lives are the best school for virtue, and I'm trying to clarify my own convictions about when biography actually teaches. Here are my verdicts on seven cases, mostly consistent but with one pair that pulls in opposite directions, and I want to know whether they point to a single principle or force a choice.

First, when I read Plutarch's paired lives of figures like Alexander and Caesar—men whose ambition achieved greatness and then destroyed them—I feel I genuinely learn about the architecture of ambition: how it drives action and then overreaches. The vices are not hidden; they are shown in operation. Second, when I read a hagiography of a medieval saint that suppresses every trace of doubt, anger, or failure, I feel I learn nothing. The figure becomes an icon, not a person, and iconography does not instruct. Third, a biography of a contemporary novelist whose personal betrayals and artistic achievements clearly grew from the same psychological root teaches me something precise about the cost of vision—provided the biography renders the internal reasoning, not merely the external events. Fourth, a biography of a slaveholding colonial administrator that meticulously records his cruelty but explains it away as inevitable for a man of his time teaches me nothing; the historical explanation replaces the subject's own practical reasoning rather than exposing it. Fifth, a purely invented biography—say, of a fictional diplomat created for a novel—can teach me the same lesson about moral complicity that a real biography teaches, if the internal reasoning is rendered with comparable precision. The fact that the diplomat never lived does not seem to diminish the lesson. Sixth, a beautifully written biography of a genuinely virtuous person who lived without dramatic failure still feels thin as moral instruction; it inspires admiration, but it does not show me how virtue operates under pressure. Seventh, and here is the tension: a biography that renders a real villain's internal reasoning with perfect transparency, yet writes with such sustained ironic contempt that the reader is never invited to recognize anything of themselves in him—I feel this teaches less than the invented diplomat in case five, even though the real figure's actions are documented and the reasoning is fully exposed.

Please state any principle or small family of principles precisely, indicating which clause is motivated by which case, how it predicts the outcome for a case I have not listed—specifically, a biography of an ordinary person whose single catastrophic moral error is narrated with complete sympathy but with no attempt to understand its moral structure—and which of my seven verdicts, especially the disparity between the real villain rendered with contempt and the invented diplomat, must be overturned if we prefer simplicity over saving every intuition. I am not asking for a bibliography or a literature review; I want the conceptual work done directly, with an honest statement of what the account cannot accommodate.
