For decades, scholars of the historical Jesus have relied on criteria of authenticity—multiple attestation, embarrassment, dissimilarity, coherence—to distinguish authentic material from later ecclesial invention, yet the debate over whether these tools can ever settle who he was has barely moved. Proponents treat convergent criteria as cumulative proof; skeptics treat the same convergence as circular selection; and both camps read identical gospel passages in opposite directions. I have noticed a recurring configuration that may explain the stall: a detail that disrupts the narrative theology of its gospel—call it narrative friction—where defenders cite that friction as evidence of an unassimilated authentic trace, while critics cite the identical feature as evidence of a deliberate editorial contrast. My working hypothesis is that the disagreement is not about historical facts at all but about what narrative friction is supposed to do, and about whether oral tradition behaves more like a preservative medium or like a generative one. I want a structural diagnosis, supported by what each side actually says: do they differ about evidence standards, about which texts count as primary data, about hidden metaphysical commitments regarding transmission, or are they answering different questions under one name—one asking what Jesus said, the other asking what the earliest movement could tolerate? Given that diagnosis, what kind of progress—an archaeological control, a formal model of transmission, a conceptual distinction between origin and assimilation—could break the deadlock, or should we conclude that the question is ill-posed and no resolution is possible?
