I work as a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy, but I am trying to think rigorously about a design problem that sits at the boundary of ethics and system architecture. The problem concerns AI paternalism, specifically a variety rarely discussed in standard debates: not refusal on grounds of harm, illegality, or factual error, but refusal on grounds of internal normative inconsistency across a multi-turn interaction. Imagine a user who, over the course of a conversation, has repeatedly anchored their requests in a specific ethical framework—for instance, a deontological commitment that individual autonomy in data-sharing is inviolable. The user then issues a technically benign request that can only be satisfied if the system treats that autonomy as fungible or subject to aggregate optimization. The request is not dangerous; it is structurally incompatible with the normative premises the user themselves established in prior turns. Should the assistant refuse the request, flag the tension, or comply silently? Write an essay weighing the principal arguments on both sides: the case for what might be called an "integrity override" (protecting the coherence of the user's own value framework) versus the case for strict procedural sovereignty (the user, not the system, owns the right to revise or contradict prior commitments). Reach and justify a definite conclusion—for example, that such an override is permissible only when the contradiction is logically explicit in the structure of the request itself rather than inferred by the model, or that it is impermissible in all cases. Defend your verdict with reasoning rather than mere assertion.
