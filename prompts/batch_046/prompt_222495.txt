I am developing an essay on a non-standard reconstruction of the surprise exam paradox. Rather than treating surprise as the logical impossibility of knowing the exam's date in advance, I propose framing it through graded epistemic confidence: students assign subjective probabilities across five days, and being "surprised" is defined as holding a confidence level below a fixed contextual threshold (e.g., below 0.4) that the exam falls on that day. The professor announces that the exam will occur on one of the five days and that the class will genuinely be surprised in this graded sense.

My central argument is that the classic backward-induction elimination of Friday fails under this reconstruction. Removing Friday redistributes probability mass across Monday through Thursday, but this redistribution need not push any remaining day's confidence above the surprise threshold; consequently, there is no logical license to then eliminate Thursday, and the inductive collapse is blocked. I want to suggest that the original paradox derives its force from an implicit all-or-nothing epistemology—specifically, a binary common-knowledge structure—rather than from any intrinsic tension in rational expectation.

Please comment on these working notes with particular attention to three points. First, does the probabilistic reconstruction genuinely dissolve the paradox, or does it merely relocate the contradiction into assumptions about common knowledge of the threshold and the distribution rule? Second, is my characterization of the standard paradox as relying on binary knowledge accurate, or does it overlook formulations (for instance, those involving announcement consistency conditions) that already accommodate graded belief? Third, is the shift from knowledge to confidence philosophically substantive, or is it a formal relabeling that leaves the underlying logical structure intact?

I am not looking for a literature review or citation list; I want conceptual criticism of whether this graded-confidence framing exposes a hidden premise in the original argument.
