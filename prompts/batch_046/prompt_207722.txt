I have been trying to determine what makes an archival silence politically significant rather than merely accidental, and my judgments on particular cases are not fully consistent. Consider the following: in a colonial census, women are not listed as economic agents; I take this absence to be an active trace of gendered power. In a post-revolutionary purge, files on a specific ethnic minority are burned; I take this absence to be a direct continuation of violence by other means. But in a provincial archive, correspondence in a local dialect was never translated and is now unreadable to researchers; I am inclined to call this absence contingent and not politically charged, even though the neglect tracks the same power dynamics. Meanwhile, a museum catalog classifies ritual objects as weapons because the collectors could not imagine non-military use; the original meaning is not missing from the record but occluded by the interpretive frame. Finally, in a digital repository, documents are preserved but tagged only in the colonizer's language, rendering them invisible to local search; I feel this is closer to the census case than to the dialect case.

Is there a coherent principle that distinguishes the politically charged absence—the census omission, the burned files, the mis-tagged documents—from the contingent one—the untranslated dialect—and that also decides whether hermeneutic occlusion belongs with the former? Or must I overturn one of my verdicts, perhaps the distinction between the dialect case and the tagging case, to preserve a simpler account? And what should the resulting principle predict for a case I have not listed: an oral history project that deliberately avoids interviewing members of a dominant group, leaving them unrecorded?
