I've been trying to sketch out an essay for my cognitive-science course about Hofstadter's Letter Spirit program, you know, the one that generates alphabet letters in different styles but keeps them recognizible. I was talking with my roomate about it and he said maybe the "letter spirit" idea works less like feature extraction and more like an ecological niche system, so now I'm playing with this concept I'm calling "alphabetic homeostasis." The basic claim is that an "a" doesn't stay an "a" by matching some hidden platonic template; instead, it maintains its identity through a kind of relational tension with every other character in the alphabet. If you deform the "a" too far in the direction of an "o", the system resists not because of an internal "a-ruler," but because the "o" is already occupying that morphological territory and the grid needs internal differentiation to stay readable. I think this might explain why Letter Spirit's grid-font outputs feel so weird yet coherent — the program isn't copying a canonical form, it's navigating a field of competing letter-spirits where each shape is defined by what it isn't, almost like negative space made active.

I'm not sure if I'm overreaching with the biology analogy here — homeostasis usually implies a fixed set-point, not just spatial competition between categories. Could you comment on whether this framing holds water?
