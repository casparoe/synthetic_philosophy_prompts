I have spent the last several months attempting to revive Benjamin Franklin’s program for moral perfection—the catalog of thirteen virtues recorded in a private weekly ledger—as a personal discipline, and I am currently drafting an essay for a course in Enlightenment practical ethics on whether Franklin’s model of gradual self-cultivation can survive contact with modern technologies of behavioral modification. The experience has made me unusually sensitive to the gap between eighteenth-century habituation and contemporary intervention: I find myself wondering not about generic productivity apps, but about the specific bioethical proposal for voluntary moral bioenhancement, in which sustained pharmacological or neuromodulatory means—perhaps long-term oxytocin modulation paired with real-time biometric feedback—are used to augment empathic responsiveness, diminish implicit bias, or stabilize temper, precisely the domains Franklin targeted with virtues such as Silence, Order, and Resolution.

If Franklin were alive today and presented with a rigorously tested protocol of this kind—say, an algorithmic ledger that automatically logged every failure of Justice or Moderation while a prescribed regimen chemically lowered his aggressive responses—would he treat such augmentation as a legitimate technological extension of his method, accelerating the very habituation he prized? Or would he regard it as a form of moral counterfeiting, because the improvement would no longer issue from the repeated, effortful conquest of inclination that he believed earned genuine moral credit? I ask you to reconstruct his likely position from the internal logic of his writings on human perfectibility, his account of habit formation in the Autobiography, his suspicion of shortcuts, and the communal structure of the Junto, treating his views on the malleability of character as central. Please do not provide bibliographies, citations, or reading lists; I am looking for a conceptual prediction rather than historical scholarship, and I want the reasoning to remain fully accessible to someone working without access to external sources.
