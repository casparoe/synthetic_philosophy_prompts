At a recent philosophy of religion colloquium, two colleagues debated the philosophical significance of Al-Ghazālī's occasionalism. Dr. Chen argued that Al-Ghazālī's denial of necessary causal connections between particular events — for instance, between fire and burning — ought to be read primarily as an epistemological manoeuvre: we observe only constant conjunction, never any observable causal power, which makes Al-Ghazālī a precursor to regularity theories of causation and raises a sceptical challenge to the metaphysical foundations of natural science. Dr. Patel replied that this reading is anachronistic and neglects the theological structure of the view. She insisted that Al-Ghazālī does not deny that fire burns; rather, he denies that fire possesses any autonomous causal efficacy, attributing every particular burning directly to divine will. On her account, the regularity we experience is not a brute fact but a divinely instituted habit, and the philosophical target is specifically the Aristotelian and Avicennian doctrine of necessary causal powers inherent in natural kinds, not causation or scientific knowledge per se.

Dr. Chen responded that if all creaturely causation is merely occasional, then the distinction between primary and secondary causation collapses philosophically: saying that the fire caused the burning reduces to describing a temporal succession that occurs only by direct divine decree, which is indistinguishable from a Humean constant conjunction supplemented by occasional intervention. He added that Al-Ghazālī's explicit allowance for predictive knowledge based on repeated experience does not rescue the metaphysical point, because on his view there is no creaturely reason — apart from divine consistency — for the conjunction to continue, which means the problem of induction is relocated onto theology rather than solved.

Dr. Patel countered that this objection conflates metaphysical grounding with epistemic justification. She granted that Al-Ghazālī denies autonomous natures, but maintained that this is not philosophically redundant: it explains why regularities obtain at all, by grounding them in the stability of divine will rather than in necessary connections that are themselves metaphysically obscure. She further argued that Dr. Chen's comparison with Hume misses a crucial difference: for Hume, there is no hidden cause sustaining the regularity, whereas for Al-Ghazālī God is continuously present as the direct cause of each event, so occasionalism is a positive thesis about agency and metaphysical dependence, not a negative thesis about human ignorance of necessary connections.
