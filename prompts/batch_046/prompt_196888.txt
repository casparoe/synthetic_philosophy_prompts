hey, I'm writing a short essay for a philosphy of science seminar and I wanted to run the core argument by you. The topic is Humeanism vs anti-Humeanism about laws of nature, but I'm trying to avoid the standard 'best system' vs 'necessary connections' replay.

My angle: I call it 'conditional lawhood'. In physics we constantly use ceteris paribus clauses—like Ohm's law only holds if temperature is constant, etc. Humeans can say these are just regularities restricted to stable backgrounds. Anti-Humeans (especially dispostional essentialists) have to treat those background conditions as part of the essential structure that grounds the law. So far so standard. But then I push it: if every law is conditional on some deeper essential structure, then the anti-Humean never actually delivers an unconditional metaphysical necessity. The distinction between law and accident just gets pushed up to the level of the background conditions. I call this the 'vacuity push-up' problem. It doesn't defeat essentialism, but it makes the view look structurally similar to a layered Humeanism where you just have regularities all the way down, except with fancy ontology.

I also wonder if this connects to the Noether theorem / symmetry debate—whether conservation laws are substantive or just structural—but I haven't worked that out.

Can you comment on whether this is coherent? Am I misrepresenting Bird-style dispostional essentialism? Is the 'vacuity push-up' just Armstrong's old objection about necessary connections being mysterious, or is it different? Any suggestions on how to sharpen the distinction between my view and regular Humeanism?

No need for references, just tell me if the idea holds water or if I'm reinventing the wheel.
