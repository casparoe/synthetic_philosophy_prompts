He summarized the view—which he attributed to a cluster of aesthetic arguments about causal history and perceptual identity—roughly as follows. First, he said that if two paintings are perceptually indistinguishable, they share every aesthetically relevant property, so any difference in value must come from something outside the work itself, such as knowledge of its provenance. Second, he claimed that because provenance is a relational, historical fact rather than an intrinsic feature of the object, treating it as value-conferring is just a version of the genetic fallacy: we confuse how something came to be with what it is. Third, he argued that this means defenders of originals are not making an aesthetic judgment at all but are instead expressing a kind of fetishism for causal contact with the artist, and that once this is recognized, the market premium on originals becomes philosophically indefensible, leaving only contingent economic scarcity as an explanation. He concluded that anyone who insists originals are aesthetically superior is therefore either confused about the distinction between aesthetic and historical value or is rationalizing an irrational attachment.

This seems to capture something real I have read about the debate over autographic versus allographic arts and the critique of authenticity, but I worry he has compressed several distinct claims into one and perhaps overstated what the view actually entails. Could you audit this summary for me?
