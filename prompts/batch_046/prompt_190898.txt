I'm taking an upper-division seminar on logical empiricism and the philosophy of value, and we've been comparing Schlick's essay on the meaning of life with his earlier epistemological writings. Schlick distinguishes between Zweck—purpose, or striving toward a future goal—and Sinn, which he associates with play and activity valued for its own sake. He argues that purpose-driven labor can never yield a meaningful life because each achieved end is immediately replaced by a new one, whereas youth and creativity embody a kind of meaning that does not depend on further results.

My study group has been debating whether Schlick's use of Sinn here is technically continuous with the logical-empiricist analysis of meaning—specifically the Fregean distinction between Sinn and Bedeutung, or the verificationist criterion that a statement has cognitive content only if it can be empirically verified. One interpretation is that Schlick is applying semantic analysis to existence itself: he treats the claim that life has purpose as a proposition lacking empirical reference, and then proposes that describing life as play is the only formulation with genuine cognitive Sinn because playful behavior is observationally accessible. This strikes me as a category mistake—collapsing an evaluative claim about human flourishing into a thesis about linguistic reference—but I am not sure I am not missing a deeper connection. Is Schlick's philosophy of life actually an extension of his theory of knowledge, such that his praise of play is really a claim about which descriptions of human activity satisfy the conditions for empirical meaningfulness? Or is he using Sinn in an entirely ordinary, non-technical sense, which would make the essay philosophically disconnected from his logical empiricism? And if it is the latter, why would a thinker so committed to eliminating metaphysics devote an essay to a concept—life's meaning—that appears to resist empirical analysis altogether?
