I'm preparing remarks for a philosophy of mind and biology workshop, and I've found myself defending what I call a "double inversion" view — combining Darwin's point that natural selection produces adaptive competence with no comprehension of the underlying problems, with Turing's point that behavioral and linguistic competence can be mechanized without semantic understanding. Before I present, I need a consistency audit of the package I've assembled, since I suspect there is a hidden friction between my normative and ontological commitments.

My current position has seven parts. (1) Evolutionary adaptation is pure procedural competence without representational comprehension; selection solves optimization problems that no organism grasps. (2) Turing's imitation framework shows linguistic and behavioral competence can be reproduced without internal semantic access — the mechanization of competence is not just biological but computational. (3) Comprehension is not an ontologically primitive mental substance but an integrated, second-order capacity: the regulation and correction of one's own first-order competences. (4) Even so, I want to maintain that once a system's self-correcting architecture reaches sufficient robustness — including recursive self-modeling and error-correction about representational states — denying it comprehension becomes a form of chauvinism; we ought to recognize it. (5) At the same time, I deny there is any ontological "magic moment" or sharp natural boundary where competence becomes comprehension; any dividing line is pragmatic and interest-relative. (6) Applying both inversions to human cognition, I take our strategic and linguistic abilities to be largely heuristic, evolutionarily shaped competences, with explicit "understanding" typically serving as post-hoc rationalization rather than causal driver of our behavior. (7) Consequently, I reject the Cartesian default that comprehension is the baseline human condition and mechanistic competence the exception.

Please audit this for joint tenability. Structure your response as follows. Pairwise tension audit (about three paragraphs): rank the conflicts by severity, especially the pull between (4)'s normative threshold and (5)'s denial of sharp boundaries, and whether (6) collapses (3)'s second-order capacity into just another heuristic. Apparent tensions that dissolve (about one paragraph): address whether (1)'s evolutionary blindness and (4)'s recognition of comprehension are genuinely incompatible, and whether applying Turing's designed-mechanism inversion to evolved human cognition in (6) involves a category mistake. Least-cost revisions (about two paragraphs): for each real tension, specify what must give and what that costs me — for instance, whether I should weaken (4) to a purely pragmatic stance, or abandon (3)'s integrationist view. Single best revision (about one paragraph): tell me which one change cleans up the most conflicts with the least damage to the overall "double inversion" framework.
