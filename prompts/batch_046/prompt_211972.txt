The first camp holds that rational beliefs about unproven mathematical sentences should be modelled as prices in a prediction market that converges, via the logical induction criterion, to coherent probabilities respecting logical relationships without requiring logical omniscience; the second camp holds that such beliefs ought to be governed by explicit, procedurally articulated deference to trusted reasoning processes, and that the market analogy improperly treats normative logical commitment as empirical forecasting about one’s own future computations.

The statement should contain: (i) a numbered list of claims both sides accept — including about the formal setting (a computable agent assigning probabilities to sentences in a fixed formal system such as Peano arithmetic, under a resource constraint forbidding exhaustive proof search), about what needs explaining (why beliefs about consistency or large prime factorisations should be non-arbitrary before proof is available), and about what the standard no-go theorems for logical omniscience actually rule out; (ii) a disagreements table stating the residual differences in wording each camp would accept for its own position, with any merely verbal disagreements explicitly flagged and separated; (iii) the cruxes — sub-questions such that resolving them would move at least one camp — including whether a logical inductor can consistently price its own future belief revisions about the induction criterion itself, and whether proceduralist deference collapses into hidden empirical assumptions about reasoning reliability. For each crux, provide a concrete case or argument both sides agree bears on it — for instance, an agent that shifts its probability on “PA is consistent” from 0.6 to 0.9 upon encountering a meta-mathematical argument without having computed any new proof within PA — together with each side’s explicit prediction or assessment of that case.

Please write in British English. Conclude with a brief note stating which argument or consideration would change your view on whether the core disagreement is substantive or partly verbal, and identify the specific point in your reasoning most likely to fail.
