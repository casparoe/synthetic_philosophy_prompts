Write a villanelle on the units-of-selection debate, using as refrains—first, the gene-level claim that evolutionary change is ultimately only change in allelic frequencies across generations, and second, the multilevel claim that some adaptive traits persist solely through between-group differences in productivity that cannot be decomposed into summed individual fitness gains. In the five tercets, alternate the refrains and let the middle lines present concrete evolutionary cases—altruistic punishment that lowers an individual's reproductive success while stabilizing cooperative norms, or a trait that spreads within groups precisely because it weakens them in competition with rival groups—so that the poem's cyclical return mirrors the philosophical problem: each level's description keeps reasserting itself without being eliminated by the other. In the closing quatrain, place the two refrains side by side without synthesis, leaving open whether group-level selection operates as an irreducible causal force or merely as a cross-level byproduct of individual-level dynamics. The form should perform this tension structurally: a reader familiar with the debate should recognize why the refrains never merge, and the poem should stand on its own for a reader who is not.
