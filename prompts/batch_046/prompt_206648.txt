I keep encountering a particular defensive maneuver in discussions that invoke the principle of indifference over continuous spaces — particularly in decision-theoretic and AI-alignment reasoning, where uniform priors are often defended by symmetry. The maneuver unfolds like this: someone assigns a uniform distribution over a continuous parameter space on grounds of symmetry; an interlocutor demonstrates that a smooth reparameterization destroys the uniformity; and rather than conceding ambiguity, the original speaker introduces an implicit constraint — "the generating process is isotropic in this coordinate system," "only linear scales are physically meaningful," or "the problem structure fixes the metric" — that wasn't present in the initial formulation. Each constraint is individually defensible, but collectively they serve to immunize the uniform prior against any reparameterization challenge without providing an independent, pre-theoretic criterion for selecting the "natural" parameterization. This feels adjacent to Bertrand's paradox, yet it isn't the paradox itself: it's a meta-level pattern in how symmetry arguments are shielded from ambiguity, not a demonstration that ambiguity exists. Does this pattern have an established name in the philosophy of probability or in the rationalist literature? If not, how should it be precisely delineated — especially with respect to ordinary auxiliary hypotheses, to the paradox proper, and to mere equivocation over the meaning of "uniform" — and what structural feature distinguishes it from a straightforward admission that the principle of indifference is parameterization-dependent?
