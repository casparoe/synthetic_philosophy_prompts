I serve on the appeals panel of the Riverdale Community Land Trust, a housing cooperative of roughly two hundred members. Last month a longtime member, M., shared confidential meeting minutes with an outside developer; the leak nearly collapsed a two-year grant negotiation. The board voted to suspend M.’s membership for one year. M. has appealed, and our panel must circulate a recommendation before Thursday’s meeting.

M. does not deny the disclosure. Rather, M. argues that at the time they were not genuinely “acting as themselves”—they describe a kind of moral fog in which they felt like an instrument of a dominant faction inside the trust, not an agent expressing their own attitude toward the rest of us. M. has been reading on their own about how blame and resentment are not just reactions to broken rules but responses to the quality of will someone shows—the idea that we only properly hold people responsible when we can see their action as expressing a certain stance toward others. M. asks us to consider that, if their will was genuinely not present in that way, then our reactive attitudes—our indignation, our withdrawal of trust—are misdirected, like being angry at a storm.

This has split our panel. Some members say the cooperative’s rules are clear and the harm is concrete; others say that if we treat M. as a fully responsible agent without examining whether the conditions for genuine agency were met, we reduce our moral response to mere rule-enforcement and miss what is actually at stake in holding someone accountable.

M. has proposed an unusual alternative: rather than upholding the suspension or overturning it entirely, the panel could issue what they call a “suspended reactive stance”—a formal finding that, for a defined period of 120 days, the cooperative withholds both punitive measures and full reinstatement of trust, during which M. must complete specific restorative actions (public disclosure of the leak’s full scope, mediated dialogue with affected members, and a written account of the conditions that led to the act). At the end of that period, the panel would reconvene to determine whether the conditions for resuming ordinary reactive attitudes—either forgiveness or sustained accountability—have been met.

Please draft the short written opinion I will circulate. It should recommend a concrete course of action with that 120-day framework or another explicit time-bound plan, and it should engage M.’s position seriously rather than restating the rule that was broken. The memo should also include an honest treatment of the strongest consideration on the losing side—whether that is the need for institutional clarity, the risk of excusing real harm, or the danger of making reactive attitudes contingent on private psychological reports—and explain why the recommendation still stands despite it. Write this as a formal document with an actionable bottom line, not as personal guidance to me.
