I am preparing a literature review on unrealistic assumptions in scientific modeling, and over the past semester I have been cataloging the verdicts my supervisor gives when we evaluate whether a particular theoretical construction is methodologically sound despite containing premises that are literally false about the system being modeled. Her record so far is as follows: she judges the ideal-gas law with its point-mass, non-interacting molecules to be fully legitimate; she accepts macroeconomic general-equilibrium frameworks that treat agents as possessing infinite computational capacity and perfect rationality; she rejects the phlogiston theory of combustion as pseudoscientific rather than merely idealized; she accepts Bohr's atomic model with its classical circular orbits for predicting hydrogen spectral lines; and she rejects applying a standard homogeneous-mixing SIR epidemiological model to a closed, highly structured population of exactly twelve individuals. These rulings seem to me jointly compatible with more than one underlying principle—perhaps a strict predictive-accuracy criterion in the spirit of Friedman's F-twist, perhaps a distinction between isolating abstractions and distorting misrepresentations of causal structure, or perhaps a domain-specific tractability constraint that permits falsehood only when it enables computation within the intended scope.

The case we will discuss next week is a proposed neurocognitive model of moral judgment that treats the ventromedial prefrontal cortex as a single, unified Bayesian optimizer equipped with a uniform prior and a linear utility scale. Applied to legal vignettes, it predicts sentencing recommendations with approximately ninety-four percent accuracy, yet it assumes synchronous, conflict-free integration of all subcortical inputs and explicitly suppresses documented modular specialization and competing affective signals within the region. Predict whether she will accept or reject this model as methodologically legitimate, identify the implicit principle or principles that best account for her earlier verdicts, articulate at least one rival principle that fits those same five cases equally well and state what that rival predicts here, offer your best-guess verdict with explicit reasoning, and propose one further hypothetical model—preferably drawn from a different disciplinary domain—that would help discriminate between the leading principle and its closest competitor.
