I'm a high school senior writing an independent philosophy essay for my final project, and I want to tackle the question of how much rule-following a society should actually demand. I know the standard sociological framing divides cultures into "tight" ones with dense norms and "loose" ones with sparse norms, but I want to move past that binary. I'm proposing a framework I call "domain-specific normative viscosity": the idea that a society should not be uniformly tight or loose, but should apply different "viscosities" depending on whether the domain regulates identity-expression—where tight norms might protect minority cohesion—or resource-allocation—where looser rules might allow adaptive redistribution. I then apply this to contemporary digital societies and argue that they have become "inversely viscous": hyper-tight about symbolic and linguistic norms because high online visibility raises the social cost of deviation, yet extremely loose about economic coordination because global platforms dissolve local labor and distributive rules. I claim this inversion is unstable because it severs the feedback loop between material practice and symbolic legitimacy, eventually producing performative conformity without genuine coordination.

Here is the outline of my draft argument:

1. Introduction: Define tight versus loose cultures in broad terms, then argue that the unit of analysis should be the domain, not the society as a whole.
2. The viscosity metaphor: Distinguish "thick" rules (hard to change, socially enforced) from "thin" rules (negotiable, locally adaptive) and apply the distinction separately to bodily presentation, economic exchange, and epistemic authority.
3. The inversion thesis: Show how digital platforms make economic exchange globally loose while making symbolic domains hyper-tight, not because of deliberate policy but because visibility structures change enforcement costs.
4. The instability argument: Argue that when symbolic rules become decoupled from material conditions, norm enforcement loses practical legitimacy and collapses into performative conformity.
5. Normative conclusion: Propose "calibrated viscosity"—tight coordination where shared expectations are essential, loose experimentation where welfare improves—while addressing the risk that this recommendation smuggles in an unjustified normative preference.
