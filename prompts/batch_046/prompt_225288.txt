Consider the following chain linking algorithmic information theory to normative epistemology, which I think pushes to an intolerable conclusion. (1) Solomonoff induction weights computable hypotheses by 2 raised to the negative Kolmogorov complexity of the hypothesis. (2) This weighting is standardly interpreted as a formalization of Occam's razor: hypotheses with shorter minimal descriptions receive greater prior probability. (3) Solomonoff's convergence result establishes that this prior predicts the true computable data-generating process with a cumulative loss bounded by the Kolmogorov complexity of that process, an optimality guarantee that holds universally across all computable worlds. (4) If a norm of reasoning carries a universal formal optimality proof of this kind, then rationality requires adopting that norm. (5) So rational agents ought to use Solomonoff induction. (6) But computing Kolmogorov complexity requires solving the halting problem, so Solomonoff induction is strictly uncomputable. (7) Therefore rationality demands performing computations that no physical agent can execute. (8) Hence no embodied agent can be fully rational—a conclusion that sounds less like bounded rationality and more like the impossibility of reason itself. Where does the argument fail? Is the misstep the normative leap from formal optimality to rational obligation in (4), the identification of Occam's razor with this particular prior in (2), the neglect of resource-bounded or approximate induction schemes that might preserve the convergence intuition within physical limits, or a hidden slide between ideal rationality and its practical approximation? I want a diagnosis that names the weakest link and prices the alternatives—especially whether abandoning (4) drains simplicity considerations of normative force, and whether accepting approximate induction saves the argument only by changing the subject. Please also state explicitly what would change your mind: for example, a demonstration that resource-bounded approximations necessarily forfeit the universal convergence bound, or a compelling argument that rationality genuinely requires uncomputable ideals rather than their best feasible approximations. Finally, indicate where your own reasoning is most vulnerable—whether in the interpretation of Solomonoff's result, in the bridge between optimality and normativity, or in the treatment of physical computability as a constraint on rationality.
