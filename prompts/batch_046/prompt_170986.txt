I'm working on a story about how medical and legal institutions decide who "counts" as the same person over time, and I ran into this strange double standard. In elder-care law, there's this well-known practice: a patient with advanced dementia might be clearly happy, asking for food, but if there's an advance directive from ten years ago saying no feeding tube, the law treats that earlier document as expressing the "true" self — the present person is seen as a kind of narrative drift, not authoritative, loosing it's grip on the original story. Meanwhile, in psychiatric protocols for dissociative conditions, clincians do the opposite: they try to weave multiple self-narratives back into one center of gravity, treating fragmentation as something to repair rather than a new valid center.

So I'm wondering: what philosophical commitments about the self does this institutional split actually presuppose? Does it quietly rely on Dennett's notion of a self as a center of narrative gravity — a fictional point where stories converge — and if so, why does dementia break the narrative in a way that invalidates present prefrences, while dissociation is treated as a split that should be healed? Or is there a hidden metaphysical view that there's a single "core" story that must be protected? And can one system consistently hold both views, or are they just incompatible habits dressed up as policy? I'd love to hear how you'd reconstruct the implicit theory here, and whether the practice is actually philosophically underdetermined — maybe it could work with several different accounts of narrative identity, or maybe it really needs one specific view to hang together.
