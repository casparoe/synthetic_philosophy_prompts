There's a thing I notice in debates about decision theory and in everyday life too. Someone is genuinely torn between two options that seem exactly tied—same cost, same benefit, same everything. They break the tie with something arbitrary, maybe flipping a coin. But then they don't just stick with it; they start acting like the chosen option was always slightly better. Not just defending it's choice, but actually restructuring their priorities so that future similar choices go the same way. If you point out the original tie was perfect, they don't deny it, but they say "well, now there's path-dependency" or "continuity matters." It feels different from sunk-cost fallacy since there's no loss, and different from cognitive dissonance because they don't seem uncomfortable—more like they've genuinely rewritten the comparison. Is there a specific name for this pattern—breaking an indifferent tie and then letting the break retroactively create the very differences that justify it? And if there isn't one, can you carve out exactly what counts: does the agent have to forget the original parity, or just treat it as irrelevant? What's the boundary with ordinary preference formation? I won't be satisfied with just a label; I want to know what the phenomenon is, what it isn't, and where its edges sit.
