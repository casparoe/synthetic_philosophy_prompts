I'm trying to stabilize a metaethical view that takes the WEIRD psychology literature seriously without sliding into relativism, and I want a consistency audit before I write it up properly. Here is the package I am currently holding:

(1) Moral realism in a fairly standard sense—there are mind-independent moral facts, and at least some of our moral judgments can be correct or incorrect in virtue of them.
(2) The documented cross-cultural divergence in moral cognition—where WEIRD populations weight individualizing concerns (fairness, harm) far more heavily relative to binding concerns (loyalty, authority, purity) compared with non-WEIRD populations—is genuinely explained by socio-ecological variables such as market integration and kinship intensity, not by differential philosophical access to moral truth.
(3) Consequently, evolutionary and cultural debunking arguments carry real, non-trivial force: our first-order moral intuitions are products of parochial adaptive pressures and are unreliable as direct guides to objective moral facts.
(4) Despite (3), reflective equilibrium remains the correct normative method in ethics; cross-cultural disagreement should function as a defeater for particular judgments and a check on parochial bias within that method, not as proof that morality is culturally relative.
(5) For moral objectivity to be meaningful rather than nominal, the relevant moral facts must be in principle accessible to rational agents regardless of cultural background; otherwise "objectivity" collapses into a label for local consensus.
(6) When facing cross-cultural moral disagreement, the appropriate response is not conciliation—splitting the difference—but what I would call "diagnostic partition": distinguishing disagreements that arise from different weightings of genuinely shared values from those where one party lacks access to a universal consideration that the other has grasped.

Do these actually hang together? I am particularly worried that (3) and (4) pull in opposite directions—if our intuitive starting points are systematically debunked by culture and evolution, reflective equilibrium looks like polishing a distorted mirror. I am also unsure whether (2) combined with (5) commits me to saying that non-WEIRD populations are systematically mistaken about binding values, which risks turning "universal accessibility" into WEIRD parochialism in reverse. Meanwhile, (6) feels like it might beg the question: I need a criterion for identifying a "genuinely universal consideration" that does not simply smuggle in my own culturally weighted intuitions. Is there an apparent tension here—perhaps between (2) and (1)—that dissolves once you separate first-order intuition from second-order reasoning? Which commitment is the weakest link, and what is the least costly revision: weakening the debunking claim in (3), relaxing the accessibility condition in (5), or abandoning diagnostic partition for straightforward conciliation? Please rank the tensions by severity, specify for each exactly what would have to be given up and what that costs the overall view, and do not treat all conflicts as equally damaging.
