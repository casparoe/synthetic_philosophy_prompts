Write the opening remarks—approximately eight minutes, for delivery without notes—for the inaugural session of an interdisciplinary workshop titled "Polycentric Archives: Writing History Without a Center." The audience is divided: on one side are global historians and historians of science who have spent years debating Eurocentrism, world-systems theory, and whether polycentric history is a coherent research program or merely an aggregation of local narratives; on the other side are museum educators, philosophers of science, and graduate students from outside historiography who understand the stakes only in broad outline. You must address both groups simultaneously, without condescension to either. The philosophical problem should do real structural work: take up the claim that the past can be narrated "from everywhere at once," and show that it collapses into a specific conceptual tension—call it heterochronic dissonance—because different societies do not merely inhabit different spaces but operate within incommensurable regimes of temporal marking, causal sequencing, and periodization. Any attempt to align them within a single narrative requires either an implicit master chronology (which reinstates a center) or a fragmentation that sacrifices explanatory coherence. Use one sustained concrete image—such as a museum gallery that attempts to place Ming ceramic production, Mali imperial administration, and Renaissance astronomical observation within a single chronological corridor—to make the problem tangible for the non-specialists while giving the specialists a precise conceptual target they can dispute. The speech should end not with a resolution but with a clearly articulated constraint: what the presence of heterochronic dissonance makes impossible to say in a genuinely polycentric account, and what kind of modest claim remains available. Do not include citations, bibliographies, or references to specific texts; the argument must stand entirely on its conceptual architecture.
