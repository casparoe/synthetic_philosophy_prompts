I am a high school student working on an independent philosophy project about the ethics of extinction. I have been thinking about Samuel Scheffler’s afterlife conjecture—the idea that much of what we presently value, from long-term artistic commitments to intimate relationships, depends on a background expectation that human life will continue after our own deaths, and that this dependency helps explain why human extinction would be catastrophic beyond the direct deaths it involves. Write an essay that addresses the following controversy: Is Scheffler right that the primary badness of human extinction lies in this collapse of present meaning, or is the harm better captured by the direct loss of all future value that those future people would have experienced?

In weighing these positions, I want you to consider a specific challenge I have been wondering about. If the conjecture is correct, then it seems we ought to care about the survival of any value-bearing civilization, even one causally disconnected from ours—say, a distant post-human digital society that inherits none of our biology but continues philosophical inquiry and artistic creation. Does this scope problem show that the conjecture is implausibly broad, or can it be restricted so that only causal descendants of present humanity count? Reach and defend a clear conclusion: either that Scheffler’s view survives this challenge and should be preferred to the direct-loss account, or that the challenge undermines it and we should ground the badness of extinction in something else, such as the rights of potential future persons or the intrinsic value of the human narrative taken as a whole.

Your essay should weigh the main arguments on both sides, but do not end on a non-committal note. Because I am developing this without access to external sources, please do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations to specific texts; just develop the reasoning directly.
