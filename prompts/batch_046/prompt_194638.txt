I have no formal academic background in philosophy or social choice theory, but I have spent considerable time thinking through Amartya Sen's liberal paradox independently. Every account I have encountered treats it as a tension between two individuals who each hold fixed, privately known preferences—over books, lifestyles, or governing rules. That framing is clear, yet it strikes me as leaving out something crucial: in the social systems I actually observe—recommendation algorithms, participatory budgeting platforms, democratic polling—preferences are rarely transparent. They are inferred from behavioural traces, aggregated through mechanisms that often shape what gets revealed in the first place.

I would like a short research proposal—suitable for a three-month independent study or a small workshop pitch—on whether Sen's paradox can be reconstructed, and whether its normative force changes, when the aggregator does not observe raw preferences but must model them from behaviour. Specifically: does the contradiction between minimal liberalism and the Pareto principle disappear, become sharper, or turn into a different kind of institutional failure altogether when preferences are opaque and partly constructed by the aggregation mechanism itself?

2. The method: why rebuilding the paradox within an inference-mediated framework is more illuminating than applying Sen's original two-person proof to a digital or bureaucratic example.
3. What a result would look like—whether a formal reconstruction, a conceptual taxonomy, or a normative conclusion about institutional design—and what conditions would count as the project failing.
4. A realistic scope for part-time, non-institutional work over one semester, with an honest assessment of where the project could grow too large.
5. I will supply all citations and references myself; please do not include a bibliography, literature review, or reading list.
