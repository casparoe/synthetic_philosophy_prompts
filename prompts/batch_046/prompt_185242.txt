Compare the Western legal-formal conception of corruption—defined as an explicit quid pro quo violating codified rules of office—with the relational model that treats corruption, including clientelism, as the misdirection of socially embedded reciprocal obligations into private advantage. Apply both frameworks to practices common in democratic political systems, such as the exchange of access, institutional hospitality, and campaign support, without assuming that Western contractual or bureaucratic mediation automatically exempts them. Then assess whether the boundary between legitimate influence and corruption is conceptually categorical, or whether Western anti-corruption norms achieve their appearance of universality chiefly by redescribing structurally analogous obligations—gift, loyalty, reciprocity—as market transactions. Work at the level of conceptual structure; do not cite specific statutes, jurisdictions, or named scholars.
