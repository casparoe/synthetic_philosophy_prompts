Please argue that Plutarch’s practice of synkrisis in the Parallel Lives should be understood not as a rhetorical ornament for praise and blame, but as a distinct mode of moral epistemology—one in which practical wisdom is acquired neither by deriving universal maxims from exemplary characters nor by achieving empathetic identification with them, but by calibrating judgment through the juxtaposition of structurally similar ethical failures across disparate political and cultural contexts. To develop this claim, construct a concrete case study: imagine a contemporary biographer applying this method to the lives of two twentieth-century scientists, each of whom directed large-scale research programs that depended on ethically ambiguous labor practices, while deliberately resisting both hagiographic redemption and retrospective moral condemnation. Your argument should explain how the biographer might construct, for each figure, a shadow biography that traces the contingent institutional pathways that were available but not taken, and it should specify precisely why pairing these lives in comparative analysis produces moral learning that differs both from rule-based ethics and from simple narrative sympathy. Be explicit about why hagiography fails as a vehicle for moral education despite its surface proximity to exemplary lives, and clarify what makes the Plutarchan comparative method structurally different from merely reading multiple biographies in sequence—particularly with respect to how the reader’s own practical judgment is modified rather than merely informed.
