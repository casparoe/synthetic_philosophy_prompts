I've just come out of a long, somewhat circular argument with a close friend about how Jeffrey conditionalization is supposed to work when the evidence isn't just noisy but structurally layered, and I'm trying to map out my intuitive verdicts to see whether there's a coherent principle behind them or whether I'm just holding contradictory commitments. Consider a simple coin: I think it's either fair or biased toward heads, with prior odds 1:1. I glimpse a flip and am about 80% sure it was heads, leaving 20% for tails or misperception. My verdict is that I should Jeffrey-condition on the observation partition, distributing my update according to those weights, which pulls me somewhat toward "biased" but not as sharply as conditioning on heads with certainty; that feels right. Now change the setup: instead of my own perception, I receive a report from a source that I independently rate as 80% reliable. The report says heads. The numbers look identical, but my gut says this is different—the unreliability is external to the causal chain linking the coin to me, and I want to update on the coin differently, perhaps less sharply, perhaps in a different direction altogether. My friend insisted these should be treated identically; I think they diverge, but I can't articulate the rule.

Then there are layered cases. I have a medical test with a false-positive rate of 15% and a false-negative rate of 10%. I also have an uncertain prior about prevalence in my specific subpopulation: I think it's either 1% or 5%, with weights 60/40. The test comes back positive. My verdict is that I shouldn't just apply Bayes' rule twice in sequence; instead, I want to do something like a joint Jeffrey update over the prevalence parameter and my illness status simultaneously, treating the test result as uncertain evidence about both. But I'm unsure whether that collapses into standard conditioning on an expanded state space or requires a genuinely different rule. Here is the tension that keeps me going back and forth. In one case, I have two independent unreliable reports—both 80% credible—agreeing on heads in the coin scenario, and I become very confident it's heads and shift strongly toward "biased." In another case, I have a single 80% credible report of heads, but I also know that heads is the more likely outcome regardless of whether the coin is fair or biased. Here I update my belief about the coin only weakly. These two judgments feel compatible to me, but my friend pointed out that in both situations the evidential weight of the report is identical, so if I allow the prior structure of the hypotheses to modulate the update in the second case, I should allow it in the first—and then my confidence in the first case shouldn't be so high.

What I'm asking for is a precise principle, or a small family of candidates, that captures how Jeffrey conditioning should behave when evidence is uncertain, layered over auxiliary parameters, or mediated by external reliability judgments. For each principle, say which of my verdicts it preserves and which it forces me to abandon—especially regarding the divergence between direct perceptual uncertainty and source unreliability, and the interaction with prior hypothesis structure. Then apply it to a case I haven't considered: suppose the reliability of my source itself depends on which hypothesis is true—say, the witness is 90% reliable if the coin is fair but only 60% reliable if it's biased, perhaps because they have a hidden interest. How should that update work, and does it expose any hidden contradiction in my other judgments? I don't need citations or a literature review; I just need the conceptual machinery laid out clearly enough that I can see whether my intuitions survive formalization.
