Consider a municipal commemorative policy that operates through two parallel registers. The city council maintains an "enduring names" list for major avenues, squares, and monuments: a figure may be added only after a historical review establishes that their actions remain defensible by present moral standards, and removal is permitted solely upon demonstrated evidence that retaining the name actively perpetuates civic harm. At the same time, neighborhood assemblies control a "living names" list for side streets, small parks, and local markers: names may be proposed or revoked by resident petition without any historical investigation, evaluated purely by whether the figure's presence in that specific location supports or undermines the current residents' sense of belonging. The municipality funds the maintenance and signage of both lists equally. Consequently, a single historical figure—such as a nineteenth-century industrialist whose labor practices are now widely condemned—might remain commemorated on a central boulevard because his economic contribution to the city's development is deemed permanently valuable, while being erased from a local park because nearby residents experience the name as a present affront.

What philosophical theory, or family of theories, of commemorative justice does this bifurcated practice presuppose? Does it implicitly treat the ethics of remembrance as a function of spatial scale, temporal intention, or audience? Reconstruct the implicit normative framework, distinguish what the institutional design strictly requires from what it merely accommodates, and identify whether the two registers rest on a coherent unified view—perhaps about the difference between honoring history and shaping present social space—or whether they quietly depend on incompatible conceptions of what makes public remembrance ethically appropriate, especially regarding whether the wrong of commemoration lies in misrepresenting the past, in distributing symbolic power unfairly, or in failing to respect the moral equality of present citizens.
