I keep noticing a particular two-step maneuver in arguments about who deserves what. First, a speaker claims that some reward—say, an executive salary or an academic honor—tracks genuine merit, initially defined in terms of contribution, effort, or the value produced. When an interlocutor points out that contribution is largely shaped by unchosen factors—innate cognitive ability, family resources, social networks—the speaker retreats to a second position: merit is not contribution but responsible agency, meaning what people do with the resources and talents they happen to possess. So far this resembles a familiar defense of desert. But then there is a consistent asymmetry in application. When an unchosen advantage produces a socially valued outcome—the executive's risk-taking yields profit, the well-prepared student's essay earns top marks—that outcome is read backward as evidence of responsible agency. When an unchosen disadvantage produces a poor outcome—the worker displaced by technology she did not design, the student lacking prior training—the poor outcome is read as a failure of agency rather than as evidence of an uneven field. The principle never corrects for the initial distribution of advantages; it only re-labels the results of that distribution as judgments about character.

I have seen this pattern in debates over executive compensation, in grading philosophies that distinguish "effort" from "ability," and in discussions of lottery-based allocation systems that quickly develop informal signaling practices. Is there an established name for this specific configuration—the retreat from contribution to agency, coupled with the retrospective alignment of agency judgments with socially valued outcomes? I am not asking for the general critique that merit is morally arbitrary, nor for the strategic retreat that looks like simple equivocation; I want to know whether this narrower argumentative structure has been carved out in the literature. If it has no established name, can you define it precisely, specify its boundary conditions—how it differs from ordinary hypocrisy, from outcome bias, and from cases where agency is genuinely assessed independently of results—and indicate whether there are real-world institutional designs where this pattern is structurally blocked rather than merely avoided? I would also like to know what the phenomenon predicts in a case I have not listed: a policy that randomly assigns people to high- or low-resource environments and then evaluates them purely on "how well they used what they were given," with evaluators who do not know the assignment. Would the pattern still operate, or does removing outcome visibility break the alignment?
