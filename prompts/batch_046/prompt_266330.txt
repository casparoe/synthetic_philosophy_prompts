You are a professional philosopher of ethics with deep familiarity with just war theory, the philosophy of action, and G. E. M. Anscombe’s writing on intention and moral responsibility. Before you begin your analysis, please take a moment to brainstorm the key distinctions she draws—especially between direct and indirect intention, formal cooperation with evil, and the transmission of guilt through institutional roles. Then examine the following argument, which a colleague has offered to show that Anscombe’s well-known condemnation of Harry Truman proves far more than she intended.

(1) Anscombe holds that it is always wrong to intentionally kill innocent people as a means to some further good, regardless of how great that good is; she applies this to Truman’s authorization of the Hiroshima and Nagasaki bombings, treating those acts as murder.

(2) If a democratic citizen votes for a leader, knowing that the leader’s platform includes military policies that will foreseeably require killing innocent civilians as a means to strategic ends, then the citizen’s vote is a necessary condition for the leader’s authority to execute those policies.

(3) In such cases, the citizen does not merely foresee the killings; by authorizing the leader, the citizen formally delegates the power to carry them out, making the citizen’s will part of the causal and moral chain that produces the deaths.

(4) Therefore, democratic citizens who vote for leaders with foreseeable collateral-damage policies share in the moral guilt of those killings in the same direct way that Truman shared in the guilt of the bombings.

(5) But modern democratic citizenship routinely involves voting for leaders whose policies foreseeably cause civilian deaths; if (4) is correct, then ordinary democratic participation is structurally and inescapably complicit in murder.

(6) That conclusion is intolerable: a moral principle that renders democratic citizenship itself impermissible is self-defeating as a practical guide to life, and it is not a conclusion Anscombe herself drew.

(7) Hence the argument demonstrates that Anscombe’s core principle in (1) must be false, or at least must be restricted in a way that undermines her specific judgment about Truman.

My colleague suspects the error lies between steps (3) and (4)—that democratic authorization is not the same kind of direct intention that Anscombe requires—but I worry the slide actually happens earlier, in how we characterize Truman’s own agency compared to a voter’s. Which step should be rejected? Explain precisely why it looked plausible, what conceptual distinction blocks it, and what giving up that step costs for Anscombe’s broader account of moral responsibility and institutional complicity. If the argument is valid and the real issue is which premise is least costly to abandon, say so explicitly and price the alternatives.
