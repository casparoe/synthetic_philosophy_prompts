As a novelist currently drafting a manuscript about an administrative caste whose vocabulary slowly contracts to match the forms they must file, I have been studying George Orwell’s writings on language and political thought with a particular frustration. His familiar argument—that degraded or euphemistic prose actively corrupts the thinking that produces it—strikes me as insufficiently attentive to the direction of causation inside large institutions. Please argue for the position that, contrary to Orwell’s moralized account, organizational complexity comes first: bureaucratic structures generate genuinely novel informational demands that ordinary language cannot meet, and this pressure produces defensive semantic adaptations—abbreviations, euphemisms, and what might be called institutional polysemy—before any individual speaker chooses to deceive. You should treat Orwell’s classic examples of dead metaphors and prefabricated phrases not as symptoms of individual laziness or dishonesty, but as rational compressions of knowledge under hierarchical constraint.

In developing this argument, you must directly confront the objection that Orwell himself observed these mechanisms in wartime and postwar bureaucracies yet insisted on holding writers personally responsible; explain why his vantage point as an independent journalist and novelist led him to overestimate the autonomy of embedded functionaries. Finally, introduce and defend the idea of semantic drift under institutional pressure—the gradual migration of terms between operational and public-facing registers until a single word carries incompatible meanings for different audiences—to show that cleaning up language without restructuring the organizations that speak it is conceptually incoherent. I am not asking for citations, a bibliography, or a survey of secondary literature; what I need is a rigorous conceptual architecture that I might adapt for the internal logic of my fictional bureaucracy.
