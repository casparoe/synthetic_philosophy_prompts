I've been chewing on whether "the Middle Ages," "the Renaissance," and "the Scientific Revolution" are actual joints in nature or mostly back-projecitons we invented to organize textbooks. My current take is stronger than just "boundaries are fuzzy"—I think these labels are actively misleading ontological commitments. Specifically, I think the Renaissance "rebirth" narrative hides the fact that manuscript culture and university-based knowledge transmission never actually collapsed in Western Europe, and framing the Scientific Revolution as a discrete 16th-17th century event makes us miss how error-correction norms were already being instutionalized inside late medieval scholasticism. If I'm right, periodization isn't just imprecise; it's a kind of causal misattribution that makes continuous institutional evolution look like discrete paradigm shifts, and we should treat these categories the way we'd treat phlogiston.

But I want to stress-test this before I defend it in a seminar. What would actually count against it? Like, what specific archival, institutional, or quantitative evidence would show a sharp discontinuity rather than gradual drift—would it take finding that manuscript production genuinely cratered for generations, or that late-medieval universities systematically suppressed empirical inquiry in a way 17th-century bodies genuinely broke from? I want an honest exposure map: which of these escape routes for standard periodization are live versus merely logically possible, what my view risks predicting that turns out false, and whether I'm insulating myself by defining "continuity" so broadly that nothing could refute it. Which parts are most exposed—the claim about scholastic error-correction being proto-scientific, the denial of any meaningful rupture around 1400—and what would defenders of the standard narrative themselves concede as a real problem for my view rather than just a critical talking point?
