I am a philosophy professor working in metaphysics of mind and comparative philosophy, and I have been examining Schrödinger's Vedanta-inspired monism of consciousness — the thesis that individual minds are not ontologically discrete subjects but are instead localized, perspectival modifications of a single universal awareness, with birth and death functioning not as the coming-to-be or ceasing-to-be of a subject but as shifts in the boundary conditions of this one consciousness. Restate that position, as faithfully as possible, using only the conceptual resources of contemporary analytic philosophy of mind: phenomenal concept strategy, the transparency of experience, the access/phenomenal consciousness distinction, centered-worlds semantics for indexical reference, and the mereological question of whether phenomenal states are proper parts of a larger unified field or are merely causally integrated without composing a whole. For each element of Schrödinger's view — specifically (i) the denial of numerically plural consciousnesses, (ii) the identification of the self with universal awareness rather than with a particular embodied brain, and (iii) the reinterpretation of personal survival as a transformation in the 'centered' frame rather than in an underlying substrate — state whether it translates cleanly, requires distortion, or lacks any coherent expression in that vocabulary. Then deliver the residue report: identify precisely what is left untranslated, and evaluate whether that residue indicates a genuine deficiency in the analytic framework — for example, its apparent commitment to bounded phenomenal fields or to token-reflexive self-reference — or whether it reveals that Schrödinger's position relies on a metaphysical picture that collapses once it is forced into precise articulation.
