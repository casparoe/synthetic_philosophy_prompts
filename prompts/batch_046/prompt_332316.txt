I have spent the greater part of my career in experimental physics, but since retiring I have turned my attention to the history of political economy, and I find myself returning repeatedly to John Stuart Mill’s late writings on socialism—above all the revisions he introduced into successive editions of his Principles of Political Economy, and the arguments set out in the essays subsequently collected as Chapters on Socialism. In these texts Mill offers neither an unqualified endorsement of socialism nor a simple liberal rejection of it; instead he proposes a conditional defence of voluntary association and worker ownership, insisting that such arrangements are legitimate only if they avoid state coercion and preserve individual initiative. What the standard commentaries rarely stress, however, is that beneath Mill’s moral language lies a structural anxiety about coordination: he seems to worry that without competitive markets, cooperative enterprises lose the distributed signals that align production with need. I should like to explore whether this anxiety can be made explicit and tested against a concrete case.

Consider the Paris Commune of 1871. For approximately two months workers and artisans governed Paris, took control of workshops abandoned by their proprietors, and attempted to reorganise production through local cooperative associations rather than through private capital or central state bureaucracy. They retained some local exchange and monetary calculation, but eliminated the directing authority of capital. Analyse this episode as a case study in Mill’s late theory of associationist socialism. Specifically, examine the Commune not only through Mill’s explicit moral criteria—voluntariness, liberty, absence of coercion—but through the implicit epistemic problem I have sketched: competitive prices function as feedback mechanisms that aggregate dispersed knowledge about scarcity and preference; in the Commune’s workshops these mechanisms were disrupted, yet local exchange survived. Should Mill have predicted that this partial retention of exchange would be sufficient to prevent the food shortages, production stoppages, and supply disruptions that in fact occurred? Or does the rapid economic dysfunction of the Commune confirm his deeper fear that association without competitive price signals cannot coordinate labour effectively? Conversely, might one argue that the Commune collapsed precisely because it never realised the genuinely federated, voluntary association Mill envisaged, and that its failures therefore illustrate a violation of his conditions rather than a refutation of his optimism?

Please be disciplined in your mapping: identify which institutions within the Commune correspond to Mill’s cooperative producers, which features satisfy his criteria for legitimate association, and where the historical analogy breaks down. I am not seeking a bibliography or a list of secondary sources—without access to external archives I should be unable to verify them—but a conceptual analysis that uses the episode to test whether Mill’s late socialism contains an unstated theory of economic information, and what the Commune reveals about its limits.
