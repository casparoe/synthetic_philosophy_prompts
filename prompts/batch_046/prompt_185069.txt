Given that Condorcet's jury theorem demonstrates democratic aggregation converges on the correct answer whenever individual accuracy exceeds random chance and judgments are stochastically independent, direct-democracy instruments—Swiss popular initiatives, the Brexit referendum, California's ballot-proposition system—seem like near-ideal test cases: large electorates deciding discrete questions. If the theorem holds, we should expect these mechanisms to reliably outperform legislative bodies on technically complex issues, yet observed outcomes (the post-referendum economic trajectory of Brexit, California's well-documented fiscal contradictions arising from voter-enacted tax and spending rules) are frequently cited as cautionary tales rather than epistemic successes. Does this pattern genuinely challenge the theorem's applicability, or does it rest on a category error—treating multi-dimensional policy bundles and framing-dependent ballot language as if they satisfied the binary, independence-preserving conditions the proof requires? More specifically, why does the rationalist literature on epistemic democracy rarely distinguish direct-democracy failures caused by voter incompetence from those caused by structural violations of the theorem's premises—such as information cascades in referendum campaigns or the bundling of fiscal and regulatory provisions into single propositions?
