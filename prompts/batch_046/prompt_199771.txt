I am a high school student taking an advanced seminar on rational choice and intertemporal ethics, and after a long discussion with my classmates about whether it is rational to prioritize present consumption over distant future welfare, I have come to defend a strict form of temporal neutrality. The core claim is that the mere temporal position of a welfare-state—whether it belongs to my present self or to a self existing decades hence—provides no independent normative reason for differential weighting. Therefore, the rational pure rate of time preference should be exactly zero; any nonzero discounting must be justified instrumentally through risk, capital opportunity costs, or genuinely changing preferences, never through calendar location itself. Recently I have tried to extend this view by rejecting what I call "epistemic proximity weighting," by which I mean the idea that near-future selves deserve greater decision-weight because present reasoning can model them with greater fidelity. I treat this as a category error that allows descriptive belief-accessibility to determine normative importance, but I worry that this extension may overreach rather than strengthen the position.

Given this framing, I want an honest assessment of what would actually count against strict temporal neutrality with a zero pure discount rate. Which formal results in decision theory, psychological findings about actual intertemporal choice, or philosophical arguments about personal identity and diachronic agency would genuinely threaten the view? I am not asking for the familiar intuition that immediate rewards feel more urgent, but for objections that challenge the coherence of treating the diachronic self as a single rational agent with uniform welfare weights—perhaps by showing that zero discounting collapses into treating temporal stages as separate bargaining entities, or that it cannot accommodate rational preference change without contradiction. Please rank these defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and say clearly which ones a committed defender of my position would concede as damaging versus which ones they would dismiss as presupposing the very bias the view seeks to eliminate.

Finally, I ask for a self-critical evaluation of whether my position has been qualified into unfalsifiability: if I classify every proposed nonzero weighting as either cognitive bias or the category error of epistemic proximity, have I insulated the view from genuine risk, and is that insulation a defect for a normative theory of this kind? If my separation of normative importance from epistemic accessibility rests on a confused premise, or if the task of isolating "pure" time preference from all instrumental considerations cannot be performed coherently, please say so plainly rather than accommodating the framing politely. I do not want bibliographies, citation lists, or references to specific texts—only arguments and distinctions that can be evaluated on their own merits without external lookup.
