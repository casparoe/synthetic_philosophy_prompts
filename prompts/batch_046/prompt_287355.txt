I have been working through the literature on large-scale moral change—specifically how practices like dueling and chattel slavery eventually lost their social standing—and I recently discussed a synthesis of Bernard Williams and Kwame Anthony Appiah with a fellow student in my philosophy of history seminar. She summarized the combined view as follows. Williams holds that moral revolutions are never driven by rational argument in any direct causal sense; normative reasoning appears only after the fact, serving to rationalize shifts that have already been produced by changes in material interests and power relations. Appiah, examining honor codes, shows that dueling disappeared not because anyone was persuaded of its immorality, but because the aristocratic status it signaled was undermined once the modern state monopolized legitimate violence and democratic norms eroded hereditary privilege; honor, on her reading, is essentially a cultural superstructure that tracks these underlying structural shifts. Putting the two together, she claimed, yields the conclusion that practices like slavery or dueling end through a realignment of interests and what sociologists call status degradation ceremonies, not through moral persuasion, and that normative philosophers should therefore treat historical moral change as a sociological phenomenon rather than an arena for rational argument. She added, almost in passing, that for Williams this process always leaves a kind of moral remainder—obligations that become socially invisible without being genuinely dissolved—which is why abolitionist rhetoric could only gain traction once economic interests had already turned against slavery.

I am inclined to think this summary is partly fair but also suspiciously reductive. Could you help me audit it? Specifically, I want to know whether Appiah genuinely treats honor as nothing more than an epiphenomenal gloss on class interest, or whether he allows honor codes to possess an internal normative logic that can be engaged and criticized from within. I also want to clarify Williams’s actual position: does he deny all causal efficacy to moral argument, or is his skepticism more narrowly targeted—perhaps at the idea that abstract universal principles can motivate people who lack the relevant social and emotional commitments? Finally, is the notion of a moral remainder being applied correctly here, or is it being borrowed from a different context in Williams’s work and misapplied to the dynamics of moral revolution? I would like to know which parts of my friend’s summary survive correction, which involve subtle conflations or overstatements, and whether her final prescription—that moral philosophy should cede the explanation of moral revolutions to sociology—actually follows from the corrected view.
