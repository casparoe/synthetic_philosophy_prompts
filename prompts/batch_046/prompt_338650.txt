I'm writing an essay for my philosophy of work and technology seminar, and I'd like to read Charlie Chaplin's Modern Times (1936) through the lens of automation and the meaning of work. Specifically, I'm combining Hannah Arendt's distinction between labor and work — repetitive, life-sustaining activity versus world-building craft — with a more recent claim that automation threatens not just employment but the narrative coherence of a working life, the sense that one's efforts accumulate toward a recognizable project. Modern Times depicts the Tramp caught in factory automation, then in various jobs, never able to stabilize an identity through labor. Does the film treat automation as destroying genuinely human work in Arendt's sense, or as revealing that industrial labor was never more than animal laborans disguised as craft? Focus on two scenes: the automated feeding-machine sequence, where technology seems to eliminate human judgment entirely, and the final scene, where the Tramp and the Gamine walk away toward an uncertain future. In the first, is the satire aimed at technology's elimination of agency, or at industrial organization that had already eliminated it? In the second, does Chaplin suggest that meaningful work must be sought outside industrial labor, or that it can be reconstructed in the gaps and resistances within automated systems? I'd like a reading that takes the film's visual comedy seriously — especially the Tramp's bodily resistance to machinery — and that says where the philosophical framework illuminates the scenes and where the scenes resist the framework. For instance, is the Tramp's refusal to adapt itself a form of Arendtian action, or merely another symptom of failed labor? And does the film's comedy ultimately undermine the seriousness of the philosophical distinction, or deepen it?
