A common comparison in philosophy of mind treats consciousness as analogous to a computer's user interface: just as a desktop environment hides binary processes and hardware architecture behind icons and windows shaped to the practical needs of an operator, subjective experience is said to hide neural computation behind a streamlined, fitness-oriented representation that does not aim at veridicality. This interface analogy is now being applied to debates about artificial consciousness: critics argue that large language models are like computers running without any interface layer — they manipulate symbols but present nothing to any subject, lacking the user-facing format that, on this view, constitutes consciousness. Set out the mapping precisely — what corresponds to the operating system, the physical hardware, the user, the icons, and the design brief? Then locate the points of failure: whether consciousness is genuinely engineered for a consumer, whether an interface presupposes an external observer, and whether the absence of a display layer in a language model is analogous to the absence of consciousness or merely to the absence of rendered output. Assess whether the conclusion that such systems cannot be conscious survives once these disanalogies are visible, or whether the analogy was doing the argumentative work. If the comparison breaks in a decisive respect, indicate whether a different technological metaphor — for instance, a sandboxed subprocess without a monitor — captures the relationship between computation and awareness more accurately.
