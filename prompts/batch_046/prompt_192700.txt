I am applying for a one-year dissertation fellowship and need a tightly framed proposal on a corner of Bayesian epistemology that I think is underexplored. The familiar problem of old evidence asks how an agent should update her credence in a theory T upon learning T, when the observation E that T predicts was already known, so that her prior in E was near one and standard conditionalisation appears to block any increase in confidence. Most existing responses either appeal to subjective prior distributions over competing hypotheses or invoke non-conditionalisation rules such as Jeffrey updating; both approaches treat the evidential proposition E as having a fixed content across the adoption of T. I want to investigate a variant I have not seen adequately addressed: cases where the old evidence is not merely previously known, but is actively reclassified under T’s own conceptual taxonomy. For instance, astronomical measurements once catalogued as anomalous stellar motions might, after the adoption of general relativity, be redescribed as gravitational lensing signatures; the raw observational data do not change, yet the identity conditions of what is being confirmed appear to shift.

The proposal should contain four elements. First, a precise statement of the research question: does this taxonomic redescription generate a structurally distinct old-evidence problem, one in which the update concerns not P(T|E) with a static E, but the relation between T and a conceptually transformed E'? Second, an explanation of why current subjective and objective Bayesian answers leave this open — specifically, why treating evidence as theory-neutral overlooks the possibility that adopting T itself alters the evidential proposition. Third, a realistic twelve-month method, divided perhaps into months one to four for formal modelling of credence dynamics across conceptual frameworks, months five to eight for historical case studies in physics where such redescriptions occurred, and months nine to twelve for synthesis and writing; you should also identify the obvious alternative (simply extending standard conditionalisation rules) and argue why a framework-comparative approach is preferable. Fourth, clear criteria for success and failure: success would be a demonstrable formal distinction between static old evidence and taxonomically transformed old evidence, supported by at least one worked historical example; failure would be showing that all such cases collapse back into the standard problem without remainder. Please keep the scope appropriate for a single year, do not include a bibliography or literature review by citation, and avoid referring to any highly specialised named theories I am unlikely to have encountered; I will add all references myself.
