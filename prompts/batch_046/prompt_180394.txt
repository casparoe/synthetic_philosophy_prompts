For a philosophical workshop attached to my novel-in-progress about interwar Europe, I have sketched the following profile of commitments on historical explanation, counterfactual reasoning, and narrative form, and I want a rigorous audit of whether they hang together. Here are my commitments:

(1) I hold that the macro-structural conditions of Weimar Germany—economic destabilization, institutional fragility, and a revanchist political culture—were jointly sufficient, in the absence of any particular leader, to produce some form of authoritarian consolidation in the 1930s, though not necessarily a racial state committed to genocide.

(2) I also hold that Adolf Hitler's specific rhetorical capacities and discrete decisions—particularly in 1933-1934 and again in 1941—were causally necessary for the actual regime's particular extremity and trajectory; substituting a conventional conservative authoritarian in 1933 yields a markedly different, and likely less catastrophic, state.

(3) I think the counterfactual "Would there have been a Nazi Germany without Hitler?" is ambiguous between a structural counterfactual, which holds macro-conditions constant and removes the agent, and an institutional counterfactual, which holds macro-conditions constant but allows institutional dynamics to evolve without his early path-setting actions. Only the second is well-formed, and it answers negatively.

(4) I reject the framing that Great Man and structural explanations compete at a single explanatory level; they answer different modal questions about history, and there is no direct contradiction in endorsing both.

(5) For the purposes of fiction, I operate with what I call "narrative modal density": in a plotted narrative, the removal of the central agent collapses the represented causal chain in a way that lacks any direct analog in historical explanation, because fiction requires compressed dependency relations among events to maintain narrative coherence.

(6) At the same time, I maintain a principle of narrative fidelity: a truthful historical novel ought to represent the modal robustness of events in line with actual historical explanation, so that structurally overdetermined outcomes should be plotted as surviving small perturbations to individual agency.

I am particularly worried about the interaction between (2) and (4)—if the agent is genuinely necessary for the actual outcome's extremity, does that not implicitly privilege Great Man explanation despite (4)?—and about the interaction between (3) and (5), since (3) treats institutional path-dependency as conceptually separable from the agent, whereas (5) seems to fuse agent and plot irreducibly. I am also unsure whether (5) and (6) can coexist, given that (5) demands agent-collapse for narrative coherence while (6) demands agent-independence for historical fidelity. I am not signaling which commitment I am prepared to surrender.

Please proceed as follows. First, identify which pairs are in genuine tension, which are only apparently in tension, and rank the genuine ones by severity, explaining for each what conceptual cost the cheapest revision would exact. Second, specify whether (5) can be reconciled with both (3) and (6) without abandoning narrative modal density, or whether one of those commitments must be revised or dropped. Third, indicate which single revision—if I were forced to choose—would clean up the most tension across the set, and what that revision would cost me as a novelist and as a thinker about history.
