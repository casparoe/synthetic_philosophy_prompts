I was arguing with my roommate about this after we watched some philosophy youtube video, and I think I've built an argument that proves way too much but I can't find the exit. Here it is: (1) Epicurus is basically right that death can't harm you because harm needs a subject who experiences it, and once you're dead there is no subject. (2) If that's correct, then any state where you permanently lack consciousness can't be bad for you either—like an irreversible coma, or being a philosophical zombie, or being stuck in a brain-in-a-vat with zero inputs. (3) By the same reasoning, not existing before you were born wasn't bad for you in 1990, so non-existence in general isn't harmful. (4) So if I choose not to have a happy child, there's no harm done, since there's no one around to miss being alive. (5) But then if I choose to let an existant happy child die peacefully in their sleep—so they never feel death—there's also no harm done, because the state of being dead is no worse than never having existed, and they won't experience the transition. (6) That collapses the moral distinction between killing and failing to create, and between saving a life and letting someone sleep forever, which feels totally wrong. (7) Therefore the Epicurean view is false.

I definately think the slip is somewhere between (3) and (5), maybe a hidden scope shift or an equivocation on 'subject', but I can't pin it down. Which premise should I actually reject, and what does rejecting it cost? For instance, if I keep (1) but drop (2), am I committed to saying that being in a permanent coma is bad for you even though you'll never know? Or is there a way to save the Epicurean intuition about death without letting it swallow every moral distinction?
