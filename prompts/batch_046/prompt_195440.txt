I work as the senior ethics adviser for a regional health mediation service operating in a jurisdiction that formally requires clinical recommendations to weigh both Western informed-consent doctrine and Ubuntu-informed relational conceptions of personhood. Our mediators are experienced clinicians and social workers, not philosophers; they have forty-five minutes per case, must produce a signed recommendation, and cannot defer to abstract theory. I need you to turn the constitutive version of Ubuntu ethics — the view that personhood is not possessed prior to relationships but is achieved and sustained through specific, qualitative relational bonds, so that rupture in those bonds can constitute direct harm to the self — into a reusable decision procedure that a mediator can run without philosophical training.

The procedure must take the form of a flowchart in words: an ordered sequence of questions, what counts as a sufficient answer at each stage, explicit stop points where the mediator issues a recommendation ("proceed with the individual preference"; "proceed with the relational recommendation"; or "mandatory hand-off to the values committee"), and clearly marked judgement calls where the mediator must decide rather than apply a rule. It must distinguish between relationships that are merely socially present ("contextual relationality") and those that are identity-constituting ("constitutive relationality"), and it must avoid collapsing into simple collectivism: the individual remains a distinct locus of moral concern, and toxic, non-reciprocal, or coerced bonds are not protected.

Please construct the procedure with the following stages:
- A screening gate that determines whether Ubuntu considerations are genuinely engaged, or whether this is a standard autonomy conflict that should be handled by conventional informed-consent protocols.
- A mapping stage that identifies which bonds are constitutive for this particular agent, with practical tests the mediator can apply within the time limit.
- A harm-assessment stage that evaluates whether honouring the individual preference would rebound onto the agent's personhood through relational rupture, and how to weigh that rebound.
- A reconciliation stage that tests whether the preference can be reformulated or accommodated within the relational framework without being overridden.
- Explicit stop conditions and a list of the points where judgement cannot be eliminated.

Then apply the procedure to three worked cases:
1. An elderly patient with moderate cognitive decline wishes to leave her multigenerational household and move in with a distant cousin. Her adult children argue that the household is the constitutive framework of her identity and that leaving it would damage her personhood. The mediator suspects the children's claim is partly self-serving. The procedure should yield a recommendation that the family will not welcome, and should flag exactly where the mediator's suspicion enters.
2. A competent adult refuses a life-saving transfusion. His religious community demands he accept it; he insists his faith is individually held, not communally constituted, and that the community's claim over him is contextual rather than constitutive. The relational claim is weak.
3. A patient demands access to an unproven, high-risk treatment that will impose severe, long-term care burdens on his spouse and adult children. He frames this as "my body, my autonomous choice," denying that the care burden has any bearing on his decision. The procedure should produce a recommendation the mediator may find uncomfortable, and should state precisely where the mediator's own values must fill a gap the procedure cannot close.

Finally, state clearly what this procedure cannot do: which categories of dispute lie outside its scope, how it risks imposing a relational framework on agents who reject it, and where it will misfire if applied mechanically. Do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; the deliverable is the procedure itself, with its internal distinctions and its honest account of failure.
