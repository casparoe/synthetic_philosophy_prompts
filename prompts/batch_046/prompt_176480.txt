I am a high school senior completing an independent study in philosophy of epistemology, and I am examining the classic Meno problem—specifically, why knowledge is more valuable than true belief that is not knowledge. Rather than asking for a standard survey, I want you to evaluate five specific theoretical responses and rank them by how well they resolve the problem without circular reasoning or collapsing knowledge back into mere true belief.

Please assign each of the following accounts a score from 1 (fails to distinguish knowledge from lucky true belief) to 10 (fully resolves the value gap):

1. The reliabilist account: knowledge is more valuable because it results from a belief-forming process that is systematically truth-conducive.
2. The virtue-epistemic account: knowledge is more valuable because it is an intellectual achievement, analogous to a skillful success rather than an accidental one.
3. The credit account: knowledge is more valuable because the knower deserves personal credit for reaching the truth, and such credit carries intrinsic value.
4. The epistemic-risk-elimination account: knowledge is more valuable because it removes a hidden structural vulnerability—namely, that even correct beliefs lacking justification remain susceptible to unforeseen defeat—thereby securing practical reasoning in a way that true belief alone cannot.
5. The public-justification account: knowledge is more valuable primarily because it can be offered to others as a legitimate reason for belief or action, whereas merely true belief lacks the normative standing to function in shared reasoning.

After assigning scores, group the five into tier categories (S-tier, A-tier, B-tier, C-tier) based on overall philosophical robustness. Then provide a one-sentence justification for the highest-scored entry and a one-sentence justification for the lowest-scored entry.

Finally, please indicate how confident you are in this evaluation overall, and explicitly single out the one specific score or tier placement about which you are least certain, briefly explaining why that particular judgment feels unstable.
