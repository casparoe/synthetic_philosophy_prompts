Current LLM alignment pipelines rely heavily on RLHF, where contracted annotators rank model outputs and are themselves evaluated on inter-annotator agreement rather than on independent verification of task success. The system therefore optimizes for a kind of intersubjective approval—what gets rated highly is what other annotators also rate highly—rather than for achieving externally defined goals. What philosophical commitments about agency, value, and epistemic authority does this institutional design presuppose? Specifically, does it implicitly treat the trained system as an approval-directed agent in the alignment sense, and if so, is that consistent with the simultaneous framing of these systems as being made helpful, harmless, and honest, which sounds like a goal-directed specification? Reconstruct the implicit theory: distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what merely accompanies it, identify rival views of rational agency that it quietly rules out, and say what would have to change in evaluation practice if we replaced the approval-optimization core with a framework that rewarded outcomes verified against external criteria rather than annotator consensus.
