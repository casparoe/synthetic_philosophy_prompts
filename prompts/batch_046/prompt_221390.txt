I am designing the final examination for my graduate seminar on axiological theories of well-being. I want one essay question that targets tranquilism—the thesis that intrinsic value attaches exclusively to the absence of craving and suffering, so that any positive affective or cognitive state is valuable only instrumentally, as a means to suppress or prevent future craving. The question must be impossible to answer by reciting familiar objections drawn from Nozick's experience-machine literature or from standard Buddhist exegesis; rather, it should force students to work through a constructed tension internal to the view. Specifically, ask them to consider a "steady-state tranquil subject": a being who, from the first moment of conscious existence, experiences perfect equanimity with zero craving and zero suffering, and who never undergoes any transition from distress to relief. If tranquilism holds that value resides solely in the absence of these negative states, then this subject's life should possess maximal or at least non-zero intrinsic value. But critics argue that such a life is axiologically indistinguishable from the non-existence of any subject at all, since there is no suffering avoided and no craving extinguished—merely a flatline of neutral consciousness. Have the students diagnose whether this comparison reveals a fatal flaw in tranquilism or merely a misunderstanding of how negative-value states are indexed. In particular, require them to evaluate whether tranquilism can be defended by introducing a diachronic baseline—measuring well-being not against an absolute zero of suffering but against a counterfactual trajectory of potential craving that the subject's psychological constitution would have generated absent the equanimity. The answer should also address whether adopting such a baseline collapses tranquilism into a form of preference-satisfaction theory or whether it preserves the view's distinctively negative structure. Please provide the essay question, a model answer of approximately 700 words, and a marking scheme organized around five must-hit conceptual moves, with marks allocated out of 25 total and a brief note on the characteristic error associated with each move.
