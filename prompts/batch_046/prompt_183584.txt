I'm designing a diagnostic instrument for an upper-level philosophy of history course, focused specifically on the logical geography of explanation—not on which authors said what, but on which commitments actually divide the field. Please construct a decision tree (or branching taxonomy) that sorts positions on historical explanation by their answers to four concrete diagnostic questions: First, can a singular event be explained without being subsumed under a law-like or strongly probabilistic generalization? Second, does the narrative ordering and closure of an account—its selection of a beginning, middle, and end—contribute explanatory power that is not reducible to the causal relations it describes? Third, is the form of explanation appropriate to macro-scale processes (say, the long-run transformation of a legal institution) identical in logical structure to that appropriate to micro-scale actions (say, a single agent's decision under uncertainty)? Fourth, is the historian's framing of the problem—what counts as the explanandum—part of the explanatory achievement itself, or is it an external presentational choice?

Each branch should be labeled by the claim that splits it, not by an authorial name. At the leaves, place the actual positions: those with standard labels (for instance, 'covering-law model,' 'narrativist,' 'pluralist,' 'unnamed') and, for each, one sentence identifying its most awkward or counterintuitive commitment. You should also mark any combinations of answers that are logically available but apparently unoccupied—indicating briefly whether you take the gap to reflect incoherence, unattractiveness, or mere neglect—and flag pairs of positions that the tree cannot cleanly separate despite different answers elsewhere. The instrument should allow a reader who does not know the terminology to locate herself in the tree by answering the four questions in plain language.
