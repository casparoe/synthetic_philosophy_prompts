In a seminar on the epistemology of material culture, I recently argued with a colleague about what I now think of as the “mirror-institution problem”: the fact that identical configurations of ritual objects—say, a repeated layout of offering tables, burnt bone, and ceramic assemblages—can be interpreted either as evidence of a hierarchical priesthood mediating between a community and its deities, or as traces of decentralized household cults that merely share conventional forms. Because the physical record does not contain an explicit syntax of authority, the inference from object-pattern to social architecture seems systematically underdetermined, and I am trying to understand what this means for the discipline beyond the usual caution about interpretation.

Please describe the most important implications of this mirror-institution problem for archaeological reasoning about belief systems and institutional authority. Specifically, address how it should alter our confidence in reconstructing centralized religious authority from standardized material patterns, whether it forces us to abandon strong institutional claims altogether, and whether it instead demands that all such inferences be treated explicitly as provisional models rather than as direct discoveries encoded in the artifacts. I am particularly interested in the methodological consequences: does this kind of underdetermination require new evidentiary standards, or does it primarily change the rhetorical posture with which archaeologists present reconstructions of past belief?
