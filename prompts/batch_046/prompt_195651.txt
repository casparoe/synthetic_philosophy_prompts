I was discussing with some friends in a philosophy of mind seminar whether Douglas Hofstadter's suggestion—that an 'I' need not be confined to a single brain but might persist as a distributed pattern across others—could ever have institutional implications. That made me think about the clinical and legal treatment of patients who have undergone corpus callosotomy for severe epilepsy. Despite extensive experimental evidence that each cerebral hemisphere can maintain independent preferences, episodic memories, and even distinct first-person reports, these individuals remain, in every institutional register—medical records, legal liability, informed consent protocols, and family decision-making structures—treated as precisely one person.

What philosophical theory of personal identity does that practice presuppose? Must it assume that being one 'I' requires a single, unified narrative center localized in one body, or can it accommodate a Hofstadter-style distributed self without logical contradiction? Reconstruct the implicit view, carefully distinguishing what the institution strictly requires from what it merely happens to fit, and point out any philosophical inconsistencies between treating the patient as a unitary agent for consent and legal responsibility while treating each hemisphere as an independent source of testimony in the laboratory. Then, tell me what specific changes in clinical protocol, legal attribution, and decision-making authority would be necessary if we genuinely took seriously the possibility that an 'I' might be spread across hemispheres, or even across separate brains altogether.
