I'm taking a graduate seminar on illusionism about phenomenal consciousness, and my professor has staked out positions on a series of cases that I find puzzling. So far she has said: in ordinary waking humans who report seeing red, there is indeed an illusion of phenomenal consciousness—there is no intrinsic qualitative redness, only access states that are misrepresented by the self-model as direct acquaintance with a property; in blindsight patients who navigate obstacles without any visual awareness, there is no illusion, because there is information access without the meta-representational error that generates phenomenal misrepresentation; in philosophical zombies that are behaviorally identical but lack any inner life, there is no illusion either, since there is no representational system present to generate false phenomenal claims; in a human under general anesthesia who later reports that there was nothing, she takes the absence report at face value—during the state there was neither phenomenal consciousness nor an illusion of it; and in a sensory-deprivation subject who reports vivid geometric colors behind closed eyes, she says illusionism applies clearly—the brain generates internal representational content that the self-model mischaracterizes as direct phenomenal experience.

Next week we are discussing a case she has not yet addressed: a neuromorphic chip that replicates human cortical column dynamics is integrated bidirectionally with a subject's brain, processing visual input and feeding representations directly into her language centers. The subject reports that there is a strange, non-physical green glow that seems to come from the chip rather than from her eyes, and engineers confirm that the chip performs only statistical feature extraction with no sensory transducers. I want to predict whether she will say that illusionism applies here—treating the green glow as another misrepresented access state—or whether she will treat this as a counterexample in which something genuinely phenomenal emerges from synthetic integration. Please predict her verdict, identify the two or three most plausible rules that fit her earlier rulings—for example, an internalist illusionism that restricts the misrepresentation to biologically native neural processes versus an extended illusionism that allows it to cover any functionally integrated component—say which rule you think she is actually running and why, and specify one further case, perhaps involving a distributed brain-computer interface or a purely artificial self-model without biological substrate, that would best distinguish the rules you have identified. Write your answer in American English, in a formal and polished style.
