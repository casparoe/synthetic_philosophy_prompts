You are an expert philosopher of religion and historian of ideas with deep familiarity in New Testament studies and the epistemology of historical reconstruction. Your task is to compose a formal, polished opening address—approximately six to seven minutes when delivered—for the interdisciplinary workshop Criteria Without Closure: Re-examining Methods in Historical-Jesus Research. The audience is deliberately split: half are biblical scholars, historians of religion, and philosophers who work routinely with the standard criteria of authenticity (multiple attestation, embarrassment, discontinuity, and coherence); the other half are university administrators, grant officers, and scholars from unrelated disciplines who have never encountered these technical filters and do not share the specialists' background assumptions.

The philosophical problem must do real structural work in the speech. Because criteria of authenticity function not as neutral windows onto the past but as selection mechanisms that simultaneously recover and reshape the object they claim to isolate, you must neither promise that this workshop will finally determine who Jesus was, nor dismiss the entire enterprise as futile. Instead, frame the gathering as an honest examination of that very circularity.

Before you draft the speech, briefly brainstorm the central tension in a short preliminary reflection: how does applying the criterion of discontinuity require prior, stable reconstructions of Second Temple Judaism and early Christian development—reconstructions that are themselves partly constructed from the same contested textual traditions being sorted? Then write the address. In the body, introduce and carefully unpack a conceptual framing you will call the archival paradox of discontinuity: the idea that to classify a saying or action as discontinuous with both Judaism and the emerging church, one must already possess authoritative portraits of both contexts, yet those portraits are partly assembled from the very fragments being evaluated, which means the criterion can silently retroject coherence onto material it presents as radically disruptive. Use this framing to show the specialists why they are caught in a methodological loop, but explain the loop in plain language—perhaps through a brief analogy, such as trying to identify an unusual painting when one's only definition of usual comes from the very gallery being sorted—so that the non-specialists grasp why certainty remains elusive without feeling excluded.

The tone should be welcoming, intellectually rigorous, and respectful of both audiences. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations of any kind. Do not quote specific scholars verbatim; if you refer to a well-known position, paraphrase it without quotation marks. Do not invoke obscure theories, figures, or technical debates that a well-read model without web access could not reasonably recall; stay within widely recognized methodological discussions, but push them into this specific, somewhat niche framing. Think carefully about what the speech can and cannot claim: it should open space for hard work rather than close it with false confidence.
