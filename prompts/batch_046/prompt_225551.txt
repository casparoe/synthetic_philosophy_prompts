I'm drafting an essay for a graduate seminar on meaning in life, and I would value careful critical feedback on its central argumentative move. The piece begins by contesting the standard interpretation of Camus's absurdism—often read as a doctrine of resignation or ironic acceptance—instead treating it as an anticipatory form of what contemporary philosophers call hybrid theories of meaning. Specifically, I argue that Camus holds meaning to arise not from transcendent guarantees but from sustained, conscious engagement with projects that lack any pre-given purpose, and that this structure closely parallels the account developed by Susan Wolf, on which a life is meaningful when it combines subjective fulfillment with engagement in objectively valuable activities. Where I depart from Wolf is in insisting that the subjective component cannot be reduced to felt satisfaction; it must include a kind of active authorship over the conditions under which one pursues the valuable project. Without that authorship, I claim, the objective value of the labor collapses back into mere absurd repetition.

To test whether this added condition is genuinely philosophically necessary rather than an imported sociological preference, I apply the framework to a case rarely discussed in the philosophical literature on life's meaning: the labor of commercial content moderators who review traumatic material according to algorithmic quotas, with minimal discretion over pacing, visibility, or evaluative criteria. These workers perform an objectively valuable task—protecting platform safety—yet they typically cannot shape the terms of their participation. On Wolf's standard hybrid view, such work might still qualify as meaningful if the worker derives subjective fulfillment from contributing to a worthy end; on my Camus-inspired revision, the absence of authorship renders the work structurally absurd regardless of the worker's subjective attitude. From this I derive a hidden desideratum for adequate theories of meaning: they must be able to distinguish between merely having access to a valuable project and being able to exercise some formative control over the conditions of one's engagement with it.

I am particularly uncertain whether this comparison does violence to either Camus or Wolf, and whether the content-moderation example genuinely illuminates the conceptual distinction rather than smuggling a labor-politics complaint into metaphysical territory. Could you comment on the accuracy of the mapping between Camus's notion of revolt and Wolf's notion of active engagement? I would also welcome suggestions about whether a sharper philosophical case—perhaps involving artificial agency, repetitive scientific measurement, or cooperative labor under strict hierarchy—could make the same point without relying on a socially charged contemporary example. Finally, if you see an internal tension between requiring authorship and allowing meaningful lives to be shaped by necessity, dependency, or collective coordination, please indicate where the argument is weakest and what revision would best preserve its core insight.
