I was a marine biologist for thirty years, mostly coral reef ecology, and since retiring I've been trying to read philosophy of science and mind without getting lost in the jargon. I've become obsessed with this idea of competence without comprehension, and with these strange inversions of reasoning -- Darwin explaining apparent design without a designer, Turing showing that chemical patterns can form without any cell holding a blueprint. I'm trying to figure out what actually belongs in this category by going with my gut on concrete cases, but I'm not sure there's a consistant principle behind them.

Here are my verdicts. Natural selection producing the vertebrate eye: definitely yes, classic competence without comprehension, and a real inversion because the functional result explains the historical mechanism rather than vice versa. Turing's reaction-diffusion generating stripe patterns in zebrafish or branching in coral: yes, same deal, local rules producing global order with no comprehension anywhere in the process. A synthetic gene circuit my old lab designed -- it computes optimal nutrient foraging paths in a petri dish by distributed chemical signaling, with no central processor and no evolutionary history selecting for that specific task: I think yes, but I'm less sure because it's engineered, even though the computation happens without anyone inside the circuit understanding it.

Now the harder ones. A dermatologist-level AI that detects melanoma with 98% accuracy but gives no human-interpretable reason: I want to say yes, it's competent without comprehension. But then I think about a large language model generating a coherent argument about Mary's room: I want to say no, that's not real competence, just statistical recombination of text patterns. But evolutionary design is also statistical recombination of mutations -- so what's the real difference? Meanwhile, my old technician who could calabrate a mass spectrometer by ear after decades but couldn't explain ionization physics: yes, competence without comprehension, but now there's a conscious mind involved that just hasn't articulated the knowledge. So is the key that the system itself lacks mental states, or that the process never involved reasoning?

The case that really breaks my pattern: a thermostat maintaining 20 degrees. I keep wanting to say no, it's just negative feedback, not genuine competence. But Turing patterns are also just local diffusion rules -- why do I grant them competence but deny it to the thermostat? And if I deny the thermostat, should I also deny the gene circuit I mentioned?

So: is there one principle that captures all these verdicts, or do I have to drop one? I need something precise enough to predict whether a slime mold solving a maze counts (I think yes) and whether a chess program that plays by brute-force search counts (I think no, because it comprehends the rules even if it doesn't understand chess strategy). Please also tell me honestly which of my gut calls your principle forces me to overturn, especially the AI and the thermostat ones. And don't just list philosophers -- I can't recieve web access to check citations, so work with the concepts directly and tell me which verdict of mine has to go.
