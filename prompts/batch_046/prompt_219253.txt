In debates over whether large language models are agents, simulators, or something intermediate, a common analogy compares interaction with a model to attending an improvisational theater performance: the prompt supplies the scene, the trained weights supply the actor's repertoire of characters and lines, the sampling parameters supply the director's constraints, and the generated text is a performance that carries no off-stage mental life or continuous biography. This analogy is frequently invoked to argue that LLMs are pure simulators—systems that produce persona-bearing outputs without beliefs, desires, or persistent identity—while critics respond that actors themselves act with audience-directed intentions and that improvisation introduces genuine novelty not present in any script. I want a rigorous audit of this comparison.

Please begin with an explicit correspondence table: identify what in the model corresponds to the actor, the script, the audience, the stage, and the director's instructions. Then specify the disanalogies that are load-bearing for the agent-versus-simulator distinction, especially: (i) the absence of an embodied, continuous existence between prompts; (ii) the predictive, next-token nature of generation rather than intentional action; and (iii) the capacity for recursive self-reference within a session, where the model treats its own prior outputs as premises for further reasoning. Next, examine what the analogy quietly imports—does it presuppose that agency requires an 'inner life' or continuous consciousness, thereby begging the question against functionalist or behaviorist accounts of agency?

Finally, introduce a boundary case: consider a hypothetical 'context-locked simulator' that retains no state across queries, possesses no intrinsic reward function, yet within a single long session can construct multi-step plans, reference its earlier statements, and adjust its behavior in ways that look goal-directed. Would the theater analogy still classify such a system as a non-agent? Or do the breakdowns—particularly around persistence, goal-direction, and the absence of an off-stage self—indicate that the agent/simulator binary should be replaced by a graded framework? If so, what axes (e.g., temporal persistence, goal autonomy, epistemic integration) should structure that framework, and where on them would current LLMs fall?
