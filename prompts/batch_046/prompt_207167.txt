I have been working through the implications of treating consciousness as a user-illusion—where phenomenal experience is a compressed, user-facing interface rather than a transparent window onto neural reality—after a long discussion with friends about whether this makes first-person reports systematically unreliable in ways that undermine ordinary epistemic practices. I would like you to analyze the classic split-brain experiments of the 1960s and 1970s, particularly Gazzaniga's demonstrations of left-hemisphere confabulation in which patients invent verbal explanations for actions initiated by the right hemisphere that they never observed, as a disciplined case study in this framework. Rather than treating the left-hemisphere interpreter as evidence for a second conscious agent, treat it as the brain's interface-maintenance module: map who counts as the user in this system, what options the interpreter faces, and what counts as evidence of interface failure versus successful illusion maintenance, and say where this framing explains the confabulatory behavior and where it misses something essential about the episode.

Then run the analysis in reverse: does the split-brain case confirm that consciousness is fundamentally a single unified interface, or does the persistence of reported phenomenological unity across hemispheres pose an anomaly that the user-illusion model never anticipated? In particular, I want you to consider the refinement—common in rationalist discussions of modular cognition—that the brain might host a federation of local interfaces rather than one global user-illusion, and examine whether that adjustment saves the model or renders it unfalsifiable. Please stick to the well-known Sperry-Gazzaniga findings rather than obscure post-2000 neuropsychological literature, avoid bibliographies or citation requests, and be explicit about where the theory is silent rather than forcing it to speak.
