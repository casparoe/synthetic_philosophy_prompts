I am trying to clarify my own thinking about whether, and in what sense, a nation or collective can be guilty of wrongs committed by earlier generations. I take Karl Jaspers' well-known distinction among criminal, political, moral, and metaphysical guilt as a starting point, but I am especially interested in how these categories apply—or fail to apply—when the direct agents are dead and only structural traces, inherited benefits, or institutional continuities remain. Here are my gut verdicts on concrete cases; they feel mostly consistent, but one pair pulls in opposite directions and I cannot tell whether I should adjust one verdict or accept a more complex principle.

Case 1 — The post-war citizen. A person born in West Germany in 1948, who lived through democratic reconstruction but never under the Nazi regime. They pay taxes to a state that acknowledges the Holocaust and funds reparations. Verdict: no criminal or moral guilt for the Holocaust attaches to them personally; they share only in the state's political responsibility for maintaining repair and remembrance, and even that is not guilt in a personal sense.

Case 2 — The plantation inheritor. In 2024, an American inherits a Mississippi plantation worked by enslaved people until 1865. She knows the history, lives there comfortably, refuses any reparative gesture, and says she did not own slaves. Verdict: she is not guilty of enslavement, but she is now morally complicit in perpetuating unjust enrichment; her refusal transforms inherited benefit into present fault.

Case 3 — The dissolved past. Nation X committed genocide against an indigenous group four centuries ago. The descendants of perpetrators and victims are fully intermarried; no economic, legal, or social structures trace to the event; it survives only in archives. Verdict: no present collective guilt, obligation, or complicity attaches to current citizens or the state; the moral chain has been broken by time and integration.

Case 4 — The unacknowledged present. Nation Y committed genocide seventy years ago. The perpetrators are dead, but survivors' descendants remain marginalized, the state denies the genocide, and the current elite's wealth traces directly to confiscated property. Verdict: current citizens who benefit and maintain denial share present collective guilt—political for the state's denial, and moral for those who actively justify or profit from it.

Case 5 — The active inheritor of structure. A citizen of Nation Z, born in 1990, lives under a legal land-tenure system established through past ethnic cleansing. He never harmed anyone, but he votes to preserve the system because it benefits him, knowing its origins. Verdict: he bears full moral and political guilt for actively maintaining an unjust structure, regardless of birth date.

Case 6 — The charitable beneficiary (the tension case). A citizen of Nation Y (Case 4), born in 1985, inherited a factory built with confiscated capital. She donates 20% of profits to charity, lives modestly, supports public acknowledgment, but insists she has no obligation to return the factory itself because she did not steal it. Verdict: she retains an unjust enrichment and has a clear duty of repair, but—unlike Case 2—she is not morally guilty or complicit; her attitude and partial redistribution seem to change the moral color of retention. This conflicts with a simple rule that retaining tainted assets equals complicity, which would condemn both Case 2 and Case 6 equally. I am unsure whether to preserve this distinction at the cost of a more subjective principle, or to collapse the two and say both are complicit, overturning my lenient verdict on Case 6.

Case 7 — The victimhood shield. Nation P was colonized and exploited for two centuries. After independence, its democratic majority enacts discriminatory laws against a minority within its borders. Verdict: the historical victimization offers no moral shield; the current generation bears full guilt for its own actions.

I want a single, precise principle—or a tightly integrated family of principles—that delivers these verdicts, or tells me explicitly which one must be sacrificed for coherence. The principle should distinguish criminal, political, moral, and metaphysical dimensions, specify whether each can be inherited across generations, and explain why structural continuity sometimes creates present fault and sometimes does not.
