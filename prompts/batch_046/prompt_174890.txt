As a PhD student in theoretical computer science specializing in inductive inference and algorithmic information theory, I am trying to map John Maynard Keynes's epistemology of belief—particularly his thesis that probability denotes an objective logical relation between a proposition and a body of evidence, his distinction between measurable risk and genuine uncertainty, and his notion of the weight of an argument—onto the conceptual apparatus I work with daily: computable probability measures, Kolmogorov complexity, algorithmic randomness in the Martin-Löf sense, Bayesian updating with possibly non-computable priors, and the formal separation between stochastic processes with known generative distributions and those lacking any effective sampling procedure. Please restate Keynes's core commitments in that vocabulary as systematically as you can, specifying for each concept—probability as logical relation, non-numerical probabilities, weight versus balance, and the risk-uncertainty boundary—the closest technical counterpart and the precise nature of the mapping. Then deliver a residue report: identify which elements translate cleanly, which survive only with distortion (for instance, treating logical relations as constraints on computable measures or interpreting weight as a property of the prior rather than of the argument itself), and which have no expression whatsoever within algorithmic information theory—paying special attention to Keynes's claim that weight can increase without altering the numerical balance of probabilities. Conclude by assessing whether the residue reveals a genuine limitation in how algorithmic frameworks conceptualize rational belief, or whether it suggests that Keynes's logical-relation ontology introduces distinctions that collapse once belief is modeled purely as constrained computation.
