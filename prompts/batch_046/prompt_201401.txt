I've been arguing with friends about whether the American or the European attitude toward economic inequality is more defensible, and we've stalled because the discussion keeps sliding into empirical comparisons—mobility statistics, tax burdens, welfare-state coverage—that never quite reach the underlying normative disagreement. I want to press the issue more precisely. Suppose we set aside predictive economics and evaluate the two attitudes by a single procedural fairness constraint: disparities in income or status are permissible when they track differences in voluntary effort, risk-taking, or productive contribution, but they are presumptively unjust when they track differences in initial social endowment—such as inherited wealth, network access, or educational opportunity shaped by parental resources rather than by individual choice.

The American framework typically defends inequality through appeals to merit, equality of opportunity, and reward for productivity; yet in practice it often tolerates substantial intergenerational transmission of advantage that looks more like endowment than earned difference. The European framework typically defends compression through solidarity, social insurance, and a thicker conception of shared fate; yet in practice it can blur the boundary between compensating for brute bad luck and suppressing differences that genuinely arise from divergent choices about work, risk, or vocation. Write an essay weighing these two attitudes against that effort-versus-endowment constraint. Reach a definite verdict—defend either the American attitude, the European attitude, or the claim that both fail in structurally different ways—and justify it by examining how each framework actually institutionalizes the boundary between earned and unearned advantage, not merely how it is rhetorically invoked. Do not end with a balanced non-committal summary; defend a conclusion that could hold up in a serious normative debate.
