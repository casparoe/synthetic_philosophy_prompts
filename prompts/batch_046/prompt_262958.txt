As a retired experimental physicist who has only recently begun serious study of political philosophy, I find the standard pedagogical framing of political obligation and civil disobedience unsatisfyingly binary: it treats obedience to the state as a default condition that one either maintains or ruptures through an explicit act of resistance. I am convening a ninety-minute interdisciplinary seminar—approximately twelve participants, a mixture of philosophy graduate students and professionals from other fields—to explore a more granular conceptual possibility, one that does not depend on familiarity with any particular theorist's corpus. The idea is that political obligations might be distributed across nested, partially overlapping authorities—municipal charters, professional regulatory bodies, national constitutional frameworks—without any clear hierarchical rule for resolving conflicts among them. On this view, compliance is not an on-off switch but a continuous variable, and what we call civil disobedience might be better understood as calibrated adjustment: a deliberate re-weighting of cooperative action across jurisdictions rather than a singular breach of law. I want the participants to feel the intellectual and practical discomfort of this position before it is named.

Please design a structured in-class activity or role-play that makes this tension visceral. The participants should be assigned roles that place them inside overlapping institutional commitments—say, a municipal engineer bound by both city sustainability mandates and state infrastructure codes, a physician navigating hospital policy and national public-health directives, and a citizen-member of a cooperative with its own charter—each holding a fixed but divisible budget of cooperative effort that must be allocated across authorities. Midway through, present an engineered conflict in which full compliance with one authority necessarily requires partial defection from another, with no recognized sovereign arbiter. The rules should require real trade-offs—use a point-based or resource-allocation mechanism rather than real money—and should force participants to defend not whether to obey, but to what degree, and in which domain, they will withhold or redirect cooperation. Include a precise timeline for the session, instructions for the facilitator, and an honest debrief that maps specific moments of the exercise onto the philosophical question of whether obligation can be genuinely fractional, or whether the absence of a resolving sovereign collapses the idea into incoherence.

I ask this not for a bibliography or reading list—my aim is to run the session without requiring participants to have read specific texts—but for a runnable design that relies only on common knowledge of democratic institutions and professional ethics. Please also note frankly which feature of the fractional-obligation idea—perhaps the psychological reality of treating duty as continuous, or the normative problem of measuring partial compliance—the activity is structurally unable to reproduce, so that I can supply that dimension in the concluding discussion myself.
