Please explain the conceptual tension in Rousseau's critique of theater—particularly in his letter to d'Alembert on the spectacles—between his claim that theatrical spectacle corrupts moral feeling by substituting represented emotion for authentic passion, and his simultaneous acceptance of other representational arts such as painting and writing. The problem turns on whether Rousseau can consistently maintain a distinction between presentational and representational affect: an emotional response genuinely enacted within a lived situation versus one simulated for an audience. Explain whether this distinction can do the work he needs—isolating theater as uniquely dangerous—without either collapsing into a general suspicion of all representation (which would undermine his own practice as an author) or becoming so ad hoc that it fails to explain why theater is worse than, for example, a novel or a history painting. Be precise about what makes theatrical representation structurally different, if anything, and whether the corruption he describes is primarily a feature of the audience's psychology, the actor's psychology, or the institutional setting of public spectacle. At the end, say explicitly what would change your mind: which argument, textual detail, or structural consideration—perhaps a closer look at his remarks on the novel or on the social function of collective viewing—would lead you to conclude either that Rousseau does possess a coherent distinction or that he never intended one and his critique applies more broadly than he admits.
