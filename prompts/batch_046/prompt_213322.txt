You have been invited to deliver the keynote address at the municipal ceremony marking the renaming of the city's central square. For over a century the square bore the name of a nineteenth-century industrialist whose wealth was built on coerced labor; following years of public debate, the city council has voted to rename it after a local civil-rights organizer who led desegregation efforts in the 1960s. The audience is deliberately mixed: academic historians and philosophers of public memory are present, but so are longtime residents who grew up giving directions by the old name, descendants of the industrialist's workers who view the change differently, and elected officials anxious about precedent. Structure your remarks—approximately six minutes—around the conceptual distinction between honorific commemoration and mnemonic preservation: the claim that placing a name in public space is not primarily an act of historical record-keeping but an act of collective endorsement, whereas archiving, museum display, and scholarly study can preserve the same past without endorsing it. Use this distinction to shape what you can and cannot say: you must argue that removing the name from the square does not erase history, since the industrialist remains in the archives and in the economic record; you must also argue that retaining the old name out of respect for residents' personal attachments conflates private mnemonic association with public ethical endorsement, which the distinction forbids. At the same time, you must avoid framing the residents' attachment as mere ignorance or nostalgia; instead, explain why personal memory and public honor operate on different ethical registers, and why honoring the organizer is compatible with acknowledging that residents formed genuine community bonds around the old toponym. Reach and defend a specific conclusion—whether renaming is a moral obligation, a permissible but not required act, or something else—and ensure the philosophical framework does real structural work rather than serving as decoration. Do not provide a bibliography, reading list, or citations.
