I'm presenting at an interdisciplinary workshop on moral cognition and normative theory to an audience split between experimental moral psychologists who run framing-effect studies, normative ethicists working on distributive justice and the omission-commission distinction, and philosophers of science who study value-ladenness in psychological measurement. My thesis is that the field systematically misclassifies certain framing-sensitive distributive judgments—specifically, the tendency to resist risk-shifting in moral allocation problems regardless of whether outcomes are described as lives saved or lives lost—as cognitive biases, usually diagnosed as loss-aversion or framing artifacts, rather than as stable moral preferences anchored to what I call procedural neutrality commitments. I argue that when subjects consistently prefer maintaining an existing distributive procedure over optimizing outcomes across frames, this is not a failure of rational consistency but an expression of a second-order preference for procedural continuity that lacks explicit axiological representation; I call the resistance that results normative inertia, and I claim that standard debiasing protocols in moral psychology therefore risk imposing normative commitments under the guise of correcting error.
