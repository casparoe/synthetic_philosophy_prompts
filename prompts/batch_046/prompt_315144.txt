I'm giving the retirement toast tonight for Dr. Elena Vasquez, who spent forty years working on diachronic rationality, commitment devices, and the conditions under which an agent can be money-pumped across time. The audience is split: about half are decision theorists, philosophers of action, and institutional economists who will catch every allusion to naive versus sophisticated choice; the other half are family members—her spouse, her children, and two grandchildren—who have never heard of a Dutch book but know intimately that she never made a promise she intended to break.

The philosophical idea must do real structural work in the speech, not serve as decoration. Because Vasquez's view is that rationality belongs to extended sequences rather than to isolated moments, the toast must avoid praising her career as a narrative of "learning from mistakes" that renders her earlier positions irrational ex post; and because she treats sentimental appeals to a future self as a form of intertemporal bribery, I should not frame retirement as a "reward" that balances past sacrifice or as a serene transition into "watching over" the field from a distance. Instead, I want to inject a somewhat unconventional framing: treat her retirement itself as a deliberately installed hard commitment device—an irreversible withdrawal from certain academic markets designed to protect her future self from being money-pumped by the very department and discipline she built, which have persistent structural incentives to extract "just one more" service, review, or lecture indefinitely. Write a five-minute toast that honors this logic without turning into a lecture: the experts should recognize the precise philosophical architecture, while the family should hear a warm, concrete explanation of why she said no so often and why that refusal was itself an act of care. Please do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; the point is conceptual structure rather than scholarly apparatus.
