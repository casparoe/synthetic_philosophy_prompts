I'm drafting a section for my dissertation on the metaphysical foundations of intellectual-property law, and I've constructed an argument that seems to show that the concept of idea-infringement is either over-inclusive or ontologically unsupported. I would prefer not to abandon the nominalist premise—that propositional content is token-specific rather than a shared universal—nor the claim that patent law's independent-invention defense presupposes a causal-access analysis of infringement, but the conclusion strikes me as too strong. Here is the reasoning.

(1) On a nominalist account, ideas are particular mental tokens rather than abstract universals; consequently, two thinkers never literally instantiate the same idea, but only numerically distinct tokens that may share structural properties.
(2) If this is correct, then copying an idea is metaphysically impossible—causal transmission can only produce a resembling token, not reproduce an identical universal.
(3) Hence, infringement of an intellectual-property right cannot be analyzed as the unauthorized appropriation of an identical idea; it must be analyzed as the unauthorized causation of a structurally similar mental token.
(4) Yet independent invention—arriving at the same structural content without causal access—also produces a resembling token.
(5) Therefore, if infringement requires only structural resemblance, then independent invention counts as infringement; but if infringement requires causal access, then the wrong consists not in possessing the token but in the causal pathway that produced it.
(6) The latter option is what the independent-invention defense presupposes, but it is hard to square with a nominalist ontology: if there is no shared universal to access, then accessing the idea is just being causally influenced in a way that produces resemblance, which makes the defense depend on contingent causal history rather than on a property of the idea itself.
(7) Conversely, if we reject nominalism and treat ideas as shareable types, then causal access makes sense—one can access or fail to access the same universal—but Lockean labor-mixing becomes incoherent, because one cannot mix one's labor with an abstract object that exists independently of particular mental acts.
(8) So either we retain nominalism and lose a principled distinction between infringement and independent invention, or we retain the distinction by adopting a type-theoretic ontology and thereby undermine the natural-rights justification for IP.
(9) The conclusion that intellectual-property rights lack coherent metaphysical foundations therefore seems unavoidable.

I'd like to know which step should be rejected, and what rejecting it costs. In particular, could the flaw lie in (3)—perhaps infringement should be defined in terms of economic substitution or market displacement rather than mental-token resemblance—or in (7)—perhaps labor-mixing applies not to the idea-type itself but to the particular expression or embodied implementation? What does each repair require of the ontology of ideas?
