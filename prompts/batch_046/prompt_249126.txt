In the study of the historical Jesus, scholars have long applied criteria of authenticity—multiple attestation, dissimilarity, coherence, embarrassment, and rejection—to Gospel traditions in the hope of isolating an original core of sayings and deeds. Yet despite broad agreement on these methods and shared access to the same synoptic texts, the results have not converged: one scholar reconstructs an apocalyptic prophet, another a wisdom teacher, another a social reformer, and the same pericopes are routinely assigned to "authentic," "developed," or "redactional" layers with little intersubjective agreement. I have been discussing this impasse with colleagues in historical methodology, and the frustration is not that people lack evidence, but that rigorous application of the criteria still leaves the figure radically underdetermined. My working hypothesis is that the stall is structural rather than empirical. The debate implicitly treats authenticity as an epistemic filter applied to a reservoir of tradition, assuming that early transmission preserved or corrupted an original layer of material that can be separated out like sediment. But what if that reservoir model is conceptually mistaken? What if transmission in early Jesus communities was not storage-with-noise but a continuous process of reconstructive co-creation, in which each retelling generated a figure responsive to present communal needs rather than preserving a stable past individual? On this view, the criteria are not failing; they are being asked to perform an impossible separation between "original" and "development" in a tradition that may never have possessed the kind of stable origin they presuppose. There may also be a hidden disagreement about the question itself: one camp treats "Who was Jesus?" as a biographical inquiry into an individual behind the texts, while another treats it as an inquiry into the generative conditions of a religious movement, with both sides using the term "historical" for these distinct projects.

I am asking for a diagnosis, not another reconstruction. Is this disagreement primarily empirical—about how oral tradition actually functioned in first-century communities—verbal—stemming from ambiguous uses of "authenticity" and "historical"—substantive—reflecting incompatible commitments about whether historiography should aim at individual biography or social formation—or is it a sign that the question is ill-posed because it assumes a separation between history and theology that the sources do not support? And given whatever diagnosis you offer, what would actually count as progress? Would it require a formal model of tradition transmission, a new conceptual distinction that clarifies what the criteria can and cannot deliver, an acceptance that they yield only probabilistic clusters rather than a unified portrait, or a reformulation of the quest itself? Please respond in American English, and comment specifically on whether this framing overstates the constructivist element or misses something essential in how the criteria function in practice.
