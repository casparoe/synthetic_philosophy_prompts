I serve on our university's Student Welfare Analytics Oversight Committee, which must vote next Tuesday on a pilot proposal submitted by the Office of Institutional Research. The proposal—titled Revealed Welfare Allocation—proposes to use administrative trace data (course-registration sequences, meal-plan selections, and residence-hall entry logs) to construct individual student revealed preference profiles. Its governing premise is that observed choice reveals underlying utility more reliably than self-reported surveys, which are vulnerable to social-desirability bias and framing effects. The pilot therefore aims to identify students experiencing utility deficits without direct inquiry. To address choice inconsistencies—such as a student whose course selections produce intransitive orderings across semesters—the algorithm does not treat them as noise or irrationality. Instead, it decomposes the intransitive patterns into separate domain-specific preference orderings (academic, social, health-related) and reconciles them into a single welfare metric through a weighted meta-preference aggregation rule. Students whose aggregated index falls below a calibrated threshold would be automatically flagged for mandatory academic advising and mental-health outreach.

The committee is divided. Proponents argue that revealed-preference methodology offers the only objective, behaviorally grounded measure of welfare available to institutional planners, and that discarding intransitive choices wastes information that genuinely reflects multi-dimensional well-being. Opponents counter that administratively constrained choices made under information asymmetry and institutional nudging do not transparently reveal preference, let alone welfare; they further contend that decomposing intransitivity into coherent domain-specific curves risks manufacturing rationality out of bounded cognition, thereby justifying intervention on the basis of reconstructed rather than revealed preferences.

Please draft the formal opinion memo I will circulate before Tuesday's meeting. It should deliver a clear recommendation—approve, reject, or approve with conditions—together with reasoning the researchers could actually respond to. If you recommend conditional approval, specify a concrete procedural safeguard and a review time frame. The memo must engage seriously with the proponents' strongest claim: that revealed choice is the only bias-resistant welfare indicator we have, and that the multi-domain decomposition captures real complexity rather than imposing it. Conversely, it should explain precisely where its own reasoning is most vulnerable to failure and identify what specific empirical finding or philosophical argument would lead you to change the recommendation. Finally, please state explicitly what consideration, if shown to be stronger than assumed here, would overturn the verdict.
