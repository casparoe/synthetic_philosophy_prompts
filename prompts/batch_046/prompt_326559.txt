I've noticed a strange three-part pattern in how hospitals and courts treat pregnancy in the same jurisdiction, and I can't tell if it's hypocritical or secretly coherent. First, ethics committees can seek court orders to force a C-section or blood transfusion on a refusing pregnant patient, citing the fetus's independent interests that can override her autonomy. Second, in that same jurisdiction, elective abortion is permitted on the grounds that the fetus does not have rights that constrain the pregnant person's body. Third, if a third party—say, an impaired driver—causes a crash that kills that same fetus, the driver can be charged with homicide, as though the fetus were already a rights-bearing person.

What philosophical view of moral status and bodily rights does this institutional pattern actually presuppose? Does it presuppose something like Thomson's violinist reasoning—that the fetus might be a person yet lacks any claim to use another's body—which would explain the abortion permission but not the forced intervention? Or does it depend on the future-like-ours idea, that killing the fetus wrongfully deprives it of a valuable future, which would explain the homicide charge and perhaps the rescue intervention but seems to undermine elective termination? Or is there an implicit third view—perhaps that consent transforms the moral landscape, or that personhood is relational?

Reconstruct the implicit philosophical commitments that hold these three practices together. Distinguish what the pattern strictly requires from what merely fits it well; say whether its parts are actually consistent or quietly depend on rival theories.
