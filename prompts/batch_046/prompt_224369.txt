Construct a thought experiment in which an agent must assign a credence to the failure of a manufactured component, where the manufacturing process has a precisely calibrated objective failure rate of 3%, but the agent also possesses a direct physical measurement of the specific component's internal stress profile that strongly predicts failure independently of the manufacturing statistics. The setting should be realistic—e.g., an aerospace quality-control laboratory—occupy exactly one paragraph, and must not invoke demons, perfect predictors, quantum branching, or science-fictional technology. Engineer the case so that strict application of Lewis's Principal Principle fixes the agent's credence at exactly 0.03, while an evidentialist who conditionalizes on both the manufacturing statistics and the stress-profile measurement assigns a credence near 0.85, with the divergence being robust and not resolvable by any further information the agent could acquire. After describing the scenario, show the reasoning for each position: state the belief, specify what counts as admissible versus inadmissible evidence about the chance in this setup, and derive the numerical verdict for each theory. Identify the single structural feature that does the work—namely, whether the stress measurement is admissible with respect to the objective chance of this particular failure—and explain precisely what happens if that feature is removed: does the case collapse back into standard Lewisian harmony, or does it remain a disagreement? Finally, present the strongest reply available to a defender of the Principal Principle: they must argue that the stress profile is inadmissible with respect to the chance without merely restating the principle, and evaluate whether that reply succeeds or begs the question.
