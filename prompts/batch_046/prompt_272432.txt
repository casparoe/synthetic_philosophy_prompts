Please construct a philosophical argument that Dennett's multiple drafts model of consciousness fails to eliminate the Cartesian theater in the way he intends, but instead fragments it into a plurality of local theaters. Your argument should proceed in three steps. First, explain what Dennett means by the Cartesian theater—the idea that there is a central place or time where all conscious processing converges for a subject—and how his multiple drafts model is meant to replace that with parallel, competing narratives that are never unified into a single canonical stream. Second, develop the specific claim that each individual draft, when considered as a candidate narrative about the subject's experience, still presupposes an internal audience or viewer for whom that draft is presented; on this view, the homuncular problem is not solved but multiplied across drafts. Third, distinguish this distributed-theater critique from the more familiar objection that Dennett reintroduces a central observer through the mechanism of probing or reporting, and defend your claim against the reply that drafts are merely informational states lacking any intrinsic phenomenological audience. Conclude by assessing whether this critique, if sound, undermines the multiple drafts model or merely forces it to be supplemented with an account of why distributed audiences do not generate an infinite regress of homunculi.
