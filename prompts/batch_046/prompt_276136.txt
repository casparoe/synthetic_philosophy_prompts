You are grading a final exam in jurisprudence. The exam asks students to apply the Hart-Fuller debate to a hypothetical constitutional design. Below is the exam question followed by one student’s answer. Do not signal your own view of the correct answer; assess the work as an instructor or TA.

Exam Question:
The Republic of Althea has a constitutional Principle of Legislative Candor. It requires every statute to include an explicit statement of its purpose, and it prohibits enacting a statute that contradicts an existing one without expressly repealing it. Parliament enacts Statute A, which criminalizes “all acts that undermine public trust in institutions” and contains no purpose clause. Parliament then enacts Statute B, which imposes mandatory minimum prison terms for “violations of Statute A” but neither cites Statute A nor repeals any prior law. A judge strikes down both statutes, holding that they violate the Principle of Legislative Candor and, more fundamentally, fail to satisfy the conditions necessary for something to count as law at all.

Using the Hart-Fuller debate, analyze the judge’s reasoning. Specifically: (a) Would Hart accept that these enactments fail to be law? Explain with reference to the rule of recognition and the separability thesis. (b) Would Fuller accept that they fail to be law? Explain with reference to at least three of Fuller’s eight principles of the internal morality of law. (c) Identify precisely where Hart and Fuller disagree: is it about the concept of law itself, about the criteria for legal validity, or about the proper role of judicial interpretation?

Student Answer:
For part (a), Hart would say both statutes are valid law. Under the separability thesis, there is no necessary conceptual link between law and morality, so a statute’s vagueness or moral badness does not affect its legal status. The Althean legislature is a recognized authority, and both statutes were enacted according to the rule of recognition. Hart’s minimum content of natural law only demands rules protecting life and property for social survival; it does not require legislative candor or clarity. Therefore, the statutes are law, albeit very bad law, and the judge is wrong to invalidate them on these grounds.

For part (b), Fuller would agree with the judge and deny that these are genuine laws. The first statute violates Fuller’s principles of generality and clarity because “undermine public trust” is too vague to guide conduct. The second statute violates non-contradictoriness, since it implicitly depends on Statute A without reconciling the conflict, and it also violates promulgation because citizens are not adequately informed of which law applies. Furthermore, Fuller’s principle that law must contain an explicit statement of its purpose—derived from the internal morality of law—is violated by Statute A’s missing purpose clause. Because these failures undermine law’s ability to guide behavior, Fuller would conclude that they are not law.

For part (c), Hart and Fuller disagree mainly about the morality of legal interpretation and the proper scope of judicial review. Hart believes that in hard cases judges exercise discretion within the open texture of law, guided by the rule of recognition but not by moral criteria embedded in the concept of law itself. Fuller believes judges must interpret statutes in light of the internal morality of law, which allows them to invalidate enactments that violate it. Their disagreement is therefore not about the concept of law—both accept that law is a social institution—but about whether moral conditions are built into valid legal interpretation.
