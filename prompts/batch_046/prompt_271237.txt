In a philosophy of mind seminar last week, two of my classmates—Maya and Jonah—got into an extended argument about Descartes's position on animal minds that I couldn't quite resolve. I was only listening and don't have a strong view of my own, but I'd like a point-by-point adjudication of their exchange, plus an assessment of what both are missing and whether one side's position has to be partly abandoned.

Maya opened by saying that Descartes's mechanist doctrine is usually overread. She granted that Descartes denies animals rational souls and therefore denies them language, reflection, and universal reason. But she insisted that this doesn't entail they are insentient clockwork. She pointed out that Descartes describes sensory pathways in animals—nerve cords, brain movements, glandular interactions—in physiological detail much as he does for humans, and that for humans these same pathways are the bodily conditions of feeling. So she argued that an animal's cry when injured expresses a genuine sensory state, just one that lacks rational reflection. She also noted that in Descartes's correspondence with Henry More, he seems to allow that animals possess some form of life and affective response, even if not thought in the strict Cartesian sense.

Jonah countered that Maya is importing modern distinctions that Descartes wouldn't recognize. He argued that for Descartes every mental state—sensation included—is a mode of thinking, belonging to res cogitans. Since animals entirely lack res cogitans, there is literally no mental state occurring in them, conscious or otherwise. The pineal gland in a human receives impressions because it is united to a soul; in an animal, the gland moves mechanically but there is no recipient. Therefore the yelp of a struck dog is purely an acoustic reflex, mechanistically determined, with no inner qualitative character whatsoever. Jonah added that if Descartes had allowed animal sentience, his willingness to defend experimental practices on live animals would be morally incoherent, and he claimed that Maya's reading collapses Descartes's sharp dualism into a kind of proto-functionalist confusion.

Maya replied that Jonah is conflating moral practice with philosophical commitment—Descartes might simply have denied moral status to beings without rational souls regardless of whether they suffer. More importantly, she said Jonah treats the category of mental states too expansively. She proposed that we should read Descartes as holding a kind of phenomenal-zombie thesis about animals: they are behaviorally and physiologically identical to sentient beings but lack phenomenal consciousness altogether. On her view, this is different from saying they have unconscious feelings; rather, they have the functional and bodily correlates of feeling without any subjective experience. She argued that this preserves Descartes's dualism without requiring that every bodily process be mentally empty, and that it explains why Descartes can discuss animal sensory discrimination and memory-like retention without attributing rationality.

Jonah responded that this zombie framing is anachronistic. Descartes lacks the conceptual apparatus to separate phenomenal consciousness from cognitive access, and for him there is no mental life outside thought. He insisted that if an animal has no soul, it has no mind, and if it has no mind, then talk of functional correlates of feeling is just talk of mechanical parts. He concluded that Maya is trying to save Descartes from cruelty accusations by attributing to him a nuanced view he never held.

They turned to me and asked for a verdict: who is right about the textual interpretation at each stage, about the philosophical consistency of each reading, and about whether one interpretation makes the other incoherent? They also want to know what both are missing—whether there is a third interpretation that respects the mechanist texts without either attributing full-blown sentience or reducing animals to pure clockwork. Could you adjudicate move by move, rate the strength of each argument, say who is more right overall, and indicate whether any part of one side's position must be dropped?
