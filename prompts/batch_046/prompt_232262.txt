Next month I present a revised dissertation chapter—working title “Relational Grammar: Li as Syntactic Structure in Confucian Role Ethics”—to a comparative ethics workshop. My thesis is that Confucian role ethics should not be assimilated to virtue ethics or care ethics; instead, normative force arises from the syntactic relations of social roles (parent-child, elder-younger, ruler-subject), with li functioning not as ritual convention but as generative rules that constitute both roles and the agents who occupy them. On this reading, moral failure is structurally analogous to grammatical error—rupture in relational syntax—rather than to vice or harm, and the care-ethical “boundary problem” dissolves without collapsing the self into communitarian submersion. The audience is specific: two senior care ethicists in the Noddings/Gilligan tradition who will resist abstraction away from emotional particularity; one Kantian liberal who will argue that dissolving the self-other boundary eliminates the autonomy required for moral agency; a comparative philosopher of religion suspicious that I am over-rationalizing classical texts; and two Confucian-studies graduate students who know the Analects and Mencius closely enough to catch anachronism but who are open to analytic framing. They care especially about inter-role conflict (filial piety versus justice) and whether the framework permits critical moral reform.

Prepare me for the objections in the order they are likely to arrive, phrased as each subgroup would actually phrase them; distinguish which carry real argumentative force from which are mere defensive deflections; advise what I should concede in my opening remarks to lower resistance; identify the one reply that must land if the proposal is to survive the session; flag the objection I am hoping nobody raises—namely, whether my grammatical reading of li can account for moral innovation and role revision without smuggling universalist principles in through the back door; and point to the weakest point in my own position, the treatment of inter-role conflict when the relational syntax itself is contested, telling me exactly how to defend it rather than hoping they overlook it.
