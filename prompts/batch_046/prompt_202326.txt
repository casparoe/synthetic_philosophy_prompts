Two friends at a bar got into it about whether being a consequentialist is even coherant if you're genuinely clueless about long-run effects. Maya said the whole point is to maximize expected value, so cluelessness just means wider confidence intervals -- you still pick the option with the best expected payoff, even if that expectation is mostly noise. Her friend Jake said that's cheating: if your probabilities are so spread out that every option has basically the same expected value, then 'maximize' becomes an empty instruction, like telling someone to pick the tallest building when they're blindfolded in fog. Maya countered that Jake is confusing epistemic limits with practical ones -- we still have reasons to think some actions tilt outcomes in better directions, even if we can't quantify it. Jake then brought up this idea he read about 'structural humility': not just that we don't know outcomes, but that causal structure is so complex that assigning any directional sign is unjustified. Maya said if Jake's right, then not just consequentialism but any decision procedure collapses, since even deontology needs some belief about what actions you're performing. They asked me to settle it. Can you go through each move and say who's right about what? Also, is Jake's 'structural humility' point actually different from standard cluelessness, or just the same thing with fancier wording? And what, if anything, are they both missing?
