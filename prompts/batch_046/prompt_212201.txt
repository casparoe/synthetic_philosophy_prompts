As a doctoral student working on epistemology and scientific communication, I have been struck by an asymmetry in clinical-trial reporting norms that seems philosophically loaded but rarely discussed. Journals enforce a strict institutional rule: assertions of treatment efficacy in the results section must be tied to pre-registered primary endpoints, and any post-hoc finding has to be relegated to an exploratory-analysis subsection with hedged framing—say, “may suggest” rather than “demonstrates.” At the same time, peer reviewers routinely assert, with full confidence and without any analogous registration protocol, that a study is methodologically flawed, that an interpretation overreaches, or that a causal claim is unwarranted. What norm of assertion—knowledge, justified belief, or mere truth—does this bifurcated design presuppose? Does the pre-registration requirement treat registered hypotheses as knowledge-conducive in a way that licenses stronger assertoric force, or is it only a procedural hedge against false belief? And is the practice coherent if reviewers may assert criticisms with the very confidence that authors are forbidden to use for unregistered findings, or does the institution quietly rely on incompatible epistemologies for producers versus evaluators of scientific claims? I would like the implicit theory reconstructed, the tension located precisely, and an account of what would have to change in publishing practice if we replaced the underlying norm with a straightforward belief-only or truth-only alternative.
