I've been working through my notes for a seminar on moral psychology and find myself holding a cluster of commitments about moral sainthood that I can't tell are coherent or just comfortably familiar. I think Susan Wolf's critique of the moral saint is essentially correct: a life in which every decision is optimized for moral value is not merely exhausting but deficient in the kind of richness that comes from ground projects—artistic practice, deep friendship, or parenthood pursued for their own sake rather than as instruments of the good. At the same time, I reject any principled agent-relative asymmetry in the weight of reasons: if I can prevent serious harm at trivial cost, I don't think I'm permitted to decline on the grounds that I'd rather paint or read; the burden has to genuinely disrupt my life plan, not merely compete with it.

But I also want to maintain that there is a categorical, not merely quantitative, boundary between what morality can demand and what remains within a protected sphere of personal life. I don't think this protection can be grounded in desert—I'm skeptical of any meritocratic framework in which moral agents earn the right to pursue non-moral goods by accumulating prior moral credit—and I reject arbitrary, context-independent thresholds. So I seem committed to three things at once: that moral reasons are in principle unlimited in strength relative to personal ones; that some personal projects are categorically shielded from being overridden; and that this shielding cannot be justified by prior performance or by a fixed line in the sand. I'm also drawn to a distinction that hasn't been developed much in the literature I'm familiar with: between moral saturation, where an agent's full psychological capacity for moral concern is engaged, and moral domination, where every deliberative frame becomes moralized. I want to say saturation is admirable but domination is defective, yet if moral reasons really are unlimited, it's unclear why saturation wouldn't necessarily slide into domination.

Is this package consistent? I'm especially unsure whether my rejection of agent-relative limits is compatible with a categorical protected sphere, or whether I'm smuggling in an arbitrary boundary while denying that arbitrary boundaries are legitimate. I also wonder if my distinction between saturation and domination actually does any work, or whether it just renames the tension without resolving it. Which of these commitments—the unlimited strength of moral reasons, the categorical boundary, the rejection of desert-based justification, or the saturation/domination distinction—is the weakest link, and what would giving it up cost me?
