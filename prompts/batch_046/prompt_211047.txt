Consider the standard archaeological practice of inferring institutional hierarchy and cosmological belief from the differential distribution of grave goods in a burial site, coupled with the disciplinary norm that such inferences must avoid presentism—the anachronistic projection of modern social categories onto past material cultures. At the same time, archaeologists routinely treat physical absences—empty burial chambers, deliberately broken and scattered tools, or the lack of domestic waste in a ceremonial precinct—not as mere gaps in the record but as positively meaningful data that constrain interpretation. What philosophical commitments about interpretation, ontology, and evidence does this dual practice presuppose? Must it commit to a form of methodological uniformitarianism about human symbolic behavior, or can it be reconciled with a radically historicist hermeneutics that denies cross-temporal conceptual stability? Does treating absence as evidence require an ontology in which material objects possess meanings independent of present interpretive frameworks, and if so, is that ontology compatible with the anti-presentist constraint? Reconstruct the implicit epistemology and metaphysics of this practice, distinguish what it strictly requires from what it merely accommodates, and explain what methodological changes would be necessary if one replaced its underlying theory of interpretation with a behaviorist or purely instrumentalist account of artifact use.
