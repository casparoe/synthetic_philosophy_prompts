I'm a software engineer without any formal philosophy background, and at a recent discussion group two acquaintances—Maya and Leo—got into a sustained disagreement about Dennett's rejection of the Cartesian theater and his multiple drafts model of consciousness. I followed the broad strokes but couldn't tell who was making the stronger point at each turn. I'd like a point-by-point adjudication: for each move below, rate it as clearly right, mostly right, unclear, mostly wrong, or clearly wrong with a brief justification; then identify what important consideration both speakers are missing; and finally say who is more right overall and why.

Here is the exchange as I heard it.

Maya opened by saying that Dennett's multiple drafts model eliminates the Cartesian theater entirely. Consciousness is not a single stream displayed on a central screen; instead, parallel drafts of sensory and cognitive processing are constantly being edited and revised across the brain. What we call a conscious experience is just whichever draft happens to be available for verbal report at that moment, so the theater is an illusion created by introspection.

Leo replied that this only relocates the theater rather than removing it. The editorial process that selects which draft gets promoted to reportable status is itself functioning as a central observer. Even if the editing is distributed, there must still be a unified point of view that "sees" the winning draft; otherwise you have nothing but unconscious data structures competing with one another.

Maya countered that there is no hidden observer. The selection is purely causal and competitive, analogous to distributed version control with no central server: branches merge and resolve conflicts mechanically, and the repository's current head is just the resulting state. Asking "who sees the merged result" is like asking "which server experiences the website"—it is a category error.

Leo said the analogy fails because a git repository does not experience anything. If you remove every locus of subjective experience and replace it with mechanical merging, you haven't explained consciousness; you have explained it away. The Cartesian theater was never merely a physical place but a logical requirement: for content to be conscious, there must be a subject to whom it is presented. The multiple drafts model describes information processing well, but it never accounts for the "for-me-ness" of the dominant draft.

Maya responded that the "for-me-ness" is just an intrinsic feature of the dominant draft itself—its content includes self-reference and temporal continuity. Once a draft achieves dominance, it naturally reports "I am experiencing X." There is no extra step in which an observer receives it; the draft is simultaneously the message and the recipient, in the same way that a running process is both the computation and the state being computed.

Leo closed by arguing that this collapses the distinction between representing and being represented. If the draft is both message and recipient, then every unconscious data structure in a computer that references itself should count as conscious, which is absurd. You need a principled reason why dominance in biological brains produces subjectivity but dominance in silicon does not, and the only available reason is that biological brains contain something functionally equivalent to the theater—a unified field of integration that silicon lacks.

Please adjudicate move by move, note what both are overlooking—perhaps regarding whether Dennett's model was intended to explain phenomenology or merely reportability, or regarding the functional role of narrative coherence across drafts—and conclude with an overall verdict.
