Consider the following judgments that Aristotle makes across his treatments of exchange, money, and acquisition. First, he permits a cobbler and a builder to trade a pair of shoes for a house when a common measure—money—makes the goods commensurable and both parties seek goods for direct use rather than for accumulation. Second, he condemns a lender who demands back more silver coins than he lent, on the ground that money is naturally barren and ought not to reproduce itself as if it were alive. Third, he rejects a merchant who buys grain cheaply in one region and sells it dear in another solely to amass coins, since this treats acquisition as an art without the natural limit imposed by household or community need. Fourth, he allows a physician to accept coins for healing provided she spends them immediately on food and tools, leaving no hoarded surplus, because here money functions only as an intermediate step between two direct needs.

Now consider a new case. A city-state establishes a public treasury in which citizens deposit surplus coins. The treasury never lends these coins out; instead, each year it melts down and destroys a fixed portion of the coins in circulation. Citizens who leave their deposits untouched for decades earn no explicit interest, but because the total number of claim-tickets shrinks while the treasury's stock of goods remains constant, the purchasing power of each remaining ticket gradually rises. One citizen deposited a large sum forty years ago, contributed no further labor, and now withdraws enough to fund the construction of an entire public bathhouse. The mechanism involves no lending, no trade, and no explicit reproduction of coinage—only storage and destruction.

What should Aristotle say about this treasury practice if he is to remain consistent with his earlier four verdicts? Please also say what would change your mind—whether an argument about Aristotle's texts, about the nature of money, or about the logic of proportionate exchange—would lead you to abandon your preferred rule for its rival.
