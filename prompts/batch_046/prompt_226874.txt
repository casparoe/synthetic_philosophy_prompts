I'm organizing a small philosophy-of-history seminar focused on whether historical contingency—of the "for want of a nail" variety—reflects an objective feature of causal processes or only our retrospective narrative framing. Before we examine particular theories, I want a rigorous specification of what an adequate account must actually deliver. Write the desiderata: the complete list of requirements a theory of historical contingency must satisfy in order to distinguish genuinely chaotic branching points, where minute variations in initial conditions generate large-scale, structurally unpredictable outcomes, from merely complicated but deterministic causal sequences. For each desideratum, state the conceptual case or distinction that motivates it—covering, at minimum, how the theory should handle counterfactual stability (whether macro-outcomes would survive minor perturbations), the problem of causal overdetermination in long causal chains, and the distinction between epistemic unpredictability (a limitation on our predictive knowledge) and ontological openness (a real indeterminacy in the process itself). Mark which requirements are non-negotiable for any adequate theory and which are preferences specific to particular camps (e.g., those demanding physical-level chaos dynamics versus those allowing high-level institutional contingency), and identify any pair or subset that appears jointly unsatisfiable—paying special attention to the tension between requiring precise, prospectively applicable criteria for identifying branching points and the widely accepted view that such points are typically visible only in retrospect. Then briefly indicate, without full scoring, how three contrasting approaches—a chaos-theoretic dynamics model applied to social systems, a counterfactual-dependence account of historical explanation, and a narrative-coherence view that treats branching as constructed by the historian's framing—each fare against the list. The goal is to establish the evaluation framework itself, not to settle the debate.
