In discussions comparing the fall of the Roman Republic to modern democratic erosion, I keep encountering the line that "the Republic was destroyed not by tyrants but by the constitutional mechanisms designed to prevent tyranny." People invoke it to argue everything from "we need stronger institutional checks" to "all legal safeguards eventually backfire," and sometimes to claim that modern emergency powers will follow the same path. Before I use it myself, I want to understand exactly what claim that sentence could be expressing. Is it a historical thesis about Rome—say, that the senatus consultum ultimum and tribunate vetoes were turned into instruments of factional war? Is it a general claim about institutional design, that safeguards contain inherent self-destruct buttons? Or is it an analogy to modern politics, implying that today's legal checks on executive power will similarly be weaponized? Could some readings be trivially true, others false about Rome specifically, and others unsupported leaps to present-day democracies? Please lay out the distinct precise theses hidden in the slogan, say which are defensible as Roman history, which are substantive general theories, and which actually justify applying the Roman case to modern politics rather than treating it as mere rhetoric.
