I just spent two hours arguing with a friend about whether germs, climate patterns, and volcanic eruptions should be treated as "historical actors" with the same explanatory standing as human agents. We both agree that environmental forces are indispensable to explaining the decline of manorialism after the Black Death, the spike in witchcraft prosecutions during the Little Ice Age, and the 1816 famine following Tambora. But my friend pushed the point further and built an argument that now strikes me as proving far too much. Here it is, laid out step by step:

(1) To call something a "historical actor" is only to say that it makes a counterfactually indispensable contribution to an outcome and that its operation is not reducible to human intentions.
(2) The plague bacillus, the cooling of the Little Ice Age, and the 1815 Tambora aerosol veil each satisfy this condition for major historical outcomes.
(3) Therefore, germs, climate shifts, and volcanoes are historical actors in precisely the same explanatory sense as human collectives.
(4) If two entities are actors in the same explanatory sense, then any explanation that assigns primary causal weight to one class while treating the other as background is arbitrarily asymmetrical, unless a further principled distinction can be shown.
(5) There is no such further distinction: human intentions are also counterfactually necessary, but they are not more necessary than the microbial or climatic conditions that enable or constrain them, and neither class is reducible to the other.
(6) So what I will call symmetrical causal historiography demands that human and non-human actors be given equal explanatory priority in every adequate historical narrative.
(7) If human intentions and non-human forces always share equal priority, then human agency is never the dominant driver of history; it is at best a mediating variable among co-equal causes.
(8) If human agency is never dominant, then attributing moral responsibility for historical outcomes—blaming a leader for a famine response, praising a reformer for curbing an epidemic—is a category error, since the decisive causal work is done by forces outside intentional control.
(9) But we clearly do and should assign such moral responsibility; for instance, we blame inadequate governance for worsening the 1816 famine rather than blaming Mount Tambora.
(10) Therefore, symmetrical causal historiography is false, and at least one of (1) through (8) must be rejected.

My friend insists that (3) and (9) are non-negotiable: environmental forces really are indispensable explanatory actors, and moral responsibility is not an illusion. I want to keep both. Yet I cannot locate the slip between (3) and (9). Is the flaw an equivocation in what "actor" means between (1) and (3)? A hidden scope or quantifier shift in (4) or (6)? Or is the argument actually valid, and the real cost of keeping (3) and (9) is that I must reject (5) or (7) and accept that human intentions possess a special kind of causal authority that environmental forces lack—even though I cannot articulate what that authority consists in without sounding anthropocentric? Tell me exactly which premise or inference to reject, explain why it looked plausible, and price each available rejection, especially with respect to whether non-human forces remain genuinely explanatory agents rather than mere background conditions.
