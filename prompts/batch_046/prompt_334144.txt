I'm a senior copy editor at an academic press and I need a reusable decision proceedure for flagging gender-default language in textbook manuscripts, grounded in the logic of Hofstadter's Person Paper satire — you know, the gender-flip trick that exposes hidden male-as-default assumptions, and the idea that so-called neutral terms often carry an invisible norm. I process about twelwe chapters a week, I've got maybe fifteen minutes per call, the author's usualy not available, and I need the editor-in-cheif to sign off on anything I flag. So I need a step-by-step checklist with explicit tests I can run on sentences or paragraphs, plus clear markers for where judgment can't be eliminated and I just have to make a call.

Cover these categories: (1) pronoun defaults in hypothetical examples ("if a researcher forgets his notes..."), (2) nominal defaults ("mankind", "chairman", "the reasonable man"), (3) quotation practices when the original source uses gendered language, and (4) historical terms where modern usage clashes with period accuracy. For each, state the test, pass/fail, and the genuinely blurry zone.

Apply it to three worked cases: a psych text using "he" for every hypothetical patient, a philosophy chapter talking about "the rights of man" in Enlightenment context, and a biology passage that describes sperm "pursuing" the egg in competitive, almost-agentive language. One of these should yield a recommendation I won't like — either telling me to leave alone something that feels wrong, or flagging something that feels defensible. Also be explicit about what this proceedure can't settle, like when an author's deliberate stylistic consistency is a legitimate reason to override the checklist.

Keep it practical: flowchart in words, no bibliography, and flag which steps are just judgment calls wearing a lab coat.
