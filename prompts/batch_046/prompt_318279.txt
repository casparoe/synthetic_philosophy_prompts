I am a retired biophysicist who, since leaving the laboratory, has taken up philosophy of mind. Next week I am delivering the opening remarks for an interdisciplinary workshop, "Identity Beyond the Skull," at my former university; the room will be split between philosophers and neuroscientists and my own family and old scientific colleagues, none of whom share my recent reading. My late wife—who died two years ago and had no patience for mysticism—was a linguist, and in the months since I have become persuaded, through Douglas Hofstadter's argument that an "I" is a pattern of causal loops rather than a substance confined to one brain, that the self can be distributed, partially copied, and persist in fragments within other minds. I want this framing to do structural work in the remarks: it must allow me to speak of her presence in my own thinking without claiming she survives in any metaphysical afterlife, and it must bar me from saying the usual false comforts—that she is "watching over us" or literally "lives on in our hearts." At the same time, the speech should reach my children, perhaps by using an analogy from my old field—say, a wave form that retains its shape across different media—to illustrate substrate independence without reducing her to interchangeable information. Please write these remarks, about five minutes, formal but intimate, so that the philosophical position shapes both what I can honestly affirm and what I must withhold; do not include bibliographies, citations, or reading lists, and do not invoke obscure named theories beyond Hofstadter's distributed-self idea. To keep the task from being mere summary, inject one further conceptual tension: the problem that if the self is genuinely replicable, grief might seem irrational, yet we still treat some patterns as irreplaceable because of their unique causal history—let this tension remain unresolved and do real work in the speech's conclusion.
