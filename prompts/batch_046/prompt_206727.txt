ok so ive been chewing on advance directives but in a weird corner case. usually we talk about them for dementia or coma—future you is incompetent so past you gets to call the shots. but what about someone who is totally lucid and just... gradually becomes a different person value-wise? like imagine a rationalist who decides to spend ten years in a monastery that systematically dismantles their old preferences, or someone who undergoes a long structured program that replaces their utility function with something else—still rational, still self-aware, but genuinely alien to the old self.

they write a directive beforehand saying if they ask to leave or change course after year five, ignore them and keep them in. not a ulysess contract about weakness—this is about predicted permanent rational transformation. i think there's actually a strong argument that the earlier self should still bind the later one, and not just because of psychological continuity (which is shaky anyway). instead, think of it like an architect with epistemic priority: the earlier self designed the transformation and knows the goal-state better than the intermediate self who's mid-drift and confused. plus there's this idea that value drift under deliberate restructuring is closer to corruption or noise than to authentic growth, so the later self's preferences dont have automatic moral weight.

can you argue for that position? like, actually defend the binding authority of the pre-transformation directive, but ground it in something other than psychological continuity or future irrationality? and be committed about it—i dont want a balanced pros-and-cons list, i want you to pick a line and defend it. also im not asking for citations or a reading list, just work it out from the concepts. (and yeah i know this sounds a bit like those commitment-device posts from years ago but its not the same—this is about competence-preserving transformation, not akrasia.)
