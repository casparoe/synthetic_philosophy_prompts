Design a 90-minute classroom activity or structured game for an advanced undergraduate or graduate philosophy seminar of about 16 students on Stoicism’s modern revival and the dichotomy of control. The activity should make participants feel the practical gap between the popular modern framing—where “control” functions mainly as an emotional-regulation filter that directs anxiety away from uncontrollable outcomes—and the ancient Stoic claim that the only true good is virtue or correct assent (prohairesis), with externals treated as genuinely indifferent (adiaphora) rather than merely unworthy of anxiety.

Inject a niche conceptual distinction into the design: participants should run into the difference between suppressing emotional investment in outcomes (the modern self-help and CBT-inflected reading) and restructuring one’s desires so that outcomes are literally not desired as goods, even when they are preferred. Do not rely on lecture up front; arrange the rules, roles, or token economy so that students apply the modern filter first, experience its partial success, and then encounter the ancient normative restructuring in a second round where the payoff scheme penalizes hidden attachment to outcomes.

Specify: the setting and group size (seminar, approximately 16, 90 minutes); materials needed (scenario cards, role assignments, or tokens—no real money required); a timeline with distinct phases; rules and payoffs that generate the tension without the facilitator explaining it in advance; a debrief that maps specific moments in the activity onto parts of the Stoic view and onto the modern misreading; an honest account of which feature of the ancient position the activity cannot reproduce and must be supplied by the facilitator; and a prediction of what will predictably fail or confuse students the first time the activity runs, with a concrete remedy.

Additionally, include in your response—whether in the design notes or the debrief—a clear separation between what is settled about Stoic doctrine (for instance, that Epictetus distinguishes what depends on us from what does not) and what is genuinely contested among scholars and practitioners (for instance, whether modern popular Stoicism is a legitimate adaptation or a substantive distortion; whether “indifference” implies absence of preference or absence of value-attachment). Do not present a contested interpretive claim—such as the claim that modern Stoicism is necessarily a misreading—as if it were the consensus view. Note explicitly where the activity takes liberties with textual interpretation or simplifies a debated point for pedagogical effect.
