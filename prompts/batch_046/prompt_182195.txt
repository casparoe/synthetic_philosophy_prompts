I have noticed a recurring pattern in disciplinary boundary-work that I cannot place precisely within the philosophy of science literature, and I want to know whether it already has a name and where its edges lie. Consider a research community that initially adopts a falsifiability-based criterion for demarcating science from pseudoscience: a theoretical claim is scientific only if some observable outcome could in principle show it false. Over time, a sequence of anomalous results accumulates. Rather than treating these as refutations that might disqualify the claim, practitioners gradually append scope conditions—stating that the claim applies only to closed systems, or only when background noise falls within a range that was never specified in advance, or only to populations that meet criteria introduced after the fact. Each addition is locally defensible, yet collectively they ensure that every observed failure can be reclassified as falling outside the claim's protected domain. What is distinctive here is not the immunization of a particular empirical theory, since that is well documented; rather, it is the immunization of the demarcation criterion itself. The boundary between science and non-science is not rejected—indeed it is repeatedly invoked—but its operational rules are expanded post hoc so that no actual observation can trigger a reclassification of the field as unscientific. The criterion survives intact in principle while becoming unexercisable in practice. I am not asking about ordinary auxiliary hypotheses, because those modify a theory's content; here the modifications affect the conditions under which the boundary-test applies, leaving the core claims untouched and the boundary formalism preserved. Does this meta-level drift—where a falsifiability standard is protected from its own failures by progressive, individually reasonable scope adjustments—have an established label? If there is a nearest concept, how exactly does this differ from it, and if there is none, what term and boundary conditions would you propose?
