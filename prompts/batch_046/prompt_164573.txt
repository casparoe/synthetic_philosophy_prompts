I've been chewing over the absent-minded driver problem — Piccione and Rubinstein cooked it up in the late nineties, I think — and I can't shake the feeling that the whole debate is stuck in mud. Causal decision theorists say one thing, evidentialists say another, and for about twenty-five years nobody's really shifted there position. The literature just spins it's wheels. The paradox is straightforward enough: our driver plans ex-ante to stay on the motorway past the first exit, knowing he'll be absent-minded and can't tell whether he's at exit one or exit two; but once he's actually at an intersection, his reasoning flips and he wants to take the exit, because he might already be at exit two. CDT and EDT split on whether the ex-ante plan or the ex-post choice is the rational one, and every reply feels like it's been made before.

My working hunch is that this isn't really about causation versus correlation at all, but about whether we should model absent-minded behaviour as a failure of self-knowledge or as a structural feature of the decision tree — and that if we take the structural view seriously, the paradox dissolves without picking sides. But I'm not sure. Is this a substantive disagreement about rationality, a verbal dispute about what 'planning' means, or are the two camps just answering different questions — one about norms for agents, one about formal representations of choice? And is there any hidden empirical or formal issue doing the work?
