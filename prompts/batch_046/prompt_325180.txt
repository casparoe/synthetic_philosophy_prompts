I run a 90-minute graduate seminar in formal epistemology with 14 students. Design an in-class simulation that makes participants feel the specific gap Jeffrey conditionalization addresses: evidence that does not determine any discrete proposition but shifts probabilities continuously across a fixed partition. The hidden-state mechanism should use cards or envelopes controlled by you—the facilitator—representing an ambiguous observation (e.g., a noisy measurement that is compatible with several cells of a partition but assigns different likelihood weights to them). Give explicit rules, a point-based payoff scheme that rewards calibrated posterior beliefs revealed against the hidden state, and no real money.

Build in a structural twist: before observing, all groups must agree on the prior probabilities inside each partition cell, but the evidence updates only the weights assigned to the cells themselves—the Jeffrey “experience probabilities.” Arrange the incentives so that groups discover, in round two, that two rational agents with identical intra-cell priors can still diverge sharply after shared observation if they assign different cell-weight updates, because Jeffrey’s rule does not determine those weights from the evidence alone. The activity should make them run into this relocation of underdetermination themselves before it is named.

Include a debrief that maps each round onto the formal distinction between Bayes’ conditioning on a specific event and Jeffrey conditioning on a partition; identify what will predictably go wrong (groups treating the noisy signal as direct evidence for one cell, or smuggling Bayes’ rule into the update); and be explicit about which feature—the dependence on a prior metric over experience that the rule itself does not update—the simulation can only gesture at and you must supply by framing. Keep everything runnable without computers, and do not ask for citations, bibliographies, or named texts.
