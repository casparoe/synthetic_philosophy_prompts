Me and my study group got into it last week over Sartre after someone brought up that quote about being condemned to freedom — not the exact wording, I don't remember it verbatim. Anyway, two of them, Marcus and Jen, went back and forth about whether calling someone "in bad faith" for staying in a soul-crushing job is fair. (1) Marcus said yes, that's literally the textbook case: the person says "I have no choice, I need the paycheck," but Sartre says we're always free to choose, even in prison, so treating yourself like a thing (en-soi) to avoid the anguish of freedom is bad faithe pure and simple. (2) Jen pushed back hard — she said that's victim-blaming dressed up as ontology. Facticty includes real economic coercion, dependents, debt; saying "no choice" isn't self-deception, it's recognizing the weight of your situation. (3) Marcus came back that facticty is never determinative for Sartre, it's just the raw material that freedom has to work with; even the prisoner chooses his attitude, so "I had no choice" is ontologically false no matter how psychologically comforting. (4) Then Jen dropped something kinda niche she called "structural bad faith" — not one person lying to themselves, but a whole system where options are so constrained that "choice" itself becomes an ideological fiction. She said Sartre's individualism can't handle this because he treats the pour-soi as always present at the personal level, but if you're embedded in structures that pre-decide your field of possibilities, then blaming you for bad faith is like blaming a fish for not walking. (5) Marcus said that's just Althusser sneaking determinism in the back door, and that Sartre's political activism doesn't change his ontology — circumstances can never nullify freedom, period. (6) Jen admitted it's not pure Sartre, but asked whether his framework can be extended without contradiction, and pointed out that if Sartre really recognized material conditions in practice, maybe his ontology needs updating rather than her idea being dismissed.

Can you adjudicate this move by move? Rate each of the six moves from clearly wrong to clearly right, briefly justify each rating, and say who's more right overall. Also tell me what both of them are missing — especially whether "structural bad faith" is a legitimate extension of Sartre or a betrayal of his core claim, and whether there's a middle ground about facticty that neither captured. Don't just pick a winner; I want to know exactly where each argument holds up and where it collapses.
