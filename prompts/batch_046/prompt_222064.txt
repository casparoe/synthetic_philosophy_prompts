I am designing a single ninety-minute seminar for first-year philosophy doctoral students who have completed one course in formal decision theory but have not yet encountered multiverse ethics or acausal reasoning. The topic is evidential cooperation in large worlds, specifically the non-standard case in which the agents are not perfect copies but "logical neighbors"—systems with structurally correlated decision procedures yet divergent empirical histories and different prior credences about whether a large world exists at all. Plan the teaching sequence: the order in which the conceptual pieces must be introduced so that the move from causal to evidential reasoning feels like a natural extension of decision-theoretic reasoning rather than a sleight of hand; the one concrete scenario that should carry the idea and what properties it must possess, including the absence of any causal connection and the presence of logical correlation despite empirical divergence; the misconceptions the audience will predictably bring or construct—such as treating evidential correlation as hidden signaling, conflating cooperation with self-sacrifice for an identical twin, or assuming that disagreement about priors blocks cooperation entirely—and the precise point in the sequence where each should be defused; a brief check for understanding that reveals whether students have grasped the dependency structure rather than memorized the slogan; and what aspects—such as the formal derivation of cooperative equilibria in continuous-world models or the debate over whether logical correlation requires a shared ontology—should be explicitly set aside at this level and acknowledged as omitted.
