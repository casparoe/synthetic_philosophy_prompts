As a retired cell biologist who has lately turned to metaphysics, I am preparing a brief reading for a small discussion group on persistence and identity, and I would like you to compose a poem of exactly eighteen lines in unrhymed iambic pentameter, divided into three six-line stanzas. The first stanza should present the ship departing harbor with each line anchored to a named structural timber—keel, rib, deck plank—so that the vessel is materially specific and singular. In the second stanza, each successive line should replace one of those named timbers with a new piece of wood, preserving the ship’s form and function but erasing its original matter line by line, until by the stanza’s end the vessel is composed entirely of replacements yet is still spoken of as Theseus. The third stanza should split at line thirteen: its first three lines describe a second vessel assembled elsewhere from the discarded original planks, fully restored to the ship’s initial form, while its final three lines pose—without answering—which of the two, if either, holds the rightful claim to the name. Crucially, weave through the imagery the biological parallel I have been considering: that over a human lifespan nearly every cell in the body is replaced, except for certain long-lived neurons, so that I now stand to my younger self in the same ambiguous relation as the rebuilt ship stands to the original. The turn at line thirteen should force the recognition that material continuity and functional continuity have diverged into rival claimants, and the poem should leave the tension entirely open rather than resolving it. The tone should be contemplative and precise, suitable for an audience of scientists turned amateur philosophers, and the poem should contain no references to specific philosophers, texts, or scholarly debates by name—only the structural logic of the problem should be visible.
