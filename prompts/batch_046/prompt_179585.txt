I'm currently working through the literature on introspective reliability for a philosophy of mind seminar, and I have constructed the following argument that now collapses into a conclusion I find unacceptable. I want a committed diagnosis—please pick the weakest link, defend that choice with reasons, and tell me exactly what I am paying for it, rather than laying out a menu of options and leaving me to choose.

(1) If a cognitive faculty gives direct, first-person access to mental state M, then a subject who possesses M should be able to determine, without inference, whether M obtains. (2) Introspection is precisely such a faculty for occurrent conscious states—beliefs, pains, perceptual experiences—giving them a special epistemic status. (3) Implicit biases are mental states: they are structured associative dispositions or evaluative patterns genuinely present in the subject's psychology, not merely external behavioral labels. (4) Therefore, if introspection is generally reliable for mental states, it should deliver direct knowledge of one's implicit biases; the subject should be able to introspectively ascertain whether she holds them. (5) But the empirical literature consistently shows that subjects are poor at reporting their implicit biases, often sincerely denying them despite behavioral and implicit-association evidence. (6) So introspection fails for implicit biases. (7) There is no principled, non-ad-hoc boundary that restricts introspective failure exclusively to implicit biases while preserving it for conscious beliefs and sensations; both are mental states, and any proposed distinction—say, between access-conscious and merely associative structures—would have to be motivated independently rather than being post hoc. (8) Hence, if introspection is unreliable about implicit biases, it is unreliable about mental states generally. (9) But then I lack authoritative first-person access even to whether I am currently in pain or believe it is raining. (10) Therefore, introspection is not a distinctive source of self-knowledge at all.

The conclusion strikes me as absurd—I do not want to surrender my knowledge of my own pain. I am inclined to protect (1) and (2), but I want to know precisely what I am paying if I reject (3) or (7) instead. Please take a definite stand: identify the specific step where the argument slips, explain why that step looked plausible, and price the cost of giving it up—especially whether rejecting it forces me to deny that implicit biases are mental states in the relevant sense, or to accept a form of self-opacity that undermines the traditional picture of introspective authority.
