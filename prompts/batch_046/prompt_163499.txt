I have been reflecting on the concept of privacy, especially after reading about the practice of building "shadow profiles"—comprehensive predictive records about individuals who have never registered for a service, agreed to its terms, or actively shared any data. This observation led me to a line of reasoning that I now suspect might be confused. It seems to me that the core harm of a privacy violation is always tied to the betrayal or exploitation of a prior autonomous choice: if I voluntarily disclose something to a doctor, and she reveals it, my autonomy has been undermined because my own decision to share was misused. But in the case of shadow profiles, there is no prior disclosure and no consent to exploit. The person never entered into an informational relationship with the observer, never made themselves vulnerable through an act of sharing, and never had their will overridden in the process. Therefore, I am inclined to think that being profiled without ever having participated might not be a privacy violation in the same conceptual sense at all—more like being observed in a public square, where no confidential relationship exists to be broken.

If this reasoning is sound, then the value of privacy might be fundamentally bifurcated: one branch protecting the integrity of voluntary disclosures, and another—if it genuinely exists—protecting people from being inferred about without ever having chosen to be known. Yet I am troubled by the possibility that this distinction lets non-consensual inference off too easily. Still, I cannot clearly see the conceptual mistake in linking privacy harm so tightly to prior consent. Could you help me identify where this reasoning goes wrong? Specifically, is there a coherent account of privacy's nature and value that treats the non-consensual construction of predictive profiles as carrying the same kind of moral weight as the breach of a voluntary disclosure, and if so, what makes that weight independent of any prior act of sharing?
