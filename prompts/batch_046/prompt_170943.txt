Two members of a philosophy-of-history seminar—call them A and B—have reached an impasse over whether Zygmunt Bauman’s account of modernity and the Holocaust can survive rigorous comparison with other mass atrocities. A argues that Bauman’s explanation turns on specifically modern conditions: bureaucratic rationalization, the fragmentation of moral responsibility through division of labor, and the instrumental administration of human life. From this, she draws the conclusion that the Holocaust is structurally exemplary rather than historically unique: any modern society with comparable organizational capacity and an ideological mandate could reproduce an analogous event. She cites not only the Rwandan genocide but also the bureaucratic machinery of colonial administration as cases that share the organizational form, even if they differ in technological scale and execution speed.

B replies that this conflates a necessary condition with a sufficient explanation. He grants that bureaucratic rationality was present, but insists that the Holocaust remains unique because it synthesized modern organizational methods with a specifically ideological objective—the total physical annihilation of an entire people regardless of territorial gain, economic utility, or military necessity—carried out as an industrialized production process with murder treated as a quota. Other atrocities, he contends, deployed bureaucratic means toward different ends: extraction in colonial contexts, rapid political elimination in Rwanda. To reduce uniqueness to a quantitative difference in scale, he says, is to miss the qualitative shift represented by a modern state dedicating its full administrative apparatus exclusively to genocide as an end in itself.

A responds by proposing a distinction between comparability for causal explanation and comparability for moral evaluation. For causal explanation, she maintains, we require structural analogues; refusing comparison disables Bauman’s own warning, because if modern organizational forms generate moral distancing only in one singular case, the diagnosis has no predictive or preventive reach. For moral evaluation, she allows that uniqueness might hold, but then uniqueness becomes a claim about commemorative priority rather than historical structure. She accuses B of guaranteeing uniqueness by definitional fiat: by specifying the Holocaust’s feature set precisely enough, any other event will necessarily fall outside.

B counters that A’s distinction still undermines uniqueness. If causal comparability is admitted, then commemorative uniqueness becomes an arbitrary collective choice rather than a historical substance. He further argues that Bauman treated the Holocaust as a warning precisely because its synthesis was not universally replicable: modernity supplies the instruments, but the specific convergence of state capacity, ideological totality, and target selection was historically contingent in ways that make direct analogy misleading rather than illuminating. Comparison as a study of degrees, he concedes, is possible, but uniqueness remains intact as the extreme realization of a specifically modern potential.

A then introduces a harder case: the genocide against the Herero and Namaqua in German South West Africa, which involved explicit extermination orders, bureaucratic registration, concentration camps, and systematic starvation—features that match B’s criteria of bureaucratic annihilation—yet occurred within a colonial apparatus that lacked the full industrial-technological infrastructure of the mid-twentieth-century state. She asks whether B must either admit that uniqueness is not tied to industrial modernity, thereby weakening the Bauman link, or acknowledge that uniqueness is not singular but a family of extreme realizations distributed across different grades of modern state development.

B answers that the colonial case actually reinforces a gradient view: modernity is not binary, and the Holocaust represents the complete actualization of a bureaucratic-annihilative potential that existed only partially in earlier contexts. Thus structural comparison tracks degrees of realization without collapsing uniqueness, which he now defines not as singular occurrence but as the maximal endpoint of a modern continuum. A immediately objects that redefining uniqueness as a maximal endpoint is exactly the definitional move she criticized: it saves uniqueness only by making it a relational property—this event is unique because it is the most extreme—rather than an intrinsic categorical distinction.

They turn to an outside observer and ask for adjudication.

Please adjudicate this disagreement move by move. For each of the five rounds above—A’s opening structural argument, B’s uniqueness reply, A’s causal/moral distinction, B’s warning-and-contingency response, and the Herero exchange ending with A’s charge of definitional relocation—rate the move from clearly mistaken to clearly right and briefly justify the rating. Then state who is more right overall, identify what both sides are missing—particularly regarding whether a gradient model of modern bureaucratic violence can preserve both causal comparability and commemorative distinctness without collapsing into either pure uniqueness or pure structural equivalence—and say whether B’s final redefinition of uniqueness as a maximal endpoint genuinely answers A’s objection or merely relocates it.
