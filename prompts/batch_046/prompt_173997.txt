I am not a philosopher or scientist; I am a carpenter in a mid-sized town where a small biotech firm has proposed distributing an at-home headset that uses gentle, targeted stimulation to lift mood in people with treatment-resistant depression. My brother was in the pilot program. The suffering stopped, but so did his urge to finish projects; he told me that completing a cabinet now felt like swallowing medicine that carried no taste at all. I have volunteered to open a public forum at our library on whether the town should welcome the proposal. The audience will be split between neighbors and family members who see only the possibility of relief, and several philosophers and neuroscientists who recognize the classic concern about wireheading and direct reward tampering. I need about four minutes of remarks. They must genuinely honor my brother’s release from pain without pretending that the hollow feeling he described is unreal; they must speak to the experts without turning the room into a seminar; and they must avoid both the claim that suffering is always noble and the claim that engineered happiness is always shallow, because each sounds like an insult to someone present. Write the opening remarks so that the tension between genuine relief and tampered reward does real structural work—determining what I can promise, what I must leave genuinely unresolved, and how to end—rather than serving as philosophical decoration.
