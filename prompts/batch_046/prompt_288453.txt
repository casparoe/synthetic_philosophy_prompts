I am a retired experimental physicist who has recently turned to political philosophy, and I am trying to articulate a conservative stance that does not simply reject the scientific habits of mind I practiced for forty years. Here are the commitments I currently hold:

(1) A Burkean view of society as an intergenerational partnership in which institutions embody accumulated practical wisdom that exceeds what any single generation can articulate or justify from first principles.

(2) The Chesterton-fence principle: one ought not to dismantle an established social practice unless one can specify the problem it was originally designed to solve and show that the problem no longer exists or can be solved more efficiently.

(3) A scientific fallibilism about social knowledge: just as in physics we treat well-confirmed theories as provisional and welcome attempts to falsify them, we should treat institutional arrangements as hypotheses subject to empirical revision.

(4) The claim that complex social institutions exhibit something analogous to evolutionary exaptation and cryptic genetic buffering—features that appear dysfunctional or redundant under stable conditions but confer resilience during rare systemic shocks—so that the absence of an obvious current benefit is not sufficient grounds for removal.

(5) The view that when an institution produces documented, sustained harm across multiple generations with no countervailing stabilizing effect that can be even speculatively identified, it ought to be reformed or abolished, regardless of its historical pedigree.

Do these hang together, or do I harbor hidden contradictions? In particular: is (3) compatible with (2), or does my scientific habit of welcoming refutation commit me to tearing down fences whenever I can construct a plausible counter-model? Does (4) provide a legitimate escape clause for (2) that saves me from (3), or is it an ad hoc immunization strategy that renders my position unfalsifiable? Is there a tension between (1) and (5) regarding the weight we should give to present suffering versus the tacit interests of future generations?

Please audit these commitments systematically. Rank the tensions by severity rather than listing them all as equally damaging. For each tension, say whether it is genuine or only apparent, what the cheapest revision would be, and what that revision costs in terms of the other commitments. Finally, tell me whether there is a single revision—giving up one of these five—that would eliminate the most tension overall, or whether the conflicts are distributed enough that I need more than one adjustment.
