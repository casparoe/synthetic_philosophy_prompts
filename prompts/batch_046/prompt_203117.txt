A philosophy blog I read summarizes strong illusionism about phenomenal consciousness as follows. The illusionist grants that people have mental states and report them, but denies that any of these states possess intrinsic phenomenal character—there is nothing it is like to undergo them. Instead, introspection operates like a theoretical modeling module: it constructs a phenomenal file for each experience, structurally analogous to the object-files visual cognition uses to track items across saccades, except these mental files lack any real referent. They are empty theoretical posits—comparable to caloric or phlogiston—embedded in our folk-psychological framework. Because experiential transparency means we access only representational content directed at the world, never mental properties themselves, there is no internal corrective standpoint from which the error could be detected. The blogger therefore concludes that illusionism collapses into a kind of self-eliminativism: if nothing is genuinely conscious in the phenomenal sense and there is no subject of experience, then access consciousness and the self must also be theoretical fictions, leaving the illusionist with no consistent way to say that anyone is conscious at all.

How fair is this? In particular, distinguish what is an accurate statement of illusionism, what is a defensible but contested extension, and what is simply wrong—and tell me whether the summary's errors are the kind that make the view look absurd, or whether they are subtle enough that an opponent could plausibly endorse them.
