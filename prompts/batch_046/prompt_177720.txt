Here are my verdicts on a range of assertion cases, and I want to know whether a single principle can accommodate them, or which verdict I should revise. In each scenario I ask only whether the speaker asserts improperly, and I am treating the cases as concrete rather than abstract.

Case 1: At a departmental seminar, a senior biologist states that a particular protein interaction is mediated by allosteric regulation. She has not run the experiments herself; she is reporting the preliminary findings of a postdoc in her lab, whose data she trusts but has not personally inspected. Verdict: permissible. The division of epistemic labor within a recognized research group makes it appropriate for her to speak on the basis of institutional trust, provided the claim falls within the postdoc’s assigned domain.

Case 2: A defense attorney in opening statements tells the jury, “My client was in another city at the time of the crime.” She believes this sincerely, based on her client’s testimony and one corroborating receipt, but she knows the prosecution has contradictory witness accounts and that she lacks conclusive proof. Verdict: permissible, because the assertion functions within a defined adversarial role and is backed by sincere belief tied to that role.

Case 3: A meteorologist broadcasts, “The hurricane will not make landfall in this region,” relying entirely on ensemble model outputs whose internal mathematical structure she does not personally verify. Verdict: permissible, since expert institutional reliance on validated, peer-reviewed models is sufficient.

Case 4: A voter posts on social media, “The election results in my county were manipulated,” citing only a viral video she has not fact-checked and no direct experience or institutional standing. Verdict: improper. There is no adequate evidential basis, no recognized division of labor licensing the claim, and no clear institutional role.

Case 5: A physician tells a patient with an atypical presentation, “The standard first-line therapy is appropriate for you,” citing clinical guidelines developed for the typical patient population. The physician knows the guidelines well but has not evaluated whether the atypical features alter the risk-benefit profile in this specific instance. Verdict: improper. The assertion relies on an epistemic proxy—the guideline—whose domain of applicability does not clearly cover this outlier case, and the speaker lacks direct justification for extending it here.

Case 6: A historian writes in a monograph, “The treaty was signed on March 14, not March 15,” relying on a well-established archival source she has not personally examined in the original manuscript. Verdict: permissible. Scholarly citation practices provide an adequate epistemic basis, and the claim lies squarely within the source’s domain.

My pattern seems to be that proxy or institutional assertions are permissible when the speaker operates within a recognized division of epistemic labor and the proxy applies to the case at hand, but improper either when the proxy is misapplied to an outlier instance or when there is no institutional backing at all. Meanwhile, sincere belief seems necessary but not sufficient—Case 2 and Case 4 both involve belief, yet Case 4 is improper because the belief lacks adequate grounding and institutional framing.

Please provide a precisely stated principle, or a small set of competing candidates, that captures these verdicts. For each clause or condition in your principle, identify the case that motivates it. Then apply the principle to two further cases I have not listed: first, a journalist who asserts that a government agency is concealing data, where she has seen a redacted document that strongly suggests concealment but has not seen the unredacted original, and where her claim falls outside any formal institutional authorization; second, a teacher who tells a student, “You are not prepared for the exam,” based solely on the student’s past performance in unrelated courses, without having assessed the student’s actual mastery of the exam material. Finally, state honestly which of my six verdicts your principle cannot accommodate without revision, and explain whether you think the principle or the verdict should give way.
