In debates over whether historical fiction and biopics owe fidelity to the real dead, I keep encountering the line: "the dead can't be harmed, so there's nothing to owe them." It's usually deployed to shut down objections that a film invented private suffering or distorted a real person's motives for dramatic effect. I'm skeptical, but I can't locate the error—whether the claim is false, ambiguous, or being smuggled into a conclusion it doesn't reach. Could you separate the precise theses this slogan might stand for, distinguishing at least: (a) that a deceased subject lacks the phenomenal or welfare-based capacity to be harmed; (b) that posthumous interests or legacy-harms don't constitute real moral injuries; (c) that obligations to the dead are always reducible to obligations to living relatives or descendants; (d) that misrepresentation harms only the audience's historical understanding, not anyone depicted; and (e) that artistic license overrides any such obligations regardless. For each reading, say whether it is trivial, defensible but substantive, or false; whether it actually licenses the dismissal of objections about invented private suffering; and which version someone using the line to end an argument is most likely invoking. Present the options as a menu I can use to press the point in discussion, rather than picking one authoritative interpretation.
