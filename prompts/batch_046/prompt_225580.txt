Since I began working in manuscript conservation earlier this year, I have been forced to confront questions about object identity that my prior training never resolved in practice. I am preparing a discussion for my restoration team and need a precisely engineered thought experiment—realistic, no longer than a paragraph, free of exotic physics or perfect predictors—to test competing answers to the special composition question. Design a scenario in which a destroyed cultural artifact (for instance, a ceramic vessel or reconstructed manuscript) is rebuilt from its original fragments combined with modern material, holding fixed both the physical arrangement and the functional or ceremonial role it serves afterward. The case should be structured so that both an unrestricted mereological universalist and a restricted-composition theorist who accepts artifacts as genuine composites agree there is exactly one object present after reconstruction, yet they disagree on whether it is identical to the original destroyed item or a numerically distinct entity. At the same time, a temporal-parts theorist must yield a verdict that neither anticipates: specifically, that there is no single enduring object at all, but rather a sequence of overlapping temporal stages lacking a persistent substrate. After presenting the scenario, show the reasoning for each of the three positions, identify the structural feature that forces the temporal-parts view to peel away from the other two, state whether removing that feature collapses the case into a standard Ship of Theseus problem, and explain how a defender of the view that fares worst—whether the one that denies identity or the one that denies a single enduring object—would attack the scenario as begging the question.
