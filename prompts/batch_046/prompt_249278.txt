I am drafting a dissertation prospectus in philosophy of mind, and I need a formal proposal for a single chapter—approximately 8,000 words, deliverable within one semester—on a gap in the causal-exclusion literature that standard surveys do not address. The familiar argument, developed in the physicalist literature on mental causation, proceeds from two premises—causal closure of the physical domain and the supervenience of mental properties on physical ones—to the conclusion that mental properties are systematically excluded from causal efficacy. The well-known responses—embracing overdetermination, denying closure, retreating to epiphenomenalism, or invoking distinct levels of explanation—have been extensively mapped and criticized. What remains unsettled, I believe, is whether the exclusion argument tacitly presupposes a productive or mechanistic conception of causation, according to which causes are competing forces or energy-transferring events, and whether that presupposition can be challenged by reconceiving mental causation in terms of organizational constraints or boundary conditions within hierarchical dynamical systems.

Your proposal should do the following. First, state the precise research question: if causation is understood not as productive mechanism but as constraint-setting or control-parameter regulation, does Kim-style exclusion still follow? Can non-reductive physicalism be rehabilitated by treating mental properties as causally relevant constraints that do not compete with physical producers, without violating the causal closure of physics? Second, explain why the existing positions—systematic overdetermination, emergent downward causation, and epiphenomenalism—fail to resolve this particular issue, specifically because they accept or reject the conclusion without examining the underlying causal model. Third, outline the approach: an analysis of mechanistic versus constraint-based causation in contemporary philosophy of science, followed by a formal reconstruction of the exclusion argument’s premises to test whether they entail exclusion under a non-productive framework. Fourth, specify what a successful result would look like—for instance, demonstrating that mental properties can be causally efficacious as organizational constraints without generating redundant physical explanations—and what failure would look like, such as showing that any constraint-based role either collapses into a physical-level mechanism or violates closure. Fifth, provide a realistic scope assessment: identify what makes the project tractable in one semester (e.g., focusing on conceptual analysis rather than empirical modeling) and flag the precise point at which it risks expanding uncontrollably, particularly if it requires resolving the broader metaphysics of downward causation or constructing a full dynamical systems model of mental-physical interaction. Do not include a bibliography or citation review; refer to positions and arguments conceptually, and indicate that specific references will be integrated during drafting.
