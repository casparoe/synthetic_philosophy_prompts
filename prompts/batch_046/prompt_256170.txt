A common defense of semantic externalism analogizes the mental states of Earthlings and Twin Earthlings to two maps with identical internal geometry but different genealogies: one traced through direct exploration of a territory containing H2O, the other copied in isolation from the first but intended to represent a territory containing XYZ. The analogy is used to license the claim that mental content is not fixed by internal psychological structure alone—since the two maps are internally indistinguishable—but rather by causal-historical contact with the environment, just as a map's accuracy derives from exploration rather than internal coherence.

Please lay out the correspondence table explicitly: what plays the role of the mental state, the process of production, the territory, and the library copy? Then identify the disanalogies that matter for the argument: does the analogy smuggle in a representationalist picture of mental content that treats thoughts as descriptive artifacts rather than, say, direct relations or functional capacities? How does it handle indexicals and social deference in reference-fixing—phenomena that maps do not exhibit? And if mental states are not representational maps at all but operate more like rigid designators or practical abilities, does the conclusion that internal identity fails to determine semantic content survive, or does the breakdown of the analogy undermine the externalist case? Finally, suggest whether an analogy drawn from biological function or naming practices would avoid these representationalist commitments while preserving the core insight.
