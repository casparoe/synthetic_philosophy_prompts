I'm advising a small interdisciplinary research group—philosophers of social science and historical sociologists, with roughly four years of funding—on the problem of whether culture can be an autonomous cause of large-scale institutional change, using Weber's Protestant ethic thesis as the central case but treating it as one instance of a broader explanatory puzzle. Rather than a survey, I want a defended prioritization: which two or three open questions in this space are actually worth the group's collective time, and in what order should they be tackled?

I see at least three competing lines of work. First, the mechanistic reconstruction problem: whether Weber's appeal to an ascetic value-orientation can be translated into a counterfactual causal model linking specific practices to capital-accumulating behavior without reducing those values to an epiphenomenon of material conditions. Second, what might be called the exclusion problem for collective representations: if macro-level economic structures already explain much variance in development, do cultural values get causally excluded in something like the way mental properties are sometimes argued to be excluded by physical properties? Third, the normative-policy downstream: if values do possess autonomous causal power, does that justify intentional cultural engineering—say, institutional design aimed at fostering particular value orientations—or does the historical specificity of Weber's case block any such generalization?

Please rank these, or substitute better ones, and defend the ordering by explicit criteria—tractability with current philosophical and qualitative-historical methods, philosophical payoff, importance for actual social-scientific practice, and dependency relations (which answers, if secured, would unlock or dissolve the others). Be explicit about trade-offs: for instance, does resolving the mechanistic problem first make the exclusion problem moot, or do they remain independent? I also want you to engage, without treating it as settled, the possibility that values function not as individual mental states but as collective commitment devices that stabilize cross-group expectations; if that social-ontological framework is right, Weber's unit of analysis might be mislocated at the individual ascetic level. How should that possibility reshape the agenda?

Finally, say what would change your mind about the ranking—what specific finding or argument would cause you to reorder the priorities—and flag the point in your reasoning that is most likely to fail, especially given that the group has philosophers rather than quantitative economic historians.
