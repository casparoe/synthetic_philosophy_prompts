Ever since I retired from biophysics and started actually reading philosophy of science instead of just running gels, I've gotten weirdly obsessed with that old Needham question — why Britain and not China? — and the way people argue about it. Last week I was talking to a friend over coffee and we kept hitting the same wall, and now I'm wondering if there's already a name for it.

The pattern is this: someone picks a factor — coal geography, property rights, whatever — and says Britain had it in the right form, China didn't, so that's the divergence. Then someone else points out that parts of China had coal too, or had legal structures that look pretty similar, so the factor can't be decisive. At that point the first person doesn't drop the factor; they just shrink or stretch its definition. In Britain it counts as "the real thing" because the outcome happened; in China it's "superficially present but functionaly blocked." The same feature is treated as genuinely shared when you want to deny sufficiency, then uniquely configured when you want to defend necessity. I see this with coal, with scientific societies, even with cultural attitudes — the boundary of the cause gets redrawn to fit the result.

Is there an established concept for this exact move in contrastive historical explanation? Not just post-hoc rationalization or confirmation bias, but the specific shell-game where a prerequisite's edges are adjusted so it can be both present in the failure-case (thus not explaining the gap) and exclusively present in the success-case (thus explaining it)? If philosophers of history have named it, I'd love to know; if not, I'd like a precise coinage that separates it from ordinary ad hoc reasoning and from plain equivocation. Also, does framing the question as "why Britain not China?" basically guarantee this trap, or could you avoid it by asking "why did industrialization cluster in north-west Europe at all?" I'm not asking for a biblography — just whether I'm seeing a named failure mode or inventing one.
