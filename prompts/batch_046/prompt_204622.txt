As a journalist who covers science and ideas, I have been invited to deliver the opening remarks at an interdisciplinary workshop—hosted jointly by a museum of modern art and a cognitive science institute—titled Strange Loops and Their Limits. The audience is divided: about half are researchers working on hierarchical predictive processing and embodied cognition who are well-versed in discussions of tangled hierarchies; the other half are museum patrons, artists, and students who came to see the Escher prints in the adjacent gallery but have no background in either neuroscience or formal philosophy. I need approximately seven minutes. I want the remarks structured around the concept of the inviolate level—the idea that any genuinely tangled hierarchy requires a ground floor that refuses to be folded back into the loop, and that this refusal is not a failure of the system but the very condition that makes self-reference visible rather than collapsing into undifferentiated noise. This should do real structural work: I want to explain why eliminating that ground floor dissolves both the artistic effect and the scientific model, and I want to close with a precise observation about Escher's Drawing Hands—specifically, that the hands appear to draw each other in an infinite regress, yet each is anchored by the inviolate physical texture of the paper itself, which the image simultaneously conceals and reveals as its hidden ground. The speech should welcome both halves of the room without lecturing either, should make the philosophical point shape the occasion rather than decorate it, and should avoid any request for citations, bibliographies, or further reading, since I want the audience to leave with a single portable idea rather than a syllabus. Please write the remarks in a tone that is formal but not academic, that respects the intelligence of the non-specialists without simplifying away the difficulty for the researchers, and that treats the workshop as an occasion genuinely shaped by the logic of levels rather than as a generic welcome with philosophical ornament attached.
