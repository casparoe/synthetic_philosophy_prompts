The exam question asked students to analyse the standard switch case with a twist: pulling the lever saves five people, but there's a faulty circuit that creates a 20% chance of killing an unrelated bystander on a nearby bridge. They needed to apply the four conditions of double effect and judge whether pulling the lever is permissable.

Student answer:
"The doctrine of double effect, as developed in the Thomistic tradition, requires four conditions: the act itself must be morally neutral or good; the good effect must be intended; the bad effect must be foreseen but not intended; and there must be a proportionate reason for allowing the bad effect. In the standard switch case, pulling the lever is neutral, saving five is intended, killing one is foreseen but not intended, and the good clearly outweighs the bad.

Applying this to the modified case: the potential death of the bystander is not intended, and it is not the means by which the five are saved, since the diversion works through the lever mechanism independantly of the bridge circuit. Because the harm is only probable, the expected bad effect is 0.2 lives lost, while we save five lives. Therefore proportionality is easily satisfied. The probabilistic nature doesn't alter the moral analysis, since double effect is about the distinction between intended and foreseen outcomes, not about certainty."
