Consider our department’s historiography review board, which judges whether proposed explanations for major historical transformations adequately respect the distinction between slow-moving geographical, climatic, and institutional structures and the actions of discrete events or individuals. Our track record is as follows. First, on the decline of the Hanseatic League between the mid-sixteenth and late seventeenth centuries: we rejected a proposal that attributed the decline mainly to the diplomatic misjudgments of particular Lübeck burgomasters, and we endorsed instead an explanation centered on the long-term silting of Baltic estuaries and the reorientation of European trade toward Atlantic ports—allowing that the closure of the Novgorod Kontor served as an occasion rather than a root cause. Second, on the Classic Maya collapse around 800–900 CE: we rejected an explanation relying solely on multi-decadal drought reconstructed from lake-sediment records, and we endorsed an explanation that treated those climate structures and the breakdown of specific inter-city dynastic alliances as jointly necessary and neither reducible to the other. Third, on the 1853 opening of Tokugawa Japan: we endorsed an explanation emphasizing the structural fragility of the rice-tax economy and the global diffusion of coal-steam navigation, but we insisted that Commodore Perry’s arrival and the specific treaty negotiations not be treated as causally inert—rather, they selected among outcomes that the structures had made possible.

We now face a new proposal. It explains the rapid adoption of the Gregorian calendar across Catholic Europe in October 1582, and its delayed adoption in Protestant and Orthodox territories for more than a century thereafter, almost entirely through Pope Gregory XIII’s personal authority, the organizational reach of the Jesuit order, and the diplomatic campaigns of 1580–1582. In this account, the accumulated astronomical drift of the Julian calendar and the spread of print culture that standardized local timekeeping are mentioned only as incidental background conditions, not as explanatory drivers.

If we wish to remain consistent with our own precedents, what should our verdict be? Identify the closest analog among our three prior cases, specify the candidate governing rules that our track record could support—for instance, rules about whether structures must be central drivers, whether events may serve merely as selectors within structural ranges, or whether both must carry substantial independent weight—and state what each rule predicts about this proposal. Give your best-guess recommendation with your reasons for preferring the rule you invoke. If our precedents genuinely underdetermine the case so that more than one rule is compatible with our judgments, state honestly what principle we would be adopting with each possible decision, and name a further hypothetical case (involving calendar reform, climate-driven institutional change, or technological diffusion) that would force us to choose between those rules.
