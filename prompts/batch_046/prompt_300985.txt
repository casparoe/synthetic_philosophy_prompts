A philosopher whose interpretive rulings I have catalogued over the past year consistently addresses one question: when private biographical or political material comes to light, does it bear on the interpretation of that thinker’s published philosophical work, or should it be bracketed? Her track record so far is these five verdicts. First, Heidegger’s Black Notebooks: she holds that they are philosophically relevant, because the same conceptual vocabulary—rootedness, being-historical, the critique of modern subjectivity—runs through both the notebooks and the published ontology, so the political embedding is not external to the philosophy. Second, Hume’s infamous footnote in “Of National Characters” advancing a racial hierarchy: she holds that this does not bear on his epistemology or metaphysics; it is a localized moral failure, thematically distant from his theory of ideas or causation, and it explains no specific feature of those arguments. Third, Rousseau’s abandonment of his children to a foundling hospital: she rules that this bears on interpreting Emile and his educational theory—there is a direct tension with the practice of natural development he prescribes—but that it carries no weight for his social-contract theory, where the subject matter is different. Fourth, Kant’s private letters containing racist remarks about non-Europeans: she rules that they do not affect the interpretation of the formal structure of his moral philosophy; they reveal hypocrisy but do not illuminate or undermine the architecture of the categorical imperative or universalizability. Fifth, Wittgenstein’s wartime trauma and documented personal struggles: she holds that these illuminate the existential urgency behind the Tractatus’s remarks on ethics, value, and the mystical, even though they do not determine its logical structure.

The case now before her is Gottlob Frege’s private political diary—the later “Tagebuch” entries, discovered after his death, containing virulent antisemitic, nationalist, and anti-democratic sentiments—specifically whether this material bears on interpreting his philosophy of logic, language, and mathematics. Predict her verdict, and defend it by identifying the implicit rule that best explains all five prior rulings. Then articulate at least one other rule that fits those same five verdicts equally well but diverges on Frege’s diary, stating clearly what that alternative rule predicts in this case. Finally, propose one concrete further case—specifying the thinker, the biographical or political material, and the philosophical domain in question—that would adjudicate between your preferred rule and the rival, explaining what each rule would predict about that case.
