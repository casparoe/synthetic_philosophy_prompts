Dr. Sarah Lin, a philosopher of psychiatry, judges whether a diagnostic category is an “interactive kind” in Hacking’s sense—that being classified changes the properties of the classified in a feedback loop. Her track record: she holds that schizophrenia is interactive, because diagnosis reshapes how patients experience and narrate symptoms; that autism (specifically the high-functioning / Asperger profile) is strongly interactive, since the label has generated a self-conscious neurodivergent identity that feeds back into behavior and advocacy; that dissociative identity disorder is the paradigmatic case, with clinical framing and narrative interaction apparently shaping multiplicity itself; and that both major depressive disorder and generalized anxiety disorder are not interactive, functioning instead as descriptions of endogenous or reactive states that the label does not restructure. Now a working group proposes a new adolescent category, “algorithmic attention dysregulation” (AAD): fragmented sustained attention and compulsive scroll-checking attributed primarily to recommendation-engine design rather than to individual neurobiology, with the explicit aim that diagnosis will help patients reframe their behavior as externally caused. Predict Lin’s verdict on whether AAD exhibits looping effects. Then identify at least two distinct rules that fit all five of her earlier judgments equally well, say what each rule predicts for AAD, argue for the best-guess verdict with reasons, and specify one further hypothetical diagnosis that would tell the two rules apart.
