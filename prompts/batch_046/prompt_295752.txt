I am working on a philosophy of mathematics essay for an independent study, and I have become stuck on a conceptual problem surrounding Skolem's paradox. I understand the standard technical resolution: if ZFC has a model, the Löwenheim-Skolem theorem yields a countable model in which there are sets the model itself treats as uncountable, because the bijection that would witness countability lies outside the model. But I want to move past that resolution and examine what the paradox implies for the objectivity of set-theoretic infinity.

Here is my specific concern. If every model of set theory that we can actually construct or describe within our meta-language turns out to be countable, then in what sense can we claim that there genuinely exist uncountable sets, as opposed to sets that merely behave in an uncountable fashion inside a particular formal system? Does this mean that cardinality is not an intrinsic property of a set at all, but rather a relational property that depends on the expressive richness of the model that contains it? And if cardinality is relational in this way, does that undermine realist claims about absolute infinity?

To press the point further, I want to consider whether a realist could defend a notion of absolute cardinality—something like a model-independent fact about whether a given collection is truly uncountable—or whether that notion inevitably collapses back into the same metatheoretic difficulty. Please explain the conceptual tension here. In particular, address whether Skolem's paradox genuinely threatens the objectivity of mathematical claims about uncountable sets, or whether it only shows that our epistemic access to such sets is necessarily mediated by the limitations of the models we can specify. I am especially interested in whether injecting this idea of absolute cardinality resolves the paradox or merely relocates it.
