In several jurisdictions, clinical protocols permit a patient who meets whole-brain death criteria to be declared dead and to become an organ donor, yet the same patient is not recognised as legally dead for inheritance, marital dissolution, or homicide law unless circulatory cessation also occurs; families may sometimes invoke religious objection to block brain-death certification without being able to block circulatory-death certification. What philosophical conception of death—unitary biological, personal, legal-conventional, or plural—does this bifurcated institutional practice presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory: does the split strictly require that death is institutionally relative rather than a single natural event, or can a unified biological view accommodate it through secondary conventions? Assess whether the practice is philosophically coherent or merely administratively convenient, distinguish what it strictly entails from what merely fits it well, and describe what would have to change in clinical protocol, legal status, and the moral treatment of the body during the interval between brain failure and cardiac arrest if the jurisdiction replaced this dual-track system with a single, uniform standard.
