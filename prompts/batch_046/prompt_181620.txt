I'm studying debates about rescue obligations and distributive ethics, and I've noticed a recurring argumentative pattern that I can't place precisely within the standard taxonomy. A speaker will insist that in choosing between saving one person and saving five—where all are actual, identifiable, and equally threatened—we shouldn't treat the five as automatically outweighing the one, often invoking something in the spirit of Taurek's claim that the numbers shouldn't count. But then, when pressed on whether they would really be indifferent between saving one and saving a hundred, they retreat to saying that of course more lives matter somewhat, but "not proportionally," or that there's a "threshold" after which additional lives don't increase the duty, without specifying whether this non-linearity comes from a lexical constraint, diminishing moral weight per life saved, or a cap on aggregation itself. The move seems distinct from standard person-affecting views, since those typically deny that merely possible people generate obligations, whereas here everyone involved is actual and equally in peril. It also differs from ordinary prioritarianism, since the speaker isn't claiming that benefiting worse-off people matters more—only that the sum of benefits doesn't scale in the way aggregation demands. Is there an established term for this particular hybrid position—anti-aggregative in rhetoric but non-committal on mechanism, applied specifically to rescue contexts where all affected parties exist? If not, could one define it precisely, distinguishing it from Taurek's strict non-counting view, from Scanlon-style contractualist limits on aggregation, and from simple risk-aversion about large-scale rescue? I won't be satisfied with a bare label; I want the carving to cover why the speaker treats small-number comparisons differently from large-number comparisons without appealing to either diminishing returns or a rights-based veto, and what boundary cases would show the position to be unstable.
