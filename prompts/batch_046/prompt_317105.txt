For instance, if a historian organizes records of seventeenth-century healing practices using modern neuropsychiatric classifications not to condemn but merely to classify, has she committed an unavoidable form of presentism? Or is a practice of "reconstructive translation" genuinely available—one that renders past concepts in terms preserving their internal coherence without forcing them into modern frameworks?

Please write a dialogue between two equally capable historians—Dr. Elena Vasquez and Dr. Marcus Chen—who disagree sharply on this point. Vasquez should argue that all historiography necessarily involves some structural presentism, since any act of description requires conceptual tools available to the historian, and that the proper methodological goal is therefore transparency about those tools rather than their impossible elimination. Chen should defend the viability of reconstructive translation, maintaining that historians can render past conceptual schemes in their own terms well enough to avoid projection, and that treating presentism as inevitable risks collapsing historical understanding into autobiography. They should respond directly to one another's actual arguments: Vasquez ought to challenge Chen's claim that translation can ever be conceptually neutral, and Chen ought to answer not by dismissing her worry but by showing how her transparency model still tacitly privileges present categories. The exchange should be rigorous, with neither character reduced to a strawman.

In your response, please clearly distinguish what is broadly settled in historiographical theory from what remains genuinely contested among specialists, and do not present any contested claim—such as the inevitability of structural presentism or the feasibility of fully reconstructive translation—as though it were the consensus view.
