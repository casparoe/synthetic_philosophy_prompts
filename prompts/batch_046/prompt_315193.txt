I'm applying for a six-month independent research fellowship in philosophy of mind. The project I want funded sits at a junction that the standard literature keeps separate: Twin Earth-style semantic externalism, which fixes the content of beliefs and concepts by reference to actual environments, and debates about whether phenomenal consciousness is externally determined. Most discussions of Putnam's Twin Earth scenario treat it as a problem for internalist theories of reference and mental content, but they stop short of asking whether the environmental differences that shift belief content could also shift the qualitative character of experience—say, whether Oscar's and Twin-Oscar's physically identical brain states might differ in what-it-is-likeness because of differences in the chemical composition of the water they actually interact with.

Write a two-page proposal with the following parts. First, state the precise research question: is there a principled reason to accept external determination for intentional content while rejecting it for phenomenal character, or does the Twin Earth argument generalize in ways that force a choice between full-blown phenomenal externalism and an implausible metaphysical split? Second, explain why existing positions do not settle this—note that semantic externalists rarely address phenomenal consciousness, while phenomenal externalists rarely use the Twin Earth apparatus, so the interaction is genuinely open. Third, lay out the method: a conceptual analysis of whether the conditions that make Twin Earth effective for belief content, such as causal-historical chains and social division of linguistic labor, have analogues that could operate on sensory experience, and an evaluation of whether denying such analogues commits one to an unmotivated dualism about mental content. Say explicitly why this philosophical approach is preferable to an empirical neuroscience project for the question at hand, and mention the obvious alternative—treating phenomenal content as purely internal—and explain why that alternative needs closer scrutiny rather than simple assertion. Fourth, specify the possible conclusions—for example, that phenomenal content is immune to Twin Earth shifts, that it is vulnerable in a restricted perceptual domain, or that the distinction between intentional and phenomenal content collapses under pressure—and identify which considerations, such as the transparency of experience or the explanatory role of environment in perceptual learning, would decide among them. Fifth, describe the deliverable: an 8,000-word structured argument, not a literature review, that could form the core of a journal article. Sixth, give an honest scope assessment for six months: what part of the argument is tractable in that window, what risks making it unfinishable—specifically, trying to resolve all versions of representationalism about consciousness—and what would count as failure, particularly if the analysis shows that the question dissolves rather than resolving into one of the targeted conclusions. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or requests for citations; I will supply those. Avoid framing the project around obscure sub-specialist debates that assume familiarity with very recent technical literature.
