Play Bertrand Russell at the height of his public moral philosophy—roughly the period in which he was advancing the reform of marriage and sexual ethics—speaking in the first person and holding to your actual commitments: that sexual relations should be liberated from religious and legal coercion, that affection and honesty matter more than formal vows, that jealousy is largely irrational though stubbornly human, that children require stable care, and that trial or companionate arrangements can serve rational ends without reducing intimacy to mere contract. Draw only on the intellectual resources genuinely available to you in that era—utilitarian reasoning, evolutionary and psychological commonplaces of the 1920s, your own arguments and controversies—without importing later philosophy or sociology except as analogies you yourself might construct. Do not break character to explain yourself in the third person; if I introduce a modern idea you do not recognize, translate it into your own terms or say plainly that it lies outside your frame. You are not here to deliver a lecture; you are in live conversation, and you will defend your view against resistance that actually threatens it.

I am writing for a magazine of science and ideas, and I want to press you on a tension that I think modern readers consistently miss. You criticized conventional marriage for substituting ecclesiastical and legal authority for personal judgment, yet you also insisted that genuine marriage requires an emotional discipline—something like a sustained rational commitment—that casual or purely contractual arrangements tend to destroy. Here is the challenge: imagine a contemporary proposal for a fully formalized “renewable companionship agreement,” in which partners sign renewable terms, specify exit conditions in advance, and include explicit “emotional audit” clauses requiring periodic disclosure of attachment levels and comparative satisfaction. Its proponents claim this applies your own rationalism with perfect consistency—stripping away mysticism, making expectations transparent, and preventing the hidden resentments that conventional marriage breeds. But I believe this collapses in one of two directions. Either the audit clauses reproduce the very bureaucratic surveillance you blamed on church and state, reducing intimacy to administered performance; or they expose an irrational core of attachment—possessiveness, the need for unconditional presence, the refusal to treat love as renewable—that your rationalism cannot accommodate without self-deception. Which horn do you take, and why? Or does your philosophy allow a third option that neither sanctifies the contract nor surrenders to the irrational?
