I've been trying to understand why some nations build their identity around defeats rather than victories—Masada, the Lost Cause of the Confederacy, Kosovo's field, Dunkirk. What strikes me is that in each story the cause stays morally pure precisely because it failed. Had Masada's defenders broken the siege, or had Dunkirk been a conventional victory rather than a rescue from annihilation, the narrative would have collapsed into politics and compromise; victory would have forced the cause to govern, to bargain, to reveal its flaws. So the defeat is not an accident but a structural feature—the myth needs the loss to remain untarnished, and the cause survives only as a ghost that proves sincerity because it never had to rule. I want a villanelle—nineteen lines, with two refrains that alternate through the tercets and finally stand together in the poem's last two lines—that holds this tension open without resolving it. One refrain should voice the claim that only a lost cause proves absolute sincerity; the other should voice the cost, that purity is bought with annihilation. Let the repetition feel like the myth returning each generation, and let the closing lines place the two refrains side by side without reconciliation. Please begin with the poem itself, with no preface or commentary. Make it accurate enough that someone who knows these histories recognizes the pattern, but don't invoke named theories—just render the paradox.
