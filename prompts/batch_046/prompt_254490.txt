A friend and I argued for hours about Kierkegaard’s essay on the present age and his attack on the press. I claimed his objection to newspapers is essentially epistemic: by saturating readers with disconnected reports they erode the capacity for sustained, committed judgment, so the remedy would be disciplined attention or slow media. My friend said that misses the structural point entirely—Kierkegaard does not care about information density but about the press fabricating an abstract, disembodied public that lets individuals hold opinions without ever being personally present or accountable. I objected that if the public is only a sociological fiction with no direct presence, then Kierkegaard’s anxiety about it is a category mistake, attributing causal power to a phantom. She answered that the public is precisely a metaphysical phantom that acquires reality only through individuals’ withdrawal from direct relations, and the press is the machinery of that withdrawal. I then suggested this amounts to blaming technology for a failure of moral courage, since newspapers merely supply the anonymity people already crave. She replied that this confuses cause with condition: Kierkegaard is not doing technological determinism, but showing how the form of publicity transforms ethical commitment itself, turning the act of willing something into the passive state of having an opinion about it. We are stuck. Please adjudicate our exchange move by move—rate each step from clearly mistaken to clearly right, say where each of us is right or wrong, and identify what we both overlook, especially whether the leveling he describes is a democratic-egalitarian phenomenon or a specifically modern, reflexive one.
