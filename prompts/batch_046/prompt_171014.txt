Two senior colleagues in my department, Dr. Aris and Dr. Bell, have been debating the case of a mutual acquaintance: a sculptor who spends every winter constructing large, intricate ice structures in a vacant lot. Each piece is engineered with embedded thermal catalysts so that its collapse is abrupt and total within days. The sculptor claims that orchestrating this rapid dissolution gives him a lucid partnership with impermanence, and he treats the practice as the central source of meaning in his life. He also acknowledges that the collapses occasionally scatter debris onto an adjacent path, though he cleans it promptly and offers to cover any damage.

(1) Dr. Aris argues that this is a sophisticated form of absurdist heroism: the sculptor recognizes that no enduring value can be secured, yet continues to create with full awareness, even accelerating the futility. She contends that contemporary hybrid accounts of meaning—those that require both subjective fulfillment and some objective worth—can accommodate this, because designing for inevitable failure involves genuine aesthetic and intellectual achievement, and the subjective fulfillment is not mere comfort but active affirmation.

(2) Dr. Bell responds that this misreads the hybrid requirement. He holds that on any credible objective-list component, value must inhere in the activity or its products, not solely in the agent's attitude toward them. Because the sculptor actively installs the mechanism of destruction, the aesthetic achievement is negated rather than realized; the work survives only as a brief spectacle of failure, and its only enduring trace is private. He further argues that framing this as lucidity is rationalization: if the sculptor genuinely valued the aesthetic object, he would not deliberately engineer its immediate ruin. The collateral damage to neighbors, even if repaired, reveals that the practice imposes external costs for a private symbolic ritual.

(3) Dr. Aris counters that preservation is not necessary for aesthetic value—ephemeral art is a recognized category—and that the collapse is the intended final movement of the work, analogous to a performance that ends in silence. The cleanup and compensation address the moral side-effect, leaving the philosophical structure intact. She suggests that Bell smuggles in an unstated preference for permanence that absurdism precisely rejects.

(4) Dr. Bell concedes that ephemeral art exists, but distinguishes works that fade naturally from those actively sabotaged by their creator. He argues that in the latter case, the agent's intention undermines the objective conditions for valuing the work: one cannot genuinely value an aesthetic object while deliberately installing the mechanism of its immediate ruin, unless the value is relocated entirely into the private experience of destruction, which collapses the hybrid view back into pure subjectivism.

(5) Dr. Aris replies that Bell's distinction between natural fading and active sabotage is arbitrary from an absurdist standpoint, since both result in impermanence; the only difference is the agent's transparency about her role in it. She further suggests that Bell's demand for non-self-undermining objective value presupposes a teleological framework—where activities must be oriented toward preservation or growth—that the absurdist explicitly denies.

Please adjudicate this disagreement move by move. For each of the five exchanges above, rate the strength of the argument from clearly mistaken to clearly correct, and briefly justify your rating. Then state who, if anyone, is right about the core issue—whether this life can be genuinely meaningful under an absurdist or hybrid account—and identify what both disputants are missing, particularly regarding the relationship between self-undermining projects and the conditions for objective value.
