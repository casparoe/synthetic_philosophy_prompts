I'm a journalist covering science and ideas, and I've become personally entangled in a philosophical problem I'm encountering in my reporting. A close colleague—someone whose analytical rigor I trust—has been studying Schrödinger's essays interpreting Advaita Vedanta, particularly his claim that the apparent plurality of conscious subjects is comparable to the way a single substance can present multiple apparent divisions—what Vedanta calls Maya—without being genuinely plural. She has begun applying this not as abstract mysticism but as a practical framework: she argues that if consciousness is genuinely singular, then the distinction between self-interested and altruistic action collapses, because harming another is structurally identical to harming oneself. At the same time, she worries that this dissolves the moral significance of individual achievement; she recently declined co-authorship on a paper she contributed to, saying that attributing the work to her separate self was a kind of metaphysical error rather than a legitimate claim of desert. I want to understand whether her practical conclusions actually follow from Schrödinger's unity thesis, or whether there is a coherent way to accept the metaphysical claim about consciousness without abandoning the ethical and institutional importance of individual agency, responsibility, and distributive justice. Can you help me see the philosophical structure here—specifically whether the move from ontological unity to ethical or distributive conclusions is valid, and what alternative interpretations of the Vedanta framework might preserve both the unity of consciousness and the practical necessity of treating people as distinct centers of accountability and merit?
