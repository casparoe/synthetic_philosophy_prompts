I am a long-standing member of a community-managed botanical garden that operates without any director, board, or formal voting procedure; instead, decisions about which beds to restore, which species to introduce, and how to allocate our modest irrigation budget emerge gradually from hundreds of informal conversations among volunteers. Over the past two decades, an elderly volunteer named Hillary—who never issues instructions—has become something like the garden's distributed institutional memory: her casual observations about soil exhaustion or past droughts seem to steer collective behaviour in directions that no single person has explicitly chosen. Recently, a new member argued that this arrangement proves the garden itself possesses something like a mind. He pointed out that if one tracks the pattern of plantings over the last three years, they form a coherent strategy—restoring native meadow grasses—that no individual volunteer consciously endorsed, not even Hillary, who insists she merely mentions facts and lets others decide. He then compared our garden to the ant colony Aunt Hillary in Douglas Hofstadter's Ant Fugue, suggesting that just as that colony exhibits unified, intelligent behaviour without any ant being aware of the whole, our garden might be a genuine cognitive subject whose beliefs and intentions are realised at the collective level rather than inside any single head. Another volunteer, who has read the same dialogue, rejected this entirely: she said the comparison is purely metaphorical, because in both cases every apparent decision reduces without remainder to local interactions—individual ants following pheromone gradients, individual gardeners reacting to Hillary's remarks and to one another's actions. She added that calling the garden a mind is no more justified than saying a thermostat has beliefs about temperature.

I find myself genuinely unsure how to adjudicate this. The situation is not merely practical—how we should organise planting—but conceptual: if there is a coherent, directed pattern that is not reducible to any individual's intentions, yet is fully causally explained by their interactions, is there a philosophically respectable sense in which the collective itself holds beliefs or intentions? Or does the presence of a complete mechanistic explanation at the individual level necessarily dissolve any claim to collective mentality? I would be grateful for advice on how to think through this, particularly on whether the ant-colony analogy supports the idea of an emergent mind or instead undermines it. Please respond in British English.
