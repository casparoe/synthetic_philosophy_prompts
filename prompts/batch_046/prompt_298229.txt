I am preparing a forty-five-minute seminar for first-year doctoral students in behavioral science who have just encountered twin-study reports stating that heritability for a particular trait is around 0.70, and who have consequently formed a cluster of related confusions: they treat heritability as a causal share belonging to individuals, they assume a high coefficient implies that environmental interventions must be futile, and they are already sliding toward the inference that between-group differences must therefore be largely genetic. I want a teaching plan—not the lecture itself, but the architectural plan for the explanation—specifying the exact order in which the conceptual pieces must be introduced so that each depends on the one before it, and explaining why reversing that order collapses the distinction I am trying to build.

For the carrying example, do not use the standard height-and-nutrition-in-the-Netherlands analogy, which these students have likely already seen in textbooks. Instead, construct the explanation around a deliberately synthetic scenario: first, a population of genetically identical clones distributed randomly across apartments that vary only in ambient noise exposure, measuring a trait like sleep quality; then, the same trait measured in a second population with full genetic variation but raised in perfectly uniform apartments. The example must be designed so that students can see heritability near zero in the first case and near one in the second—not because the biology changed, but because the variance structure changed—and it must clearly lack any feature that would invite them to read the coefficient as a property of the trait itself rather than of the population-in-environment combination.

Map the misconceptions in the sequence they will actually arise: (1) “this means 70 percent of my outcome is genetic” (the individual-level fallacy), which should be defused immediately after variance decomposition is introduced but before any coefficient is interpreted; (2) “if heritability is high, the environment cannot matter” (the immutability fallacy), which should be addressed right after the clone-apartment example by showing that altering the uniform environment in population two changes the coefficient; (3) “high within-group heritability explains between-group differences” (the ecological fallacy), which should be headed off only after the population-relativity point is secure, using the two-population contrast; and (4) “heritability is a fixed biological constant,” which should be dissolved last by asking what happens to the coefficient if the clone apartments are replaced with a wider range of noise levels. For each, specify the precise moment in the sequence to intervene and the one-sentence correction that lands it. Include a check for understanding that would distinguish a student who genuinely grasps that heritability is an environment-relative population statistic from one who has merely learned to repeat the phrase “it depends on the environment.” Finally, state explicitly what this session should refuse to explain—such as ACE model identification assumptions, molecular genetic mechanisms, or polygenic score interpretation—and say so outright, so that I do not drift into territory that requires technical machinery these students do not yet have.
