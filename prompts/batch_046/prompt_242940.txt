I recently began working in a civil-service archive and discovered that the filing protocols and decision hierarchies through which a past regime administered systemic injustice remain structurally intact, even though every individual who used them is dead. This made Jaspers's problem of collective guilt suddenly personal: if I now occupy one of those institutional roles, does some moral burden travel with the office rather than with the person? In discussions with colleagues—historians, lawyers, and philosophers—the debate stalls in the same way every time: one side asserts that guilt requires individual consciousness and is therefore non-transferable, the other asserts that a nation or institution carries an enduring stain, and neither has moved in months. I suspect the deadlock is not empirical but conceptual—perhaps the sides use 'guilt' for criminal culpability, for metaphysical burden transmitted through institutional continuity, and for distributed political liability, so that they answer different questions under one heading—yet I want a real diagnosis, not a verdict. Using what each side actually says about inheritance, bureaucratic continuity, and the conditions for moral responsibility, is this disagreement verbal, substantive, or rooted in bedrock intuitions about whether structures can bear moral properties? And if your diagnosis is correct, what conceptual distinction, reframed question, or clarification—rather than more historical evidence—would count as genuine progress?
