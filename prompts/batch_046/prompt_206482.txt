Having recently served on a municipal climate-adaptation board, I have come to think that Scanlon's contractualist rejection of aggregation is being misapplied to policy contexts in which harms are distributed yet structurally linked. Next month I defend this view at an interdisciplinary workshop: climate-policy analysts accustomed to cost-benefit aggregation sit alongside moral philosophers for whom Scanlon's non-aggregationist stance is nearly axiomatic. My thesis is that contractualism prohibits only what I term anonymous statistical aggregation—summing probabilistic harms across individuals who share no relational grievance—while permitting grievance-based constituency aggregation, in which a collective harm generates a shared reason to reject a principle without collapsing into utilitarian summation. I sketch this through Scanlon's distinction between individual complaint and principle justification, though I have not fully secured the boundary against rule-utilitarianism.
