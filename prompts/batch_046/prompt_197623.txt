I'm writing this after a seminar discussion in my comparative historical archaeology course, where we spent the week asking whether "collapse" is a useful analytical category or merely a modern narrative imposed on past societies that were actually transforming in ways we misread as failure. Two of my classmates—I'll call them Mara and Leo—got into a sustained disagreement over lunch that struck me as more precise than the seminar itself, and I want a direct adjudication rather than a balanced survey of positions.

Mara opened by arguing that labeling the Late Bronze Age Mediterranean and the Classic Maya lowlands as "collapses" commits an etic fallacy: we observe the disappearance of palace economies, long-distance trade, and literate administration, but for the people living through these shifts they were not failures—just reorganizations into smaller-scale, more localized societies that may have been less extractive. She pointed specifically to Easter Island, noting that while the Rapa Nui statue-building tradition ended and deforestation was severe, the population did not vanish; they adapted agricultural practices and maintained social cohesion, so calling it a "collapse" reveals our own bias toward monumental statecraft rather than an objective societal failure. Leo replied that this confuses normative evaluation with descriptive analysis: collapse need not mean total extinction or universal misery, but rather a rapid, multi-generational loss of sociopolitical complexity—measured in settlement hierarchy, interregional connectivity, and institutional continuity—relative to the society's own prior baseline. He insisted that the Maya case is not merely "change" when we see the abandonment of dynastic courts, the cessation of monument dedication, and demographic contraction in the southern lowlands within a few generations; similarly, Easter Island experienced a sharp population decline and the irreversible loss of a specialized ritual economy, which is not the same as a smooth transition. Mara then countered that Leo's "complexity metrics" are themselves value-laden—why should settlement hierarchy matter more than subsistence security?—and that without a principled criterion for how rapid or severe a change must be to count as collapse, the category becomes a rhetorical device for labeling any transformation we find aesthetically disappointing. Leo's final move was that even if we purge moral language, there remains a quantitative discontinuity in institutional memory and coordination capacity that distinguishes the Bronze Age dissolution from ordinary evolutionary change; the burden, he said, is on the transformation theorists to explain why simultaneous, widespread decentralization across independent polities should be treated as coincidental local adjustments rather than a systemic breakdown.
