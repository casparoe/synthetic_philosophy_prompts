As a retired condensed-matter physicist who has only recently begun reading philosophy of mind in earnest, I have tried to organize my intuitions about consciousness into a coherent package, but I fear there may be hidden fractures. I would like an audit of the following commitments.

First, I am an ontological physicalist: every mental event is realized by a physical event, and there are no non-physical mental properties. Second, I regard the zombie thought experiment as imaginatively coherent but metaphysically inert; my scientific training leads me to treat conceivability as a poor guide to possibility, especially for properties to which we have no independent causal access. Third, I am persuaded by the argument, associated with Chase and Sanborn, that the notion of a pure qualitative character—an intrinsic, non-relational, subjective feel—is incoherent: if a mental state lacks all structural and relational properties, I do not see how it could satisfy conditions of individuation, enter law-like generalizations, or be composed into complex experiences. Fourth, despite this, I find myself unable to deny that there is something it is like to be a bat, or at least that bat consciousness is not excluded by physicalism alone; I interpret this not as evidence for bare qualia, but as indicating that phenomenal experience possesses a structural, relational character that introspection presents in a misleadingly intrinsic guise. Fifth, I hold that phenomenal concepts are special only in their mode of presentation—functionally analogous to indexicals in physics—not in their ontological import; they generate an explanatory gap in our cognition, not in nature itself. Sixth, I accept a methodological principle derived from my scientific background: if a posit plays no distinctive role in explanation or prediction, and its alleged properties violate compositionality and identity conditions, it ought to be eliminated rather than retained as an unexplained residue.

My question is whether these six commitments form a stable equilibrium. Specifically, if I reject bare qualia as incoherent, I seem compelled to reconstruct "what it is like" in purely structural terms; yet if that reconstruction does no explanatory work beyond what neural dynamics already provide, my sixth commitment appears to push me toward an eliminativism that conflicts with my fourth. Meanwhile, my dismissal of conceivability in the second commitment might seem to undermine the conceptual-analysis basis of the third. Is the tension between the fourth and sixth commitments the most severe, or does the combination of the second and third pose a deeper problem? Which single revision—weakening the Quinean elimination principle, conceding that phenomenal concepts carry genuine ontological weight, or abandoning the structural reconstruction of bat experience—would restore consistency at the lowest cost? Finally, please state what this equilibrium predicts for a case I have not considered: could a silicon-based system with an architecture isomorphic to that of a conscious biological brain possess phenomenal experience reconstructed in the same structural terms, or does the physical substrate itself carry indispensable phenomenal relevance?
