I'm trying to work out whether illusionism about phenomenal consciousness captures my own judgments case by case, and I need to see if there's a principle behind them or if I'm just picking and choosing. Here are my verdicts.

When a blindsight patient correctly identifies objects in their blind field while sincerely denying any visual experience, I think illusionism is correct: there is no phenomenal consciousness of those objects, and the denial is accurate rather than a failure of introspection.

When a patient emerging from deep general anesthesia reports, "There was nothing at all—not blackness, not emptiness, just a total absence," I think they are describing a real phenomenal state. The absence of experience itself seems to have a distinctive "feel" of nothingness, which illusionism struggles to accommodate because it denies there is any phenomenal property present to be absent.

When someone under a high dose of psilocybin reports seeing vivid geometric patterns with no corresponding retinal stimulus, insisting, "The green-red lattice is right there in my visual field," I think the illusionist should say there is no such phenomenal quality. The subject is misrepresenting their own neural processing as containing a quality that it actually lacks.

But when a synesthete reports that the numeral 5 is experienced as "intrinsically reddish" in exactly the same direct, unmediated way they experience an actual red patch, I think there really is a reddish phenomenal quality present in consciousness. It is just causally triggered by a number rather than by light. So illusionism is false here: the quality exists, though its cause is atypical.

When a sleepwalker performs complex actions and later reports complete blankness for that period, I think there was genuinely no phenomenal consciousness during the episode, despite the rich behavior.

Now consider a hypothetical case: a subject with a direct cortical stimulator activates the face-recognition pathway in isolation and reports, "I see a face, but I know it's not there—it's like the face-quality is floating free, unattached to any object." I keep going back and forth. Part of me says this is like the psychedelic case: an illusory phenomenal quality with no real presence. Part of me says it is like the synesthesia case: a real quality that is correctly identified but mislocated.

Is there a coherent principle—perhaps about the relation between representational content, introspective access, and causal origin—that delivers my first four verdicts? If so, what does it predict for the sleepwalker and for the cortical-stimulation case? And which of my verdicts, if any, must I drop to keep the principle simple and not ad hoc?
