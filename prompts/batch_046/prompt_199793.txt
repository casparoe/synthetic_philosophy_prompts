I am currently enrolled in a course on grand historical narratives and the critiques advanced against them, and I want to test whether Yuval Noah Harari's concept of inter-subjective realities — shared fictional constructs that enable large-scale cooperation among strangers — can be applied rigorously to a modern episode that he does not discuss, and whether David Graeber and David Wengrow's challenge to linear progress narratives changes the analysis.

Please analyze the 2008 global financial crisis, specifically the collapse of Lehman Brothers and the preceding breakdown of confidence in mortgage-backed securities and credit ratings, through this combined lens.

First, apply Harari's framework with discipline. Identify the specific shared fiction or nested set of fictions that made coordination possible among banks, rating agencies, investors, and regulators: what exactly was the collectively believed construct (for example, the idea that securitized risk could be objectively measured and distributed), who were the players in this cooperative system, what options did they face, and what counted as evidence within the shared reality? Explain what the crisis illuminates about the framework — for instance, whether the rapid evaporation of belief in credit ratings operates analogously to the collapse of a religious or political myth in Harari's prehistoric and early-modern examples — and where the framework misses or misdescribes the episode, particularly regarding regulatory power, material incentives, or asymmetries of information.

Second, incorporate Graeber and Wengrow's critique of grand narratives that present human history as an inevitable march toward greater complexity, with crises serving as mere growing pains of advancement. Apply this to the common post-2008 narrative that the crisis was an unavoidable correction or a necessary step toward more sophisticated financial governance. Does their emphasis on contingency, human agency, and the non-inevitability of institutional forms undermine that narrative? Does the crisis confirm their skepticism, or does it complicate it?

Third, I want you to work out a specific conceptual extension: the notion of nested inter-subjective entities, in which one fictional construct (such as the limited-liability corporation) depends on another (the legal system recognizing corporate personhood) and both depend on a third (the belief that credit ratings reflect objective risk). Analyze whether the 2008 collapse is best understood as a failure at one nesting level that propagated upward, or whether the framework requires an explicit account of how material defaults — actual mortgage failures — interact with belief collapse. State clearly where Harari's theory is silent on this interaction, and whether Graeber and Wengrow's attention to concrete social practices and material conditions fills the gap or introduces new difficulties.

Keep the mapping precise: specify who is playing which role, what counts as a move within the shared fiction, and where the theory offers no determinate answer and judgment must take over. Do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations.
