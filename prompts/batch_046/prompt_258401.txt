I have recently begun advising a firm that operates a persistent virtual environment in which users build elaborate digital estates, and I am currently helping to draft an ethical policy on the status of user-created structures that are destroyed during server migrations or data corruption. A senior colleague argues that because these structures exist solely as rendered data and lack spatiotemporal continuity with physical matter, any distress experienced upon their deletion cannot be a genuine harm, but only an irrational attachment to ephemeral displays. I initially accepted this view, but I now suspect it conceals a false presupposition: that for a harm to be real, the entity affected must possess independent physical instantiation, rather than functioning as the intentional object of a socially recognised practice. Could you clarify whether the inference from the non-physicality of virtual objects to the illusoriness of virtual harm commits a category mistake? Specifically, I wish to understand whether a virtual object—for instance, a procedurally generated garden that a user has tended for several years—can carry sufficient ontological weight to ground a real loss, despite being entirely dependent on software architecture and server state, and whether acknowledging such loss requires us to treat digital artefacts as something more substantial than mere representations.
