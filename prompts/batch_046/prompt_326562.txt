I've been stuck in this philosophy of well-being reading group where we've spent like six months on tranquilism — the idea that only the absence of craving and suffering actually matters for how well a life goes, and that positive joy or achievement is basically just decorative or even a hidden source of attachment. We all agree suffering is bad, we all agree craving is bad, and we even agree on the psych data that people adapt fast to success and stay stuck in misery. But then the tranquilists say the best possible life is a steady state of zero suffering and zero craving with nothing added on top, and the other side says thats not a life well-lived, thats a life emptied out — like praising a painting just for having no ugly colors. Nobody has budged an inch. My own guess is that the stall isnt really about well-being at all but about what counts as a moral reason: the tranquilists are running an eliminative framework where reasons only point against harm, while the other side thinks reasons can also point toward constructing something worth having. But maybe thats wrong and its actually a hidden empirical question about whether sustained absence of positive affect eventually produces a kind of suffering people cant report well? Or maybe we're just using "well-being" to ask two different questions — one about therapeutic targets, one about what makes a life worth living — and treating them like the same debate. Can you diagnose what kind of disagreement this actually is? Is it verbal, substantive, bedrock intuition, or an ill-posed question hiding under one name? And given your diagnosis, is there any move — a conceptual distinction, an empirical finding, or just a reframing — that could break the deadlock, or should we accept that this one just wont move?
