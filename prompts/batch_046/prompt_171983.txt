I am a doctoral student in moral and political philosophy, and I am trying to work through a conceptual problem that arises when luck-egalitarian distinctions are applied not to external goods or natural talents, but to the formation of one's normative framework itself. The standard taxonomy—brute luck versus option luck—presupposes that we can identify a baseline of resources, capacities, or circumstances that are then modified by voluntary choice. Yet the criteria by which we classify something as a resource or a capacity are themselves shaped by the normative traditions into which we are socialized. This creates a reflexivity problem: if growing up in a particular moral tradition is a matter of brute luck (the cultural and linguistic environment is unchosen), but the subsequent reflective revision of that tradition is treated as option luck (for which one is responsible), then the boundary between compensation and responsibility seems to depend on prior evaluative commitments that are themselves outcomes of the luck being assessed.

Please explain the conceptual issue in detail. Specifically: can the brute-luck/option-luck distinction be coherently applied at the level of what we might call framework luck—the luck of inheriting the very standards by which we judge luck? Or does this application cause the distinction to collapse into circularity, because identifying a choice as genuinely voluntary requires a prior normative framework that was itself partly luck-determined? In your answer, clearly separate what is settled in the literature—such as the basic structure of the Dworkin–Arneson taxonomy and the standard objections regarding expensive tastes or adaptive preferences—from what is genuinely contested among specialists, particularly regarding whether reflective endorsement of a framework can ever be sufficiently voluntary to bear the full weight of distributive responsibility.
