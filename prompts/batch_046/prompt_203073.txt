I've been working through the logic of updateless decision theories—specifically the view that in Newcomb-like problems an agent ought to treat its own decision not as an event to be updated upon, but as a logical node jointly determined with world-states that share computational or algorithmic ancestry, regardless of causal or temporal ordering. On this account, one-boxing in Newcomb's problem is not a quirk but an instance of a broader principle: when an agent's reasoning is physically implemented, correlations between its output and external conditions should be evaluated by examining the shared logical structure of the computation, not by applying standard Bayesian conditioning on observed evidence. This strikes me as more defensible than causal decision theory, particularly once we allow that the predictor might be a deterministic simulation of the agent running in parallel rather than a temporally prior observer.

But I am trying to understand what would genuinely undermine this position—not merely the familiar charge that it violates causal intuitions or licenses "spooky" reasoning across time. I want to know which specific cases or formal results would count against it: for instance, scenarios involving nested self-prediction, self-modification where the agent alters its own decision algorithm, or problems where logical coupling seems to overgenerate correlations that do not correspond to any actual shared computation. Which intuitions about belief updating or embedded agency does this view risk? Would its defenders concede that a successful formalization of embedded agency requiring evidential updating is fatal, or would they absorb that result by refining what counts as algorithmic ancestry? And if the view turns out to be well-insulated—if every apparent counterexample can be dissolved by redefining the scope of logical correlation—does that insulation count as a theoretical virtue or as a sign that the position has been made unfalsifiable?

Please rank the potential defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, distinguishing what critics typically raise from what proponents of this updateless framework would themselves admit counts against them. I am particularly interested in whether there are results in adjacent domains—say, about the persistence of computational identity across time, or about the limits of logical omniscience in bounded agents—that this position implicitly depends upon and might collapse with if they fail.
