Consider the explanatory divide in accounts of moral revolution—how practices once embedded in honor codes or economic structures become, within a generation, unthinkable. One family of explanations, associated with Bernard Williams, stresses the limits of rational argument and the embeddedness of norms in social practices; another, developed by Kwame Anthony Appiah, emphasizes honor and reputation dynamics as the engine of rapid normative collapse; a third treats shifts as primarily driven by changing material interests that eventually render old practices costly. These frameworks are not merely historical: they bear directly on how one should intervene in emerging normative transitions—whether in professional ethics around artificial intelligence, climate-related consumption norms, or institutional commitments to transparency. Assuming you have limited research resources—say, a single multi-year project or a small grant—what are the two or three open questions within this landscape that most deserve priority? In particular, defend an ordering among the following: whether honor-driven cascades can be triggered without a pre-existing institutional crisis; whether rationalist arguments ever function as independent causes of change rather than post-hoc legitimations of interest- or honor-driven shifts; whether hybrid models that integrate all three mechanisms are conceptually coherent or merely obscure the predictive differences between them; and whether we can construct real-time indicators that distinguish an interest-driven realignment from an honor cascade before the transition completes. Your answer should specify the criteria for importance—such as tractability with existing social-scientific tools, the number of downstream intervention strategies that depend on the answer, and the risk of misallocating normative entrepreneurship if the question is left open—and explain which lines of work you would explicitly deprioritize and why.
