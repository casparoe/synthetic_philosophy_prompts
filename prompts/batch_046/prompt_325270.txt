After a long and somewhat circular argument with a friend last night about Fitch's paradox of knowability, I'm trying to map out exactly where people land without relying on textbook labels they've memorized. I want a decision tree — or, if the logical space fits better, a taxonomy with branch-labels — that takes a respondent from the bare shared commitment "there is at least one truth that nobody currently knows" to a named (or clearly unnamed) position on the paradox.

Please build the tree from logical space rather than from a roster of famous names. Start with the shared premise that some unknown truth p exists. The first branch should ask whether the respondent accepts that individual knowledge is factive and closed under conjunction: specifically, if one knows that p-and-q, then one knows p and one knows q. The second level should split those who accept closure from those who deny it. For the deniers, include three distinct sub-branches: (i) they deny closure specifically for conjunctions that embed knowledge operators; (ii) they allow collective or distributed knowledge to satisfy closure conditions that no individual meets; (iii) they read "knowable" as practical or technological feasibility rather than logical-modal possibility. For those who retain closure, the tree should ask whether they restrict the universal knowability principle, and if so whether the restriction targets only self-referential propositions, all propositions containing negative epistemic operators, or every proposition that embeds a knowledge claim.

Include a separate branch for respondents who reject factivity altogether, even if that position is rarely defended, and place the "indexicalist" who time-relativizes "knowable" — reading it as "knowable at some time t that may differ from the time of p" — at the appropriate node rather than leaving them floating. Each leaf must carry a position name (e.g., "the closure-blocker," "the collective-knowledge restrictor," "the practical-modal weakenist," "the self-reference restrictor," "the indexicalist," or "unnamed") and exactly one sentence describing its most awkward commitment — the specific cost its defenders must accept. Where the logical space permits a combination of answers that no position actually occupies, label it "unoccupied" and briefly diagnose whether the gap is due to incoherence, unattractiveness, or being genuinely unexplored. Specifically, flag whether your tree can genuinely separate the collective-knowledge restrictor from the practical-modal weakenist; if the diagnostic questions fail to distinguish them, say so explicitly instead of merging them.

The diagnostic questions should be concrete cases or claims answerable without knowing the philosophical label they lead to — for example, "If a research team collectively verifies p and separately verifies q, but no single member has verified both, has the team known p-and-q?" rather than "Do you accept collective epistemic closure?" The audience is someone with basic logic and epistemology but no specialized training in Fitch's literature. Use about five to seven nodes, leading to six to eight leaves, with all unoccupied spaces marked. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations, and do not ask for them; the instrument should work for someone reasoning entirely offline.
