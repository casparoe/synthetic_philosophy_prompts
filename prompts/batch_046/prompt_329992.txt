I am drafting a two-page proposal for an independent six-month research fellowship in philosophy of mind, and I need help turning a half-formed idea into a precise, defensible plan. The subject has become personally urgent since my grandfather's recent death: I inherited his working library, hundreds of volumes filled with decades of marginalia, cross-references, and argumentative notes that seem to preserve not merely his opinions but the rhythm and structure of his reasoning. This led me back to the well-known thought experiment—discussed in the philosophy of consciousness—where Einstein's brain is replaced, neuron by neuron, with a book containing all his knowledge and responses, and to the question of whether such a book could be conscious. Existing treatments tend to frame this as a binary dispute between functional equivalence and substrate-dependent naturalism: if the book replicates the brain's input-output patterns, functionalism grants consciousness; if consciousness requires biological neural tissue, naturalism denies it. I think both sides assume, without argument, that the relevant property is static informational equivalence at a single time rather than what I will call diachronic integration—the continuous temporal binding of mental states into an ongoing stream. My proposal asks whether making diachronic integration explicit dissolves the stalemate and reveals a hidden structural question. I will examine three replacement variants: instantaneous substitution of the entire brain; gradual page-by-page replacement during live interactive dialogue; and a hybrid case in which a human reader's cognition completes patterns left implicit in the printed text. For each variant I will ask whether diachronic integration is clearly present, clearly absent, or ambiguously distributed, and whether that judgment tracks our pre-theoretic intuitions about consciousness and personal identity. The method is conceptual, not empirical: I will formalize minimal conditions for diachronic integration without committing to a specific neuroscientific framework, apply them systematically to the three variants, and produce a taxonomy of failure modes—cases where the criterion either clearly excludes the book or collapses back into pure functional or substrate-based conditions. The project succeeds if it demonstrates that the standard debate obscures a tractable structural distinction; it fails if diachronic integration proves reducible to either static functional equivalence or biological substrate dependence, leaving the original puzzle untouched. I need the proposal to state the research question precisely, explain why current answers leave it open, outline the three-phase conceptual method, specify what counts as success and failure, and remain realistic for six months of solo work.
