I am preparing a long-form piece on Plato’s philosophy of intoxication, particularly the tension between the Symposium’s chaotic revelation and the Laws’ carefully regulated civic drinking. Rather than asking for a survey of scholarship, I want to test whether my own intuitive verdicts about concrete cases can be captured by a single precise principle—something I could defend in the article without merely recounting what every reader already knows about the symposiarch or Alcibiades’ speech. Here are my judgments. First, in the Laws, citizens over thirty gather under a sober master of drinking, consume diluted wine in a communal setting, and allow shame to dissolve so that hidden traits of courage or cowardice become visible; I regard this as excellent practice and genuinely philosophical, because the loss of inhibition is harnessed for civic self-knowledge under rational supervision. Second, in the Symposium, Alcibiades arrives already drunk on undiluted wine, lacks any supervisor, behaves disorderly, yet delivers an encomium to Socrates that strikes me as profoundly truthful and philosophically significant; I want to preserve the value of that revelation, even though the disorder makes it an unreliable method. Third, a twenty-two-year-old drinks moderately in private before dialectic, hoping merely to lower anxiety, and remains fully rational; I judge this corrupting and useless, despite the absence of disorder. Fourth, a magistrate institutes supervised group drinking to extract truthful confessions from criminal suspects; structurally this resembles the civic test, yet I feel it is an abuse, not philosophy. Fifth, a solitary scholar deliberately intoxicates himself alone to achieve the “revelatory” state Plato describes, hoping to solve an ethical puzzle; I regard this as a degradation of philosophical practice. The difficulty is that Cases One and Two both yield genuine truth through intoxication, yet only one is structured and civic, while Cases Three and Five are both solitary but differ in whether rational control is preserved, which makes my distinction feel unstable. Is there one coherent account—perhaps centred on the purpose of the dissolution, the presence of mutual vulnerability, or the retention of a guiding rational faculty—that can accommodate all five verdicts, or must I drop one? Please state the principle precisely, indicate which clause addresses which case, be honest about which verdict it cannot save, and say what it predicts for a case I have not considered: a modern clinical facilitator running a voluntary, structured group session with low-dose alcohol and a sober therapist present, designed to help participants discuss repressed conflicts. Please provide your answer in British English, with no bibliography or citation list, and keep the principle stated clearly enough that it could guide a practitioner’s judgment without further theoretical apparatus.
