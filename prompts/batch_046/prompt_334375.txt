I am drafting notes for a paper on whether dreams are experiences, and I would like your critical comments on the line of argument I am developing. Rather than rehearsing the familiar Malcolm–Dennett exchange, I want to test a more granular distinction that I think both sides miss.

Here is the core of the essay. I begin by accepting Malcolm’s central negative point: during sleep there is no observational stance available to the sleeper, so if experience requires the possibility of first-person observational access, then dreams are not experiences. But I reject his further claim that dream reports are therefore on a par with fictional narratives or morning-after confabulations. There is, I suggest, a residual phenomenal presence—an “it seemed to me that …” quality—that constrains the narrative constructed upon waking in a way that pure confabulation is not constrained. Dennett’s cassette model captures the constructive aspect of reporting but treats the phenomenal residue as either non-existent or epiphenomenal. I propose instead that dreams involve phenomenal presence without experiential access: there is something it is like to be in the dream-state, but that “something” is not accessible to an internal observer during the state itself.

To sharpen this, I introduce a specific edge case: false awakenings. In a false awakening, the subject reports upon waking that she observed herself getting out of bed, turning on a light, and so forth, yet upon actual awakening she realizes no such observations occurred. The standard Dennettian might say this is just a more elaborate cassette playing. The Malcolmite might say it is a post-hoc narrative with no experiential basis. I want to argue that false awakenings actually support my intermediate view: they display phenomenal presence (the feeling of having observed) decoupled from experiential access (there was never an observing self present). If that decoupling is coherent, then dreams in general can be understood as states with phenomenal presence but without the observational access that Malcolm takes to be essential to experience.

The essay then considers an objection: if phenomenal presence is not accessible during the state, by what criterion do we distinguish it from non-phenomenal brain activity? I reply that the distinction is functional rather than introspective—the phenomenal residue shapes the narrative in characteristic ways (emotional tonality, spatial perspective, temporal flow) that differ systematically from waking confabulation.
