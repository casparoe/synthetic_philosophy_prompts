For my bioethics seminar I am fixing my view of valid consent and want an audit before I present it. Here are my commitments: (a) consent is valid only if both voluntary and informed, where being informed requires grasping the specific risks, benefits, and alternatives of that particular intervention, not merely a generic idea of medical treatment; (b) competence is domain-relative and scalar rather than globally binary—one may be competent to consent to a routine extraction but not to an experimental neural implant; (c) deception vitiates consent only if it is "act-structural," misrepresenting the physical or legal nature of the procedure itself, while "contextual deception"—for instance, concealing that the surgeon is a trainee or that the trial is funded by the device manufacturer—does not invalidate consent so long as the procedural facts are transparent; (d) psychological pressure that stops short of an agent-imposed, illegitimate threat—such as family insistence or the patient's own panic—does not constitute coercion that undermines voluntariness; (e) in emergency settings, consent obtained from a patient who is in severe pain and terrified is fully valid, provided they can still understand the intervention, because the duress is state-based rather than externally coerced; and (f) for invasive bodily interventions there is no ethically significant distinction between express and implied consent: both must satisfy identical competence and disclosure standards. Which pairs are genuinely in tension, which conflicts dissolve on inspection, and what is the least costly revision for each real one? Rank the tensions by severity, identify the weakest link in each case, and say whether fixing them all together requires abandoning the domain-relative competence thesis or only refining the deception distinction.
