I'm working through my own commitments about Dutch book arguments, and I realize my intuitions about particular cases don't line up cleanly with any single principle I know. Here are my verdicts, and I want to know whether there's a unified account behind them, or whether I need to drop one.

Case 1 — The textbook numerical incoherence. You assign credence 0.7 to "it will rain tomorrow" and 0.7 to "it will not rain tomorrow," and you accept separate fair bets on each at those odds. Verdict: irrational, full stop. The incoherence is internal to your belief set.

Case 2 — The imprecise-credence dilation case. Your credence in some event A is genuinely indeterminate: you represent it with the interval [0.3, 0.6]. A bookie offers you a bet on A at odds that correspond to 0.5, and separately a bet against A at corresponding odds. You refuse both, maintaining the interval. But then the bookie offers a sequence of bets on related propositions that, combined with your interval-valued commitments, allows her to guarantee a loss if you maintain consistency with your interval across all of them. Verdict: I don't think you're irrational. Your refusal to pinpoint a value is epistemically respectable, and the vulnerability seems to come from forcing a precise betting framework onto an imprecise mental state, not from a flaw in your reasoning.

Case 3 — The self-aware bias case. You know, from prior calibration studies, that you systematically overestimate probabilities for vivid scenarios by about 20 percentage points. Nevertheless, you feel the overestimation in your gut and bet according to it. Verdict: this feels irrational, but in a different register than Case 1. It's not that your beliefs contradict each other; it's that you knowingly fail to correct for a recognized distortion.

Case 4 — The dynamic Jeffrey update case. You plan to update on evidence E using Jeffrey conditioning with a specific weighting. A bookie sets up bets now at prices reflecting your current credences, with a clause that settlement values are adjusted after you observe E according to your own updating rule. The structure is such that, whatever E you observe, your updated commitments plus the settlement terms leave you with a net loss. Verdict: I want to say this is not irrational, because Jeffrey conditioning is a defensible updating rule and the loss is an artifact of the settlement mechanism, not of your reasoning. But I'm unsure, because it still looks like you're walking into a guaranteed loss.

Case 5 — The comparative-confidence money pump. You don't have numerical credences at all; you only rank propositions by comparative confidence: you're more sure of A than B, more sure of B than C, and—due to framing effects—more sure of C than A. A bookie exploits this intransitive cycle, charging you a small fee to "upgrade" your confidence ranking from one to the next, eventually cycling back to A with a net loss of three fees. Verdict: I think this reveals a genuine irrationality, but only if comparative confidence is supposed to guide action in a transitive way. If it's just a heuristic for attention, maybe not. I lean toward irrationality.

Case 6 — The undefined-proposition forced bet. You regard "the present King of France is bald" as lacking a truth-value altogether, so you assign no credence and refuse to bet. A bookie constructs a contract where you pay $1 now; if the proposition turns out to have a truth-value and is false, you get $10 back, but if it lacks a truth-value, the contract is void and you lose the $1. Verdict: I don't think you're irrational to refuse, because entering the contract requires treating the proposition as bettable, which you've denied.

My tentative principle is something like: Dutch books reveal irrationality only when they exploit an internal structural failure in a system of commitments that the agent already accepts as governing her reasoning—whether that system is precise probabilities, interval constraints, updating rules, or transitive rankings. But Cases 3 and 5 make me wonder whether the principle should also cover failures to correct recognized biases or intransitive heuristics, and Case 4 makes me worry I'm just protecting my favorite updating rule.

Can you state the principle (or a small family of competing principles) that best captures Cases 1, 2, 4, 5, and 6? For each clause of the principle, say which case motivates it and what verdict it delivers on the cases I haven't listed—particularly on Case 3, and on a further case: suppose an agent uses a non-classical probability calculus (say, one satisfying quantum coherence conditions rather than Kolmogorov's axioms) and is subjected to a betting scheme that assumes classical additivity. Does the principle say she's irrational? Also, please be explicit about which of my six verdicts the principle cannot accommodate without strain, and whether overturning Case 3 or Case 4 is the more defensible move.
