As a novelist drafting a reflective essay for a literary quarterly, I have been working from notes on John Maynard Keynes's 1930 essay "Economic Possibilities for our Grandchildren." Keynes predicted that within a century economic growth would largely solve what he termed absolute needs—those we feel regardless of our fellows' circumstances—while leaving humanity with the more enduring difficulty of living wisely with abundant leisure, since relative needs, those whose satisfaction depends on feeling superior to others, are essentially insatiable. I wish to argue that contemporary literary fiction functions as an unintended archive of Keynes's miscalculation, not because labor has failed to diminish, but because leisure itself has been structurally colonized.

To develop this, I have proposed a distinction I am calling leisure capture: the process by which time freed from necessary labor is not experienced as autonomous freedom but is immediately reinvested in competitive self-optimization, status maintenance, or performative consumption, leaving the subject under the same psychic discipline as the worker. This is not identical to workaholism or hustle culture, since the activities involved—curated wellness routines, prestige travel arranged for social comparison, the compulsive micro-management of lifestyle metrics—can be entirely passive in appearance.

My essay traces three recurring narrative patterns: the retired protagonist whose unstructured vacation collapses into crisis; the wealthy heir whose empty days generate obsessive order-making; and the post-labor utopian setting that fiction consistently renders as dystopian precisely because relative needs offer no natural terminus. I suggest that novelists do not merely reflect a sociological reality here but actively amplify it, since narrative requires conflict and absolute satisfaction provides none. Yet this raises a problem I have not resolved: does fiction reveal that Keynes underestimated the persistence of competitive psychology, or does it construct a false inevitability by requiring that leisure always be corrupted?

I would welcome comments on the accuracy of my reading of Keynes's distinction between absolute and relative needs; on whether the concept of leisure capture is coherent and properly demarcated from adjacent notions such as status anxiety or workism; and on whether the literary observation seems genuinely insightful or largely an artifact of narrative form. If the framing strikes you as promising, I would also value suggestions about which parts of the argument most require sharpening, qualification, or abandonment.
