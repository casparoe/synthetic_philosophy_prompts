You are a senior philosopher of science specializing in historical ontology and the epistemology of deep time. Before writing, take a moment to brainstorm the conceptual architecture of geological periodization—specifically, distinguish chronostratigraphic units, which are anchored by physical boundary markers, from historical epochs, which are anchored by collective human significance—and consider whether these are competing categories or nested ones.

Write a formal essay addressing the following controversy. The Anthropocene is conventionally debated as a dating problem: does it begin with the Great Acceleration around 1950, with the Columbian Exchange and associated biotic homogenization around 1610, or with the onset of large-scale fossil-fuel combustion around 1784? I want you to set aside the purely chronological dispute and treat the deeper issue as one of jurisdiction and ontology: what kind of entity is being declared when an epoch is named, and who possesses the authority to make that declaration?

To sharpen the analysis, adopt and defend the distinction between stratigraphic discovery—the identification of a globally synchronous physical signal, such as radiogenic isotope horizons or synthetic polymer deposits—and epochal ratification, the institutional recognition that human activity has become a dominant Earth-system process. Then evaluate this thesis: the Anthropocene is not primarily a stratigraphic problem awaiting better physical evidence, but a ratification crisis in which abundant physical signals already exist, yet lack the proper institutional form—analogous to a legal or social status that operates in fact long before it is formally recognized in law.

Weigh the principal objections in detail: that geology ought to remain methodologically independent of human institutions; that ratification without a precisely designated Global Boundary Stratotype Section and Point collapses the distinction between natural history and political history; and that framing the Anthropocene as a juridical or institutional event rather than a physical one renders its start date arbitrarily conventional, thereby undermining its scientific legitimacy.

Reach and justify a clear, committed conclusion. Either argue that the "who decides" question is genuinely resolved by recognizing epochal ratification as constitutive rather than derivative of stratigraphic discovery, or argue that this move dissolves the Anthropocene into a purely normative or political category and therefore sacrifices its claim to be a geological epoch in the strict sense. Do not end on a non-committal or purely expository note.
