I'm writing a response paper for my upper-division philosophy of mind seminar, where we've been working through eliminative materialism—specifically Paul Churchland's argument that folk psychology is a degenerating research program comparable to phlogiston theory or witchcraft. Rather than summarizing the standard objections, I'm trying to reconstruct how Aristotle might have engaged with this modern position without collapsing into anachronism. Assuming he retains his core commitments—hylomorphism, formal and final causation, and the view that the soul is the form of the body—how would he likely diagnose the eliminativist claim that there are no discrete neural correlates corresponding to beliefs and desires? Would he treat propositional attitudes as formal descriptions that neuroscience merely fails to isolate as material parts, analogous to the way a statue's shape is not an additional chunk of bronze? Or would he grant that, like phlogiston, they are pseudo-entities that should be abandoned? I'm particularly interested in whether his distinction between potentiality and actuality gives him conceptual resources to argue that beliefs are genuinely real but not the kind of thing that should appear as localized brain states—much as the form of an organism is actualized through matter without being a separable part of it. For my paper, I need the conceptual reconstruction rather than a bibliography: which Aristotelian commitments must remain fixed for the response to be authentically his, and exactly where does the modern neuroscientific framing generate a tension he could not resolve without giving up hylomorphism altogether?
