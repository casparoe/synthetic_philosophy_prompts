A member of our cooperative woodworking shop was permanently expelled after deliberately contaminating shared finishing supplies and ruining months of work by other members. Our founding charter states that expulsion is meant "to preserve the conditions of mutual trust, not to settle moral debts." Several years have passed. The expelled member has since become the only local technician able to repair the shop's obsolete but legally required dust-collection system; without that repair, we will fail safety inspections and likely have to dissolve. He now offers to restore the system at no cost, asking only for reinstatement. One faction of the board argues that readmitting him treats the original destruction as a mere transaction: he caused harm, then canceled it with a later benefit, which ignores the idea that some wrongs demand a permanent response regardless of present utility. They insist that even if the charter frames exclusion instrumentally, there is an independent reason he must remain outside — that a community's integrity requires treating certain deliberate violations as placing a person irrevocably beyond cooperation. The opposing faction replies that this so-called independent reason is indistinguishable from a desire for suffering: if exclusion is truly meant to protect trust, and he poses no further threat, then refusing his help serves no protective purpose and merely punishes for its own sake. They say the charter's language should govern us, and that appealing to an unarticulated "integrity" principle just smuggles revenge in through the back door. I must cast the deciding vote. Is there a coherent way to treat the original wrong as genuinely serious — not reducible to a calculable cost — without thereby committing ourselves to his permanent exclusion, even when that exclusion destroys the very community the wrong originally harmed? Or does taking the wrong seriously necessarily require us to reject his return, whatever the consequences?
