In debates about whether hypocrites can legitimately blame others for faults they themselves share, a common analogy treats standing to blame like a court's jurisdiction and hypocrisy like a conflict of interest that strips a judge of authority. The idea is that just as a judge who is secretly also a party to the dispute has no standing to render a binding verdict — even if the defendant is plainly guilty — a person who shares the fault has no procedural standing to blame, regardless of whether the blame is factually correct. The interlocutor who offers this analogy presses it further: she says hypocrisy is not merely a character flaw that weakens the blame's force, but a disqualifier analogous to fraud on the court, because the hypocrite conceals her own party-status while acting as if she were an impartial arbiter.

First, provide an explicit correspondence table: what plays the role of the court, the judge, the defendant, the verdict, the conflict-of-interest rule, the possibility of recusal, and the specific defect of concealing one's party-status? Second, separate the disanalogies that matter for the intended use from those that do not. In particular, where does the legal notion of jurisdiction depend on institutional rules that have no interpersonal counterpart, and where does interpersonal blame differ from a judicial verdict in ways that undermine the claim that hypocrisy nullifies standing rather than merely weakening it? Does the analogy quietly import the assumption that blame requires a kind of moral purity or institutional impartiality that it does not actually require? Third, assess whether the conclusion — that hypocrisy is a procedural nullifier of standing, not just a pragmatic or moral defeater — survives once the disanalogies are on the table. If the analogy collapses, suggest what a better analogy, if any, would capture the relevant relation.
