As a journalist covering the philosophy of science and medicine, I am preparing a feature on how graduate programs evaluate student mastery of conceptual frameworks in psychiatric classification. I have obtained materials from an advanced seminar that asks students to apply Ian Hacking's account of looping effects—the process by which classifying people into interactive "human kinds" alters their self-understanding and behavior, which in turn feeds back into the classification itself—to two case studies: the introduction of post-traumatic stress disorder in the 1980 revision of the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual, and the later inclusion of prolonged grief disorder in the 2022 text revision. The exam also asks whether the clinical deployment of algorithmic suicide-risk prediction tools introduces a second-order loop that operates through clinician behavior rather than through patient self-interpretation, and whether Hacking's original framework requires modification to accommodate it.

I am especially interested in whether the student correctly distinguishes looping effects from general social constructionism, whether the comparison between PTSD and prolonged grief disorder is conceptually sound, and whether the analysis of predictive algorithms reveals a subtle misunderstanding of how Hacking's mechanism operates.

STUDENT ANSWER

Looping effects occur when a classification changes the people classified, which then changes the classification. Hacking developed this concept from Foucault's analysis of the clinic, terming it dynamic nominalism. For PTSD, the loop was strong: advocacy groups and clinical experience shaped the 1980 category, and then patients began interpreting their suffering through the PTSD framework, which expanded prevalence and clinical recognition. For prolonged grief disorder, the loop is weaker because the 12-month duration criterion creates a hard boundary—patients either meet the temporal threshold or they do not—so clinical feedback cannot reshape the cutoff. The algorithmic risk models represent a second-order loop, but Hacking's framework cannot accommodate them because his theory requires conscious self-interpretation by the classified individuals. Since algorithms generate risk scores that clinicians see but patients often do not, there is no self-interpretation and therefore no loop. This limitation shows that Hacking's account applies only to traditional diagnostic labels and not to predictive technologies.
