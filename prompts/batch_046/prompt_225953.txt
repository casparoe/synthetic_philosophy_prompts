Student answer:
Plantinga's free will defense solves the logical problem by showing God and evil are compatible. He argues there's transworld depravity: for any free creature God might make, there's a possible world where that creature does wrong, so God can't actualize a world with free beings who always choose good. That covers moral evil. For natural evil, Plantinga extends this to free non-human spirits—like demons—who cause earthquakes and disease, so the same logic applies. Now, the evidential problem asks whether the amount of evil makes God unlikely. The distributive problem is basically the evidential problem focused on patterns: suffering hits people randomly rather than tracking moral choices. I think Plantinga's defense can cover this too, because if freedom produces both moral and natural evil, then the random spread is just an unavoidable byproduct. Also, Hick's soul-making theodicy fits here—uneven suffering lets different people develop character at different rates. So the pattern doesn't lower God's probability; it just means we can't read His reasons from the distribution, which is skeptical theism. Therefore the defense works for logical, evidential, and distributive versions.
