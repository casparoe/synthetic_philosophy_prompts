I'm preparing a discussion for a philosophy of language seminar that pairs classic films with theories of truth, and I'm stuck on Akira Kurosawa's Rashomon. The film gives four incompatible eyewitness accounts of a violent encounter in a forest, and it famously refuses to adjudicate between them; even the woodcutter's final version is offered hesitantly, and the listener who hears it seems to reject any resolution. I want to read this through deflationary theories of truth—specifically the claim that true is not a substantive predicate picking out a correspondence relation between statement and world, but rather a device for semantic ascent or disquotation.

But I want to push past the obvious reading in which the film is just a naive relativist parable about unknowable reality. Instead, I'm interested in whether Rashomon dramatizes a more precise tension: between representational failure (each witness misrepresents the event because of ego, shame, or self-interest) and the redundancy of the truth-predicate (even if all representations fail, saying it is true that X happened does not add a further property beyond asserting X). The woodcutter's account might be read not as a closer approximation to reality, but as an assertion stripped of rhetorical embellishment—closer to bare disquotation—while the commoner's refusal to accept it might illustrate that we keep wanting truth to do explanatory work that deflationists say it never performs.

Can you offer a reading that takes both the film and deflationary accounts—disquotationalism, minimalism, prosententialism—seriously? Where does the structure of competing narratives illuminate the deflationist claim that truth-talk does not explain representational success, and where does the film resist because it still treats the event in the forest as something that could be correctly or incorrectly represented? I'm especially interested in whether stripping away representation in the final account leaves nothing, no bare fact, or whether it leaves exactly the kind of unadorned assertion that deflationists think is all there is to truth. For my seminar essay, I'd also like you to say what the film adds to the philosophical debate about redundancy that a purely conceptual argument doesn't capture—perhaps something about the emotional or narrative cost of treating testimony as mere endorsement rather than correspondence.
