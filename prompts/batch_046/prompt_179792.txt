I work as a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy, but I recently encountered Daniel Dennett’s idea of “real patterns” together with his three explanatory stances—physical, design, and intentional. I brought it up at dinner with two friends, and they got into a sustained disagreement about whether the framework genuinely applies to a failure mode we maintain at work. I am reporting the exchange below as a neutral bystander; I do not have a settled view of my own.

Here is the back-and-forth as it happened.

(1) The first friend argued that the recurring failure in our distributed logging pipeline—writes consistently drop when they span exactly three database shards and cross-region latency exceeds 200 ms, but not when either condition holds alone—is a real pattern in Dennett’s sense. She said we can predict and intervene on it reliably at the design-stance level (the coordinator abandons the commit because of a timeout heuristic) without ever computing the physical-level packet flow, which shows the pattern is genuinely present at that level of abstraction rather than being a mere mental shortcut.

(2) The second friend replied that this only demonstrates that the design stance is a useful compression. Every regularity is ultimately just physical events—CPU instructions, voltage states, packet transmissions. Calling some patterns “real” and others illusory is just grading our cognitive limitations. He said that adopting the intentional stance—claiming the coordinator “wants” atomic consistency or “believes” it has failed—is straightforward anthropomorphism. We should stick to the physical or design stance and discard talk of real patterns as ontologically empty.

(3) She countered that Dennett’s point is precisely that reality is stance-relative: a pattern is real if it supports counterfactual predictions that disappear when you switch stances. If we alter the coordinator’s retry logic at the design level, the failure pattern shifts in a predictable way, whereas a purely physical-level model of the same code change would require rebuilding the simulation from the transistor level upward. So the design-level pattern has predictive autonomy; it is not merely compressed physics.

(4) He responded that predictive autonomy is not ontological autonomy—the lower-level code still fully determines every event. And if “real” merely means “predictively useful at some abstraction level,” then every regularity qualifies, which trivializes the concept. He added that she has not shown why the intentional stance is ever genuinely necessary: the design stance, expressed as the protocol specification, already explains every case she cited. Therefore she is either smuggling mentalistic language in without justification, or she is reducing “real pattern” to a banal claim about model selection.

Then say who is more right overall, identify what both parties are missing, and explain whether their dispute turns on a genuine ambiguity in Dennett’s framework or on a misapplication of it to software systems. Do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations.
