I work as a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy, and I have been trying to understand the claim that I could not have come from a different sperm and egg — that my actual gametes are essential to my being me. In discussions I have encountered, this idea is mapped onto concepts from my field: personal origin is likened to a deterministic, reproducible software build or to content-addressed storage (for instance, Git commits or IPFS hashes). On this view, the genome together with the specific gametes functions like the source tree and build inputs; the uterine and developmental environment functions like the compiler and build environment; and the resulting person functions like the compiled binary or the addressed object, fixed by a "hash" of its origins. The analogy is used to argue that origin essentialism is almost tautological: change one gamete and you change the hash, so the resulting object is strictly not me, just as altering one bit of source produces a different commit.

Please audit how far this analogy holds. First, give an explicit correspondence table — what plays the role of the source inputs, the build environment, the compiler, the hash function, and the retrieved object. Then set out the disanalogies that actually matter for the philosophical claim: in particular, where biological development violates the build determinism the analogy assumes; whether content-addressing mistakes a retrieval mechanism for an ontological identity condition; and what the analogy quietly imports about persons being static, finished artifacts rather than continuously developing organisms. Tell me whether the conclusion that origin essentialism is trivially true survives once these breaks are on the table, or whether the analogy was carrying the argument all along. If there is a fairer software-engineering analogy — one that serves either the essentialist or the anti-essentialist without smuggling in determinism or static identity — suggest it and explain why it fits better. Please write your answer in British English.
