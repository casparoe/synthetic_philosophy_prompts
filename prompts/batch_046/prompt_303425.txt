For a philosophy of mind seminar, I am trying to specify what an adequate theory of phenomenal unity must actually deliver if it is to accommodate split-brain cases without collapsing into either naive dualism or an unfalsifiable holism. Do not just list generic virtues; I want the full specification of requirements—each paired with the specific case or experimental consideration that motivates it, and marked as non-negotiable or camp-specific. The list should cover: (1) how it handles interhemispheric arbitration failures where one hemisphere initiates action that the other hemisphere verbally denies; (2) whether it can distinguish functional disintegration (loss of information transfer) from phenomenal disintegration (loss of a single subject of experience) without treating them as automatically coextensive; (3) its consistency with the biological fact that corpus callosotomy does not produce two independently reportable subjects in ordinary clinical contexts, despite the absence of direct interhemispheric cortical transfer; (4) how it accounts for left-hemisphere confabulation regarding right-hemisphere-initiated behavior without either dismissing the reports as mere noise or treating them as authoritative for the whole system; (5) whether it respects the distinction between access-consciousness fragmentation and phenomenal-consciousness fragmentation, and if so, whether it allows them to come apart; and (6) whether it can accommodate the possibility—suggested by some interpretations of split-brain data—that a single organism might host competing intentional systems without either multiplying subjects beyond necessity or denying the competition altogether. For each requirement, state the motivating consideration and flag whether satisfying it rules out particular theoretical moves (for instance, global workspace theories that identify unity with global broadcast, or integrated information theories that treat callosal disconnection as a straightforward division of phi). Then identify which two or three requirements, taken together, form a jointly unsatisfiable cluster—this is the part I most want articulated clearly. Finally, briefly score how the main contenders—the unified-field view, the dual-stream interpretation, and a virtual-workspace arbitration model—fare against the non-negotiable subset, but keep the weight on the criteria themselves rather than on the scorecard.
