I serve on my uni's Committee for Science-Humanities Public Displays — I'm mostly a backend engineer so I just check if the wifi works, but they asked me to draft the decision memo for Thursday's vote. There's a proposal to install a plaque in the physics atrium summarizing Galileo's view that scripture teaches how to reach heaven, not how the heavens actually move, framing him as the guy who separated science and religion. Our history-of-science advisor submitted a counter-memo saying thats a misreading. She argues Galileo's actual hermeneutic draws on Augustine's accommodation idea but modifies it with this niche distinction between conclusions that are sensibus manifestum — obvious to direct sensory experience — versus those reached only by demonstrative reasoning. On her reading, Galileo doesn't seperate the two books; he ranks them epistemically, letting demonstrated science override literal scripture only for the latter category, not for direct observation. The committee must choose: approve the plaque as-is, approve only with an added contextual panel explaining the accommodation rule and its sensory-vs-demonstrative boundary, or reject it because teaching Galileo's interpretive hierarchy implicitly endorses scientific authority correcting religious texts, which might violate our institutional neutralty policy. Draft a memo recommending a concrete course with a timeline — say, approve with conditions and require the contextual panel by next semester — with reasoning the researchers and the historian can both respond to. Give the strongest consideration on the losing side honest weight. And please say exactly what would change your mind, like if Galileo's sensory/demonstrative boundary turns out incoherent, or if the accommodation principle applies to ethical passages and not just cosmological ones, which would break the neat separation the exhibit assumes.
