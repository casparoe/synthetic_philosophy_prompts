I just finished a long argument with a friend about whether future events already possess truth-values before they occur — the classic sea-battle worry, but dragged out of the seminar room into actual decision-making. I want a short story that makes the problem felt rather than summarized. Invent a maritime republic where the Admiralty Court enforces contracts according to a doctrine of contingent determinacy: a clause is legally binding only if the future event it references has been assigned a settled truth-value by the court's panel of logicians. The protagonist, Captain Mira, holds a contract that promises double wages to her crew if a specific storm strikes the eastern reef on the solstice, but the court has just ruled that the storm is neither true nor false — only open. She must decide whether to sail into the reef's path, knowing that if she survives and the storm arrives, the contract may retroactively become enforceable, yet if she dies or the weather clears, she has staked her crew on a proposition that the law refuses to validate. The story should hinge on the moment Mira realizes that her choice to sail might itself be what collapses an open future into a determined past, or might prove that the future remains genuinely unsettled regardless of human action. Do not simply retell Aristotle; build the tension into the institutional rules and Mira's bodily risk. Keep it to a single decisive scene, under about 800 words.
