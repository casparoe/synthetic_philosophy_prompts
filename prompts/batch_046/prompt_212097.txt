Hey — I'm a retired physicist who only got into philosophy a couple years ago, mostly through a reading group with other ex-scientists. We've been going through Smiths moral sentiments book, and there's this debate about sympathy and the impartial spectator that just won't move. One side treats sympathy as imaginative projection: you mentally swap places, run a simulation of the other person's situation, and then compare your simulated feeling to what they actually show. The other side says it's direct affective resonance — more like emotional contagion — and that the imparital spectator isn't some internalized social eye but a rational construct you build by repeatedly stepping back and judging your own passions. The arguments have been circling since the 1980s, every paper cite's the same few passages, everyone concedes Smith uses both metaphors, and yet nobody changes position.

My hunch — maybe because I used to think about measurement problems — is that this stalemate happens because both camps are answering different questions under one heading. One group is asking about the causal mechanism of fellow-feeling (what's actually going on psychologically), while the other is asking about the normative structure of moral approval (how we justify judging a sentiment proper or improper). They think they're disagreeing about Smith's 'sympathy,' but they might just be describing different parts of a feedback loop: an initial affective calibration followed by a representational update that gets socially indexed. So is this disagreement substantive, verbal, or just two disciplines talking past each other? Is there a hidden empirical question — maybe about whether perspective-taking and emotional contagion can actually be dissociated — or is it purely conceptual? And honestly, what would count as progress? A new distinction, a historical discovery about Smith's sources, or is the whole framing ill-posed? I don't want a literature survey; I want a diagnosis of why we're stuck, using what each side actually says.
