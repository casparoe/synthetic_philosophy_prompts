I'm trying to work out my actual commitments about material constitution and persistence, testing my intuitions case by case. The classic statue and its clay: if squashed, the clay survives but the statue does not, so they are not identical. A human being: every cell is eventually replaced, yet I think the same person persists, so a person is constituted by but not identical to any particular body. The ship of Theseus: if planks are gradually replaced over years, the continuously repaired vessel is the original ship; but if the old planks are stored and later reassembled into a ship elsewhere, that yields a different object, not the original. Now consider a "living statue" of genetically engineered moss that maintains a human form through internal feedback loops, with individual cells dying and being replaced constantly; after a decade no original cells remain, yet I think this is still the same statue, sustained by uninterrupted causal-functional organization. But if that same moss is killed, its DNA extracted and preserved, and then years later a new colony is grown from that DNA into the identical form with no continuous biological process linking the two living systems, then I think we have only a perfect replica, not the original statue. Finally, imagine a horse-shaped sculpture constituted not by arranged matter but by a swarm of autonomous drones that hold positions relative to one another, each drone replaced every minute, with coherence maintained by a central algorithm; if that algorithm is copied onto an entirely new swarm in a different location, I think the original sculpture has ceased to exist and a second one has begun—here continuity of the specific material vehicles, or at least their spatiotemporal path, seems to matter in a way it does not for the moss.

These verdicts feel mostly sensible, but I feel real tension: I allow the moss statue to survive total material turnover via internal regulation, yet I deny that the drone sculpture can survive replication of its organizing principle; and I allow a person to survive complete cellular replacement, yet I deny the moss can survive a broken biological chain even when the genetic material is preserved. Is there one precise principle of material constitution—perhaps framed in terms of spatiotemporal continuity, causal dependence, or functional persistence—that delivers all six verdicts? If not, which verdict should I overturn, and why? Also, apply the best principle to a case I have not listed: a statue made of ice that is continuously melted and refrozen in the same mold by an automated machine, with every water molecule replaced from a reservoir over time. Please take a definite position, state the principle precisely with the case that motivates each clause, say exactly which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate, and explain what it predicts for the ice-machine case—without merely surveying the options and leaving the choice to me.
