I'm a software engineer without formal training in philosophy, currently auditing a graduate seminar on epistemic value theory. The instructor has asked me to propose a final exam item that a student could answer in about 90 minutes, but I want it to avoid the standard exercise of summarizing the accuracy-first view—that true belief carries intrinsic value and false belief intrinsic disvalue—since every student has already encountered that framing in the assigned material. Drawing instead on concepts I use in my own work—statistical overfitting, cross-domain generalization failure, and regularization—I want the exam to present a synthetic framework I am tentatively calling "domain-sensitive veritism."

In this framework, epistemic utility is not aggregated globally across all propositions, but computed within bounded "inquiry domains," each with its own noise profile and base-rate structure. A belief can maximize local accuracy inside its home domain yet become systematically misleading when exported to a domain with different statistical properties, analogous to a predictive model with low bias but high variance that fails under distribution shift. The essay question should ask students whether this framing genuinely preserves the core accuracy-first commitment, or whether it collapses into pragmatic encroachment by allowing domain-bound instrumental success to determine the epistemic value of belief.

Please draft the exam prompt itself, a model answer of approximately 600 words, and a marking scheme organized around five specific analytical moves that separate excellent answers from merely competent ones. Each move should carry assigned marks totaling 20, plus a brief one-line note describing the characteristic student error—whether conflating statistical overfitting with ordinary false belief, treating domain boundaries as purely pragmatic, or assuming that invisible error eliminates epistemic responsibility. The question should require students to: (1) explain why global veritism struggles to accommodate cross-domain accuracy collapse without abandoning its intrinsic-value claim; (2) evaluate whether domain-weighting secretly introduces non-epistemic values; (3) assess whether "overfitted" beliefs that remain locally accurate can still be epistemically blameworthy; (4) distinguish variance-driven failure from simple factual error; and (5) defend or reject the claim that domain-sensitive weighting is just regularization applied to belief systems. The prompt must be fully self-contained, should not ask for bibliographies, citations, or reading lists, and should not depend on knowledge of any particular philosopher's published corpus beyond the general conceptual distinctions students should have covered in the seminar.
