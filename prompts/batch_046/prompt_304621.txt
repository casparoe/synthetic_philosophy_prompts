Essay notes: "Sandboxing the Artist — A Dependency-Isolation View"

Thesis: The aesthetic evaluation of an artwork should be treated like executing code in a sandboxed environment. The artifact ought to be assessed on its internal properties, and biographical moral information about the creator should be treated as an external dependency that is imported into evaluation only if the work's own structure explicitly requires it.

Conceptual framework:
- Layer 1 (Artifact): The work itself — its formal structure, emotional dynamics, and internal coherence.
- Layer 2 (Execution Context): The audience's knowledge of the artist's biography, including moral failures.
- Layer 3 (Dependency Tree): The causal and intentional links through which the artist's actions and character are embedded in the work's meaning.

Argument:
1. Moralism — the view that immoral art is necessarily worse art — typically operates by importing Layer 2 into Layer 1 without inspecting Layer 3. This is analogous to importing a software library that the running program never actually invokes: the corruption exists in the environment, but it does not affect the artifact's execution.
2. I distinguish two modes of importation. Explicit importation occurs when the work's meaning or effect depends on the immoral act (for instance, a piece whose authority or emotional force derives from an act of exploitation). Implicit importation occurs when the audience uses biographical knowledge to reinterpret a work that does not itself invoke that knowledge. Only explicit importation should diminish artistic value; implicit importation concerns the ethics of consumption, comparable to deciding whether to use open-source software maintained by someone with objectionable views.
3. I concede that autobiographical or confessional works collapse the sandbox, because Layer 3 is densely connected: the artist's moral character is part of the work's subject matter. I deny that this exception generalizes to all art.
4. I address the "reward" objection — that appreciating excellent work by immoral creators rewards them — by arguing that this is a question of distributive ethics (attention and revenue) rather than aesthetic evaluation. One can consistently judge a work excellent and still refuse to provide the creator with support.

- Does the distinction between aesthetic evaluation and consumption ethics hold, or is it an artificial separation?
- Is the sandbox analogy more misleading than helpful, given that artworks are not executable programs and audiences are not isolated runtimes?
