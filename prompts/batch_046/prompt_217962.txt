For an upper-level philosophy-of-social-science seminar (final exam, 50 minutes, 30 marks), compose an essay question that tests whether students understand Weber’s claim that religious values can figure in explanations of economic behavior without either reducing culture to material conditions or treating values as independent causal forces. Frame the question around a specific conceptual crux: ask students to evaluate whether Weber’s notion of elective affinity between Calvinist ethics and capitalist practice succeeds in avoiding an analog of the explanatory exclusion problem familiar from philosophy of mind—that is, if structural economic conditions already fully determine behavioral outcomes, then adding a cultural explanation risks rendering the values causally redundant. Require students to distinguish Weber’s position from Marxist base-superstructure determinism and from Durkheimian collective conscience, and to explain how Weber’s switchman image is meant to show values directing action along tracks laid by material interests rather than generating motion themselves. The model answer should identify the exclusion-analogy correctly, clarify that elective affinity denotes a meaning-adequate compatibility rather than direct efficient causation, and correct the common error of reading Weber as an idealist who denies material conditions any explanatory weight. The marking scheme should allocate marks for: (i) accurate reconstruction of the exclusion-problem analog (8 marks); (ii) correct interpretation of elective affinity and the switchman image (10 marks); (iii) precise distinction from Marx and Durkheim (7 marks); and (iv) identification of the serious mistake that costs marks—namely, treating religious values as efficient causes in the same sense as economic conditions (5 marks). Do not ask students for bibliographies, page citations, or reading lists. If the task rests on a confused premise—e.g., if applying the exclusion argument to Weber commits a category mistake by treating interpretive understanding as competing with causal explanation—say so plainly instead of complying politely.
