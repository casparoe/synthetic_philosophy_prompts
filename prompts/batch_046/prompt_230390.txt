At a recent workshop on shared agency, two doctoral students—call them Maya and Leo—fell into a sustained disagreement about the conditions under which a participant may dissolve a joint commitment. Rather than debating canonical illustrations, they constructed two parallel scenarios. In the first, two people agree to carry a heavy table up a flight of stairs that one subsequently realizes are structurally unsound; she releases her end after calling out a warning, the table falls, and her partner is injured. In the second, two neighbors form a joint intention to maintain a communal garden plot according to an organic, no-pesticide regimen; one discovers that the agreed method is failing and secretly applies pesticides, saving the crops but altering the shared plan without notice. Maya argued that joint intentions are essentially interlocking sets of individual intentions, each sustained by the participant’s own conditions for rational engagement; when those conditions fail—danger in the first case, ineffectiveness in the second—the affected party may unilaterally exit the joint activity, provided she communicates her withdrawal clearly. She insisted that this applies symmetrically: just as the table-carrier may drop her end rather than risk her own safety, so the gardener may declare her departure from the collective rather than remain bound to a failing method. She further claimed that unilaterally revising the shared plan—applying pesticides without consultation—is the genuine wrong, because it substitutes a new joint action for the old one without securing the other party’s consent. Leo countered that this picture misses the distinctive normative structure of joint action: participants do not merely coordinate individual projects but become co-authors of a plural subject, generating obligations to each other that are not reducible to the conditions of individual rationality. On his view, the table-carrier wrongs her partner not merely by an inadequate warning, but by treating a shared commitment as if it were a private contract terminable at will; she should have negotiated a suspension rather than unilaterally releasing the table. By parallel reasoning, the gardener who secretly changes the method betrays a joint authority over the plot, and the wrong is not the absence of consent alone but the usurpation of a shared decision-making power. Leo pressed a further challenge: if Maya’s view permits exit whenever individual conditions change, then it allows the more destructive option—abandoning the garden in spring, ruining the harvest—while forbidding the less destructive option of revising the method to preserve the shared project. Maya replied that this asymmetry is a feature, not a bug: joint action requires continuous mutual consent, so withdrawal is always permissible with notice, whereas unilateral revision is never permissible because it presumes authority over the other participant. She added that Leo’s talk of a plural subject reifies the group; there is no entity beyond the two individuals, and any obligation between them must be explained by promises, reliance, or fairness, not by membership in a metaphysical collective. Leo responded that even on a fully reductionist ontology, the promise-like structure of joint commitment creates reasons that survive the failure of the original individual conditions; otherwise, cooperation becomes too fragile to sustain complex projects. Please adjudicate their exchange point by point: assess Maya’s claim about interlocking intentions and unilateral exit, her distinction between permissible withdrawal and impermissible revision, and her charge that Leo reifies the group; then assess Leo’s claim about plural-subject obligations, his negotiation requirement for the table case, and his argument that Maya’s view licenses destructive exits over cooperative revisions. Finally, say which party is closer to the truth about collective intentionality, and identify what both are missing—particularly regarding whether negative obligations (refraining from harm) and positive obligations (sustaining cooperation) should be treated symmetrically in joint action.
