The examination question was this:

"In psychiatric classification, the philosopher Ian Hacking argues that human kinds are interactive: the classification affects the people classified, and those changes feed back into the classification itself. Some recent discussions have proposed that this feedback operates at two distinct levels—first-order looping, in which diagnosed individuals alter their self-conception and behavior in response to the label, and taxonomic looping, in which the diagnostic manual itself is revised because the prevalence and symptom profiles generated under the previous version render the old categories epidemiologically unstable. Evaluate whether taxonomic looping is genuinely distinct from first-order looping or whether it collapses into it. In your answer, discuss at least one concrete example—such as the restructuring of personality disorders between DSM-III and DSM-IV, or the broadening of autism spectrum criteria during the 1990s—and explain whether the revision in that case was driven by looped behavior of the classified population or by independent clinical discoveries."

The student submitted the following answer:

"Taxonomic looping is genuinely distinct from first-order looping because the two operate at different levels. First-order looping is individual: once a patient receives a diagnosis of borderline personality disorder, she may internalize the label and begin to exhibit greater emotional dysregulation and impulsivity, thereby confirming the clinician's initial judgment. Taxonomic looping, by contrast, is institutional. It occurs when a committee revises a manual not because of new laboratory findings, but because the population created by the previous criteria produces prevalence data and symptom clusters that no longer fit the taxonomy.

Consider the transition from DSM-III (1980) to DSM-IV (1994) in the personality disorder cluster. Under DSM-III, borderline personality disorder was defined with broad, somewhat impressionistic criteria. By the time of DSM-IV, the criteria had been refined into nine specific items, including marked impulsivity and affective instability—dimensions that had not been present in the DSM-III formulation. This change was not driven by neurobiological discovery. Rather, clinicians observed that large numbers of outpatients displayed partial symptom profiles that did not meet the full DSM-III threshold yet seemed clinically similar. The prevalence data generated under the old system had become epidemiologically unstable, so the taxonomy was adjusted to capture this loop-generated population.

One might object that taxonomic looping is merely first-order looping writ large: the patients changed, and those changes eventually forced the manual to adapt. But this objection conflates causal linkage with conceptual identity. First-order looping modifies the classified individuals; taxonomic looping modifies the classification system. They are causally connected, but conceptually separate.

There is, however, an important limit. Hacking himself denies that psychiatric kinds are purely interactive in the manner of social categories like child abuse. He maintains that psychiatric classifications are anchored in biological reality, and this biological substrate acts as a brake that prevents looping from running entirely free. Consequently, taxonomic looping in psychiatry is always constrained by underlying neurobiology, which means it is less powerful than in purely constructed domains. Thus, while the distinction holds, it should not be overstated."
