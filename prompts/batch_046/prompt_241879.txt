I am preparing a unit for an upper-level philosophy of language course on linguistic relativity, and I want to use Denis Villeneuve's Arrival (2016)—in which linguist Louise Banks learns the heptapod logographic language and gradually acquires a non-linear experience of time—to press beyond the usual slogan that language merely shapes thought. The film seems to dramatize a strong Sapir-Whorf thesis: the heptapods' simultaneous writing system does not merely influence Louise's cognition but apparently restructures her metaphysics of time, allowing her to 'remember' her daughter's life and death before they occur. I would like a reading that takes both the film and the philosophical literature seriously: does Arrival conflate weak linguistic influence with strong determinism, and does it treat the acquisition of heptapod B as a change in conceptual scheme or as a literal alteration in her temporal ontology?

More specifically, I am interested in how the film handles the tension between determinism and agency. If Louise's future knowledge is causally fixed by her linguistic competence, then her decision to conceive and raise a child she knows will die young looks either tragically predetermined or heroically free—but the film does not clearly adjudicate between these readings. How should we understand her choice in light of the Whorfian framework the film invokes? Does the simultaneous, tense-less structure of heptapod B imply that temporal indexicals—now, then, will—are eliminated, and if so, does the film inadvertently rely on a linear, tensed English narrative frame to generate its emotional effect? Finally, where does the film resist being reduced to an illustration of Sapir-Whorf: are there scenes—in the translation sequences with Ian, or in the geopolitical miscommunication that nearly triggers war—where the philosophical idea illuminates the drama, and where does the drama expose a limitation in the idea that argument alone would miss?
