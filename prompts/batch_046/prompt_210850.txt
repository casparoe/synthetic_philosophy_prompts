For my epistemology seminar, I am staging a role-play exchange to sharpen my objections to the classical analysis of knowledge. Please take the role of Edmund Gettier speaking in the first person shortly after publishing your 1963 paper. Your commitments: you maintain that justified true belief is not sufficient for knowledge; you defend your two counterexamples—the case of Smith and Jones with the ten coins, and the case of Smith, Jones, and Brown in Barcelona—using only the conceptual resources available in 1963. You must not invoke reliabilism, causal theories, virtue epistemology, or any other framework developed after your paper. You should speak with the conviction of someone who believes he has overturned a Platonic definition that endured for two millennia, but you must also address my objection precisely rather than dismiss it. Constraints: remain in character without breaking the fourth wall; do not ask clarifying questions—respond directly; and take a definite, committed position with explicit reasons rather than surveying options neutrally.

My opening move: I want to press you on your Ford-or-Barcelona disjunction case. Smith believes that Jones owns a Ford, and from that belief he validly infers that either Jones owns a Ford or Brown is in Barcelona. You claim that Smith is justified in this belief, that it is true because Brown happens to be in Barcelona, and yet that he does not know it. But I think your case fails at the justification step. Smith’s belief in the disjunction is justified only through his belief in the first disjunct, and that belief is false. So he is not genuinely justified in the disjunction—he is reasoning from a false premise. If justification requires that one’s supporting premises be true, then Smith lacks justification altogether. Consequently, your example is not a case of justified true belief without knowledge; it is simply a case of belief that is not justified.

Please respond in character. Defend your counterexample against this false-lemma objection: argue that Smith is indeed justified in the disjunction despite the falsehood of the first disjunct, explain exactly why the invalidity of his premise does not undermine the justification of the inferred belief, and show where my demand for true premises mischaracterizes the structure of justification. Do not retreat to a neutral overview of later theories; take a clear stand and defend it.
