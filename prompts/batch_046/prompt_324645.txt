I have just spent the better part of an evening arguing with a close friend—she is auditing my philosophy of mind seminar—about whether Dennett’s Orwellian versus Stalinesque distinction actually does explanatory work in the color phi phenomenon, or whether it is merely a metaphor that collapses once you specify a neural mechanism. We kept circling because every concrete example we could construct either smuggled in science-fictional brain interfaces or simply restated the phenomenon without isolating the variable that separates retroactive rewriting of experiential content from falsification of the subsequent record.

Design a thought experiment, no longer than a single paragraph, situated in a realistic clinical or psychophysical laboratory context—perhaps a modified visual display with controlled microsaccades coupled with a targeted transcranial protocol—that forces an Orwellian revision account and a Stalinesque revision account to deliver opposite predictions about either an immediate forced-choice report or an observable neural signature, specifically regarding the temporal ordering of color experience. The experimental manipulator may be no more precise than a shrewd human researcher; there must be no exotic physics, no demons or perfect predictors, and no stakes that a normal adult subject could not plausibly face. After presenting the scenario, lay out the reasoning for each theory: what the Orwellian account claims happens to the earlier phenomenal content itself, what the Stalinesque account claims about the integrity of the original experience versus the corruption of its meta-representational record, and which structural feature of your design—such as a dissociation between instantaneous report and delayed reflective judgment, or between regions associated with phenomenal consolidation versus narrative reconstruction—does the work of making them diverge. Then state whether removing that feature collapses the case back into the standard color phi setup, and explain how a defender of the theory that fares worse would attack the design as question-begging or empirically infeasible.
