Consider the institutional patchwork that governs voting in many liberal democracies. In first-past-the-post systems, the secret ballot is legally protected as a space for sincere political expression, yet voters in competitive districts are routinely urged—by campaigns, media guides, and peer norms—to abandon a genuinely preferred but non-viable candidate and vote instead for the “lesser evil” among the front-runners, a switch treated as rational and even obligatory when the stakes are high. At the same time, compulsory-voting regimes such as Australia’s penalize failure to attend a polling place but permit blank or informal ballots, thereby treating physical presence as a baseline civic duty while leaving the content of the vote entirely to individual conscience; voluntary regimes, conversely, frame turnout itself as a supererogatory act of citizenship with no parallel expectation of strategic calculation. The result is a practice in which voting is simultaneously constructed as an expressive right, an instrumental lever for harm prevention, and a minimal duty of democratic membership—with strategic desertion praised in swing districts, criticized as “wasting a vote” in safe ones, and never formally required by law.

What philosophical commitments about democratic agency, moral compromise, and the normative structure of the ballot does this patchwork presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory that renders these three elements—compulsory presence, protected sincerity, and context-dependent strategic abandonment—mutually coherent. Does the practice depend on a hybrid account in which the ballot operates both as a speech-act, expressing political identity, and as a causal intervention, altering electoral outcomes, governed by distinct normative rules in each register? Or can a unified framework—whether purely consequentialist, purely expressive, or purely deontological—accommodate all three? In particular, examine whether the practice implicitly relies on a threshold deontology of voting: a pro tanto duty to vote one’s true preference that is overridden only when the expected harm of a particular outcome crosses a certain magnitude, and ask whether such a threshold can be maintained without collapsing into pure outcome-based reasoning. Distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, and assess whether its parts depend on consistent or on incompatible conceptions of the voter—as author of a public statement versus as causal agent responsible for collective results. Finally, describe what electoral institutions and civic norms would have to change if we replaced this implicit theory with an alternative that either denies any special weight to strategic compromise (treating sincere preference-expression as an absolute duty regardless of district competitiveness) or, conversely, strips voting of all expressive value and reconceives it as a purely instrumental calculation.
