I am writing this for my philosophy of cognitive science seminar, which this term is examining historical inversions in reasoning about design, adaptation, and mental attribution. I want you to draft the joint statement that should emerge from an adversarial collaboration between two interpretive camps on whether Darwin's evolutionary explanation and Turing's behavioral criterion for machine intelligence represent the same kind of inversion—namely, accounting for or recognizing complex competence without appealing to internal comprehension—or whether they are structurally different in ways that matter for philosophy of mind and evaluation.

The two camps are:

(1) The Structural Parallelists, who hold that Darwin's demonstration that functional design accumulates through selection without any designer's understanding, and Turing's proposal that we should replace the question of whether machines think with an operational test of behavioral indistinguishability, are analogous methodological moves. On their view, both legitimately explain or attribute a complex capacity—adaptive functional structure in the evolutionary case, intelligent behavior in the computational case—without requiring access to mental representation, subjective understanding, or an internal point of view.

(2) The Asymmetry Theorists, who grant the surface resemblance but argue the inversions are categorically different. They maintain that Darwin's mechanism offers a genuine causal explanation of how functional competence is historically distributed and accumulated across populations over time, thereby dissolving the need for a designer's mind while retaining real explanatory substance. By contrast, they contend that Turing's inversion relocates evaluation entirely to an external observer and risks collapsing the distinction between genuine cognitive competence and mere behavioral simulation, thereby not explaining intelligence but redefining it away. For this camp, Darwin closes an explanatory gap that Turing merely obscures.

Please produce the collaboration document with the following structure and content:

- A numbered section of agreed claims that both camps can sign without reservation. These should cover what evolutionary selection actually establishes about functional adaptation, what a behavioral evaluation criterion actually tests, and what neither inversion by itself proves about internal mental states.

- A disagreements table or clearly demarcated list stating the precise points of divergence, with each camp's position phrased in wording that camp would accept as an accurate statement of its own view. Do not flatten the disagreement into a straw man.

- Identification of the cruxes—sub-questions such that resolving them would move at least one camp. I take two cruxes to be central. First, whether competence in evolutionary and computational contexts denotes the same kind of functional achievement, or whether evolutionary competence is essentially distributed across populations and diachronic while machine competence is instantiated in a single artifact and synchronic. Second, whether an external behavioral criterion can ever be sufficient for attributing a cognitive capacity, or whether it necessarily leaves open an explanatory gap that Darwin's selection mechanism actually closes.

- For each crux, specify a concrete conceptual case, thought experiment, or line of argument—not dependent on obscure empirical findings—that both sides agree bears on the question, together with each camp's predicted interpretation or outcome for that case.

- A brief assessment of whether part of the dispute is partly verbal: specifically, whether disagreement over terms such as understanding, design, or competence itself explains a portion of the gap, and if so, exactly which portion and why.

The document should be formal and precise enough that both camps could endorse the agreed-claims section, and explicit enough that a reader can locate the exact boundary of remaining disagreement. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, citation requests, or references to specific page numbers; the argument should stand on its conceptual structure alone.
