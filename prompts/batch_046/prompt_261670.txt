I am preparing the final assessment for an upper-level philosophy of mind seminar on the ontology of affect. Rather than asking students to rehearse the familiar taxonomy—cognitivism, Jamesian feeling-theory, and perceptualism—I want an essay question that targets a more granular problem: sustained emotional orientations, such as ambient grief or objectless dread, in which the phenomenal quality persists well beyond both the original judgment that initiated it and the perceptual conditions that originally triggered it, yet remains intentionally directed in a way that resists reduction to mere bodily sensation. The question should ask students to determine whether such recalcitrant affective salience is best accommodated by treating the emotion as a judgment that has outlived its evidential warrant, as a perception operating without its standard stimulus, or as a sui generis feeling-state bearing its own irreducible intentionality. Please write the exam prompt suitable for a 75-minute answer, together with a model answer of roughly 700 words and a marking scheme out of 25 points built around five essential conceptual moves. For each move, specify the marks allocated and the characteristic error that causes students to miss it—for example, conflating phenomenal persistence with propositional residue, or misapplying the transparency condition from perceptual theory to emotional experience. The assessment should reward conceptual precision and argumentative structure rather than recall of specific authors or texts; do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citation requirements.
