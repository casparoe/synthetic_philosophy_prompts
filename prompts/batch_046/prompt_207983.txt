In preparing a long-form essay on the afterlife of classical idealism, I have been studying Berkeley’s master argument against material substance. It strikes me that Berkeley establishes something stronger than mere skepticism about unperceived matter: he demonstrates that the very idea is internally unstable, since any attempt to conceive of an object existing independently of all perception necessarily imports perceptual content into the conception itself. If that reasoning holds, then materialism is not an empirically falsified theory but a conceptual confusion—comparable to asking for the weight of Tuesday. Given this, why does contemporary philosophy of mind continue to treat physicalism as a live, substantive metaphysical option rather than as a position already dissolved at the conceptual level? Is the persistence of the debate best explained by modern structural realists having developed an indirect, relational conception of physical properties that avoids Berkeley’s direct-conception trap, or does a deeper explanatory commitment—perhaps regarding the causal closure of physical processes—allow materialists to concede Berkeley’s point about direct conception while preserving an ontology that never claimed to be directly conceivable in the first place?
