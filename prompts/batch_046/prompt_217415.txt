I was discussing with a friend last night whether honoring sunk costs is always irrational, and our argument quickly moved past the usual business-school examples into something more structurally puzzling. We imagined a municipality that has spent two decades and massive public funds on an ambitious underground rail loop; the engineering is now obsolete, the projected ridership has collapsed, and finishing the project would require further debt, yet the citizens have consistently voted to fund it precisely because they do not want prior sacrifices to have been 'for nothing.' My friend argued that any decision to continue based on those past expenditures is definitionally a sunk-cost fallacy—rational practical reasoning must be forward-looking, and backward-looking attachments are either emotional distortions or disguised forward-looking commitments that should be articulated as such. I countered that in collective contexts past investment can generate genuine present obligations—something like a promissory or fiduciary duty to those who contributed taxes and labor—so that honoring the cost is not a cognitive error but a form of diachronic consistency that practical rationality ought to accommodate rather than dismiss.

Elias should defend the view that there are structurally rational cases for honoring sunk costs, yet he must avoid collapsing into unreflective attachment or treating past expenditures as direct reasons without a principled account of how backward-looking considerations enter rational deliberation.
