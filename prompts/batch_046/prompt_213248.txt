A colleague in our philosophy of science seminar summarized Van Fraassen's Reflection principle for me as follows: the principle requires that if an agent now expects her future credence in a proposition to be r, then her present credence in that proposition should already be r. He added that Van Fraassen therefore uses the principle to defend an extreme conservatism about prior probabilities—once initial credences are fixed, learning from experience becomes impossible without incoherence—and that the standard Bayesian objection is that reflection illegitimately treats expected future belief change as a kind of present expert testimony, thereby confusing epistemic humility with probabilistic irrationality.

I am unsure which parts of this are accurate. Is the core formulation correct? Does reflection actually entail that belief revision is impossible, or is there a more nuanced relationship between anticipated change and present coherence? Is Van Fraassen committed to the strong claim that learning is ruled out, and does the objection my colleague describes genuinely capture why Bayesians reject the principle?
