Write the desiderata a theory of historical explanation must meet if it is to avoid both hindsight bias—the retrospective treatment of actual outcomes as overdetermined—and the narrative fallacy—the imposition of coherent causal arcs on events that may lack unified underlying structure. Specify the phenomena the theory must explain, including why some historical events feel inevitable in retrospect despite having been genuinely open, and why competing narratives can be equally coherent yet mutually incompatible. List the distinctions it must preserve: between explaining why an event occurred and explaining why it seemed inevitable; between path-dependent processes, in which small early differences compound, and threshold effects, in which outcomes flip discretely at critical points; and between narrative coherence and actual causal efficacy. Include the constraints it must respect from cognitive psychology and historiographical practice, particularly regarding the archival silence problem—the fact that the absence of records for counterfactual paths must not be treated as evidence that those paths were unavailable, and that any assessment of historical openness must be made without retrospective knowledge of which path was taken. Identify which desiderata are non-negotiable and which are preferences of particular camps, such as whether macro-historical determinism is required or whether the theory must permit genuine contingency at the level of individual agency. Flag any pair of requirements you suspect are jointly unsatisfiable, especially any tension between the demand for causal specificity and the demand for openness to counterfactual alternatives. Then briefly indicate how a counterfactual-dependence account, a mechanistic-process account, and a hermeneutic account each fare against the list. Do not provide bibliographies, citations, or reading lists; the specification itself is the deliverable.
