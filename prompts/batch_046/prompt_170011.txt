I'm taking an upper-level course on moral philosophy in wartime, and our discussion turned to G.E.M. Anscombe's well-known pamphlet criticizing Oxford's decision to honor President Truman for his role in authorizing the atomic bombings of Hiroshima and Nagasaki. Several students in my discussion group interpreted Anscombe's core argument as a particularly stringent version of the proportionality principle: specifically, that the bombings constituted murder rather than legitimate warfare because the number of non-combatant deaths exceeded any plausible military gain by such a vast quantitative margin—perhaps on the order of hundreds of thousands of innocent lives against a comparatively modest strategic objective—that the act falls outside the bounds of just war reasoning altogether.

From this reading, I started constructing what I thought was a formal reconstruction of her position. It seems to me that if Anscombe's condemnation is genuinely rooted in a proportionality failure of this magnitude, then her argument should be vulnerable to empirical counterarguments about the projected casualties of Operation Downfall, and that defenders of the bombings could in principle salvage the moral status of the decision by showing that the ratio of lives saved to lives lost actually favors the bombing. But this makes her position sound like a contingent historical claim rather than a categorical moral judgment, which feels at odds with the strength of her condemnation.

So my question is this: is there a specific quantitative threshold—say, a precise ratio of innocent harm to military good—implicit in Anscombe's reasoning that transforms a disproportionate act of war into the specific moral category of murder? And if not, does her argument instead depend on a metaphysical claim about the intentional structure of the act—namely, that one cannot intend the death of innocents even as a foreseen side effect without violating an absolute deontological constraint? I want to understand whether her critique of Truman can be separated from her Thomistic account of human action, or whether accepting her normative conclusion necessarily commits one to a particular metaphysics of intention.
