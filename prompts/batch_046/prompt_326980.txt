Paul distinguishes two ways experiences transform you: epistemically you can't know what it's like beforehand, and personally your core preferences shift. The epistemic problem is basically Jackson's knowledge argument applied to life decisions—you lack the phenomenal concept needed to evaluate the outcome. Because you don't know what it's like, you can't assign a subjective value using your current utility function. The personal transformation part means the agent who chooses isn't the agent who lives with the result, so there's no stable self to do the comparing. Paul says we should just go with what we care about now, like curiosity. But if values are incommensurable across the transformation, then caring-about-now is just one more value that gets replaced, so her advice collapses into emptiness. She avoids this, I think, by adopting a phenomenal concept strategy: she limits the epistemic gap to first-person acquaintance, leaving personal value change as a separate, non-rational shift. So the incommensurability worry only bites if you wrongly conflate epistemic and personal transformation into one big problem.
