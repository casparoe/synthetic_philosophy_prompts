Consider the institutional practice—spanning clinical medicine, criminal law, and civil administration—of treating a patient who has undergone complete corpus callosotomy as a single legal and moral agent. Despite well-documented cases in which each hemisphere sustains independent perceptual awareness, preference formation, and motor initiation, the patient receives one medical record, one informed-consent signature, one criminal liability, and one civic identity; the system never bifurcates into dual subjects, even when hemispheres generate conflicting responses in controlled lateralized tasks. What view of consciousness, personal identity, and the conditions for agency must this practice presuppose in order to be coherent? Reconstruct the implicit philosophical theory, distinguishing what it strictly requires—perhaps a brainstem-based integration criterion, a pragmatic convention compatible with multiple metaphysical accounts, or a substantive unity of consciousness—from what it merely happens to fit. Assess whether the practice is internally consistent, given that it permits hemispheric conflict to be studied experimentally without triggering any legal or administrative division of the subject. Then specify what institutional structures—consent protocols, liability rules, or civic registration—would have to be redesigned if the underlying view were abandoned in favor of a model that genuinely recognized divided subjects. Please also indicate what empirical finding or philosophical argument would change your verdict about the implicit theory, and point out where your reasoning is most likely to fail.
