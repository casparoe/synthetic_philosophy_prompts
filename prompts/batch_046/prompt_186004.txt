ive been trying to map why the james vs clifford debate on the ethics of belief never actually resolves in seminars. people keep replaying the shipowner case and religious belief, but i wonder if the stall is deeper: maybe its not about evidence standards but about whether belief is even the kind of mental state you can have obligations toward. james seems to treat belief as partly volitional commitment, clifford treats it like a cognitive tracker that should just follow probabilities. heres a weird twist case: you have strong but not conclusive evidence your partner is cheating, and believing it would destroy the relationship but witholding belief lets you act supportively. is that james-style pragmatism, or just self-deception? and does it help to distinguish belief proper from what we could call an epistemic stance -- a practical orientation that guides action without being a full doxastic commitment? i want a diagnosis: is the debate stuck because theyre talking past each other using belief for two different mental kinds, or is there a real normative crux about when practical costs can enter reasoning? and be honest -- is there any case or distinction that would actually move it, or is it just bedrock intuition?
