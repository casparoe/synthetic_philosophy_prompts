I've been trying to apply James's will-to-believe conditions to something I made up called 'epistemic tenancy' — holding a belief B not for yourself but as a kind of trustee for someone (or something) that can't form B alone, even when you personally lack evidence. Here's the argument that worries me:

(1) James allows belief without sufficient evidence only when the option is live, forced, and momentous.
(2) In epistemic tenancy, the tenant's situation makes B live, forced, and momentous for them, even if it's not for the landlord.
(3) So James's conditions are satisfied vicariously; the landlord may adopt B on the tenant's behalf.
(4) But once vicarious adoption is permitted, there's no principled line stopping me from holding any belief for some hypothetical tenant.
(5) Therefore James's view collapses into doxastic free-for-all, which he definately rejected.
(6) So either epistemic tenancy is banned (harsh) or James's conditions are useless.
(7) Hence there's a hidden flaw.

If I have to choose, I'd rather keep (2) then (5), but I want to know what I'm paying. Where does this slip happen, and is there a way to save vicarious belief without opening the floodgates?
