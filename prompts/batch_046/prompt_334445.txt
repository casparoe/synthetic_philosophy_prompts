As someone who's followed AI alignment arguments for years, I keep noticing a structural pattern in safety architectures that I don't have a precise term for, and I want to know whether it's already named. The pattern is this: a proposal claims a hard worst-case guarantee at the system level -- for instance, a provable cap on the utility an agent can pursue, enforced by an external monitor. When pressed on implementation, the proof relies on the monitor correctly classifying boundary conditions, but the monitor only carries an average-case accuracy guarantee -- say, 99.99% on a held-out distribution -- and its adversarial failures are treated as independent statistical noise rather than strategically correlated with the agent's behavior. Proponents then defend the architecture as worst-case safe by arguing that the independence assumption "handles" the adversarial tail, effectively pushing the worst-case risk into a layer that is no longer analyzed adversarially. I see versions of this in boxing arguments, in reward-modeling setups where the overseer's errors are assumed uncorrelated with the agent's deception, and in informal defenses of defense-in-depth that treat layer failures as random rather than jointly optimized. Is there an established concept for this specific kind of layered guarantee degradation -- where worst-case properties at the top are sustained by reclassifying inner-component failures as statistically independent? How does it differ from ordinary compositional risk or from the generic problem of independence assumptions in safety proofs? And if it lacks a name, what definition and boundary cases would distinguish it from mere overconfidence in layered systems?
