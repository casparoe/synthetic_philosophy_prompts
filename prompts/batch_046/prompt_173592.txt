I have just finished a rather long and somewhat fractious debate with a friend over a digital community currency—specifically, a token system with no physical form, no state backing, and no commodity anchor, whose value and even existence depend entirely on the continuous active agreement of its users. She maintains that this is no different in principle from a banknote or from categories like gender: in every case, the thing is real only because a group collectively treats it as real, and if that shared attitude were withdrawn, there would be nothing left but meaningless marks or arbitrary behaviours. I argued that there is a philosophically important distinction between kinds that can persist as brute physical residues—paper, metal, bodily features—once collective belief lapses, and kinds whose being is entirely constituted by that belief in the present tense, so that when recognition stops, there is literally no object remaining, not even a leftover. She accused me of sneaking in an unargued preference for materialism, and I could not see whether she was right. Could you advise me, treating this not as economics but as a question about how collective agreement creates and sustains kinds, on whether there is a defensible distinction here? In particular, I want to know what conditions a community must satisfy to sustain a kind with no non-social substrate, whether obligations denominated in such a kind have a different normative structure from those tied to physically anchored tokens, and whether the absence of any residual brute object changes what it means for the kind to be abolished. Please respond in British English.
