As a retired experimental physicist who has recently begun studying philosophy of science and decision theory, I am facilitating a ninety-minute workshop for twelve graduate students in philosophy of science. I want them to feel, rather than merely discuss, the tension in Pascal's mugging: the point at which expected-utility reasoning seems to compel acceptance of outcomes with astronomically small probabilities and correspondingly large payoffs, while intuitive judgment resists. To avoid replaying the standard formulation, please design an in-class simulation built around an invented scenario—a hypothetical "resonance cascade" technology with an independently estimated one-in-ten-billion chance of unlocking unlimited carbon-neutral energy, but which requires each participant to pledge tangible, non-monetary personal resources, specifically a written commitment of future research hours, should they choose to "fund" it.

The activity should assign roles—investor, auditor, and skeptic—and include rules that require every participant to record their own prior credence, then update it using staged ambiguous-evidence cards distributed at fixed intervals, so that the pressure to pledge arises from their own calculated numbers rather than from an external authority. Include a timeline within the ninety minutes, a symbolic-token payoff scheme that tracks cumulative commitments without using real money, and a structured debrief that maps specific moments of the game onto the formal structure of the mugging: the scaling of probability, the assumption of linear utility, and the breakdown of comparative judgment at extreme values.

Be explicit in the debrief about which psychological feature of the paradox—specifically, the collapse of linear utility scaling when outcomes approach unbounded value—the simulation necessarily fails to reproduce and that I must supply verbally. Also include an honest prediction of what will predictably go wrong the first time this is run and how to correct for it.

Finally, please state how confident you are in the pedagogical effectiveness of this design, and identify precisely the point about which you are least certain—most likely whether the tangible-pledge mechanism genuinely captures the normative structure of the mugging or instead introduces an emotional confound that distorts the reasoning.
