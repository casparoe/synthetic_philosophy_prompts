A colleague in a philosophy-of-science seminar summarised Ian Hacking's looping-effect argument, especially regarding psychiatric classification, in roughly the following way. Psychiatric categories—such as borderline personality disorder or attention-deficit hyperactivity disorder—function as interactive kinds rather than indifferent kinds like chemical elements. Once a diagnostic label is applied, the people classified can become aware of the criteria and begin to model their own behaviour and emotional reports around those criteria. Clinicians then observe these adjusted presentations, revise the diagnostic manual's definitions, and the cycle repeats, so that psychiatric kinds become moving targets that shift with each revision. From this, my colleague inferred that Hacking regards manuals like the DSM as essentially historical records of negotiation between clinicians and patients, lacking the stability of biological taxonomy, and that looping effects turn psychiatric diagnosis into a self-fulfilling prophecy: the category creates the clinical reality it purports only to discover.

This sounds mostly right, but I worry that it makes the feedback loop sound too deliberate—as if patients consciously perform symptoms to match the manual—and that it exaggerates Hacking's anti-realism by suggesting he thinks there is no underlying dysfunction being mapped at all. Would Hacking himself accept the conclusion that psychiatric classification is merely a negotiation record with no claim to describing real dysfunction?
