I have been thinking on my own about what is often called the meta-problem of consciousness — not whether physical processes can produce experience, but why people report that there is an unbridgeable gap between brain mechanisms and what it is like to be aware. Without an academic background, I have been trying to make sense of this by distinguishing two possibilities that seem different to me: there is what I call the "reporting gap," which concerns why our first-person descriptions make consciousness sound inexplicable even when a physical explanation is available; and there is the "modeling gap," which concerns whether a complete physical model of cognition could ever generate or contain the kind of first-person judgment that says "this experience remains unexplained." I am unsure whether these collapse into one another or whether one is prior.

Given that distinction, I want to know where focused effort should actually go. Suppose someone has a limited span of work — perhaps a single sustained project or a small group with a few years — and wants to make genuine progress on the meta-problem rather than just survey it. I see three directions that might matter, but I need help prioritizing them.

First, investigating whether the reporting gap is purely an artifact of cognitive biases and linguistic framing — for instance, whether people systematically misjudge the completeness of physical explanations only when those explanations are presented in the third person, and whether this can be corrected without addressing consciousness itself.

Second, determining whether the modeling gap can only be assessed after the so-called easy problems — neural correlates of awareness, global integration, predictive processing — are fully solved, so that we can check whether any explanatory residue specifically attaches to first-person reports.

Third, assessing whether resolving the meta-problem carries practical weight for ethics and policy — particularly whether arguments for the moral status of animals or artificial systems depend on phenomenal consciousness being a genuinely distinct property, or whether they collapse if the hard problem's appearance turns out to be a reporting artifact.

Please rank these three directions in order of importance for that limited effort, and defend the ranking. Specify the criteria you are using — such as tractability, dependence of other questions on this one, or real-world stakes — and explain the trade-offs: what you gain and lose by putting one first. Also tell me what kind of finding or argument would cause you to reorder them. I am not asking for a bibliography, a survey of the literature, or citations; I want a concrete, defended agenda that tells me where the work should go and why.
