I have been working through Douglas Hofstadter’s treatment of superrationality in symmetric decision problems—particularly the Platonia dilemma introduced in Metamagical Themas and the Luring Lottery that Scientific American actually conducted in the early 1980s—and I keep returning to a conceptual tension that the standard expositions do not seem to resolve. In the Platonia dilemma, twenty symmetric agents face a one-shot choice about whether to send a telegram, with superrational reasoning prescribing a uniform 1/20 randomisation so that exactly one message is expected to arrive. The Luring Lottery, by contrast, invites contestants to submit postcards bearing any number of entries, with the prize divided by the total number submitted; here the optimal strategy depends on beliefs about others’ behaviour, generating a runaway feedback loop rather than a stable symmetric equilibrium. I want to understand whether these two cases reveal genuinely different structures of rationality, or whether the lottery dynamics are merely the Platonia logic pushed into an environment with observable entry counts.

To probe this, I have constructed a hybrid thought experiment—let us call it the Noisy Platonia—in which the payoff structure remains identical to Hofstadter’s original scenario (exactly one telegram must arrive for anyone to win), but each of the twenty potential participants receives an independent noisy signal about the actual number of active players. The signals are drawn from a distribution centred on twenty, yet with sufficient variance that no agent can be certain of the true pool size. The question is whether superrationality, which derives its force from the perfect symmetry of indistinguishable reasoning agents, can survive this epistemic asymmetry. Does the noisy signal break the symmetry argument that justifies the 1/20 randomisation, or does it merely transform the superrational calculus into a Bayesian expectation over possible group sizes? Moreover, if the Noisy Platonia is iterated—agents play successive rounds, observing only whether a prize was awarded but not who sent the message—the dynamics begin to resemble the Luring Lottery’s feedback structure, since agents might adjust their probabilities based on inferred participation rates. Could you please explain, using American English, how the conceptual foundations of superrationality in one-shot symmetric dilemmas relate to the runaway dynamics observed in entry-based lotteries, and specifically whether the Noisy Platonia scenario exposes a hidden dependency in Hofstadter’s argument upon perfect common knowledge of the participant pool?
