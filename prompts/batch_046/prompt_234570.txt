State-sponsored Holocaust education and memorial-museum design typically present the event as both historically singular—resistant to comparison with other mass atrocities—and as an instructive case study in the dangers of modern bureaucratic rationality, a framing closely linked to Zygmunt Bauman's analysis. Treating this institutional practice as philosophically committed rather than merely rhetorical, reconstruct the implicit theory of historical explanation, moral uniqueness, and generalizable moral knowledge it requires. Does the practice depend on a strict separation between causal explanation, where modern bureaucratic mechanisms play a generic role, and moral evaluation, where the event remains sui generis? Or does that separation collapse, forcing the institution either to abandon its universalist pedagogical aim or to surrender its claim to incomparable moral gravity?
