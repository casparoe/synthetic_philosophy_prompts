I am preparing a brief philosophical profile for a seminar on practical reason and indirect normativity, and I should like an audit of whether my commitments cohere. Here is roughly where I have landed.

First, I accept indirect consequentialism for institutional evaluation: a policy or social arrangement is justified if it is the output of a decision procedure that, followed generally, tends to produce the best outcomes, not if it maximises welfare in this particular case.

Second, I hold that any adequate model of idealised deliberation should correct only for ignorance and logical error, leaving agents' actual attachments, loyalties, and particular commitments entirely intact; I reject the veil of ignorance because stripping away those relationships erases what partly constitutes practical identity.

Third, I maintain that direct reasons are genuinely irreducible in personal morality: that this person is my child, or that I promised this specific friend, gives me a reason that does not derive from any general optimising procedure.

Fourth, I think that reflective equilibrium, carried out with full information about one's actual circumstances but without altering one's motivational structure, is the authoritative standard for personal norms.

Fifth, I also believe that upon careful reflection any apparently direct reason can be reconstructed as the conclusion of an indirect procedure about which patterns of attachment, social roles, or character traits are worth sustaining across a life.

Please examine these five positions and tell me which pairs are in real tension, which pairs only seem to conflict but dissolve on closer inspection, and for each genuine tension how severe it is and what the cheapest revision would cost. Then advise me which single change to the set would restore the most coherence. Write your answer in continuous prose only — no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables — as in a letter or an email, and please use American English.
