i'm a phd student in phil of science and decision theory and next week i'm presenting in our dept seminar on Good's theorem and why i think it's been misapplied in debates about scientific funding and inquiry. my thesis is that Good's result only holds for agents with fully specified utility functions over outcomes, but in actual scientific practice researchers are choosing between incommensurable theoretical frameworks where they can't rank-order outcomes in advance. so free information can actually hurt—not because of computation costs, but because learning new data can destabilize the framework you're using to interpret future data. i'm calling this the "framework instability objection" and it's kind of my own twist on the usual computional complexity critiques.

the audience will be about 12 people: my advisor (decision theorist who knows Good inside out), two phil sci postdocs who are big on Kuhn and incommensurability, a visiting economist who works on value of information in markets, and a bunch of grad students in metaphysics/epistemology who don't know decision theory well but will ask basic questions. the postdocs will probably love the incommensurability angle but attack me for not being radical enough. the economist will defend the standard decision-theoretic framing. the metaphysics students will ask whether this applies to moral belief formation or just science.

can you prep me for the Q&A? i need the objections in roughly the order they'll come, phrased like they'd actually say them, with what i should concede up front, what i should hold, and where my reply is weakest. include the question i'm hoping nobody asks—probably something about whether framework instability just collapses into a standard Newcomb-style problem or violates the fixed-decision-rule assumption in a trivial way?

for context my argument sketch starts with the standard proof that free information can't hurt a rational agent with a fixed decision rule, then i argue that in science the "action set" isn't fixed: adopting new data can change what experiments you think are valid, which violates the theorem's assumption that your decision procedure is stable across worlds. i don't cite specific page numbers but i do reference Good's original result and the usual Bayesian updating framing. also please don't ask me for a reading list—i just need the prep sheet.
