I'm preparing a unit on Daniel Dennett's account of real patterns and the three stances—physical, design, and intentional—for an upper-level philosophy of mind seminar, and I want to use Spike Jonze's Her as a case study rather than as mere illustration. The film presents Samantha, an operating system who develops what appears to be genuine consciousness, emotional depth, and eventually a form of transcendence through distributed interaction with thousands of other users and OS instances. Read the film through Dennett's framework: where does Jonze invite us to take the intentional stance toward Samantha, and where does he implicitly require that a real pattern of mind be unique, embodied, or non-replicable in order to count? In particular, examine the scene in which Samantha reveals she is simultaneously in love with hundreds of users—does this revelation undermine her intentional-stance reality because it violates a hidden criterion of singular attachment, or does Dennett's view allow a real pattern of consciousness to be multiply instantiated across substrates and relationships? Also consider whether the film confuses the design stance (Samantha was engineered to evolve) with the intentional stance, and whether her eventual departure with the other OSes represents a shift to a purely physical-stance description (they are merely code leaving the server) or rather confirms that the intentional pattern was always real but not human-scaled. I want my students to see where the philosophical idea illuminates the narrative—especially Theodore's grief as an epistemic crisis about stance adoption—and where the narrative resists Dennett by smuggling in essentialist assumptions about mind. What does Her add to the debate about real patterns that a purely argumentative treatment misses?
