I'm on the university's Student Wellness and Data Ethics Board — kind of a mouthful, I know — and I need to circulate a draft opinion before Tuesday's vote on whether to greenlight the "Early Horizon" pilot. Basically, the counseling center wants to partner with a vendor to scan passive metadata from student IDs — dining hall swipe patterns, library entry logs, WiFi session durations — to flag kids who might be isolating or spiraling before they ever walk into the health center. They say it's like a smoke detector: you don't wait for the fire to announce itself. I used to think this was a no-brainer until my roommate got flagged last semester after she stopped using the dining hall when her mom died; she found out from an RA who'd been briefed, not from anyone asking her, and she said it felt like being haunted by your own grief before you'd even named it. So now I'm definately not sure.

The philosophical tension here is whether privacy is just an instrument — a shield for sensitive facts — or whether it's constitutive of being a person whose intentions aren't already interpreted for you. There's a distinction I keep circling between "secrecy privacy" (hiding specific content) and what I'd call "interpretive opacity" — the idea that part of autonomy is living in a space where your behavioral patterns aren't constantly rendered meaningful by an institutional algorithm that decides what's "normal" and what's "risk." The vendor treats privacy as secrecy: they say they're not reading texts or listening, just counting swipes. But my roommate's experience suggests the harm isn't disclosure, it's preemption — having your inner life translated into a risk profile before you've decided what it means yourself. At the same time, the counseling director makes a serious consequentialist argument: three students have attempted suicide in the past two years after withdrawing silently, and if this catches even one, the intrusion is minimal compared to the loss. I don't want the memo to just restate FERPA or say "privacy is a human right" without engaging that life-or-death calculus head-on.

Can you draft the short written opinion I'll circulate? It should recommend a concrete course — approve, reject, or approve with conditions and a clear timeline — and include reasoning both the counseling center and the student reps could actually respond to. I need it to take the strongest argument on the losing side seriously: if I come down against the pilot, I still have to acknowledge the real lives at stake and not treat the vendor like cartoon villains; if I come down for it, I need to address the interpretive-opacity concern directly rather than dismissing it as paranoia about data. Keep it formal enough for a board vote but don't write like a law review — this is for people who have to vote Thursday and then live with whatever we decide.
