In a philosophy-of-biology course I'm currently taking, a recurring argument against the ontological reality of species relies on a cartographic analogy that I find intuitively compelling but philosophically slippery. The claim runs as follows: defining a biological species is structurally identical to drawing national borders on a map. Political boundaries, the argument notes, rarely coincide with deep geographic or ecological discontinuities — rivers flow through them, mountain ranges straddle them — and their precise locations are fixed by convention, treaty, and administrative convenience rather than by any intrinsic feature of the terrain. By parallel reasoning, reproductive isolation, morphological clustering, and interbreeding barriers are said to be merely convenient lines that taxonomists impose on continuous evolutionary variation. The intended payoff is anti-realist: species are pragmatic fictions, not natural kinds that carve nature at its joints.

I want you to audit this analogy rigorously rather than accepting it at face value. Please begin with an explicit correspondence table: identify precisely what in the source domain — cartographers, surveyed terrain, treaty negotiations, border enforcement, and the absence of "natural" frontiers — is meant to map onto hybrid zones, gene-flow gradients, phylogenetic branching patterns, and the practice of taxonomic revision. Then isolate the disanalogies that actually matter for the anti-realist conclusion. Does the analogy quietly import an assumption of deliberate human agency — that borders are placed by decision-makers with interests — that has no evolutionary counterpart? Does it presuppose that there is no underlying structural reality beneath the lines, and if so, does evolutionary history provide a branching, hierarchical structure that physical geography lacks? Separate the cosmetic mismatches from the ones that undermine the inference from "boundaries are conventional" to "species are unreal."

Finally, assess whether the anti-realist conclusion survives once these breaks are fully on the table, or whether the analogy was doing the bulk of the argumentative work all along. If the map comparison collapses, suggest a more honest substitute — for instance, treating species as nodes in a branching river delta rather than lines on a flat plane — and explain whether that replacement actually supports the anti-realist thesis or instead reveals that evolutionary branching supplies exactly the kind of "ground truth" that makes species more like natural joints than like political borders. I am not looking for a bibliography or a literature review; I want a conceptual audit that shows which imported features are load-bearing and which are misleading.
