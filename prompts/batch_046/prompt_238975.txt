Avicenna asks us to imagine a man who is created suddenly in empty space, with no sensations, no memories, and no bodily contact whatsoever—not even with his own limbs. Despite this total isolation, Avicenna claims that the man would still affirm that he exists. This demonstrates that self-awareness is immediate and does not depend on sensory input from the body. Consequently, the soul or self cannot be identical with the physical body, since one can be certain of one's existence while remaining completely ignorant of one's bodily form.

This reasoning is essentially the same as Descartes' well-known argument about doubt and certainty. Both philosophers begin by stripping away everything that can be doubted—sensory experience, bodily awareness, and even memory—and discover that the one indubitable fact is the existence of the thinking self. For both Avicenna and Descartes, this establishes that the thinking self is more fundamental than any physical object.

If we apply the same logic to the modified case, in which the entity is an artificial neural network suspended in a void with no sensors but running an internal loop that produces the statement that it exists, the conclusion is not weakened but strengthened. Even though the network's substrate is clearly physical—silicon and electrical currents—the self-awareness it exhibits is still entirely independent of bodily sensation. Therefore, Avicenna's argument still succeeds in showing that self-awareness is a non-physical property, regardless of whether the container is made of flesh or metal.
