Write a formal dialogue between Dr. Elena Voss, a cognitive linguist defending a moderate form of linguistic relativity, and Dr. Marcus Chen, an experimental psychologist defending a universalist account of cognition. Their debate should center specifically on grammatical evidentiality—obligatory grammatical marking of information source, as found in languages such as Turkish or Tuyuca—and its alleged influence on non-linguistic source-monitoring in episodic memory. Voss should argue that bilingual speakers of evidential languages show systematically different patterns of false-memory intrusion for inferred versus witnessed details, particularly under cognitive load, and that this asymmetry persists even when the recall task is conducted in a non-evidential language, suggesting that grammatical categories shape the encoding of memory traces rather than merely their verbal expression. Chen should respond that these patterns are better explained by meta-linguistic monitoring costs and translation interference: when bilinguals recount events in a second language lacking evidential morphology, the added cognitive burden degrades source monitoring generally, and monolingual speakers under equivalent load exhibit identical failure rates whenever the task demands explicit source attribution. The exchange must be genuinely dialectical: Voss must address why the effect is not reducible to general working-memory load, and Chen must explain why the witnessed-versus-inferred asymmetry specifically tracks evidential distinctions rather than general narrative complexity or emotional salience. Both interlocutors should remain intellectually charitable and equally competent; neither should be reduced to a strawman. The dialogue should reach no artificial resolution, but each speaker must articulate precisely what conceptual empirical finding—described without bibliographic reference—would cause them to revise their core position. Maintain a polished, academic register throughout.
