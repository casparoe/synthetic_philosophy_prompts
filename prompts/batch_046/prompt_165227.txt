I have recently been drawn into a sharp disagreement with a colleague in my interdisciplinary reading group—three philosophers of decision theory, three computer scientists who work on distributed algorithms, and two cognitive scientists—after I defended cooperation in a one-shot strategic interaction and was accused of sneaking in telepathy or altruism. I now need to teach the concept of superrationality to the same group next week: cooperation justified not by causal expectation but by logical correlation between structurally identical reasoning procedures. The session is fifty minutes, whiteboard only, and I want it planned rather than delivered. The audience knows causal decision theory but has no formal background in algorithmic self-reference, and the philosophers are already skeptical that non-causal reasoning can be rational without collapsing into evidential decision theory.

Plan the explanation in the following sections, with approximate word counts:

1. Dependency chain and introduction order (about 180 words): Specify the exact sequence in which the pieces must be introduced—distinguishing causal influence from logical correlation, establishing what transparency of reasoning structure means for algorithmically identical agents, and then formulating superrational cooperation as a constraint on the decision procedure rather than a prediction about outcomes. For each step, state what depends on the previous one, and identify precisely where in the sequence the philosophers’ resistance (that this is merely evidential reasoning in disguise) should be anticipated and neutralized.

2. The carrying example and its design (about 200 words): Construct one concrete, non-standard scenario to carry the idea—do not use the Prisoner’s Dilemma. Design a one-shot interaction between two agents with fully transparent, algorithmically identical decision architectures who must each independently choose whether to commit a shared computational resource or withhold it, where mutual commitment yields high joint value and unilateral commitment yields severe loss for the committer with no gain for the other. Specify what properties the example must possess (symmetry of reasoning access, absence of causal links between choices, observable logical structure of the procedure) and what it must lack (repeated interaction, communication channels, modified payoff structures that import altruism). Justify why this example prevents the audience from attributing cooperation to reputation mechanisms or hidden causal mediation.

3. Misconception map and defusion points (about 220 words): Identify four predictable misconceptions for this audience: (a) that superrationality requires mental-state identity rather than structural isomorphism of decision procedures; (b) that logical correlation is reducible to hidden causal correlation via a common past or shared environment; (c) that the argument proves too much by mandating cooperation in every symmetric game, including those where defection remains dominant even under correlation; and (d) that this is only a rebranding of evidential decision theory applied to social choice. For each, specify the precise point in the sequence where it should be headed off, the counter-example or conceptual distinction that defuses it, and why delaying that defusion would allow the misconception to harden.

4. The check for understanding (about 100 words): Propose one concise question or brief exercise—answerable without external resources and requiring no citation of literature—that I can pose at the end to determine whether the group understands superrational cooperation as a constraint on reasoning rather than an empirical claim about behavior. The check should distinguish genuine comprehension from mere verbal agreement with the conclusion.

5. Boundaries and omissions (about 100 words): State explicitly what must be left out at this level—such as formal logical counterfactual treatments of game-theoretic correlation, detailed comparison with Newcomb-style problems, or debates over the uniqueness of rational decision rules—and justify each omission by reference to the audience’s composition and the fifty-minute limit.

Write in a formal, polished register. Do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations. Do not explain the concept to me; produce only the teaching plan.
