Read George Orwell’s Animal Farm through a philosophical lens on populism and the will of the people. I want you to treat the novel not merely as an allegory of totalitarianism, but as a dramatization of the conceptual tension between the will of all—the aggregate of private preferences—and the general will, a common good that may conflict with every individual desire. Focus your reading on the pigs’ progressive claim to represent the animals, the alteration of the Seven Commandments, and the final scene in which the animals cannot distinguish pig from human. At each stage, ask whether the farm’s politics instantiate a corruption of democratic representation or reveal that populist identification is structurally incapable of maintaining the distinction between those two wills. I am particularly interested in whether Orwell’s narrative anticipates the problem of the empty signifier—where the people must be constituted by excluding part of the actual population—without resolving whether that exclusion is a betrayal of populism or its necessary condition. Give a reading faithful both to the novel’s specific scenes and to the conceptual framework, and conclude by saying what the allegory contributes to philosophical debates about representation that abstract argument alone tends to miss.
