I'm trying to figure out what I actually think about material constitution, going by my gut on cases. There's a clay statue and then it's crushed into a lump: the statue is gone, the clay is still there, so they're not the same thing. Then there's a statue where over decades every atom of clay gets replaced by a conservator but the shape never breaks: I think it's still the same statue, even though none of the original clay is left. But if you melt the statue down into a ball, wait ten years, then reshape that excatly same clay back into the statue: I think that's just a copy, not the original, even though it has all the original material. If you split the clay in half and make two smaller statues: neither is the original, they're both new. A perfect hologram of the statue in empty air: not a statue at all. And the case I keep going back and forth on -- you disasemble the statue into chunks, store them in boxes, then years later reassemble them excatly: is that the same statue or not?

Is there a principle that gets all of these, or close, and what does it say about the case I haven't listed -- a statue made of living coral that grows and replaces all its cells continuously but keeps the same form? Please answer in British English. I want the principle stated precisely, with the case that motivates each clause, its verdict on the coral thing and the disassembly case, and an honest statement of which of my verdicts it can't accommodate if we want it simple.
