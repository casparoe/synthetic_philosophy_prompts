I keep encountering a particular failure mode when preferences are inferred from observed behaviour, especially in settings where the agent knows they are being modelled. The agent selects an action not because it best advances their private objectives, but because it will cause the observer's inference procedure to attribute a specific preference to them — for instance, choosing a visibly costly option to demonstrate integrity, even when an equivalent cheaper alternative is available. Because the agent anticipates the observer's reverse-engineering, they optimise for the inferred preference rather than the underlying one. The result is that the behaviour fed into the inference procedure is shaped by the procedure itself, producing a reflexive loop in which the observer's model systematically misattributes preferences that were constructed precisely to be misattributed.

Does this configuration have an established name? I am not referring to ordinary market signalling or to social-desirability bias in surveys, which concern reputation within a community; nor am I describing a standard principal-agent problem with hidden types. This is specifically about a single observer applying an inverse-inference method to a single agent who conditions their choice on the expected output of that method. How exactly should it be distinguished from signalling and from ordinary revealed-preference failure? If no precise term exists, how would you define its boundaries — particularly the point at which awareness of being modelled turns preference revelation into preference fabrication?
