I've been convinced that the old-evidence problem in Bayesian epistemology is basically solved once you stop treating knowing E as fixing P(E) = 1 globally and instead allow for localized conditioning frames — call it frame-relative confirmation. On this view, when a scientist realizes an old fact was predicted by her theory, she updates not on E itself but on the structural relation R between her theory's axioms and any auxillary assumptions that were needed to derive it, and since R was previously assigned low prior weight, the update is legitimate. I'm less worried about Howson's original objection than about wether this move collapses Bayesianism into some kind of structural realism about confirmation. What would actually count against this frame-relative approach? Which intuitions about learning from experience does it violate, what formal results (say about conditionalization norms) does it risk running afoul of, and are there cases in science where old evidence genuinely failed to support a theory despite this framing? Rank the defeaters: which ones are fatal, which ones can the view absorb with a tweak, and which ones do its defenders just have to bite the bullet on?
