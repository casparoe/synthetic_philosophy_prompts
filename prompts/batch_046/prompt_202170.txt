I am a novelist working on a novel in which a central character—a composer who has deposited an unalterable final score with an archive—must decide whether to perform an earlier, suppressed version of the work. In drafting the philosophical dialogue that surrounds her choice, I want to avoid the familiar hidden-controller or neurological-block Frankfurt scenarios that readers will recognize from introductory texts. Instead, I am drawn to a less commonly mapped conceptual issue: cases in which the mechanism that eliminates alternative possibilities is not an external agent but the protagonist's own prior institutional or artistic commitments, which render a technically available path conceptually hollow rather than physically impossible.

Give an example—constructed with a named agent, a specifically described blocked alternative, and the exact feature that makes that alternative spurious—of a conceptual problem that arises within Frankfurt-style counterexamples to the principle of alternative possibilities under this internal-blocking structure. Please explain why the scenario either succeeds or fails as a counterexample, and in doing so carefully separate what is settled in the literature (for instance, that Frankfurt cases target the link between moral responsibility and the availability of alternatives) from what remains genuinely contested (such as whether self-authored constraints differ in kind from external interference, or whether the principle survives if reconceived as a demand for conceptual rather than physical openness). Do not present any contested interpretation—such as the claim that all internal-blocking cases necessarily preserve responsibility—as if it were the consensus among philosophers of action.
