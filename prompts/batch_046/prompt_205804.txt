I run a 90-minute graduate seminar in moral psychology and practical reason, about fourteen students who have read some normative ethics but are not specialists in Bernard Williams. Design a structured in-class simulation — with assigned roles, explicit rules, and small real stakes — that makes participants experience the specific self-alienation of the integrity objection, particularly the moment when an agent introduces an impartial consideration that should not be part of her reasoning about her own commitment. Do not let the point emerge only in a post-hoc lecture; the rules, role obligations, or payoff structure should force participants to run into the conflict themselves before anyone names it.

The simulation should center on ground projects that are partly constitutive of practical identity — commitments whose value is essentially tied to partial loyalty and cannot be translated into general benefit without distortion. Construct a concrete crisis scenario in which honoring such a commitment requires allowing a greater aggregate harm, but preventing that harm demands not merely neglecting the commitment but actively dissolving the relationship or practice that sustains it. To make the alienation visceral, include a justification cascade: each participant must defend the same decision sequentially to (1) the person to whom their ground project binds them, (2) a neutral observer with no knowledge of the relationship, and (3) themselves in a private written reflection. The rules should make it impossible to use identical reasons across all three without either contradiction or a felt sense of intrusion — the reasoning appropriate to audience 2 should feel like a foreign element when addressed to audience 1.

Specify: full rules and materials (role cards, score sheets, or verbal protocols); a payoff or stakes mechanism tied to a real but small outcome (e.g., selecting the seminar's next primary text or distributing a shared resource, not hypothetical currency); a minute-by-minute timeline for the 90 minutes; the debrief that maps specific moments in the cascade onto the distinction between ground-project reasoning and impartial moral reasoning, with explicit attention to where participants inserted an extra impartial consideration that displaced the first-personal reason they actually acted on; an honest prediction of what will go wrong the first time (for instance, students playing the neutral observer with excessive sympathy, or mistaking strong emotional preference for a ground-project commitment); and a frank statement of which feature of Williams's view — for instance, that some commitments are conditions of having a life rather than strong preferences within it — the simulation cannot reproduce mechanically and must be supplied by the facilitator.

Keep the scenario fresh: avoid the classic spouse-and-drowning case and similar examples that students have likely encountered. Instead, design a situation in which the ground project requires active partiality — favoring one party in a way that is not defensible from an outside perspective — and where the impartial solution requires actively undermining the very structure that makes the agent's life meaningful. Do not ask for bibliographies, citations, or reading lists.
