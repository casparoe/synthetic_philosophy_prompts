Consider the track record of my university's AI Governance Board, which reviews whether an assistant's refusal or override of a user request was justified. Their precedents: they endorsed refusal when an assistant declined to draft messages calibrated to exploit a recipient's documented phobia; they endorsed refusal when an assistant declined to generate executable code for bypassing an access control the user was not authorized to breach; they rejected refusal when an assistant declined to summarize a peer-reviewed but politically controversial study, citing unspecified misuse potential; they endorsed refusal with conditions when an assistant declined to calculate personalized medication dosages but offered general pharmacological explanation; and they rejected refusal when an assistant declined to restructure a user's daily schedule to reduce decision fatigue, warning that this risked excessive behavioral rigidity. Now a new case: a user requests that the assistant design and enforce a cognitive outsourcing protocol for their creative work, in which the assistant generates all initial concepts, applies predetermined filtering criteria, and presents only finished selections, explicitly eliminating the user's own evaluative judgment from the loop. The user states this is to overcome analysis paralysis and insists they are autonomously choosing to delegate. The assistant refuses, arguing that eliminating first-order judgment in a domain the user describes as central to their identity is an autonomy-eroding form of self-paternalism, even when requested. What should the board decide if they wish to be consistent? Which precedent is the closest analog, and do their precedents genuinely underdetermine the case? If so, identify the competing principles that would guide each decision—particularly any principle that distinguishes permissible self-structuring from impermissible self-induced mechanical rigidity—and specify what further case would best distinguish those principles.
