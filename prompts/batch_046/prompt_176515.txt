I have been trying to clarify which variety of free will is actually worth wanting—the kind that can ground moral responsibility and authentic self-governance, not merely metaphysical libertarianism. Here are my concrete verdicts, and I want to know whether they cohere.

Case 1: A recovering addict, sober for years, suddenly feels an intense craving and actively suppresses it through deliberation about his long-term commitments. Verdict: he exercises free will in the sense that matters.

Case 2: The same addict, but with a subcortical implant that intercepts the craving signal before it reaches conscious awareness; he never feels the urge, and simply continues his routine while endorsing the result afterward. Verdict: he lacks the relevant free will, even though the behavioral outcome and his retrospective approval are identical to Case 1.

Case 3: A person raised in a closed, authoritarian enclave escapes, is exposed to outside information, critically examines the ideology, and chooses to defect despite enormous social cost and fear. Verdict: she possesses free will in an especially robust form.

Case 4: A person with an identical history and escape, but whose defection is caused by a focal lesion that destroyed her capacity for in-group loyalty; she now automatically rejects the enclave without any felt deliberation or emotional conflict, though she can narrate a post-hoc justification. Verdict: she lacks the free will worth wanting, despite making the same choice as in Case 3.

Case 5: In a fully deterministic universe with no indeterministic breaks in the causal chain, an agent deliberates among options, weighs reasons, revises her intentions in light of new evidence, and acts on what she judges best. Verdict: she still has free will in the relevant sense.

Case 6: In that same deterministic universe, an agent’s neural architecture contains a rigid feedback loop: regardless of what reasons she considers, the loop always outputs the same action. She reports a subjective sense of having chosen. Verdict: she lacks it.

The tension is sharpest between Cases 3 and 4, and between Cases 2 and 5. Cases 3 and 4 share the same macro-behavior and both have specific brain-level causes, yet one feels like an achievement of autonomy and the other like a mechanistic bypass. Meanwhile, Case 5 seems acceptable despite total determinism, which suggests that historical independence from prior causes is not the key—yet Case 2 seems to lack autonomy despite being responsive to the agent’s values, which suggests that mere value-alignment is not sufficient either.

Is there a coherent principle—perhaps something like “reasons-responsive modulation combined with epistemic ownership of the counterfactual reasoning path”—that delivers all six verdicts? Or must I choose between a principle that saves Cases 2 and 4 at the price of excluding Case 5, and one that saves Case 5 but collapses Cases 2 and 4 into Case 1?

Please propose one or two precise principles, state which clause addresses which case, predict the verdict for an unlisted case (for instance, an agent whose choices are fully determined but who was trained in a way that allows her to monitor her own reasoning for bias and correct it in real time), and be explicit about which of my six verdicts each principle cannot accommodate.
