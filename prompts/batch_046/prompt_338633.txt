I've bought into a Jaspers-flavored view of collective guilt that I think applies across generations. The core claim is that a nation can bear metaphyscial guilt in a literally non-metaphorical way even when every individual perpetrator is dead, because the collective subject doesn't just vanish — it persists through institutional memory-structures (legal continuity, cultural narrative, economic inheretance) that keep reproducing the conditions of past wrongs. So current citizens aren't personally culpable like criminals, but they're structurally complicit in a way that adds up to genuine collective guilt, not just a figure of speech. What would actually move me away from this? I want the defeaters sorted by damage: fatal ones that destroy the idea of a persisting collective subject capable of guilt, serious ones that only break the transmission mechanism across generations, and merely awkward ones. Be honest about whether this view has been qualified into unfalsifiability — like, every counterexample gets shunted into 'that's political guilt, not metaphysical' — and whether that's actually a problem for a view of this kind. Also tell me which objections a committed defender would concede hit hard, versus which ones they'd dismiss as category errors. Don't just recycle the standard 'no one should be blamed for ancestors' line; dig into what it takes for collective identity and moral persistence to actually hang together.
