I have been developing what I call the autological extension of program equilibrium—the idea that an agent applies equilibrium reasoning not across distinct agents but across her own temporal stages, treating her future reasoning architecture as readable source code. I present the argument below as a reductio, yet I cannot locate the precise exit, and I would like your diagnosis.

(1) In a standard program equilibrium, rational agents submit decision procedures that are transparent to one another; when those procedures are symmetric, they necessarily converge on identical outputs, which explains cooperative behavior even in one-shot Prisoner's Dilemmas.

(2) A rational agent's present and future temporal stages stand in an analogous relation of transparency: the future stage inherits the same cognitive architecture and can, through introspection and the causal continuity of memory, inspect the reasoning that produced the present decision.

(3) If the architecture is genuinely unchanged and the relevant inputs are held constant, the symmetry condition applies across time just as it applies across agents; therefore, the future stage must reproduce the present stage's decision, on pain of violating the equilibrium constraint that identical procedures yield identical actions.

(4) Consequently, rational revision—changing one's mind upon further reflection without altering one's underlying reasoning architecture—is incoherent within this framework; any apparent revision either involves new inputs that break the symmetry or amounts to becoming a different agent.

(5) Yet we routinely revise judgments—about moral trade-offs, theoretical commitments, and practical courses of action—while maintaining both continuity of personal identity and the coherence of our rational agency.

(6) So either the autological thesis is false, or rational revision is an illusion that masks either hidden architectural change or hidden input-variation.

The conclusion strikes me as clearly too strong, but I am inclined to preserve both the inter-agent validity of program equilibrium and the reality of intra-personal rational revision. Which step should be rejected? I am particularly suspicious of the inference in (3), yet I worry that blocking it requires allowing agents with identical source code to diverge whenever their informational environments differ—which seems to undermine the cooperative guarantee that makes program equilibrium philosophically interesting in the first place. If the flaw lies instead in the analogy drawn in (2), what does abandoning that analogy cost for the project of treating rational agency as a kind of self-transparent computation? And if the problem is in (4), does saving rational revision force us to accept that personal identity over time is more discontinuous than we ordinarily suppose?
