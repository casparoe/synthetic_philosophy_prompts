I'm a doctoral student in moral philosophy, and in a recent workshop on moral criticism the phrase "only the blameless may blame" was invoked—first to defend a public figure accused of hypocrisy, then to dismiss a whistleblower who had herself violated minor rules. The phrase seemed to do different normative work in each case, but I cannot articulate the precise ambiguity. Could you disambiguate the slogan into its distinct theses? I want a menu rather than one reading: separate the claim that blame requires moral standing from the claim that hypocrisy undermines the blamer's message; distinguish whether the disqualification is all-or-nothing or scalar; and clarify whether "blameless" means free of the specific fault being blamed, free of any comparable fault, or free of fault altogether. For each reading, indicate whether it actually supports the two workshop uses—defending the public figure and dismissing the whistleblower—whether it applies symmetrically to all participants, and whether it survives the case where the blamer's fault is of an entirely different kind. Please also flag any reading that covertly conflates lacking moral purity with lacking the social position to issue blame, since that seemed to be the hidden move in both arguments.
