I'm taking a course in comparative ethics and have noticed that whenever students defend karma as a theory of moral desert — as opposed to a mere causal law of action and consequence — they instinctively reach for the analogy of a cosmic bank account. In this picture, every intentional deed makes either a deposit or a withdrawal in an invisible moral ledger; the balance accrues across lifetimes like compound interest with no bankruptcy protection; and present suffering or flourishing is simply the settlement of that account, mechanically inevitable and personally deserved. The analogy is usually deployed to argue that the universe is ultimately just and that anyone experiencing misfortune must be paying down an old debt.

I want a rigorous audit of this comparison. Please lay out the explicit correspondence table: what plays the role of the account holder, the currency of moral value, the interest rate, the central creditor, and the settlement mechanism? Then show where the mapping breaks down — for instance, whether karma requires a unitary, continuous self to hold the account, or whether distributed causal chains make the single-ledger image misleading; whether karmic debt can be forgiven, transferred, or annulled in ways that have no banking equivalent; and whether the absence of bankruptcy implies that every negative action must eventually be repaid in kind, or whether other traditions allow for dissolution or transformation of karma that breaks the financial comparison. Finally, tell me whether the conclusion — that karma guarantees a perfectly calibrated moral desert across lives — survives once these disanalogies are made explicit, or whether the banking analogy was carrying the entire burden of making retributive justice seem like an impersonal mechanical process rather than a metaphysical claim in need of independent defense.
