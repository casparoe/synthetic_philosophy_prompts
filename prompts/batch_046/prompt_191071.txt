I have been trying, without any formal training in logic or probability theory, to understand a long-running argument about whether ordinary "if-then" statements—like "if the switch is flipped, the light comes on"—can be assigned probabilities in a coherent way. As I understand it, there is a well-known result, associated with David Lewis, that starts from a few intuitive principles—roughly, that the probability of a conditional equals the corresponding conditional probability, together with some natural closure rules for belief—and shows that these principles together force all beliefs to become independent in a trivial way. For decades, philosophers have split over this: one camp treats the derivation as a proof that indicative conditionals simply do not have probabilities, while the other camp insists that the result only shows one of the intuitive principles must be abandoned, and they keep proposing refinements about which one. The same formal steps are accepted by both sides, and yet nobody seems to move; one side appeals to the derivation, the other to linguistic intuition about how people actually reason with "if" sentences, and the cycle repeats.

My own working guess is that the stalemate is not really about logic or language at all, but about what question is being asked when someone asks "how likely is this conditional?"—whether they mean rational subjective credence, objective statistical tendency, or something closer to conversational expectation. But I am genuinely unsure whether this diagnosis is correct, or whether there is instead a hidden substantive disagreement about belief revision, or about whether the formal apparatus should apply to natural language in the first place.
