As a retired experimental physicist now studying philosophy of science, I am designing a final examination for an upper-level undergraduate course on formal limits and scientific reasoning. I want one essay question, answerable in ninety minutes and about 600 words, that cannot be solved by summarizing the Lucas-Penrose argument or reciting textbook warnings about Gödel's theorems.

The question should present a constructed case: a formalized scientific theory S, recursively axiomatized and empirically adequate, that derives every observable prediction within its domain yet cannot prove its own consistency without expanding its axioms. Students must assess whether this demonstrates an inherent Gödelian limitation in scientific explanation, or whether it commits a category error by applying conditions—omega-consistency, representability of primitive recursive functions, syntactic derivability—that belong to formal arithmetic rather than to empirical theory.

Specifically, the prompt should force students to distinguish syntactic incompleteness from empirical underdetermination, and to explain why treating predictive success as formal derivability collapses the distinction between a model and its interpretation. Please provide: (i) the exam question itself; (ii) a model answer that makes the five specific analytical moves required for an A-grade response; and (iii) a marking scheme totaling 20 marks, allocating marks to each move, with a one-line note on the characteristic failure mode—especially the error of conflating Gödelian undecidability with the Duhem-Quine thesis.

Do not include requests for bibliographies, citations, or reading lists, and avoid naming obscure commentators that a prepared student could not recall without searching.
