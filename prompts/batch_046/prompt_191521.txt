I am a retired experimental physicist who has only recently begun studying philosophy in a structured way. I have come to hold a particular interpretation of Rawls's treatment of religion in democratic politics, and I want to know how exposed it is. The view is this: Rawls's requirement that citizens offer public reasons for coercive political measures should be understood not as a demand to suppress comprehensive religious doctrines, but as an invitation to practice what I call normative bilingualism—the capacity to articulate one and the same moral commitment in both the internal vocabulary of one's faith and in the shared vocabulary of democratic citizenship, without either vocabulary being reduced to the other. I claim that this bilingualism is epistemically symmetrical; the secular liberal enjoys no privileged standpoint from which to judge religious translations, and the religious citizen suffers no loss of doctrinal integrity by offering them. I further claim that the stability of an overlapping consensus depends precisely on this symmetrical bilingual capacity being widespread, and that where translation apparently fails we should diagnose a failure of democratic competence rather than an inherent conflict between faith and public reason.

What would count against this position? I want an honest map of its vulnerability. Are there religious doctrines whose normative content is structurally inseparable from their revelatory justification, so that any translation necessarily strips away the reason-giving force? Are there empirical patterns in plural societies showing that citizens who maintain this bilingual posture suffer cognitive fragmentation, reduced political efficacy, or alienation from their religious communities? Could it be shown that public reason itself carries hidden metaphysical commitments about human autonomy that make the alleged symmetry illusory? I also want you to distinguish the objections that defenders of my view would themselves concede as damaging from those they would dismiss as misunderstandings of bilingualism. Rank the defeaters from fatal—those that, if sustained, collapse the view—to merely awkward adjustments that require only qualification. Finally, assess whether my position has been insulated into unfalsifiability: if I can always respond to a failed translation by saying the citizen lacked sufficient bilingual competence rather than admitting structural impossibility, does that insulation protect the theory or render it empty? Please be direct about whether that risk is a genuine problem for a normative view of this kind.
