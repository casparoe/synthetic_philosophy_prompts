As a novelist developing a narrative that moves between rigorous historiography and the seductive architecture of pseudohistory — the Atlantis mythos reconstructed by nineteenth-century occultists, the ancient-astronaut narratives that reinterpret Mesopotamian cylinder seals as technological blueprints, and the radical chronological revision associated with Fomenko's New Chronology, which collapses ancient empires into a compressed medieval mirror — I find that I need a conceptual specification rather than another catalog of errors. Before I commit to any theoretical framework for my novel's internal logic, I want the full list of requirements.

Write the desiderata that any adequate theory of demarcation between history and pseudohistory must meet: the cases it must classify correctly, the distinctions it must preserve, the puzzles it must dissolve, and the established results it cannot contradict. For each requirement, state the motivating case or consideration behind it, and be explicit about the correspondence — what feature of legitimate historiography maps onto each criterion, and what feature of pseudohistory violates it.

In particular, include these pressures in your list. The theory must correctly handle Atlantis-style claims that lack archaeological stratigraphy without dismissing all speculative reconstruction. It must address ancient-astronaut interpretations that exploit genuine ambiguity in iconography while introducing unfalsifiable extraterrestrial agency. And it must account for Fomenko-style revisionism that applies statistical methods to chronicle data yet rejects radiometric and astronomical calibration wholesale. The theory must also distinguish revisionism that overturns a specific claim — such as the geographic location of a site — from revisionism that overturns the methodological framework itself. It must preserve the distinction between possessing pseudohistorical status and the degree of cultural influence or harm such claims exert. It must remain consistent with archaeology and physics, including radiocarbon dating and orbital mechanics, without making those disciplines tautological premises. Finally, address what I will call archival mimicry: the phenomenon in which a pseudohistory cites genuine archives and primary documents but reorders them according to a concealed pattern, producing high internal coherence. Must a demarcation theory treat archival mimicry as a distinct failure mode, or does it reduce to a more general defect?

Flag any pair of desiderata you suspect are jointly unsatisfiable — especially given that pseudohistories can always absorb anomalies by expanding their hidden pattern. Then, briefly, assess how three familiar approaches fare against the list: a Popperian falsifiability criterion, a Lakatos-style evaluation of research programmes, and a sociological boundary-work account. Which single requirement eliminates the largest number of popular theories? And if one of your desiderata must be sacrificed to preserve coherence, which should it be, and what does the remaining theory predict for a case you have not yet listed?
