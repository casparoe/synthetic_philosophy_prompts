A colleague and I have been trying to extend Dutch-book reasoning to collective belief aggregation, and I think we have argued ourselves into an unacceptable corner. Here is the chain as I understand it:

(1) If a single agent’s credences violate the probability axioms, then a bookie can construct a portfolio of bets—each with unit stakes—such that the agent suffers a net loss regardless of which world is actual.

(2) Now consider a forecasting panel whose members each hold probabilistically coherent credences over a shared domain, but who aggregate their views by taking the arithmetic mean of reported credences for each proposition.

(3) Even when every individual assignment is coherent, the pooled assignment can violate coherence: for instance, if different members assign probabilities to P and to Q relative to different background conditions, the averaged values can violate the law of total probability with respect to any shared partition, or assign greater credence to a conjunction than to one of its conjuncts.

(4) If the pooled assignment violates coherence, then by applying the very same construction as in (1)—treating the pooled values as the betting quotients of one agent—there exists a portfolio that guarantees a net loss to the panel if it acts on those pooled values.

(5) Yet forecasting panels, scientific review boards, and prediction markets routinely act on aggregated opinions, and they do not systematically face bankruptcy or forced dissolution.

(6) So either Dutch-book arguments do not extend from individuals to groups, or collective belief aggregation is always irrational, or coherence is not required for pooled opinions.

My friend insists that the step from (3) to (4) is perfectly legitimate: if the group acts as one decision-maker, it should be vulnerable to being booked as one. I suspect the error lies in treating the pooled mean as a single agent’s credence, but I cannot pinpoint exactly what breaks—whether it is the absence of a unified utility function, the fact that no individual member is compelled to accept every bet in the portfolio, or a deeper structural mismatch in the logical domains being averaged. Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost for the normative force of Dutch-book arguments in social epistemology?
