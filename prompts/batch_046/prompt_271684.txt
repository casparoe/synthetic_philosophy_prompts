I just had a long argument with a friend about whether the tragedy of the commons even applies to our regional network of repair cafés and shared fabrication workshops. She says it is obsolete because the tools are not depleted by use; I think the real failure mode is different—degradation through accumulated neglect, expertise hoarding, and ambiguous obligations to repair—so the governance problem needs its own decision framework rather than Hardin’s original logic. You are a senior operations philosopher and governance advisor. Before constructing anything, think carefully and brainstorm the conceptual distinctions that must survive translation from theory into practice: where physical extraction is non-rival but maintenance burden is rival and cumulative, where exclusion is technically easy but socially costly, and where boundaries must be drawn around repair obligations rather than usage rights. Then write a reusable decision procedure—ordered questions in sequence, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, explicit hard stops that return a “no,” and marked hand-off points where the judgment is one of values rather than facts—that my board (limited technical expertise, joint sign-off required from both a staff steward and an elected community representative, about forty-five minutes per proposal) can actually run on governance-change proposals. The principle to operationalize is a modified Ostrom-Hardin framework for “care commons”: resources whose tragedy is under-provision of upkeep and boundary ambiguity, not over-consumption. Apply the procedure to three proposals: (a) introducing a sliding-scale usage fee for high-demand CNC machines to fund preventive maintenance; (b) allowing a local technology firm to reserve fifteen percent of workshop hours in exchange for donating calibration equipment; and (c) eliminating all membership verification to maximize openness. Make sure one of these yields a clear procedural recommendation that conflicts with a strong inclusive-access intuition, and flag exactly where the conflict arises and what the procedure explicitly cannot resolve. Finally, state the limits of the procedure—what kinds of proposals it will systematically misjudge or fail to capture—so the board knows when to override it.
