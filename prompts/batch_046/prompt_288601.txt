Write a parody of a software End-User License Agreement—formatted as numbered clauses, approximately 700 words, in the deadpan legal-bureaucratic register of an imaginary Silicon Valley startup’s terms-of-service page—for a fictional platform called PramāṇaVault that certifies user knowledge-claims according to the classical Nyāya framework of six pramāṇas. The target is not any living scholar but the Nyāya epistemological apparatus itself, particularly the live internal disputes about whether śabda (testimony) is an independent pramāṇa or reducible to anumāna via speaker reliability; whether upamāna (comparison/analogy) generates genuinely novel cognition or merely redistributes prior pratyakṣa and anumāna; whether anupalabdhi (non-apprehension/non-cognition) can establish negative existential claims without collapsing into a disguised form of perception; and what counts as a valid hetu (middle term) when vyāpti (invariable concomitance) must be satisfied in domains that are only probabilistic. The satire should exaggerate real structural features of the tradition rather than invent cartoon versions: include a Definitions section that briefly glosses pramā (valid cognition), the five-membered anumāna syllogism, and the conditions for pramāṇa, so the piece remains readable to a non-specialist, but reward a specialist who recognizes that each liability cap, arbitration clause, and refund policy quietly maps onto an actual philosophical tension—for instance, who bears the burden when testimony is disputed, or whether analogy can be used to certify knowledge about entities never perceived. Inject one modern, applied twist that is not standard textbook material but does not depend on obscure scholarship: treat the outputs of a large language model as a candidate form of śabda, and specify in Clause 7 whether such “algorithmic testimony” requires independent certification or is disqualified on the grounds that the model lacks a jñātṛ (knowing subject) endowed with buddhi (cognition/awareness). Do not state the objections explicitly; let them emerge from the absurdity of applying jurisdictional rules, warranty disclaimers, and continuity-of-identity provisions to epistemic conditions. End with a Governing Law clause that stipulates whether Navya-Nyāya or classical Nyāya determines jurisdiction over disputes about the definition of pramāṇa itself. Maintain sustained deadpan irony throughout—no direct announcement that the view is being satirized, no winking, and no call for bibliographies, citations, or further reading. The piece should feel to a philosopher of epistemology like a fair reductio, and to an outsider like an unusually precise, if slightly unhinged, software contract.
