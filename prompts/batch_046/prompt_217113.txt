I am taking a graduate seminar on ontology and epistemology in the sciences, and in the readings I keep noticing a specific posture that I cannot name precisely. Researchers identify an entity—say, a quantum state characterized by its interaction profile with surrounding fields—while simultaneously treating a specific class of its internal parameters as permanently inaccessible, not because of technological limits, but in principle: observing those parameters would destroy the very interaction profile that serves as the state's defining feature. Despite this, they do not treat the state as partially undefined or fictional; they reidentify it across different experimental contexts as the same entity, even though those hidden internal parameters might vary. A parallel case appears in some cognitive modeling, where a mental module is defined solely by its input-output function, with its internal representational format declared inaccessible in principle, yet the module is tracked as identical across different brains and tasks regardless of whether that hidden format is constant.

In both instances, the researchers are neither straightforward instrumentalists—since they insist the entity is fully real—nor are they practicing structural realism in the usual sense, because they affirm specific causal and functional properties rather than merely relational structures. Instead, they appear to cordon off one particular information domain as epistemically unreachable while leaving the remaining ontology intact and stable across identity judgments. Is there an established concept or term for this exact posture? How should it be distinguished from treating an entity as a black box, or from ordinary epistemic humility about measurements we have not yet made? And if it lacks a precise name, what boundaries should define it—particularly with respect to whether the inaccessible information is genuinely irrelevant to the entity's identity, or is simply ignored for practical and methodological reasons?
