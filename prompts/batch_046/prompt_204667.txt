As a retired experimental physicist who has only recently begun studying moral philosophy and decision theory, I am moderating a colloquium Q&A with a philosopher who defends a refined version of expected-value reasoning in practical ethics—specifically, the view that so-called fanaticism can be disciplined without abandoning the expectation calculus. Her proposal, which she calls scaling-interval attenuation, holds that when outcome magnitudes grow exponentially, credences should be treated not as point estimates but as interval-valued distributions whose width expands with the size of the stake; this is meant to prevent minuscule probabilities from dominating moderate-certainty goods. I want a sequence of eight to ten interview questions that press on the actual mechanics of this constraint rather than rehearsing generic objections to utilitarianism. At least one question should ask how her interval-valued distributions avoid collapsing back into point estimates under repeated aggregation—anticipating her likely reply that aggregation is non-linear—and should include the appropriate follow-up. Another should ask what conceptual or empirical motivation she draws from the natural sciences, such as measurement error at extreme scales or the behavior of statistical estimators under heavy-tailed distributions, with a follow-up keyed to the answer that she appeals to analogies with physical measurement. A third should probe whether applying the attenuation constraint symmetrically to catastrophic risks and to utopian outcomes drains the view of the practical urgency she wants to preserve, and should include the follow-up that presses her on whether that symmetry undermines her normative conclusions. Please end with a question asking what concrete decision scenario—perhaps one weighing present welfare against long-term existential risk—would cause her to abandon the expectation framework altogether rather than further refine the attenuation rule. The tone should be rigorous but accessible to an audience of scientists crossing into philosophy.
