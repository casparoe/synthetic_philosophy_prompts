I was helping a friend who TAs a metaethics course grade some exams, and they sent me one answer they're stuck on. The exam question was: Explain the Frege-Geach problem for moral expressivism, with attention to how it arises in embedded contexts like conditionals and why it threatens the view's account of moral reasoning.

The student wrote:

Expressivism holds that moral sentences like "murder is wrong" dont describe reality but express the speaker's attitude of disaproval. The Frege-Geach problem, which Geach developed from Frege's point about assertions in subordinate clauses, shows this view collapses inside complex sentences. Take "If murder is wrong, then persuading your little brother to do it is wrong." In the antecedant the speaker neednt actually feel disapproval — they might just be assuming it for the sake of argument — yet "wrong" clearly keeps teh same meaning in both clauses. So the expressivist has to either split the semantic value across contexts, breaking compositionality, or invent a hidden attitude being expressed in the antecedant, which is totally ad hoc. Geach took this to prove that expressivism is flat-out incoherent, and the standard response in the literature is to drop non-cognitivism and treat moral language as descriptive, just like empirical claims. Also, the problem really only applies to conditionals, not to negation or disjunction, so its actual scope is narrower than people usually claim.
