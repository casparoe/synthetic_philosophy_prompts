In mechanistic anomaly detection, a reference model is built from the mechanism that produces normal behavior, and deviations flag anomalies. A long-running but unresolved split has emerged between two research camps working on industrial heat-exchanger diagnostics. One camp defines a mechanism by its functional-causal graph: if two units share the same directed relations of energy transfer, flow regulation, and thermal feedback, they are the same mechanism, and anomalies are detected as graph-structural deviations regardless of material substrate. The other camp holds that the mechanism includes the specific material substrate and its degradation dynamics: replace copper tubing with a ceramic composite that preserves the identical thermodynamic function but introduces brittle-fracture and thermal-shock failure modes, and you have a different mechanism with genuinely different anomaly signatures. Both sides accept the same thermodynamic equations and the same sensor datasets; they publish competing benchmarks that perform well on their own substrate-specific test sets but never converge, and neither side has persuaded the other to change evaluation criteria. My working hypothesis is that the disagreement is not empirical at all, but about what kind of explanation a mechanistic model must provide—whether it is an abstract causal schema portable across substrates or a concrete material trajectory tied to particular failure physics. Is that diagnosis correct, or is there a hidden empirical claim—perhaps about cross-substrate generalization or about predictive accuracy under distribution shift—actually driving the stalemate? I want a structural diagnosis with reasons: is the dispute verbal, substantive, or a mismatch of evidential standards? Do the sides answer different questions under the single term “mechanism”? Are they disagreeing about which cases count as data, or about an intuitive starting point neither can defend? And given your diagnosis, what would actually constitute progress—a formal result on representational equivalence, an experiment measuring transfer learning across substrates, a conceptual distinction between causal role and material realization, or an argument that the question is ill-posed and should be dissolved?
