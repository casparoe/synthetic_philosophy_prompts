I'm presenting next Thursday to a clinical neuroethics and philosophy-of-medicine seminar on the claim that pain asymbolia—patients who report nociceptive experience without distress, avoidance behavior, or negative affect—undermines the view that pain is intrinsically bad and supports an integrative model: pain is harmful only when nociception activates a specific affective-cognitive binding mechanism. I want to extend this to animal-research ethics, arguing that nociceptive evidence alone is insufficient to establish suffering in organisms that lack the neural architecture for such integration, without thereby denying those organisms any moral status.

The audience is precisely this mix: two clinical neurologists who treat asymbolic patients and are skeptical of philosophical overreading; three philosophers of mind working on embodied cognition who think pain is fundamentally affective; one comparative psychologist studying nociception in cephalopods who is committed to expanding welfare protections; and a bioethicist on an animal-use oversight committee who cares mainly about regulatory consistency. They know the basic clinical literature—classic cases involving insular or anterior cingulate lesions—but are not specialists in the philosophy of pain.
