As a retired physicist who has turned, rather late, to philosophy of science and historical epistemology, I find myself preoccupied with a methodological tension that seems to me under-theorized. Practitioners of what is called the outside view forecast the trajectories of large institutions—empires, regimes, complex bureaucracies—by assigning them to reference classes of past analogues and applying base rates of collapse, reform, or persistence. The practice is empirically seductive, yet I am unsure which of its conceptual vulnerabilities actually obstruct knowledge and which are merely ornamental difficulties. I want not a survey but a defended agenda: if one had limited resources—say, a small research group or the remaining productive years of a late-career shift—where should conceptual labor go first?

I see at least four interdependent lines of open work, and I want them ranked by importance, tractability, and dependency. First, there is the ontology of historical kinds: does the term empire denote a natural kind, an institutional kind defined by functional roles, or a singular causal network with no genuine type-membership? The answer determines whether base-rate reasoning is valid inference or a category mistake. Second, there is the problem of decomposition and reference-class fragmentation: if we disaggregate an empire into fiscal, informational, and coercive subsystems, each carrying its own reference class and decay dynamics, do we preserve predictive power or destroy the holistic mechanisms—such as cascading legitimacy failure—that drive collapse? Third, there is the temporal-scale mismatch: base rates for imperial decline are calibrated over centuries, while policy-relevant forecasting operates over decades. Is this merely a statistical smoothing problem, or does it reveal that the underlying causal processes are not time-scale invariant, so that short-range forecasts are not just noisy but structurally misaligned? Fourth, there is the normative-epistemic question of whether calibrated ignorance—knowing, say, that seventy percent of structural analogues failed without knowing the operative causal pathway—constitutes knowledge adequate for institutional design, or whether it is epistemically hollow for anyone who must act.

Please prioritize these, or substitute others you think more central, with explicit criteria: which question is upstream of the others in the sense that settling it reframes the tractability of the rest; which is most neglected relative to its potential impact on actual forecasting practice; which should be deprioritized because resolving it would not alter downstream conclusions; and what you might call epistemic yield per unit of conceptual labor. Indicate also what result—formal, empirical, or philosophical—arriving tomorrow would cause you to reverse your ranking. Do not provide bibliographies, citation lists, or reading recommendations; I am interested in the architecture of the problem space and in a ranking I can defend without institutional access to archives.
