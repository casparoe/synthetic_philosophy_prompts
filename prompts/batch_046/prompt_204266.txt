I'm prepping a talk for a joint seminar of classicists and economic historians, and I need to anticipate the blowback. My thesis is that Aristotle's attack on usury in Politics I isn't just the familiar money-is-barren line — it's actually grounded in his reproductive biology from Generation of Animals. I argue natural exchange works like biological generation: form (the active principle, male seed) plus matter (passive substrate, female contribution) produces something genuinely new. Money supplies only form — it directs growth but doesn't grow itself — so charging interest is like claiming you can generate offspring from form alone, without matter. I'm using this to reinterpret the oikonomia vs chrematistike distinction: interest violates the material conditions of actualization because it treats future value as deterministically extractable from present capital, ignoring that the lender's risk-bearing labor should supply the necessary 'matter'.

The audience will be philologists who think I'm overreading, plus historians of economic thought who know chrematistike cold but will bristle at treating biological analogies as explanatory rather than decorative. I want a prep sheet: the objections they'll actualy raise, ordered by how likely and damaging, phrased in their vocabulary, with a short reply for each and a note on where that reply is weakest. What should I concede up front so I don't sound defensive? And what's the one reply that has to land or the proposal dies?

Also — this is the objection I'm praying nobody asks — Aristotle allows partnerships where risk is shared. Doesn't that show risk-bearing labor isn't always the necessary 'matter', and therefore collapse my biological analogy?

Please separate what's settled in Aristotelian scholarship from what's genuinely contested, and don't present a contested reading as the consensus.
