I'm preparing a conceptual analysis for a graduate seminar on the ethics of self-cultivation, and I want to frame a disagreement that has become sharper in recent years between two camps that both claim Stoic lineage but have been arguing past each other. On one side are the Modern Therapeutic Revivalists—interpreters influenced by cognitive-behavioral practice who treat Epictetus's opening distinction in the Enchiridion as a psychological boundary-setting device, essentially mapping the ancient division onto modern social-psychological notions of internal versus external locus of control, and who hold that Stoic practice aims to reduce emotional distress by expanding the sphere of what can be cognitively reframed. On the other side are the Classical Orthopraxists—philologically oriented practitioners who insist that the ancient diairesis between ta eph' hēmin and ouk eph' hēmin is not about emotional management but about ontological and ethical jurisdiction: only prohairesis (moral choice) is fully in our power, while all externals—including health, reputation, and even the outcomes of actions—are strictly indifferent, to be engaged with, if at all, only through the reserve clause (hupexhairesis). The revivalists accuse the orthopraxists of life-denying rigidity; the orthopraxists accuse the revivalists of reducing Stoicism to a wellness protocol that silently smuggles in attachment to outcomes.

Please draft the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between these two camps. The document should open with a numbered section of claims both sides can sign—covering at least the formal structure of the dichotomy in Epictetus, the functional role of the reserve clause in Stoic action theory, and the observation that modern popularizations have indeed imported psychological frameworks not present in the ancient texts. This should be followed by a disagreements table in which each camp states, in wording it accepts, its precise position on three issues: whether indirect causal influence over externals counts as a form of "control" compatible with the dichotomy; whether the telos of Stoic practice is eudaimonia conceived as rational virtue or as psychological resilience; and whether the modern therapeutic framing necessarily distorts the ethical core or can be defended as a legitimate conceptual translation. The table should separate substantive disputes from verbal ones—for instance, if both agree that one ought not be emotionally disturbed by outcomes but disagree on whether this requires treating those outcomes as genuinely indifferent rather than as strategically manageable.

Then identify the cruxes—sub-questions such that resolving them would move at least one side. These should include: (1) Does cognitive reframing of an external event constitute controlling the event itself or only controlling one's judgment of it, and does that distinction hold up under pressure? (2) Would robust clinical evidence that Stoic-informed cognitive therapy produces durable well-being count in favor of the revivalist framing, or would the orthopraxist reject it as measuring the wrong kind of good? (3) In the specific test case of a practitioner facing an unexpected serious medical diagnosis—where the revivalist might advise strategic information-gathering and lifestyle adjustment without emotional attachment, while the orthopraxist might advise focusing exclusively on the moral quality of one's response to the news—what exactly does each side predict about the correct Stoic course of action, and is there any observable behavioral difference that could not be explained away by both? Conclude by stating which crux you take to be genuinely empirical, which is conceptual, and whether any portion of the disagreement dissolves once the ambiguity in the word "control" is fully disambiguated. Do not provide bibliographies, citations, or reading lists; work entirely from the conceptual architecture described.
