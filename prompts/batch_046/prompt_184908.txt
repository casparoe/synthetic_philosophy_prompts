In my philosophy of language working group, a dispute about testimonial knowledge has reached an impasse that I find instructive. The reductionists hold that accepting what others tell us is epistemically reducible to individual faculties—perception of the speaker, memory of past reliability, and inductive inference—so testimony is not a basic source of knowledge but a derivative application of reason. The anti-reductionists counter that this reduction presupposes the very thing it tries to eliminate: without an initial, non-inferential entitlement to take speakers at face value, we could never accumulate the reliability data needed to run the induction, so testimony must function as a primitive epistemic source with its own normative structure. Both camps accept the same developmental-psychology findings about early childhood trust and the same formal models of belief updating; neither denies that speakers are sometimes unreliable. After years of exchange, the positions have not shifted. What kind of disagreement is this? Is there a hidden empirical question about which cognitive mechanisms are actually operative, a verbal divergence over whether "basic" means causally fundamental or normatively irreducible, or are the two sides answering different questions—one about the genealogy of belief, the other about its justification—under one label? Diagnose the stall with reference to the actual commitments of each position, identify what would genuinely advance the debate (a conceptual distinction, a formal result, an empirical finding, or a change of framing), and say whether, on your diagnosis, resolution should be expected at all.
