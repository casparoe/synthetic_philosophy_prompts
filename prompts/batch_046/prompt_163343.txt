At a recent colloquium on art ontology, two colleagues—Dr. Alina Vasquez and Dr. Marcus Chen—debated the value of originals in a case that straddles forgery and conservation. Vasquez held that an artwork's value is anchored in its causal-historical uniqueness: being the specific spatiotemporal object that stood in direct physical relation to the artist's intention. She argued that even a perfect molecular replica, reconstructed from original pigments and canvas fragments after a fire, lacks the ontological residue of continuous existence; it is a successor object, not the original, and displaying it as such is a form of epistemic fraud regardless of disclosure. Chen replied that this view smuggles in a mystical non-physical essence. He proposed functional essentialism: what makes an object the original is its performance of the art-historical role—being the reference point for reproductions, the object of scholarly citation, the bearer of institutional provenance—not an invisible causal thread. He noted that museums already accept paintings with the majority of their surface replaced; if continuity of matter does not matter, why should continuity of spatiotemporal trajectory? Vasquez answered that restoration preserves a single continuous trajectory interrupted only by maintenance, whereas replication severs it entirely; the difference is not quantitative but structural, like the difference between healing and cloning a person. Chen countered that this analogy begs the question, since persons have psychological continuity conditions that artworks lack, and that without such conditions we are left with bare metaphysical intuition. They turned to the case of a disclosed, perfect replica exhibited with full transparency: Vasquez said viewers are still wronged because they are relating to a stand-in rather than the authentic locus of artistic achievement; Chen said that if the aesthetic experience and the epistemic contract are both satisfied—viewers know exactly what they see—no wrong occurs, and any remaining discomfort is mere sentimentalism. They asked me to referee and I found myself genuinely torn. Please adjudicate point by point: is Vasquez right that causal continuity carries non-functional value? Is Chen right that functional role exhausts artistic identity? Where does the restoration analogy hold and where does it collapse? And what, if anything, are both missing about the ethics of display when disclosure is perfect?
