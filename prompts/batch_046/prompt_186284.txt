I'm drafting an essay for an advanced seminar in epistemology and philosophy of mathematics, and I would value critical feedback on its structure before I develop it into a full draft. The essay takes Benacerraf's classic dilemma—broadly, that a uniform causal theory of knowledge seems to exclude mathematical knowledge if mathematical objects are causally inert, while abandoning that uniformity carries heavy epistemological costs—as its point of departure. Rather than rehearsing the familiar standoff between platonism and nominalism, I propose to examine whether the dilemma can be dissolved, or at least reshaped, by distinguishing two modes of mathematical belief formation. I call these "proximal" and "distal" cognition. In proximal cases, the mathematician operates directly on mental or notational representations—grasping that a particular diagram commutes, or that seven plus five equals twelve—where the causal chain runs through the cognitive processing of representations rather than through interaction with an abstract object. In distal cases—accepting the Four Color Theorem on the basis of computer verification, or adopting a large-cardinal axiom on the strength of an indirect consistency argument—the belief's content concerns an abstract fact, but its formation depends on extended symbolic artifacts, collaborative derivations, or physical computation whose reliability is established only indirectly.

My tentative claim is that Benacerraf's causal constraint applies with markedly different force across these two modes. For proximal cognition, the platonist need not invoke causal contact with numbers or sets themselves; the causal story concerns the representation, and the knowledge claim can be satisfied without ontological commitment to interaction with abstracta. For distal cognition, however, a causal chain is present—it runs through hardware, code, and peer verification—but it is thoroughly mediated. This generates a modified dilemma: either we allow such mediated causal chains to ground knowledge of abstract objects, which weakens Benacerraf's motivation for a uniform epistemology, or we deny them, which threatens to delegitimize large parts of contemporary mathematical practice. I further suggest that this distinction might explain why intuitions about mathematical knowledge vary so sharply between elementary arithmetic and high-level set theory, without requiring a split ontology.

I would appreciate comments specifically on the conceptual coherence of this framework. First, does the proximal/distal distinction genuinely help the platonist, or does it merely relocate Benacerraf's problem from ontology into cognitive architecture? Second, are there well-known formal results or conceptual cases—perhaps involving undecidable propositions whose acceptance depends entirely on prior commitment to a formal framework—where the distinction collapses, because even apparently proximal intuition is already saturated by distal theoretical commitments? Third, have I mischaracterized the causal theory of knowledge that Benacerraf presupposes, particularly regarding whether mediated causal access was ever meant to be ruled out in principle? I am interested in whether allowing mediated access for computer-assisted proofs while denying direct access for intuitive arithmetic introduces a hidden inconsistency, and whether this framing risks insulating platonism from refutation rather than resolving the original dilemma. The essay is exploratory; I am not seeking a bibliography, but rather precise conceptual criticism of the argument's exposure—where it is vulnerable, where it is merely awkward, and whether its defenders should concede that the distinction fails.
