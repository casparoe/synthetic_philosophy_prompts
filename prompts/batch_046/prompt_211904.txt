Explain the conceptual tension in evolutionary-cognitive accounts of religion—such as hyperactive agency detection or theory-of-mind overextension—when they characterize religious belief as a cognitive misfiring or evolutionary byproduct, yet must simultaneously explain its high-fidelity transmission through ritual, testimony, and institutional scaffolding. Specifically, address whether labeling such cognition a "misfire" presupposes a normative baseline for correct mental functioning that is itself calibrated within culturally constructed developmental niches, and whether adopting a niche-construction perspective—where religious practices partly constitute the scaffolded environments that shape the very mechanisms said to generate them—collapses the distinction between religion as a biologically natural phenomenon (analogous to language acquisition) and religion as a self-stabilizing cultural system that retroactively constitutes its own evolutionary causes. Be precise about whether anything remains of religion's status as a unified natural kind once this bidirectionality is taken seriously, and whether the byproduct/adaptation dichotomy survives the recognition that the "byproduct" may be developmentally necessary for the maturation of the cognitive capacities invoked to explain it.
