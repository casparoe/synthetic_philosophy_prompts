At the pub last night my mates Maya and Leo got into it about Friedman's F-twist and whether unrealistic assumptions in models actually matter. They were arguing over a fisheries model that assumes fish reproduce at a constant rate all year — no seasons, no age structure, totally unrealistic — but it predicts long-run catch yields pretty well.

Maya: "The whole point is that unrealistic assumptions dont hurt if predictions are good. The constant-reproduction bit is just an instrument to isolate fishing pressure. If it gets the yield curve right, who cares that fish dont actually breed in january?"

Leo: "But its not just predictions — its explanation. Last year there was a sudden collapse after a warm spring, and the model had no idea why because it cant represent seasonal vulnerability. So the false assumption masked the mechanism that actually mattered for policy."

Maya: "Thats just adding an ad-hoc temperature term. The core abstraction still holds; you modularise the seasonal bit. The unrealistic reproduction schedule isnt infected, its just incomplete."

Leo: "If you have to replace the false assumption to handle new data, then you werent using Friedman's defence properly. You kept the lie and hoped it stayed harmless, but it turned out to hide the missing mechanism. Thats not modularity, thats patching a leak."

Maya: "You're moving the goalposts. The model was built to ask about equilibrium yield under constant pressure, not about seasonal shocks. Judging it for failing at something it never claimed to do is unfair — its like complaining a map with wrong scale gives bad distance estimates."

Leo: "But defenders always retreat to 'it was only meant for X' whenever it fails at Y. That makes the F-twist unfalsifiable. And in practice the policymakers used the yield number but missed the timing — they reduced catch in autumn instead of before spawning, so the 'right' number was useless."

Maya: "So your real gripe is with policy misuse, not with the model itself. The abstraction did its job; the users applied it outside its domain. Friedman never said unrealistic assumptions are safe for every purpose, just that theyre not automatically fatal."

Leo: "If thats the defence, then its empty. Every failed model can be saved by saying 'wrong domain'. I think unrealistic assumptions are dangerous precisely because they let you think you understand the mechanism when you dont — and thats exactly what happened here."

They asked me to referee and I honestly cant see it clearly. Can you adjudicate move by move — rate each of Maya's and Leo's points from clearly wrong to clearly right, say who is more right overall, and point out what both are missing? Im especially unsure whether Leo's charge of unfalsifiability actually lands against Friedman's view or just against Maya's version of it.
