After a long and somewhat exhausting argument with a close friend last night about whether dreams count as experiences, I have settled—tentatively—on a hybrid position that I want to stress-test before committing to it further. I maintain that dreams are neither fully present phenomenal experiences during sleep (contra the view Malcolm targets) nor purely post-awakening narrative fabrications in the stronger confabulatory spirit sometimes associated with Dennett. Instead, I think they occupy a kind of dispositional phenomenal middle ground: they are latent cognitive structures—perhaps encoded in the consolidation dynamics of REM-related memory replay—that acquire experiential character only through the specific act of recall upon waking, without being wholly invented by that act. So they possess what I would call a dispositional phenomenal status: not experienced in the moment of sleep, but not entirely constructed afterward either.

What would actually count against this view? I want more than the standard Malcolm-Dennett exchange replayed. Specifically:

- Which empirical findings—say, in sleep neuroscience, memory consolidation research, or the phenomenology of lucid and non-lucid dreaming—would be decisive, and which would merely be awkward?
- Which conceptual distinctions does the view depend upon—between occurrent and dispositional consciousness, between memory retrieval and imaginative reconstruction, between narrative coherence and phenomenal presence—and at which points do those distinctions risk collapsing into one of the rival positions?
- If the view is correct, what should it predict about cases of anterograde amnesia during REM sleep, about the reliability and detail of dream reports after non-REM awakenings, or about the phenomenological differences between recalled dreams and waking confabulations?
- Which of these potential defeaters would I, as someone defending this middle-ground position, have to concede as genuinely damaging, and which could I plausibly dismiss as misinterpreting the claim about latent structures?
- Finally, is there a risk that this position has been insulated from refutation—say, by redefining dispositional phenomenal status to accommodate any neurocognitive finding—and if so, is that insulation a conceptual virtue or a vice for a view of this kind?

Please rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and do not provide bibliographies or reading lists; I am interested in the conceptual and empirical pressure points themselves, not in mapping them onto specific authors or texts.
