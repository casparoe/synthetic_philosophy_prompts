You are a senior speechwriter with training in value theory, and I need you to work with philosophical precision rather than generic sentiment. We have just finished a long, rather intense argument with a friend about tranquilism — specifically, the strong claim that only the absence of craving and suffering possesses intrinsic value, and that all apparently positive goods, achievements, or relationships are valuable solely as instruments towards that absence. Before you draft anything, please take a moment to brainstorm: identify three structural constraints this view places on what can legitimately be said in praise of a life’s work, and note the particular performative tension that arises when one tries to honour someone for reducing suffering without treating that honour itself as a positive good that violates the doctrine.

The occasion is the retirement dinner for Professor Eleanor Vance, held in a college hall with an audience split evenly between moral philosophers and her family. She spent four decades defending a rigorous version of tranquilism that treats intellectual anxiety — the craving for certainty, for metaphysical closure, for final answers — as itself a form of suffering to be dissolved rather than satisfied. Roughly half the room will catch every allusion; the other half should leave feeling genuinely moved rather than lectured.

Please write a toast of approximately five minutes (around 650–750 words), spoken by me, her former doctoral student. The philosophical idea must do real structural work: you must not praise her for having ‘enriched the world’, ‘left a lasting legacy of knowledge’, or ‘created beautiful truths’, since under her view those are not intrinsic goods. Instead, shape the praise around her having made certain cravings unnecessary — easing the suffering of false certainty, dissolving the anxiety of unanswered questions without replacing it with dogma, leaving people less burdened by the need for answers. You should also acknowledge, without resolving, the objection my friend kept pressing: that the very desire to eliminate craving is itself a craving, and therefore that tranquilism risks being self-defeating when enacted. Let that tension sit in the room rather than smoothing it away.

Please think step by step: first outline the structural constraints in brief, then draft the speech in a warm, formal register appropriate to a mixed audience, using British English spelling and syntax throughout. Do not include bibliographies, citations, or reading lists. Avoid referencing obscure technical literature that a well-read philosopher might not recognise; rely on the conceptual structure itself. Ensure the speech lands as affection rather than as a defence of her doctrine, and make sure it could not be delivered by someone who had never heard of tranquilism — the idea should shape every paragraph.
