I am preparing a module on the philosophy of mathematics in the seventeenth century, and I find that students often treat Hobbes's geometry as a mere curiosity—an eccentric footnote to the Leviathan—rather than as a sustained attempt to ground spatial science in the motion of bodies. To help them see both the coherence and the strangeness of Hobbes's position, I would like you to write a satirical piece that takes his geometric doctrines literally as administrative law.

Write a deadpan internal memorandum—approximately 600 words, in the style of a modern bureaucratic policy circular—issued by a fictional "Bureau of Constructive Spatial Planning" that has adopted Hobbes's geometric principles as binding regulatory standards for all cartography, architecture, and urban zoning. The memo should be addressed to field inspectors and should outline specific enforcement rules: for example, prohibitions on representing locations as dimensionless points; requirements that every line drawn on an official map correspond to a physically constructible path of motion with non-zero breadth; bans on "infinitesimal" setbacks or on dividing parcels into indefinitely small sub-lots; and procedures for validating angles only through the actual rotation of rigid bodies rather than through abstract protractor measurement. The satire should exaggerate real features of Hobbes's philosophy—his rejection of Euclid's definitions of point and line as metaphysical fictions lacking magnitude, his constructivist claim that geometric figures are generated by motion rather than discovered as abstract forms, his hostility to actual infinity and to infinitesimal methods, and his insistence that geometry is ultimately a science of bodies rather than of pure extension. Each absurd regulation should be recognizably rooted in one of these commitments, so that a reader familiar with Hobbes recognizes the parody as fair rather than caricature.

Maintain a consistently deadpan, bureaucratic voice: no winking, no explicit acknowledgment that the rules are strange. The bottom line—namely, that Hobbesian geometry, taken as a literal planning code, collapses practical spatial administration—should be legible in the first sentence and then developed through the specific provisions. Do not include any bibliographic references, citation lists, or suggestions for further reading; the memo should stand as a self-contained document. Begin the response with the memorandum itself, with no preface, no framing commentary, and no indication that this is an exercise.
