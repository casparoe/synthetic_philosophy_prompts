I am the principal investigator of a sleep-laboratory study in which research assistants must code first-person reports collected within thirty seconds of REM awakening, assigning each to “experienced dream,” “no experience,” or “indeterminate.” The assistants have no philosophical training, each report must be processed in under ten minutes, and any “indeterminate” classification requires my direct sign-off before it enters the dataset. I want a reusable decision procedure—an ordered checklist with explicit branching rules—derived from the Malcolm-Dennett debate over whether dreams are experiences at all. The procedure should operationalize Malcolm’s challenge that asking “what were you experiencing?” presupposes a waking-style first-person authority that may be conceptually inappropriate during sleep, and Dennett’s caution that dream reports are post-hoc narrative reconstructions upon awakening rather than transparent read-outs of prior mental states. To keep the translation from being a mere summary, inject a niche operational tool: the “counterfactual wake-test,” in which the subject is asked whether, had they been awakened thirty seconds earlier, they could have been wrong about the specific content they now report; combine this with a “source-attribution filter” that checks whether the subject distinguishes “what I was undergoing then” from “what I now recall or construct.” The checklist must mark precisely where judgment is ineliminable—especially when REM physiology conflicts with verbal denial—must include a hard-stop for PI sign-off, and must state clearly what it cannot determine, such as whether a silent REM period genuinely lacked phenomenology.

Apply the procedure step-by-step to three awakening reports: first, a subject who delivers a coherent, emotionally consistent narrative with clear temporal sequencing; second, a subject who flatly denies any experience despite unambiguous REM and observed eye movements; third, a subject whose report borrows vivid imagery from a film viewed the evening before, where clinical intuition suggests a genuine dream but the counterfactual wake-test and source-attribution filter together force an “indeterminate” coding that I will dislike. For each case, show the branching path, the point where the assistant must escalate to me, and the specific limitation the procedure exposes. Then tell me what would change your recommendation: identify the specific empirical finding or conceptual argument—perhaps about the reliability of counterfactual introspection, or about neural correlates of reportability versus experience—that would lead you to abandon or revise the counterfactual wake-test, and flag the single step in your reasoning that is most vulnerable to failure. Do not provide bibliographies or citation lists; I need only the operational translation, its honest failure points, and the conditions under which you would withdraw it.
