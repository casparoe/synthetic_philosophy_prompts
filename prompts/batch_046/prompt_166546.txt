Next month I defend my dissertation chapter to a bioethics and legal-theory committee on what makes consent valid. My thesis is that deception and coercion are not the only vitiating conditions: there is a distinct relational failure I call "epistemic subordination," in which a consenting party retains standard individual competence yet is structurally dependent on the information-provider for the very evaluative framework needed to authorize the act, so that autonomy is undermined without any false statement or threat. The audience includes two clinical ethicists who treat competence as an individual cognitive threshold, a legal scholar who holds that only misrepresentation or duress can invalidate consent, and a philosopher of agency skeptical of expanding vitiating conditions beyond those two. Give me the objections in the order they are likely to arise, phrased as each of those three would phrase them, with for each what I should concede, what I should hold, and how to reply within a short exchange. Identify the weakest point in my own position—the boundary between benign expert guidance and vitiating subordination—that I should prepare to defend rather than hope they miss. Finally, include the objection I am hoping nobody raises: whether my view collapses into an impossibly demanding standard that would invalidate nearly all expert-layperson consent.
