I'm giving the keynote address at the retirement symposium for Judge Elena Vasquez, who served thirty years on the appellate bench and is widely known for her work on sentencing equity. The audience is deliberately mixed: on one side sit legal philosophers, judges, and criminal-law theorists who have published on moral responsibility; on the other sit her family, former clerks, and community advocates who have never read Nagel or Williams and will not follow technical distinctions unless they are made audible through concrete example. The speech should run about twelve minutes and must take moral luck seriously—not as decorative framing, but as a real constraint on what I can honestly claim about her career. In particular, I want the address to navigate, without resolving, the tension between circumstantial luck (the demographic accidents, institutional openings, and historical moments that placed her on the bench at precisely the right time to influence reform) and constitutive luck (the temperament, cognitive habits, and emotional dispositions she did not choose but that made her capable of sustained judicial patience). The philosophical idea should do structural work: I need to praise her achievement genuinely, without pretending she authored herself from nothing, and also without reducing her legacy to a product of fortune. Please write the address so that it avoids both the false consolation that "everything happens for a reason" and the false modesty that denies agency altogether; it should be precise enough that specialists recognize I am not smuggling in an unexamined compatibilism, yet entirely accessible to listeners who have never encountered the concept of moral luck before. Do not include citations, a bibliography, or any reference to further reading; the speech must stand on its own reasoning and on the specific institutional details of her tenure, which I will supply from personal knowledge rather than from texts.
