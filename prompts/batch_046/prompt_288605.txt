I am presenting a paper next Thursday at an interdisciplinary workshop on philosophy of action. The audience is composed primarily of Aristotelian virtue ethicists who treat the practical syllogism and the diachronic unity of the agent as non-negotiable frameworks for understanding moral failure, alongside two behavioral economists who accept hyperbolic discounting as an empirical description of intertemporal choice but are deeply skeptical of any philosophical reification of "the will." My thesis is that akrasia should not be analyzed as weakness within a single, continuous deliberative subject, but rather as a structural consequence of hyperbolic discounting: under non-constant discount rates, the evaluative perspective at t1 and that at t2 lack the transitive preference ordering and shared normative authority that Aristotelian practical reason requires. Rather than one agent knowing the good but failing to act, we have temporally indexed evaluative standpoints linked only by contingent psychological continuity, not by a unified practical intellect.

For the virtue ethicists, phrase each objection in their vocabulary—concerning practical syllogisms, the integrity of character, and conditions for moral responsibility—indicate what I should concede up front, provide the reply that must land, and note exactly where that reply is weakest. For the behavioral economists, do the same regarding whether I am illicitly importing metaphysical personal-identity claims into an empirical discounting curve, and whether this framing undermines predictive modeling. Finally, flag the weakest point in my own argument that I should prepare to defend rather than hope they miss, and identify the one question I am hoping nobody asks—specifically, whether this temporal fragmentation collapses into a paralogism that eliminates the very subject of moral responsibility.
