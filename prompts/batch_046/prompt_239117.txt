I am writing for my high school philosophy seminar on moral exemplars, and my instructor has been advancing a particular view about whether maximal moral goodness is genuinely desirable. To help me anticipate her grading of our final case studies, I have reconstructed her verdicts across four hypothetical and historical examples she discussed in class. She holds that figures like Mother Teresa represent authentic moral saints—people for whom moral obligation consumes every significant domain of life—yet she insists there is something subtly undesirable about that completeness, as if an essential dimension of human flourishing has been sacrificed. By contrast, she classifies a dedicated firefighter who saves lives but also nurtures family bonds and personal hobbies as not being a moral saint, and she treats that absence of maximal dedication as a virtue rather than a deficiency. She similarly judges that an individual who donates nearly all disposable income to effective charities while sustaining a serious artistic practice fails to reach saintly status, and she approves of that failure. Finally, she describes a purely hypothetical agent who is unfailingly honest, perpetually assisting strangers, entirely without anger, and completely lacking in private projects or close relationships, and she concedes that this agent is indeed a perfect moral saint, but she maintains that we ought not aspire to become such a person.

Our next assignment asks me to apply her framework to a case she has not yet addressed: a person who employs algorithmic decision-tools to direct every surplus dollar to the most cost-effective global health interventions, lives in deliberately minimal conditions, spends exactly two evening hours writing unpublished poetry, and maintains precisely one deep friendship that is not justified by any moral calculus. I need to predict whether she would classify this individual as a moral saint or as falling short of that status, and I need to identify the precise rule she seems to be running—whether it concerns the mere presence of non-moral goods, the psychological sustainability of the life, the retention of some autonomous sphere insulated from moral optimization, or something else entirely. If her previous verdicts genuinely underdetermine this outcome, I should explain what principle she would be adopting with each possible answer. Furthermore, I want to know which additional comparison case—perhaps an agent who performs the same algorithmic optimization but replaces the poetry with a purely recreational digital habit, versus an agent who donates equivalent resources but refuses all computational aid in her giving—would best reveal whether her rule tracks the content of the non-moral activity or the manner in which moral choices are made.
