I'm giving the opening remarks at a local workshop next month called "What We Honor and Why." The room will be split—some philosophy and history students, but mostly neighbors and retirees with no academic background. Reading on my own about G.E.M. Anscombe's protest against Oxford honoring Harry Truman for the atomic bombings, I've been struck by her claim that bestowing honor doesn't just celebrate an outcome; it joins the institution in the agent's intention, and that killing civilians as a means is murder regardless of the war's end. I want the speech to use that idea structurally: it should frame how I talk about our college's recent decision to keep a building named after a benefactor whose fortune was built on labor practices now widely condemned, without turning into a lecture. It needs to land for experts—who should recognize her rejection of consequentialist reasoning here—while remaining accessible to everyone else. Please include a brief closing note that separates what is settled in her view—an absolute prohibition on intending the death of innocents—from what is genuinely contested among ethicists, such as whether institutional honors really entail shared intention or whether her position can accommodate any modern account of just war, and do not present her stance as the consensus.
