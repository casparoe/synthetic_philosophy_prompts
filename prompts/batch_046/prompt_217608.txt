I’ve just come out of a long argument with a friend about whether critical scholarship on Jesus can ever yield more than a reflection of its own rules. The practice I have in mind is the standard institutional protocol in historical-Jesus research: scholars isolate discrete gospel pericopes, run them through the familiar criteria of authenticity—multiple attestation, embarrassment, dissimilarity, coherence, Aramaic substratum—then aggregate the “red” and “pink” results into a composite portrait, treating historical knowledge as a forensic mosaic built from independently verified fragments. What philosophical commitments does that workflow actually presuppose? Must it assume an atomistic ontology in which sayings are separable from their narrative frames; a specific philosophy of testimony that treats the evangelists as unreliable deponents rather than tradition-bearers; and an implicit falsificationism in which dissimilarity operates like a demarcation criterion? Are these parts internally consistent—does coherence, which presupposes a unified psychological subject, sit comfortably with dissimilarity, which depends on prior reconstructions of both Second Temple Judaism and the early church that the criteria themselves are meant to establish? Or is the practice philosophically underdetermined, so that several rival theories of historical knowledge could license the same grading sheet? Reconstruct the implicit theory, distinguish what the practice strictly needs from what merely fits it well, and say what an alternative method—say, one built on a holistic or narrative-first epistemology—would actually have to change in its day-to-day procedures. Please separate what is broadly settled among specialists from what remains genuinely contested, and do not present any one contested interpretation—such as the reliability of the criteria or the feasibility of a non-atomistic reconstruction—as though it were the consensus view.
