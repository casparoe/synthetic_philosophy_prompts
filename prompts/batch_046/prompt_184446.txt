Student Answer:

Schlick argues that the meaning of life is not found in achieving some ultimate purpose—religious salvation, historical destiny, or the maximization of future utility—but in the intrinsic quality of present activity. He distinguishes work, which is instrumental and driven by necessity, from play, which is done for its own sake and involves creative or intellectual engagement without external justification. Meaning belongs to play alone, and Schlick emphasizes that play requires objective conditions: leisure, freedom from economic coercion, and the vitality of youth.

Applying this to longtermism, a life devoted entirely to far-future risk reduction is pure work. Every decision is instrumentally justified by distant outcomes, leaving no space for present intrinsic satisfaction. Schlick would therefore regard such a life as meaningless.

There is, however, an important existentialist dimension to Schlick's view. Because he denies all objective metaphysical values, the individual must invent meaning through autonomous choice; there are no universal criteria for what counts as play beyond personal preference. At the same time, Schlick's status as a logical empiricist means he applies the verification principle to ethical discourse, treating talk of transcendent purpose as cognitively meaningless and leaving empirical pleasure as the sole source of value. Consequently, justifying present sacrifice by appeal to far-future states involves not merely imprudence but a category error: those states are not empirically verifiable in the present, so they cannot rationally ground action.
