I'm preparing a feature on the hidden architecture of indirect moral reasoning, and I've reconstructed an argument from Kant's writings on animals that now strikes me as suspiciously overpowered. Here is the chain: (1) For Kant, direct duties extend only to rational beings capable of autonomous legislation; animals lack this status and therefore cannot be wronged in themselves. (2) His well-known condemnation of cruelty to animals is not grounded in the animal's suffering but in an indirect duty: cruelty hardens the agent's heart and weakens the disposition of compassion that we owe to rational beings. (3) If the wrongness of treating an animal badly is wholly derivative of its causal impact on the agent's moral psychology, then in any scenario where that psychological spillover is absent—say, an isolated, undetectable act—the act carries no moral weight at all. (4) By parallel reasoning, if contemporary research on empathy allocation suggests that compassion for animals can deplete the emotional resources we need for human strangers—what psychologists sometimes frame as moral licensing or compassion fatigue—then Kant's framework actually obligates us to suppress kindness to animals whenever doing so preserves our tenderness toward people. (5) So the view does not merely deny animals direct rights; it reduces our obligations toward them to a contingent empirical calculus about human psychology, one that could in principle mandate callousness rather than forbid it. (6) That seems to prove far too much, turning Kant's specific condemnation of cruelty into a variable output of behavioral prediction. Where does this go wrong, if it does? I would most like to preserve (1) and (2) as faithful readings, but if one must fall, I want to know which step is least costly to reject and what the standard interpretive positions—whether Schopenhauer-influenced, neo-Kantian, or recent revisionist accounts—each have to pay to abandon it.
