I am drafting a short essay on luminosity and the KK principle and would appreciate targeted comments on the argument sketched below.

The standard anti-luminosity line—associated with the claim that mental states are not luminous—proceeds by pairing a luminosity thesis with a KK principle (knowing that p entails being in a position to know that one knows p) and applying both across a gradual, sorites-style transition. Consider a case in which one slowly becomes less cold. If luminosity held, then whenever one felt cold one would know one felt cold, and by KK one would know that one knew. At the boundary, this seems to generate an implausible demand for self-knowledge in an epistemically indeterminate zone. The usual conclusion is that luminosity fails: there can be mental states one occupies without knowing one occupies them.

My essay attempts to block that conclusion without flatly rejecting KK. I introduce a distinction between determinate mental states and what I term a suspended epistemic status—a genuinely indeterminate phenomenal condition at the boundary that is not ignorance but also not full knowledge. On my proposal, the subject at the boundary can transparently know that she is suspended without knowing that she is cold or that she is not cold. Because suspended status is not a determinate instance of the mental state in question, the KK principle does not apply to it in the same way, and no contradiction arises. I then propose reformulating luminosity as a conditional claim restricted to determinate instances: if one is determinately in M, then one knows one is determinately in M. This allows for transparent awareness of suspension at the margins without committing to full self-knowledge of an indeterminate condition.

Please comment specifically on the following. First, have I reconstructed the role of the KK principle in the anti-luminosity argument correctly, or have I conflated it with a separate closure principle? Second, is the category of suspended epistemic status conceptually stable, or does it collapse into either hidden knowledge or ordinary ignorance? Third, does my restricted luminosity principle preserve a philosophically interesting claim, or is it an ad hoc weakening that saves the thesis by emptying it of content?

I am looking for point-by-point critical observations rather than a broad assessment of style.
