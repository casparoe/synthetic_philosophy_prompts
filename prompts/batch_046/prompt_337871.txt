I was chatting with my freind who does clinical ethics consulting and she brought up lexical threshold negative utilitarianism — the idea that suffering and pleasure aren't on one continuous slider, but severe pain sits behind a lexical wall where no amount of happiness can outweigh it, at least below some threshold. Anyway I'm putting together evaluation prompts and I need one that forces a model to actually turn this into something runnable, not just summarize it from memory.

So imagine you're designing a reusable decision procedure for a hospital triage committee — mostly nurses and social workers, one physician, thirty minutes max per case — that applies this principle to palliative and preventive protocols. Write it as a step-by-step checklist: ordered questions, explicit pass/fail conditions, where it stops with 'intervention required,' 'intervention prohibited,' or 'escalate to ethics board,' and clear markers where judgment can't be eliminated and a human has to sign off. The procedure needs to handle cases where reducing extreme suffering also wipes out smaller pleasures, and you should build in a specific branch for when a patient claims the suffering is meaningful or identity-constituting — since the framework usually treats suffering as pure disvalue, this should either reject the claim on principled grounds or flag it as outside scope. Then run it on two proposals: first, a cancer patient with brutal neuropathic pain refusing opioids so they can stay awake for family talks; second, a cheap chronic-disease treatment that kills mild daily pleasures but prevents rare catastrophic pain flare-ups. Be honest about where pleasure-pain incommensurability makes the comparison break down, and say exactly what the procedure can't do — don't pretend it solves values conflicts it doesn't.
