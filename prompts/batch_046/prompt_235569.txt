Consider the socialist calculation debate not as a settled episode of twentieth-century economics, but as a living framework for assessing whether algorithmic planning can overcome the epistemic and incentive objections advanced by Hayek and Mises, and whether Lange-style market-socialist adjustments retain independent value under modern computation. Rather than asking for a historical summary, I want you to construct a defended research agenda: which open questions in this revived debate actually matter, for whom, and in what order?

To keep the problem concrete but not derivative, focus on its intersection with three under-examined issues. First, the discovery-without-ownership problem: whether distributed machine-learning aggregation can replicate entrepreneurial discovery — the identification of local profit opportunities — without private property rights and without the specific incentive structure of capital loss. Second, the tacit-versus-distributed crux: whether the knowledge problem is ultimately one of computation that sufficiently powerful algorithms could solve, or whether it involves irreducibly embodied, context-dependent cognition that cannot be extracted into data protocols. Third, what I term the reflexive measurement problem: the possibility that the instruments used to gather distributed information for planning (pricing algorithms, predictive metrics, behavioural data protocols) do not merely mismeasure but actively reshape agent behaviour, generating institutional knowledge decay rather than static misallocation.

Assume the audience is a small interdisciplinary group — two economists, a philosopher of science, and one policy designer — with approximately three years, no obligation to produce bibliographies, and an interest in whether these debates have any purchase on contemporary algorithmic public administration. Your deliverable should be a prioritisation, not a survey.

Specifically: identify the two or three questions (drawn from the three above or from others you judge more consequential) that should command the bulk of the group's effort, and explicitly deprioritise the remainder. Defend the ordering with clear criteria — for instance, tractability with current formal tools, dependency relations (which answers are upstream of others), normative weight for actual institutional design, and resistance to rapid dissolution once answered. Map the dependencies: which resolutions must come first because later work presupposes them, and which can proceed in parallel? Be honest about trade-offs, particularly where tractability and importance conflict, and state how you weight them. Also specify what empirical or conceptual development would cause you to reorder the agenda.

Finally, distinguish sharply between implications that genuinely follow for policy and those that do not. If resolving the calculation problem in principle has little bearing on whether algorithmic planning is democratically legitimate or autonomy-preserving, say so and defend the separation. Conversely, if one of these open questions is genuinely upstream of that normative judgement, show the link.

Write in formal prose; do not include citations, reading lists, or requests for external sources. The reasoning should stand on its own.
