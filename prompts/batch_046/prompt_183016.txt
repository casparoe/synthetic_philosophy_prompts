I am grading a final exam for an upper-level undergraduate course in modern political philosophy. One question asked students to explain Hegel's account of war in the Philosophy of Right, particularly in his discussion of international relations and state sovereignty, with three explicit tasks: distinguish Hegel's position from Hobbes's conception of war as a pre-political state of nature and from Kant's project of perpetual peace; clarify whether Hegel regards war as a mere failure of international politics or as possessing positive ethical significance; and explain the connection between war, state sovereignty, and what Hegel describes as the preservation of citizens' ethical commitment to the universal rather than to private interest.

Student answer:

Hegel does not treat war as an absolute evil; rather, he assigns it an ethical moment within the life of the state. This distinguishes him sharply from Hobbes, for whom war is the natural condition of humanity prior to the social contract, a pre-political chaos that the state is instituted to escape. For Hegel, by contrast, war occurs only between fully formed states that are already ethical entities; it is not a regression to a state of nature but a relation between sovereign powers that have actualized the ethical idea. Hegel also rejects Kant's proposal for perpetual peace, viewing it as an abstract moral ought that fails to recognize the reality of particular state wills and the absence of any super-state authority capable of enforcing peace.

Furthermore, war possesses positive ethical significance because it protects citizens from the stagnation of civil society. When private economic interests dominate, citizens lose their sense of belonging to a universal purpose. The risk and discipline of war remind them of the finitude of particular existence and renew their commitment to the state. In this respect, war functions analogously to the master-slave dialectic presented in the Phenomenology of Spirit: just as individual self-consciousness achieves recognition only through a life-and-death struggle, so too do states achieve full self-consciousness and sovereignty only by risking their existence in conflict. Consequently, war is not merely a diplomatic failure but the highest expression of the state's freedom, through which the particular will transcends its own finitude and attains absolute ethical standing.

Nevertheless, Hegel insists that modern war must be limited in its conduct. The enemy state is itself an ethical entity with rights, and warfare should not seek its total annihilation. The goal is not conquest or destruction but the preservation of one's own sovereignty in a system of recognized states.
