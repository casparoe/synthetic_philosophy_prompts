Two colleagues in a philosophy of education reading group disagreed about Dewey's claim that democracy is a form of social inquiry. Marcus said Dewey's experimentalism means democratic deliberation should work exactly like a laboratory: citizens propose policy hypotheses, institutions provide feedback through measurable outcomes, and unsuccessful policies are rejected just as falsified theories are, so the only relevant difference is scale. Elena replied that this collapses a crucial distinction: scientific inquiry seeks consensus about what is true, whereas democratic inquiry seeks agreement about what is worth doing among people who hold incommensurable values, so importing laboratory norms risks treating dissent as mere error rather than legitimate pluralism. Marcus countered that Elena smuggles in a false dualism—values themselves are hypotheses about what will improve shared experience, and Dewey explicitly rejects the fact-value split—so democratic disagreement is still empirical, just about complex social consequences rather than physical ones. Elena then argued that even if values are empirical in some sense, democratic legitimacy requires that citizens see themselves as authors of collective decisions, not subjects in an experiment designed by experts, which means the process must include recognition and respect that have no analogue in scientific peer review. They asked me to settle it.
