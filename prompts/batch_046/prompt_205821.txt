I chair a small advisory board that classifies whether a group counts as a single joint agent for moral-responsibility attribution. Our precedents: we classified a three-person committee that unanimously passed a resolution—each member privately interpreting the text differently—as a joint agent because they shared an explicit procedural commitment; we denied that status to a panicked crowd exiting a stadium in orderly rows, since coordination was purely reactive; we granted it conditionally to a corporation whose board members held contradictory private goals but followed a unified governance charter; and we denied it to an orchestra playing from identical scores but wearing noise-canceling headphones that blocked all awareness of fellow musicians. The new case is a digital-art collective: fifty anonymous contributors upload fragments to a server running an algorithm that merges them into a coherent mural based on hidden thematic rules; no contributor knows the others, each believes they are producing solo work, and the algorithm—not any human—ensures thematic unity. Identify the plausible rules our precedents jointly allow, say what each predicts about this collective, identify the closest analog, give your best-guess verdict with reasons, state what principle we would adopt either way, and specify the further case that would force us to choose between the competing rules.
