I'm putting together a specification for a formal seminar on bounded rationality and self-reference, and I need a rigorous desiderata list for any theory of logical uncertainty that is meant to govern reasoning in agents with limited proof-checking capacity—agents who must hold probabilistic beliefs about arithmetical and logical claims without being able to verify arbitrarily long derivations. Please lay out the full set of requirements such a theory must satisfy: the coherence conditions it should preserve with standard empirical probability, the convergence properties it should exhibit as computational resources expand, the puzzles about self-reference and consistency it must avoid (such as assigning credence 1 to false mathematical statements or collapsing into paradox when reasoning about one's own future beliefs), and the distinctions it must maintain—for instance, between uncertainty about contingent empirical facts and uncertainty about logical truths. In particular, I want you to treat two conditions I am introducing as serious candidates for inclusion. First, counterfactual proof-stability: an agent's logical probabilities should remain invariant under counterfactual scenarios in which it might have discovered a proof, shifting only upon actual discovery, so that mere possibility of access doesn't alter belief. Second, the resource-tradeoff constraint: the theory should provide a principled framework for weighing the expected epistemic gain from extending a proof search against the opportunity cost of diverting computation away from empirical observation or action. For each desideratum, indicate whether it is non-negotiable or reflects a preference of a particular camp, and point out any pair that appears jointly unsatisfiable in light of standard computability or logical-induction results. Keep the focus on the criteria and their motivating cases rather than on grading existing formalisms, though a brief note on how logical induction addresses one or two of them is acceptable.
