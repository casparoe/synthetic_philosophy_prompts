I'm currently taking an advanced seminar in historiography and the philosophy of history, and my discussion group has split into two camps over the origins of the Cold War. Rather than continuing to talk past each other, we want to produce an adversarial-collaboration document. One camp—the attributionist camp—maintains that asking who started the Cold War is not only legitimate but indispensable, and that specific decisions by identifiable leaders between 1945 and 1949 bear differential causal and moral weight. The other camp—the systemic-emergence camp—holds that "starting" is a category error when applied to a macro-historical process; they treat structural conditions (the security dilemma, the power vacuum in Central Europe, ideological incompatibility) as the proper explanatory units, and they think assigning blame to individuals obscures the mechanisms that produced sustained rivalry.

We need a joint statement with four parts. First, a numbered section of claims both sides can sign: these should cover the basic chronology, the operation of the security dilemma, the distinction between triggering events and sustaining structures, and the kinds of evidence (archival, comparative, counterfactual) that are admissible in principle. Second, a disagreements table in which each side's position is phrased in wording that side would accept for its own view—specifically, the attributionist claim that there exists a minimal sufficient set of leader-decisions without which containment and bloc-formation would not have crystallized by 1949, versus the systemic claim that any such set is arbitrarily selected from a dense causal web in which structural pressures made some form of rivalry virtually inevitable. Third, the cruxes: sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one side. I suspect the two central cruxes are (1) whether the concept of counterfactual avoidability can be applied coherently to collective strategic decisions made under radical uncertainty, and (2) whether there is a non-arbitrary boundary between a "response" and an "initiation" within an escalating interaction sequence. For each crux, identify a concrete historical case or piece of evidence both sides agree bears on it—perhaps the decision sequence surrounding the Marshall Plan and Cominform in 1947, or the Berlin crisis of 1948–49—and record in advance what each side predicts that evidence shows about the crux. Finally, if part of the disagreement turns out to be verbal—say, if "blame" and "causal contribution" are being used with different scopes—specify exactly which part and why. Please format the output as the numbered agreed-claims section followed by the disagreements table, and keep all formulations neutral enough that neither camp feels misrepresented.
