I have been working through the idea that positive liberty, negative liberty, and republican liberty are not genuinely distinct categories but different descriptions of a single underlying failure: the rupture of what I call "continuity of agency," meaning that one's present choices cannot flow into future ones without an arbitrary break, whether that break is imposed by another person, caused by one's own present incapacity, or made possible by a master who need not even act. On this view, to lose one liberty is to lose all three, and debates about which kind is missing are really debates about which cause of the rupture is most visible.

What would have to be true for this to be wrong? I am not asking for the standard reply that they "feel different." I want the concrete cases, the intuitions about rights or democratic participation, and any empirical or conceptual results that show the three come apart in practice — ranked from fatal to merely awkward. Which of these would someone committed to my view concede as genuinely damaging, and which could they dismiss without cost? Are there cases where continuity of agency is intact yet one liberty is clearly absent, or where it is broken but one liberty survives? I also want an honest assessment of whether I have defined "continuity of agency" so broadly that no counterexample could ever reach it, and whether that insulation is a defect in a view of this kind.

Please lead with your bottom line in the first sentence and then give your reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question. Write in British English.
