I work as a curatorial assistant at a small regional museum and we keep running into this weird aquisition problem that our standard checklists don't cover. Last week I was arguing with my roommate about it — she thinks any fake should be destroyed on principle — but I realized I don't actually have a framework for deciding, and we need one because these cases come up every few months. Sometimes we get offered objects that are technically forgeries, made with the intent to decieve buyers, but they also have these strange secondary properties: maybe the original artist actually praised them in letters, or they were made using metallurgical or painting techniques that were lost and later rediscovered, or they ended up influencing authentic works in ways art historians now think are genuinely important. I need a decision procedure my team can run in about twenty mintues per object, without needing a PhD in aesthetics, grounded in what philosophy of art actually knows about authenticity and value — not just aura as a vague vibe. It should list the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a hard no, and where it has to hand the decision to a values committee because the question isn't factual. The procedure needs to handle hard cases where the forgery is technically perfect but ethically murky, and it should flag the points where it will probably misfire — like when displaying the fake actually boosts the original's reputation versus when it just makes people cynical about museums in general. Then apply it to two cases: a painting done in the style of a well-known old master that scholars later said taught them new things about that master's technique, and an ancient coin that was faked using a lost alloy method nobody had seen since antiquity. Please be honest about what the philosophy can't settle here, and don't just list philosophers; translate the distinctions into something I can actually check off on a form.
