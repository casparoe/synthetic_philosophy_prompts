I’m a journalist covering science and statistics, and — long story involving a panel in Manchester — I also sit on the methods oversight board for the UK Social Science Replication Programme. We’ve got a decision to make by next Tuesday, and I need a memo I can circulate before the meeting.

There’s a proposal in front of us, drafted by a working group I won’t name here, that wants to replace our current reporting rules with a dual-track system for every study we fund. Track A stays frequentist: fixed sample size pre-registered, exact p-values reported, confidence intervals, no optional stopping. Track B is Bayesian, but with a specific twist: the prior has to be an 'empirical Bayes prior' calculated automatically from a hidden meta-analysis of the last fifty published effect sizes in that exact sub-field, shrunk towards zero via a hierarchical model that the applying researcher can’t tweak. The claim is that this stops both p-hacking and prior-gaming, since the prior is locked in by an algorithm before data collection starts. About half the board thinks this is brilliant; the other half thinks it’s just frequentist multiplicity correction wearing Bayesian makeup, and that forcing Bayes factors on reviewers who barely understand them will produce cosmetic compliance rather than real understanding.

Can you draft the board’s decision memo — recommend adopt, reject, or adopt with conditions — with a concrete bottom line and a clear time frame? It needs to lay out what philosophical view of statistical evidence each track actually presupposes (is Track B really subjective belief updating, or is it just a calibrated frequentist correction in disguise?), and it should take seriously the strongest argument from the side that loses. Please don’t ask for bibliographies or citation lists — I need a document people can act on without a trip to the library. Also, keep the tone fairly casual; I’m writing this after recieving a badly formatted version of the proposal, so precision over polish is fine.
