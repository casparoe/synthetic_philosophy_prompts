In news coverage and political speeches, famine is almost always framed as a natural disaster—likened to an earthquake, a drought, or a flood that strikes a population from the outside. The analogy does heavy lifting: if famine is like an earthquake, then it has no human author, governments can only deliver charity after the fact, and asking who caused the starvation is as pointless as asking who caused the tectonic plates to shift. I have been trying to work out whether that framing holds, especially because I have also been reading about Amartya Sen’s claim that famines are rarely caused by an absolute absence of food. He points to Bengal in 1943, where rice was still available but wages and exchange rights collapsed, and to the Great Leap Forward, where grain was physically present in state stores and being exported even as rural populations starved. So I want to audit the earthquake analogy carefully: what plays the role of the seismic fault, the ground shaking, the buildings that fall, and the emergency responders? Where does the mapping break once you introduce distribution failures, price spikes, and state procurement rather than crop failure? Does the analogy quietly import the assumption that scarcity is always physical and never political? I also want to know whether the conclusion—that no one is really to blame—survives once the disanalogies are laid out, or whether it was the analogy carrying that conclusion all along. If the natural-disaster picture fails, is there a better analogy? I have been playing with the idea that famine might be closer to a bank run: the vaults are full, but a collapse in confidence and exchange mechanisms means people cannot access what is technically there, and panic or hoarding makes the breakdown worse. Could you lay out the correspondence table for the earthquake analogy, identify which disanalogies matter for the question of responsibility and which do not, assess whether the “no fault” conclusion stands, and then evaluate whether the bank-run replacement actually fixes the problems or just introduces new ones?
