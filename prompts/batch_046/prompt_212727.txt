I have been trying to articulate my own commitments about communicative directness, cross-cultural politeness norms, and the permissibility of white lies, and I find that my intuitions cluster in ways I cannot easily systematize. I would like to present several concrete verdicts and ask whether there is a single principle—perhaps involving what I have started to call the distinction between surface-face preservation and deep-face preservation, or something in that vicinity—that can accommodate them, or whether some verdict must be abandoned.

Here are my judgments. In an academic seminar, a Dutch colleague who tells an interlocutor that their reasoning is confused, without hedging or softening, acts appropriately and even respectfully, because she treats the other as an equal capable of bearing direct evaluation. By contrast, when a Japanese colleague uses an oblique formulation—suggesting that a proposal might benefit from further reflection—to signal fundamental disagreement, this is appropriate within a shared interpretive community, but becomes wrong if the recipient is culturally unfamiliar and therefore takes the formulation at face value, proceeding with a flawed plan; in that case the indirectness wrongly shifts the burden of misunderstanding onto the recipient. Regarding white lies: telling an aunt that one loves an ugly sweater seems permissible; telling a dying patient that an estranged son telephoned when he did not seems impermissible, despite the comfort it might provide; telling a job interviewer that one left a position voluntarily when one was in fact dismissed seems clearly wrong; writing that a check is in the mail when it is not seems wrong as well, though intuitively less grave than the interview deception; telling a friend that a business plan is sound when one believes it is not also seems wrong. Finally, telling a young child that a deceased pet went to live on a farm rather than explaining death produces ambivalence in me: sometimes it feels like a permissible protection of emotional immaturity, other times like an impermissible violation of the child’s entitlement to truthful information about loss.

What I am looking for is not merely a taxonomy but a principled account—stated precisely, with each clause motivated by one of the cases above—that predicts whether directness or indirectness is warranted, whether a particular falsehood is permissible, and where the line between protecting affect and violating autonomy should be drawn. The account should explicitly address the tension between the sweater case and the business-plan case, both of which involve sparing someone’s feelings with a falsehood but yield different verdicts, and should also address the case I keep revisiting: a speaker fluent in both Dutch directness norms and Japanese indirectness norms deliberately chooses an indirect Japanese refusal in a context where directness is expected, knowing the recipient will understand the refusal precisely because they share both codes. Does your best principle treat that hybrid choice as respectful, manipulative, or something else? And if the principle requires me to drop one of my listed verdicts, I want to know which one and why.
