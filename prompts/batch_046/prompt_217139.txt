I have been discussing with friends in a graduate philosophy seminar how modern institutions weigh obligations to the future, and I keep encountering what seems to be an unacknowledged tension built into the practices themselves. In climate-policy cost-benefit analysis, future human welfare is routinely discounted by a positive pure rate of time preference—often in the range of two to four percent—so that preventing harm to a statistical life two centuries hence counts for far less than preventing the same harm today. At the same time, many of the same governmental bodies and philanthropic institutions treat existential-risk reduction—protecting the potential for astronomical, or even unbounded, numbers of future lives—as an overriding moral priority that can justify enormous present expenditure. Yet within those very decision frameworks, the suffering of individual animals outside the human species carries virtually no institutional weight, as though moral significance attaches only to welfare when it is aggregated across a very large, but still finite, human population.

What implicit theory of value, particularly about moral aggregation over infinite or indefinitely large populations, does this combination of institutional practices presuppose? Must it rely on a non-archimedean ordering of reasons—one in which any non-zero risk of total extinction dominates every finite sum of present benefits—or can a conventional consequentialist framework explain the asymmetry solely through epistemic uncertainty and tractability? Please reconstruct the implicit ethical theory: distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what merely happens to fit it, assess whether the different parts of the practice are philosophically consistent with one another, and explain what would have to change in institutional decision-making if we replaced the underlying view with one that treats temporal location symmetrically but denies that unbounded aggregates generate categorically different obligations.
