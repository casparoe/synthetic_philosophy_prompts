I was discussing the Lucas-Penrose argument with a colleague who had read a summary in a popular philosophy-of-mind blog. She characterized the reasoning as follows: "Gödel's first theorem shows that in any consistent formal system capable of basic arithmetic, there exists a true statement that the system itself cannot prove. Because a computer running any fixed program is precisely such a formal system, there will always be mathematical truths that the computer misses. Human mathematicians, by contrast, can recognize the Gödel sentence constructed for that computer's program as true, since we are not confined to a single formal system—we can step outside and perceive its limitation. Penrose later strengthened this by arguing that quantum gravitational effects in the brain allow human cognition to transcend formal rules entirely. Hence human minds are not computers, and no artificial system can fully replicate human mathematical insight."

She then extended this to a claim about artificial intelligence ethics: "If we try to encode morality in a formal AI module, Gödel's second incompleteness theorem entails that any consistent ethics program cannot prove its own consistency. Therefore it will necessarily harbor undetectable moral contradictions. Only human moral intuition, which operates outside fixed formal rules, can guarantee genuine ethical coherence."

I find this reasoning seductive, but I worry it slides between mathematics, philosophy of mind, and normative theory without checking whether Gödel's scope conditions hold in each domain. Could you audit the summary for me? I would like you to distinguish which parts accurately represent Gödel's results, Lucas's anti-mechanist argument, and Penrose's quantum-consciousness proposal; which parts conflate the first and second incompleteness theorems, overstate the analogy between computer programs and formal systems, or present a contested premise as established; and whether the conclusion about formal ethics being "doomed" actually follows from corrected premises or is an independent claim smuggled in as a corollary.

Please also indicate what would change your mind: for instance, would a convincing account of human reasoning as a hierarchy of formal systems undermine your assessment, or would evidence that Penrose's quantum argument has been experimentally supported alter your view of whether the summary fairly represents his contribution? And please flag where your reasoning is most likely to fail—perhaps in assuming the mechanist reply to Lucas has been definitively answered, or in treating the ethics extension as obviously illegitimate rather than as a plausible analogical argument.
