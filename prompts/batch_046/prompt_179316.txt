I'm working through where I stand on Lucas-Penrose-style anti-mechanist arguments, and I'd like to know whether there's a single principle behind my case judgments. Here are my intuitions. (1) A human mathematician who can see that Peano arithmetic is consistent, and therefore grasp a Gödel sentence that PA itself cannot prove, strikes me as genuinely not identical to PA or any fixed formal extension of it. (2) A computer running one sound, fixed proof-checker strikes me as fully captured by that program; the corresponding Gödel limitation is just a feature of the program, not evidence that the computer exceeds it. (3) A perfect brain emulation with exactly a human mathematician's functional architecture, running fixed and consistently, still seems like it should be able to grasp its own Gödel sentence—so either the emulation misses something essential, or consistency can't be maintained. (4) A present-day large language model with frozen weights, prompted about arithmetic, doesn't seem to instantiate any single formal system with a well-defined consistency statement, so I think the Lucas-Penrose machinery simply doesn't apply. (5) A self-modifying system that updates its own axioms each time it proves consistency of its prior version seems to evade the argument, since there's never one fixed system to assign a permanent Gödel sentence. Is there a principle—about fixity of the formal object, about the distinction between derivability inside a system and semantic insight available only outside it, or about whether self-reference is anchored to a single architecture—that captures all five? If the answer requires overturning one of my verdicts, say which and why. Finally, apply your best principle to a case I haven't mentioned: a human mathematician who binds herself by an external, tamper-proof monitor never to accept axioms beyond PA.
