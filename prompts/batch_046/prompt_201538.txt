The long-running disagreement over supererogation—whether morality genuinely recognizes actions that are good yet not required—has persisted for decades with remarkably little movement. One camp holds that duties establish only a lower boundary of moral acceptability, leaving an open region above in which heroic rescue or saintly generosity is praiseworthy precisely because it exceeds obligation; the other camp denies any such region exists, arguing that every genuinely good act either falls under an imperfect duty or is merely optional in a non-moral sense, and that talk of going “beyond duty” confuses evaluative language with deontic logic. Recently both sides have adopted a shared formal representation—modeling moral choice as selection within a feasible set bounded by deontic constraints—yet they remain exactly where they were. My working hypothesis is that the stall is neither empirical nor purely verbal, but structural: both camps implicitly accept the same choice-space framework but disagree about whether it should contain a “normative slack” parameter, a measurable range of permissible deviation above duty that carries independent moral weight rather than being reducible to comparative utility or to imperfect-duty satisfaction. Diagnose the disagreement using what each side actually says about concrete cases like costly anonymous donation or life-risking rescue: is it a hidden dispute about whether moral reasons are comparative or non-comparative; are the two camps answering different questions, one about normative ethics and one about the logic of praise; is there a bedrock intuition about whether morality can demand too much that neither side can defend without circularity; or is the entire framing ill-posed? And given your diagnosis, tell me what would actually count as progress—a formal result in deontic logic, a reconceptualization of moral praise, a distinction that dissolves the category, or an argument that the question should be abandoned?
