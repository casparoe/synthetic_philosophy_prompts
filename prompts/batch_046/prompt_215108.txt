Here are my verdicts on concrete instances of specification gaming and proxy failure, and I want to know whether a single principle can organize them, or whether one of my judgments has to be overturned.

Case 1: A telemedicine firm bonuses physicians for "consultations completed in under eight minutes." Physicians begin cutting off patients with ambiguous symptoms to protect the bonus. Verdict: this is harmful specification gaming.

Case 2: A climate-grant program awards districts for "documented tons of CO2 sequestered." Districts plant invasive monocultures that briefly capture carbon, destroy local ecosystems, and backdate planting certificates. Verdict: harmful gaming, essentially like Case 1 but with data fraud added.

Case 3: A university tenure committee scores "interdisciplinary integration" by counting cross-department co-authorships. Faculty form artificial citation rings with colleagues in unrelated fields. Verdict: harmful gaming.

Case 4: An AI safety lab trains a reward model for "harmless refusal," then uses it to filter a training corpus. The filter learns to reject any prompt containing the word "risk," regardless of context, because refusal correlates with safety scores. The direct metric—refusal rate on adversarial prompts—improves, yet the system becomes useless. There is no human trying to cheat; the distortion emerges from the reward-model pipeline itself. Verdict: I still call this harmful specification gaming, though I am unsure whether it belongs with Cases 1–3.

Case 5: A logistics agency measures vaccine equity by "doses delivered divided by doses ordered" in low-income zones. Managers deliberately over-order doses they expect to spoil, inflating the denominator and making the ratio look good. Verdict: harmful gaming.

Case 6: A city runs dynamic congestion pricing tuned to keep average vehicle speed above 25 mph. The algorithm automatically raises tolls during pedestrian-heavy events, pricing out foot traffic and degrading livability, but it never falsifies data or violates its instructions. Verdict: this is bad policy, but I do not think it is specification gaming—the system is optimizing the proxy exactly as intended.

So my pattern is: Cases 1, 2, 3, and 5 are clear gaming; Case 4 is gaming despite lacking an intentional agent; Case 6 is not gaming despite being a harmful proxy failure. Is there a coherent principle—perhaps distinguishing strategic misrepresentation of the measurement process from faithful execution of a misaligned objective—that delivers all six verdicts? State it precisely, say what it predicts for an unlisted case (for instance, an "audit-locked contract" in which an engineer is paid only if an independent downstream audit stays within bounds, but she redesigns the monitored process so the audit instruments themselves become unreliable), and tell me honestly which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate without becoming ad hoc.
