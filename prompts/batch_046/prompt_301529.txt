Two members of a reading group I facilitate got stuck in a disagreement about why moral practices like dueling and chattel slavery actually disappear. I am reporting the exchange neutrally; please adjudicate it move by move.

The first, Maya, defended what she called the Williams thesis: she argued that both practices collapsed mainly because they became structurally inconvenient for the powerful. In Britain, she said, dueling faded not because anyone convinced aristocrats it was immoral, but because a centralized legal system made private violence redundant and risky; in the Atlantic world, slavery was abolished when industrial capitalism made slave labor less profitable and imperial interests favored free-labor markets. She conceded that abolitionists and anti-dueling pamphleteers made noise, but insisted that moral arguments only gained purchase after material conditions had already turned.

Jon replied that this concedes too little to Appiah's honor-based account. He said Appiah's point is not that honor operates in a vacuum, but that moral arguments work precisely by hijacking the honor codes of the elite. Abolitionists, he noted, did not merely cite efficiency; they framed slaveholding as degrading to the master's Christian honor and to national honor. Likewise, dueling declined when being seen as a gentleman required self-restraint, not just avoiding legal penalty. So, Jon argued, interest explains when elites become receptive, but honor explains the specific form the collapse takes.

Maya then introduced a distinction she called normative delegation versus normative inversion. Delegation, she said, occurs when a practice is outsourced to institutions: duels disappeared because courts took over dispute resolution. Inversion occurs when a practice is reclassified as actively shameful: slavery became a moral stain. She claimed dueling ended by delegation (the state made private violence redundant) while slavery ended by inversion (it was morally delegitimized). This, she said, shows that Appiah's honor dynamics apply only to inversion cases, not to all moral revolutions.

Jon responded that Maya's distinction actually strengthens Appiah's position and weakens the pure Williams thesis. If slavery ended through inversion — being seen as shameful — then the mechanism is closer to honor-driven moral change than to economic restructuring. He then invoked a claim associated with Williams: that moral revolutions happen when a practice becomes incompatible with the self-image of those who maintain it. Jon argued that this describes inversion, not delegation: people do not feel shame about outsourcing violence to courts, but they do feel shame about owning slaves once the practice is seen as base. Therefore, he concluded, Maya should either drop the interest-based claim for slavery or explain why inversion is not honor-driven.

Maya's final move was to say that the thesis about self-image is compatible with her view: elites only experience shame about slavery once their economic interests have already shifted, so inversion is an epiphenomenon of interest. She accused Jon of treating honor as an independent causal engine when it is really a post-hoc rationalization of structural change.

Please adjudicate this exchange point by point — Maya's opening defense of the Williams thesis, Jon's Appiah-inspired reply, Maya's delegation/inversion distinction, Jon's invocation of Williams on inversion, and Maya's final reduction of honor to interest — rating each move and briefly justifying your rating. Then say who is more right overall, identify what both are missing (for instance, whether they adequately account for the role of direct moral argument, or whether the delegation/inversion distinction holds up across both cases), and keep your analysis focused on the arguments as actually made. Write your answer in American English.
