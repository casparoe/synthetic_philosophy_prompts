I'm a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy, but I've been thinking about whether consciousness is just a matter of running the right kind of information-processing architecture, or whether there's something irreducibly extra. To get clearer, I've been tracking my own gut-level judgments about what's genuinely conceivable — specifically, whether I can imagine a being that is behaviorally and physically just like a conscious being, but with no inner experience at all.

Here are my verdicts, case by case.

First, the classic philosophical zombie: a being physically and behaviorally identical to me, including brain structure, neural dynamics, and verbal reports about pain and color, but with no phenomenal consciousness whatsoever. I find this genuinely conceivable. It seems coherent to imagine the lights being out inside despite everything functioning normally.

Second, a partial zombie: a being that processes visual and auditory information with identical architecture, behaves identically, and reports both seeing and hearing, but only has conscious experience of vision; the auditory stream is processed fully but occurs entirely "in the dark," with no subjective quality. This also strikes me as conceivable. Consciousness could be present for one modality and absent for another with no external difference.

Third, a temporal zombie: a being whose brain state and behavior are continuous and unbroken over time, but during one specific ten-minute interval — with no physical or behavioral discontinuity at either boundary — all phenomenal experience ceases, then resumes exactly as before. This I find genuinely inconceivable. If the substrate doesn't change and behavior doesn't change, I can't make sense of consciousness simply dropping out for a window and then returning.

Fourth, a modal zombie: my exact physical duplicate, but existing in a possible world where one fundamental law is slightly different — say, quantum coherence decays at a different rate — so that the same atomic arrangement produces no consciousness. This feels conceivable to me. The emergence of consciousness might depend on specific laws being in place, not just on arrangement.

Fifth, my actual-world duplicate: created atom-for-atom right here, running in parallel with identical dynamics and environment. I think it is inconceivable that this being lacks consciousness. If every physical fact is duplicated in the actual world, experience must be duplicated too.

These judgments feel somewhat consistent, but I can feel the tension. If partial zombies are conceivable, I'm allowing consciousness to be selectively absent despite full functional parity; but then why can't it be temporarily absent despite full temporal continuity? And if I think actual-world physical duplication guarantees consciousness, why do I allow modal zombies where only the laws differ?

My request is this: is there a coherent principle — or a small family of competing principles — that captures all five verdicts? Please state any principle precisely, identify which case motivates each clause, and tell me what it predicts for cases I haven't considered, such as a being with inverted color experience but normal behavior, or a being whose consciousness is present only during sleep. Crucially, please be explicit about which of my five verdicts the principle cannot accommodate, and whether overturning that verdict is more defensible than complicating the principle. If no single principle works, present the competing candidates with the same structure for each.
