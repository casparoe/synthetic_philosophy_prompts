Exam question (graduate seminar, Philosophy of Scientific Modeling):

Milton Friedman’s well-known F-twist holds that unrealistic assumptions in a positive scientific model are defensible when the model yields accurate predictions, because the aim of science is predictive success rather than descriptive realism of its premises. Consider a macroeconomic forecasting model that (a) assumes fully rational expectations among agents, despite extensive evidence of bounded rationality, and (b) relies on a high-frequency consumer-sentiment index as a surrogate for the unobservable long-run utility construct it aims to track. A critic argues that the F-twist cannot license ignoring both problems: the rational-expectations postulate is an unrealistic theoretical assumption, whereas the sentiment index involves measurement error and potential structural drift relative to the target variable. Is the critic correct? In your answer, clearly distinguish between representational falsehood in core theoretical assumptions and the use of surrogate variables, and explain whether predictive success alone resolves both concerns.

Student answer:

Friedman’s F-twist says that if a model predicts well, we should not care whether its assumptions are true or false. So in this case, both the rational-expectations assumption and the sentiment index are justified, as long as GDP forecasts remain accurate. The falsehood of rational expectations is actually beneficial because it simplifies the mathematics, which is exactly what Friedman meant by unrealistic assumptions being acceptable. Meanwhile, the sentiment index is just a practical substitute for utility, and since the F-twist makes predictive success the only criterion, any measurement error or drift in the proxy does not matter either—prediction absorbs all epistemic worries. Therefore, the critic is wrong: the F-twist fully licenses ignoring both issues, and representational accuracy is irrelevant to model evaluation.
