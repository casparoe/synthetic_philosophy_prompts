I've been covering this conciliationism vs steadfastness row for a while, and I'm starting to favour a pretty specific version: it's not just "meet in the middle" but the claim that holding firm is a kind of epistemic malpractice, because you've just recieved direct evidence that an equally good reasoning system processed the same data and came out elsewhere. The position I'm playing straight with is that rational belief revision should always shift towards your peer's view unless you can point to a specific, articulable asymetry in access or competence — and that "I just see it differently" isn't enough on its own.

So what would it take to knock this over? I want the honest exposure map: which assumptions about peerhood, credence, or reasoning transparency could in principle fail, what the world would have to look like for each, and which escape routes are live rather than merely logical? Is there decisive counter-evidence, or only awkward cases? What would conciliationists themselves concede counts against them — not just what the steadfast camp shouts? And is the view well insulated, maybe too well — can it always retreat to "they weren't really peers" or "there's hidden asymetry"? I'm especially stuck on a niche twist: disagreement about the calibration of one's own confidence function, not about the world. If two peers disagree on how reliable they are, does conciliationism spiral into self-undermining recursion? That's the bit I think might actually break it, but I want to know if it's a real defeater or just a puzzle. Give me the principle and where it's genuinely exposed.
