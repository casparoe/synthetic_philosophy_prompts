I'm taking this course on collective memory and now I'm totally stuck on an argument about what I'm calling defeat valorization. Basically it's the move where a society turns a military loss into a moral win, like with Masada or Dunkirk. I tried to formalize why this feels okay for some cases but not for others, and now I have this chain that proves way too much and I can't find the crack.

(1) A defeat can only be valorized if the cause it was suffered for was genuinely worthy.
(2) If a cause is worthy, then its narrative deserves commemorative protection, messy historical details and all.
(3) Dunkirk and Masada get this protection because their causes—resistance to fascism, religious-national survival—are seen as worthy despite the tactical failures.
(4) The Lost Cause narrative of the Confederacy also frames Appomattox as a noble defeat suffered for a cause its defenders describe as worthy—states rights, agrarian virtue, whatever—despite including chattel slavery.
(5) So if (1) and (2) hold, then commemorative protection should extend to the Lost Cause too, as long as its defenders sincerely claim worthiness.
(6) But that's clearly wrong, since the cause included racial domination and shouldn't be protected.
(7) Therefore either (1) or (2) fails, which means Dunkirk and Masada can't be valorized the way they usually are.

I really want to keep (1) and (6) but they seem to collide. Is there an equivocation hiding in 'worthy cause'—maybe between moral worth and strategic worth? Or does (4) just mischaractarize the Lost Cause by treating slavery as incidental rather than central? Where exactly does the slip happen, and what does fixing it cost me? If I have to choose, I'd rather keep (6) than (5).
