First, clarify how the self-sampling assumption treats one's own existence as a random draw from a specified reference class of observers, whereas the self-indication assumption takes the very existence of observers to be evidence that favours worlds containing larger populations. To prevent the analysis from remaining purely generic, extend the discussion to a niche case: artificial cognitive systems that can be instantiated in arbitrarily large numbers across distributed hardware, where functional equivalence rather than biological continuity defines membership in the observer class. Does the presumptuous philosopher's charge of arrogance become more or less forceful when the reference class is composed of such easily replicable artificial minds? Finally, judge which assumption yields the more coherent account of rational belief formation in this extended setting, and identify any hidden premises that might still undermine your preferred view.
