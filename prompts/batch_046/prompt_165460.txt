I'm trying to get my commitments on weakness of will and intertemporal choice straight before I present them in a practical-reasoning workshop, and I want a consistency audit rather than a lecture. Here is where I stand: (1) I think akrasia is real—agents can sincerely judge that φ is best, not change that judgment, and still do not-φ. (2) I accept hyperbolic discounting as the correct descriptive model of human intertemporal choice: discount rates fall with delay, producing time-inconsistent preferences. (3) I think that in many standard cases—breaking a diet, procrastinating—the reversal is rational relative to the curve, not a failure to execute a stable judgment; there is no single 'true' preference being betrayed. (4) Even so, I want to preserve a residual category of 'pure' akrasia: cases where the hyperbolic curve predicts stability, the agent's reflective values remain constant, and yet they act against their own ranking. (5) I deny that the temporally distant or more psychologically continuous 'future self' has any special normative authority over the present self; there is no diachronic 'true self' whose preferences should rule. (6) I explain the guilt of akrasia by saying the agent identifies, at the moment of judgment, with the long-range preference, and I claim this identification tracks reflective values rather than being arbitrary. (7) I think reflective equilibrium should privilege judgments formed in 'cool' deliberative states over 'hot' affective ones, but I reject that this privilege comes from cool states belonging to a more authentic self.

Go through these and tell me which pairs are genuinely in tension, which only appear to conflict but dissolve on inspection, and rank the real tensions by severity. For each genuine tension, say what the cheapest revision would be and what it costs. Then tell me which single commitment, if revised, would clean up the most tension with the least damage to the overall picture.
