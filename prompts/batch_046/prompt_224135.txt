At a recent interdisciplinary seminar on medical ontology, two scholars—Dr. Sarah Kwan, defending a biostatistical naturalist position, and Dr. Elias Thorne, advancing a value-laden normativist framework—became deadlocked over whether a condition they termed sustained hypometabolic adaptation should be classified as a disease. This state, observed in certain long-term practitioners of severe caloric restriction, involves a persistent 25–30 percent reduction in basal metabolic rate, altered peripheral conversion of thyroid hormones, and suppressed leptin signaling, yet is accompanied by subjective reports of heightened mental clarity and, in some longitudinal cohorts, reduced inflammatory markers. Kwan argued that regardless of these subjective benefits, the condition represents a clear deviation from the statistically defined range of human energy-homeostasis mechanisms; on her view, disease is fundamentally a failure of some internal part or process to perform its species-typical function, and the hypothalamic-pituitary axis is operating outside its design parameters. Thorne responded that selecting species-typical function as the normative baseline already embeds a hidden value judgment—evolutionary history is not a moral authority—and that without an independently demonstrable harm, such as diminished reproductive success, accelerated cellular senescence, or an inability to fulfill basic social roles, we are merely labeling an unpopular physiological strategy as pathological.

Kwan then pressed that Thorne’s harm requirement fails to explain why clinicians aggressively treat asymptomatic conditions like stage-one hypertension or pre-symptomatic arterial plaque, where no present harm exists; she claimed that if harm is conceptually necessary, medicine collapses into a purely reactive practice that misses genuinely dysfunctional but silent processes. Thorne conceded the clinical urgency but insisted it reveals only a pragmatic conflation of risk and disease, not a conceptual identity: risk states warrant intervention without being diseases proper, just as carrying a genetic predisposition is not identical to having the disorder itself. He added that Kwan’s language of functional design covertly relies on a normative notion of proper functioning that she claims to have expelled from her account. They asked me to adjudicate, and I found myself genuinely unsure how to weigh these moves.

Please go through their exchange step by step—rating each of Kwan’s and Thorne’s four central argumentative moves from clearly mistaken to clearly correct, with brief justification for each rating—and then deliver an overall verdict: which framework, if either, better accommodates the case of sustained hypometabolic adaptation, and what is the single most important conceptual point that both participants overlook in their framing of medical ontology?
