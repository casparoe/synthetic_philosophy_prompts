Having spent my career measuring spectral lines and quantum transitions in a condensed-matter laboratory, I have only recently begun to work through the philosophy of mind with any seriousness. The classic scenario involving Mary, the neuroscientist confined to a monochrome environment who learns all physical facts about color vision before encountering red, strikes me as a well-engineered conceptual experiment—except that its conclusions seem to depend on assumptions about informational completeness that a physicist might wish to interrogate. I therefore want you to design a modified version that meets precise specifications.

Construct a single-paragraph thought experiment, realistic and free of exotic physics or hypothetical brain surgery, in which Mary acquires exhaustive physical knowledge of chromatic experience—including the quantum electrodynamics of photoreceptor excitation and the full cortical mapping—while remaining in her black-and-white room, but in which her eventual direct encounter with red is mediated not by natural eyesight but by a calibrated clinical sensory-substitution interface (for example, a tactile or auditory encoding of spectral data mapped onto her cortex through an established prosthetic pathway). The case must be arranged so that Mary genuinely acquires a new discriminatory capacity upon activation—she can now distinguish hues she previously could not—yet the standard knowledge argument for non-physical phenomenal properties fails to apply, and this failure must not come from the familiar ability-hypothesis response but from a structural feature of her epistemic situation.

After presenting the scenario, do the following. First, state Mary’s belief, the method by which it is formed, and the relevant nearby possible worlds; then show why the original argument applies in the standard Mary’s-room case but misfires in yours. Second, identify the single structural feature of your design that does the blocking work, and explain precisely what happens if that feature is removed—whether the case collapses back into the standard argument or becomes something else entirely. Third, explain how a defender of the knowledge argument could revise your scenario—without altering Mary’s internal cognitive state—to restore the conclusion that she gains genuinely novel phenomenal knowledge. Keep the analysis within ordinary conceptual resources; do not invoke bibliographies, reading lists, or citations, and avoid putting any real textual passages in quotation marks.
