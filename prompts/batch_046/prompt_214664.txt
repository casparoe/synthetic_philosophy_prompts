I have recently begun drafting ethical guidelines for an anti-corruption program in a region where patron-client obligations are central to social life, and I want to check whether my own commitments are coherent before I finalize them. They are: (1) corruption is best defined functionally, as the misuse of entrusted public authority for private or partisan benefit; (2) the boundary of "private benefit" must be drawn relative to local norms of reciprocity and obligation rather than to Western liberal individualism, since otherwise anti-corruption work becomes moral imperialism; (3) patron-client exchanges—officials directing public jobs or contracts to kin or co-ethnics in return for loyalty—are corrupt even where they are socially normative; (4) in those same societies, refusing to assist one's relatives or community members from a position of office is widely regarded as a serious moral failure, sometimes graver than corruption itself; (5) routine gift-giving to bureaucrats to expedite licenses or avoid penalties counts as corruption even when there is no explicit quid pro quo and even when the practice is culturally expected as a sign of respect; and (6) I reject universalism about corruption: I deny that it is wrong in precisely the same form and degree regardless of cultural context.

Please audit this package. Which pairs are genuinely in tension, which conflicts are only apparent, and for each real tension what is the least costly revision? Rank them by severity. Then say which single change to my commitments would resolve the most tension at once. I am not asking for bibliographic references or lists of scholars—just whether these six claims can jointly be held and where the pressure points lie.
