Having spent thirty years in experimental physics and only recently turned to serious study in moral philosophy, I am struck by an institutional asymmetry that seems philosophically loaded. In many legal systems, a competent person may execute an advance directive authorizing antipsychotic treatment should she later be diagnosed with psychosis, and clinicians may invoke that prior authorization to treat her even when she presently and vehemently refuses. The framework treats the prior author as the legitimate decision-maker and the present, refusing patient as, in some relevant sense, not the same autonomous agent. Yet for physical illness—delirium arising from infection, for example—an analogous prior refusal is typically treated as revocable the moment capacity returns; the law does not presume that the delirious patient has been replaced by a different self, but allows her restored judgment to override the document.

What theory of mental illness, personal identity over time, and moral agency does this differential treatment presuppose? Must it commit to the view—famously advanced by Thomas Szasz in his critique of psychiatric power—that mental illness is not a genuine disease but a myth that obscures conflicts of values and social control, so that the "psychotic" refusal is merely an inconvenient expression of the very same person? Or can the asymmetry be defended on narrower grounds—perhaps a theory of rational capacity, value coherence, or episodic authenticity—that do not require Szasz's broader anti-psychiatric thesis? Reconstruct the implicit philosophical anthropology that makes psychiatric directives binding across a presumed discontinuity of the self, distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what it merely accommodates, and say whether a framework that treated all mental-state changes as continuous modifications of one agent could consistently maintain the same institutional rules without collapsing into incoherence.
