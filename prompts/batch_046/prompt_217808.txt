my study group cookd up this argument and now it feels like it proves way too much. (1) if you're morally responsible for doing something, then you could have done otherwise in some real sense — otherwise you're just a puppet. (2) Frankfurt cases show that's false for individuals: Jones is still blameworthy for voting even though Black would have forced him if he hesitated, since Black never actually steps in. (3) so actual alternative possibilities aren't necessary for personal responsibility. (4) now apply the same logic to an institution: a hiring algorithm screens out applicants from certain zip codes, and there's technically an appeals board that could overide any decision, but in practice it never meets and the protocol is buried on page 47 of the employee manual. no individual worker could have changed the outcome. (5) by the Frankfurt reasoning, the institution shouldn't be blamed for discrimination, because the "blocker" (the appeals board) never activated — there's no actual alternative path anyone took. (6) but that's absurd, we still hold the company responsible. (7) therefore Frankfurt reasoning must be wrong, and we need real alternatives for responsibility, even for collective agents. where's the slip? i feel like there's a real difference between Black in Jones's head and a bureaucratic board that never convenes, but i can't pin it down. does saving institutional blame force me to reject Frankfurt cases for people too, or is there a hidden distinction i'm missing?
