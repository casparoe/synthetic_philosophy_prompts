I have been discussing with colleagues the ways in which Buridan's ass is usually presented as a problem of external symmetry—two identical bales of hay—when, I suspect, the more philosophically fertile tension lies in whether the paralysis can be generated or dissolved by features internal to the agent's reasoning itself.

Would you please construct a realistic thought experiment, set in a professional or clinical environment and confined to a single paragraph, in which an agent faces two genuinely equivalent courses of action with no hidden preference between them, but in which the symmetry is broken solely by an internal feature of the agent's deliberative structure rather than by any difference in the options? The case should be engineered so that a rationalist theory of practical reason—holding that rational choice requires a sufficient reason—predicts that the agent remains paralysed, while a voluntarist theory—holding that the will can determine itself without prior reason—predicts decisive, non-random action. After presenting the scenario, please demonstrate why each theory delivers its verdict, then isolate the precise structural feature that does the work in separating them. In addition, explain whether removing that internal feature causes the case to collapse back into the standard Buridan formulation, and describe how a rationalist opponent might criticise the scenario without denying that arbitrary choice is ever possible. I should be grateful if you could present your answer in British English.
