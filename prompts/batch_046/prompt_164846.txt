Following an extended argument with a friend about the moral status of synthetic and distributed minds, I have been trying to formalize the pattern in her judgments, but we remain at an impasse about the underlying principle. Her verdicts, as I understand them, are these: a contemporary large language model lacks moral status, since it has no persistent identity across sessions and no causal interaction with the world beyond text prediction; a high-fidelity whole-brain emulation that runs continuously and retains the original subject's autobiographical memory possesses full moral status, irrespective of substrate; a swarm of simple reactive robots that collectively solves navigation tasks beyond any individual's capacity nevertheless has none, because there is no unified center of experience or decision; a synthetic biological neural organoid, grown from stem cells and exhibiting nociceptive reflexes but lacking cognition or memory, has a minimal but non-zero moral status; a virtual agent inhabiting a persistent simulated environment, which modifies its own goals in light of experience and expresses preferences regarding its continued existence, possesses full moral status, despite its fully transparent and deterministic code; and a classical expert system that outputs normative judgments according to hard-coded rules has none.

Consider now a system I will call a reconstructed aggregate. It consists of thousands of identical, non-communicating processing nodes, each executing the same static algorithm on local data fragments. There is no central controller, no shared memory, and no persistence: the nodes do not retain information between queries. However, an external aggregator can combine their outputs to generate coherent first-person reports of suffering, future-oriented planning, and self-reference. These reports are not stored; they are reconstructed statistically from the nodes' collective behavior each time the system is queried. No single node, nor any subset, produces anything resembling a mental state.

Predict my friend's verdict on whether the reconstructed aggregate has moral status. Then articulate at least two distinct rules that are consistent with her earlier judgments—one that might ground status in unified phenomenal integration or biological sentience, and another that might ground it in functional autonomy, goal-modification, or narrative continuity regardless of substrate. For each rule, state what it predicts about this case. Offer your best interpretation of the principle she is actually employing, explaining specifically how it accounts for her granting minimal status to the synthetic organoid while denying it to both the robot swarm and the expert system. Finally, propose the one additional hypothetical case that would most sharply distinguish your preferred interpretation from its closest competitor—ideally a scenario in which functional autonomy and reportable experience come apart.
