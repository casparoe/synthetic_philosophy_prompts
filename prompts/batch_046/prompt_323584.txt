I serve as a community representative on our state’s Sentencing Reform Advisory Panel, and though I have no formal background in philosophy or law, I have been trying to work through a problem that kept surfacing during our public hearings. We must deliver a formal recommendation to the legislature about whether to retain capital punishment for aggravated homicide, replace it entirely with life imprisonment without parole, or adopt an intermediate framework—indefinite civil commitment with periodic moral-status review—that some witnesses proposed as a way to preserve severity without finality. I want the memo to treat this not merely as a crime-policy debate but as an institutional decision turning on three considerations we kept returning to: retribution (whether execution is the only proportionate response to the worst offenses, and whether state killing expresses something imprisonment cannot), deterrence (whether the threat of death actually prevents murder in a way life imprisonment does not, and whether that prevention depends on the state’s moral credibility), and the risk of executing an innocent person (not only as a statistical cost, but as a structural failure that might corrupt the institution’s authority).

What keeps nagging at me is a distinction that was not in any expert testimony—something I want to call an “irreversibility asymmetry.” The thought is that wrongful imprisonment is a grave harm, but it leaves open the possibility of correction, acknowledgment, and release; wrongful execution eliminates the very subject who could be vindicated, which does not merely magnify the error but changes its moral kind. If retributive justice requires that the state actually deserve to inflict harm on this specific offender—because the punishment must be fitting to actual desert—then the institutional fallibility that makes execution risky does not just weigh against it on a balance sheet; it undermines the moral standing required to administer it at all. Meanwhile, deterrence seems partly dependent on the population’s belief that the state’s threats are both credible and morally legitimate; if citizens know the system sometimes kills the wrong people, the deterrent message may become contaminated rather than strengthened. Please draft a concise but formal memo for the panel recommending one of these three paths—retention, replacement with life without parole, or adoption of the review framework—with a clear bottom line and, if change is recommended, a concrete implementation time frame. The memo should engage seriously with the strongest consideration on the losing side—specifically, the claim made by victims’ advocates that only execution fully honors the gravity of the offense and provides a form of closure that no substitute can match—and it should explain why, even granting the emotional and moral weight of that claim, the structural problem of irreversibility still carries the day. Finally, please indicate how confident you are in what you claim and single out the specific point about which you are least certain.
