Below are notes for an essay I am drafting on institutional failure in speech-act theory. Austin distinguishes misfires, where conventional conditions are not met, from abuses, where they are satisfied but the act is hollow or insincere; both treat authorization as binary. I want to propose a neglected intermediate case—what I call performative residue. Consider a wedding ceremony performed by an unauthorized officiant: by Austin’s lights this is a classic misfire, yet it frequently leaves institutional traces—joint tax filings, property entanglements, social recognition, and sometimes obligations that require formal annulment rather than simple negation. The event does not merely fail; it produces partial effects that must be actively unraveled, which suggests that illocutionary force might be graded rather than all-or-nothing. This raises a problem for Searle’s account of institutional facts: if collective acceptance establishes that X counts as Y in context C, can there be degraded or partial acceptance that generates obligations without full authorization, or does this only reveal a gap between philosophical analysis and administrative remedy? I am especially unsure whether graded force conflicts with the collective-intentionality basis of institutional reality, and whether I am conflating legal disentanglement with a genuinely linguistic phenomenon.

Please comment on three points: first, whether my reading of Austin and Searle is accurate and whether performative residue picks out something philosophically interesting rather than mere bureaucratic aftermath; second, whether graded illocutionary force is coherent with collective-acceptance accounts; and third, whether the marriage example illustrates a feature of speech acts or only a feature of legal practice.
