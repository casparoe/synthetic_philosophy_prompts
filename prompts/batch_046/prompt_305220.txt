I've been reading rationalist forums and philosophy of language for years, and I've noticed a recurring argumentative pattern that I don't have a precise term for. It involves Quine's thesis of the indeterminacy of translation—specifically, the idea that behavioral evidence alone cannot fix whether a speaker's term 'gavagai' refers to rabbits, to undetached rabbit parts, or to other gerrymandered entities, because multiple translation manuals can preserve all predictions about usage and action.

The pattern I see is this. In debates about AI alignment or mental-state attribution, a speaker will invoke this underdetermination to block an interpretation they reject: they will say that since the behavioral data are equally compatible with an agent optimizing for human welfare and with one optimizing for paperclip maximization under a different internal ontology, we have no fact-of-the-matter basis to claim the agent 'really' has the benign goal. So far, this is a standard Quinean move. But then the same speaker will proceed to describe the system's architecture—using terms like 'reward function', 'world-model', or 'optimizer'—with complete confidence that those descriptions have fixed reference, never applying the same skeptical translation-manual swap to their own vocabulary. The indeterminacy is not treated as a universal limitation on interpretation; it is deployed asymmetrically, precisely where it undermines a normative or explanatory conclusion the speaker dislikes, and suppressed where it would destabilize their own framework.

I've seen analogues with human psychology: a critic will argue that we cannot determinately attribute 'justice' to someone's mental state because of reference indeterminacy, yet will confidently assert that they themselves understand 'rationality' in a determinate way within the same argument. The asymmetry is systematic rather than accidental.

My question is whether this selective, weaponized deployment of Quinean skepticism has an established name—perhaps in the philosophy of science or in the epistemology of interpretation. How exactly does it differ from ordinary motivated reasoning, and how does it differ from the standard objection that radical translation skepticism is self-undermining if applied globally? I am not looking for a bare label; I want to know the boundaries of the phenomenon. Under what conditions is it legitimate to restrict the scope of reference indeterminacy—say, via causal-historical chains or theoretical-role constraints—without falling into this asymmetry? And if there is no established term, how should it be defined so as to distinguish it both from legitimate scope-limitation and from plain equivocation?
