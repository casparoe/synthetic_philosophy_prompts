I am preparing the final assessment for my graduate seminar on metaphysics and literature, and after a recent tutorial in which students conflated Borges's denial of temporal succession with a straightforward endorsement of eternalism, I want a question that forces them to work with the internal tension in his essays rather than with textbook summaries. Please write one essay question suitable for a 90-minute exam for MA-level philosophy students who have read Borges's 'A New Refutation of Time' and 'The Doctrine of Cycles' in translation but who are not Borges specialists. The question should require students to reconstruct Borges's argument that time is an illusion sustained by language and memory, then evaluate whether his reliance on Berkeley's idealism and Hume's empiricism actually undermines his conclusion—particularly given his claim that the present moment is both unique and unrepeatable, a claim that appears to smuggle linear succession back into his cyclical framework. It should also ask them to consider whether Borges's literary form—the essay as structured paradox—insulates him from philosophical contradiction or merely disguises it.

Please provide a model answer of approximately 800 words, followed by a marking scheme totaling 25 marks that separates an excellent answer from a competent one by naming specific moves. The scheme should allocate marks for: (1) precise reconstruction of Borges's argument using Berkeley and Hume without mere paraphrase; (2) identification of the tension between cyclical recurrence and the uniqueness of the present in 'The Doctrine of Cycles'; (3) engagement with the objection that denying time requires denying personal identity; (4) a reasoned judgment on whether the literary form saves Borges from contradiction; and (5) accurate deployment of conceptual distinctions such as B-theory versus A-theory, without allowing mere name-dropping to substitute for analysis. For each criterion, include the point value, a one-line description of what a top answer does, and a one-line note on the typical error—especially the tendency to treat Borges as a straightforward McTaggartian B-theorist or to confuse his cyclical view with Nietzsche's eternal recurrence without noting Borges's distinctively idealist framing.
