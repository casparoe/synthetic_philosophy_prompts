In debates about originals versus perfect replicas, I keep seeing a specific argumentative structure that I do not know how to name. A defender concedes that a replica is visually, chemically, and tactilely identical to an original painting. They then claim the original is more valuable because of its direct causal link to the artist's hand. If one proposes a technology that reproduces that exact physical trajectory robotically, they shift ground: the value now lies in the artist's mental states being present in the causal chain. If one then imagines recording and reproducing those mental states during execution, they retreat again, usually to historical uniqueness or to being the actual object that occupied that specific studio. The retreat is not arbitrary; each step preserves some non-replicable connection to the past.

Is there an established concept for this precise pattern of progressive retreat to ever-subtler historical connections? How does it differ from Benjamin's notion of aura and from ordinary arguments that tie value to causal origin? If no precise term exists, please coin one, define it with clear boundary conditions, and distinguish it from a standard no true Scotsman fallacy and from mere preference for rarity.
