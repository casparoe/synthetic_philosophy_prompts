I run an undergraduate philosophy seminar of about eighteen students, with one sixty-minute session available. Design an in-class activity—complete with rules, materials, and a token-based payoff scheme—that makes participants feel why transformative experience undermines not merely predictive knowledge but the coherence of rational deliberation itself. Frame the activity around the following specific idea: certain transformations (for example, becoming a parent, or undergoing a profound religious conversion) do not simply reveal hidden preferences; they replace the conceptual framework within which preferences are weighed, leaving the pre-transformation agent and the post-transformation agent with incommensurable evaluative metrics. The rules should force participants to commit tokens representing time, identity, or social capital before knowing which evaluative framework will govern them afterwards, and then require them to justify that commitment using the framework they only acquired through the act of committing. The debrief should map each stage of the activity onto the corresponding structural feature of transformative choice—ex ante deliberation, irreversible commitment, ex post framework shift, and retrospective justification—explain what will predictably fail on the first run (for instance, participants treating the two frameworks as interchangeable, or refusing to take the token economy seriously), and state frankly which feature of genuine transformative experience—the conceptual inaccessibility of the post-transformation state—cannot be reproduced in the classroom and must be supplied by the facilitator. Please present your design in British English, and ensure the idea emerges from what happens in the activity rather than from any upfront lecture.
