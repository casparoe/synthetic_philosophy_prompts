I'm working through a problem in epistemology for a seminar I'm auditing, and I keep getting stuck on the interaction between higher-order defeat and what is sometimes called rational akrasia in belief. Specifically, I want to compare two frameworks for handling cases in which an agent possesses strong first-order evidence for P yet also possesses higher-order evidence that her reasoning about P is systematically unreliable. On one side stands the level-splitting approach, which permits an agent to maintain a first-order belief that P while simultaneously holding a second-order belief that she ought not to believe P. On the other side stands the calibrationist approach, which requires the agent to reduce her first-order confidence in P proportionally to her estimated unreliability, thereby treating higher-order defeat as directly constraining doxastic strength.

What I find unclear is whether these frameworks are genuinely competing accounts of the same normative failure, or whether they instead regulate different targets—perhaps doxastic coherence across levels versus long-run predictive accuracy. To fix ideas, consider a senior meteorologist who has direct access to raw atmospheric data strongly indicating an approaching severe storm, but who also knows that recent institutional meta-analyses reveal meteorologists in her region consistently overpredict storm severity because of liability-averse forecasting norms. If she retains her first-order belief that the storm will be severe on the basis of her raw data, is she exhibiting a permissible form of epistemic akrasia under level-splitting, or is she failing to calibrate properly? Compare the two frameworks with respect to this case, and evaluate which better explains why her overall belief-state might be rationally defective—if indeed it is—without collapsing the normative distinction between first-order reasoning and higher-order self-assessment.
