Then discuss why dropping the common prior assumption doesn't just 'fix' the result for real-world experts who disagree—there's a deeper issue about how the theorem models disagreement in the first place."

The student wrote: "Aumann showed that if two rational agents share the same prior and it's common knowledge that they're rational, then after they share there posteriors they must agree on the probability of any event. The proof uses induction on the partition structure to show that disagreement can't survive common knowledge of rationality alone. If they still disagree, it just means they started with different priors, so the common prior assumption is necessary—without it you can build cases where agents never converge, like when each one thinks the other is systematically biased. This is why the theorem is controversial for real experts: people clearly keep disagreeing after talking, so either they lack rationality or they lack shared priors. Aumann also assumed common knowledge of the actual reasoning steps, not just the posteriors, which makes the result even stronger but also more unrealistic. In continuous belief spaces the result breaks down entirely, which is why applied work usually uses the Geanakoplos extension instead."
