In debates about split-brain patients following commissurotomy, a common move is to compare the two hemispheres to roommates who share an apartment but have installed a locked door between their rooms: each roommate has private experiences, private memories, and private preferences, and they only coordinate when they must share common resources—the hands, the mouth, the legs. The analogy is deployed to license the conclusion that a single human body can literally contain two distinct conscious subjects, and therefore that the unity of consciousness is contingent rather than essential. Lay out the correspondence table explicitly—what corresponds to the roommates, the locked door, the shared kitchen, the lease? Then show where the analogy breaks: consider that both hemispheres share a single emotional and autonomic system, a single body schema, and that the left hemisphere often confabulates explanations for actions initiated by the right. Identify which disanalogies actually undermine the two-subjects conclusion and which are merely decorative. In particular, does the analogy quietly import a real-estate model of personal identity—spatial separation equals subject separation—that the brain does not actually support? Finally, if the analogy fails to carry the conclusion, what is a more accurate way to describe the relation between the hemispheres? Answer in no more than 350 words.
