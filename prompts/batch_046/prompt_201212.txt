I am writing an extended essay for my sixth-form coursework on whether 'collapse' denotes a coherent historical phenomenon or merely groups together disparate processes that happen to look similar to modern observers. I have been considering three episodes that are frequently cited together — the crisis of the Late Bronze Age palace and network economies, the abandonment of the Classic Maya lowlands, and the ecological transformation of Rapa Nui — yet I remain unsure whether they belong in the same analytical category at all.

My tutor introduced the notion of 'institutional decoupling', whereby formal administrative structures dissolve while local kinship networks and subsistence practices persist largely intact; this seems to fit the Maya case rather well, but I struggle to apply it to Rapa Nui, where the contraction of the resource base appears to have affected the entire population rather than merely stripping away an elite superstructure. Meanwhile, the Bronze Age crisis involved the fragmentation of long-distance trade and writing systems, yet many local communities apparently continued with little demographic disruption. Could you explain the central conceptual issue here? Specifically, I should like an intuitive account of why treating these three episodes as 'collapses' might conflate the loss of political complexity with an actual decline in welfare or demographic continuity, and whether there is a defensible criterion for distinguishing a society that has genuinely failed from one that has simply reconfigured itself into a less centralised but still viable form.
