I run a grad-level philosophy of mind seminar, about 9 people, 90 minutes, and I want an activity that gets them inside the meta-problem of consciousness — not the hard problem itself, but the puzzle of why we think there's an explanatory gap in the first place. The idea should hit them through the rules, not a lecture.

Split the group into two teams of four plus one observer. Team A gets physical descriptions of five systems: a human reporting pain, a bat navigating, a thermostat regulating heat, a current language model outputing 'i feel confused', and a hive mind solving mazes. Team B gets only the first-person-style reports or judgments each system might produce about its own state — but framed differently, some with rich vocabulary but clear behavioral triggers, others with flat vocabulary but high confidence.

The game: Team A has to assign Team B's reports to the right physical system using only third-person functional descriptions. They score points for correct matches. But there's a hidden rule: Team B's cards include a 'gap judgment' card for every system, saying 'there's still something missing from the physical story.' Team A gets bonus points if they can explain why that gap judgment appears without using any phenomenal-language themselves — they have to account for the intuition using only cognitive-access, framing, or conceptual-role differences.

After 35 minutes, tally scores. Usually Team A realizes the best explanations all reduce the gap-judgment to reportability asymmetries or conceptual framing, never to an intrinsic property. That's the meta-problem moment: they've explained away the intuition without solving the hard problem.

Debrief (last 20 min): map each round onto the hard vs meta distinction. Be honest that the activity smuggles in first-person access for the human participants, which is exactly what a real phenomenal-concept theorist or illusionist has to bracket away. Also note the language-model card is deliberately underdetermined — small models probably don't recieve the right kind of report, but the structural parallel is what matters. Include a quick note on what will go wrong if one person dominates the matching game.

Keep it paper-only, no tech. And please don't make it sound like a TED talk — I want it a bit rough around the edges, maybe with a typo or two in the handout.
