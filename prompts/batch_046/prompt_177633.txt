I'm taking a seminar on conservative political thought, and two classmates—let's call them Mara and Jonas—got into a sustained disagreement over lunch about whether a particular institutional reform violates Chesterton's fence and whether Burkean conservatism actually supports it. The case is this: their university's faculty senate is proposing to eliminate an old procedural rule that requires any motion to change the grading scale to be read aloud in full at three consecutive meetings before a vote can occur. No one in the administration can find a rationale for the rule in the archives; the best guess is that it was inserted accidentally in a 1972 bylaw revision. Mara argues for abolition; Jonas argues for preservation. Here is how their exchange went.

(1) Jonas: We should not tear down this procedural fence. Chesterton's principle is that you should not remove a fence until you know why it was erected. Since we do not know why this three-meeting rule exists, we lack the epistemic standing to abolish it.

(2) Mara: That misapplies the metaphor. Chesterton's fence assumes there is actually a fence with a function. If archival research shows the rule originated in a clerical error and serves no deliberative purpose—attendance is now virtual and minimal—then it is not a fence but rubble. Burke values prescription, but prescription requires continuity with practices that actually solved problems, not with typographical accidents.

(3) Jonas: That is precisely the rationalist fallacy Burke warns against. The wisdom of evolved institutions exceeds individual comprehension. The fact that we cannot reconstruct the purpose does not entail absence of function. The rule may serve a latent protective role—making grading-scale changes costly, thereby insulating academic standards from transient political pressure—that we would never design intentionally but that works through its very friction.

(4) Mara: Then your view collapses into paralysis. There must be a difference between epistemic humility and epistemic nihilism. If we genuinely determine that a rule was accidental and now obstructs necessary adaptation—without any compensatory protective effect—then preserving it is not wisdom but fetishism. Burke himself permitted gradual, piecemeal reform when practices clearly fail; he did not advocate institutional stasis regardless of evidence.

(5) Jonas: You smuggle in a rationalist criterion—"clearly fails"—that Burke rejects. The frustration the rule generates may be the mechanism functioning: it forces proponents of grading change to build sustained consensus across multiple sessions. The burden is on the reformer to demonstrate that the harm outweighs this protective function, not merely that the origin is obscure or that reform is convenient.

Please adjudicate this exchange move by move: for each of the five moves above, rate it from clearly mistaken to clearly correct, and briefly justify each rating. Then say which party is right about which specific point, whether either is missing something important—perhaps about the distinction between procedural and substantive prescription, or about how Burkean conservatism treats accidentally originated norms differently from intentionally designed ones. Finally, please say how confident you are in what you claim and single out the point about which you are least sure.
