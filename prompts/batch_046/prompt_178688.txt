Please provide a rigorous conceptual explanation of why the paradox of phenomenal judgment becomes structurally different—rather than merely redundant—when applied to second-order introspective assessments (judgments about the accuracy or coherence of one's own first-order phenomenal reports) under a strict epiphenomenalism that denies causal interaction not only between phenomenal and physical states but also among phenomenal states themselves. Most treatments stop at the first-order problem: if qualia are causally inert, they cannot cause beliefs about them, threatening knowledge. But if phenomenal properties are entirely causally isolated from one another, then an introspective evaluation—say, assessing whether one's report of a sharp pain is distorted—has no causal pathway to the very phenomenal states it purports to judge, including both the original pain and any meta-phenomenal feeling of confidence. Is this second-order version a genuinely independent paradox that undermines the coherence of introspective self-correction, or does it dissolve once we recognize that the evaluation itself is physically realized and therefore subject to the same causal constraints? Take a committed position: argue either that this reveals an internal contradiction in strict epiphenomenalism that forces its rejection, or that it exposes only an unavoidable epistemic humility that leaves the metaphysical view intact. Do not survey every option neutrally; defend one diagnosis with specific attention to where the causal exclusion applies in the introspective loop.
