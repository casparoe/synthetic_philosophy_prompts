I am currently drafting a chapter for my graduate seminar in epistemology, and the issue has acquired an unexpected personal urgency. Last month I served as the junior voting member of an institutional review board evaluating a proposed clinical protocol. The principal investigator presented robust first-order neuroimaging and behavioral data supporting the intervention's efficacy, yet she also volunteered that her own statistical team had misinterpreted structurally similar datasets in two prior publications and that she now suspects a systematic bias in their analytical pipeline. Several board members argued that this higher-order evidence should defeat her present confidence in the new data, obliging her to withhold endorsement; others insisted that the first-order results must govern our collective verdict, treating her track record as biographically relevant but epistemically inert for this decision. The dispute struck me as a practical instance of epistemic akrasia—specifically, of what we might call institutional akrasia, where an agent is formally required to register an authoritative collective judgment even while possessing higher-order evidence that undermines her entitlement to that very judgment.

Please describe the most important implications of the interaction between higher-order defeat and epistemic akrasia for rational decision-making in such institutionally coupled settings. In particular, I want you to take a committed position: identify the two or three implications that you regard as genuinely decisive—whether about the rationality of maintaining first-order belief in the face of self-undermining evidence, about the conditions under which an agent should recuse herself from collective authorization, or about how institutional structures should be redesigned to accommodate reflective failure—defend that selection with explicit reasons, and avoid the format of a neutral survey of competing interpretations. If your argument turns on a substantive distinction—such as between allowing second-order doubts to remain isolated from first-order commitments versus requiring them to bridge into belief revision, or between a calibration-focused and a reliability-focused conception of rational belief—state your commitment clearly rather than presenting it as one disposable option among many.
