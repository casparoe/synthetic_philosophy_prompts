I am drafting the final examination for an upper-level philosophy of mind seminar that I teach each spring, and I find myself drawn to the same problem that haunts a character in my current novel: a woman who receives a neural replay of her deceased mother’s REM-state trajectory and wakes convinced she has lived through her mother’s dreams. To test whether my students can think with the concepts rather than merely about them, I want a single essay question—forty-five minutes, thirty marks, closed-book, answers of roughly eight hundred words—on the classic exchange between Norman Malcolm, who denies that dreams are experiences on logical-grammatical grounds, and Daniel Dennett, who defends their experiential status through his distinction between presentational consciousness and the cassette-theory of dream construction. The question must not be answerable by summarizing that exchange.

To force the work into the open, the prompt should embed what I will call the Archive Replay Case: neuroscientists record the complete neural trajectory of a sleeping subject D during REM sleep, then induce an identical trajectory in a fully awake subject S. S reports a vivid, first-person narrative that exactly matches D’s waking report. S has no knowledge of D and takes herself to be recalling a spontaneous dream she never actually had. The exam question should ask students to determine whether Malcolm’s criterion of wakeful reportability and Dennett’s loading mechanism can treat S’s state as an experience of the original dream, as a new experience with borrowed content, or as neither; and, more importantly, whether the replay case exposes that the Malcolm-Dennett debate was ultimately about logical grammar, causal history, or the conditions under which phenomenal ascription is warranted.

Please write three things: first, the exam question itself, precisely framed so that students must apply both frameworks to the replay condition and identify where each breaks or adapts; second, a model answer of approximately eight hundred words that includes the specific moves an A-grade response must make—particularly the distinction between the causal origin of the narrative and the grammatical conditions for calling it an experience, and the point at which Dennett’s cassette theory either accommodates or collapses under vicarious induction; and third, a marking scheme that separates an A answer from a B answer by naming the exact argumentative steps required, including the identification of the pressure point where Malcolm’s wakefulness condition is either satisfied or circumvented, and the evaluation of whether Dennett’s presentational account can distinguish replayed consciousness from constructed consciousness. No bibliography or citation list is needed; the students have the primary texts in memory, and the point is conceptual discipline, not reference-hunting.
