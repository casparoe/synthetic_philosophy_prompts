Consider an institutional choice architecture—designed but not yet implemented—where a public retirement system assigns each birth cohort a portfolio that rebalances automatically based on the cohort's aggregate contribution patterns, with individuals able to opt out only at fixed five-year windows. The default is not a static fund but a collective feedback mechanism: if the cohort saves less aggressively, the portfolio shifts toward safer assets, on the theory that the aggregate behavior reveals a collective risk preference that should be respected rather than overridden. From a rationalist framing, this is neither pure libertarian paternalism nor hard paternalism; it embeds a dynamic, cohort-relative theory of welfare into the mechanism itself. I want to reconstruct the philosophical commitments this design presupposes. Does it require that autonomy is exercised through periodic reflection rather than continuous exit, and if so, is that a view of the self as discontinuous across time? Does it presuppose that revealed preference at the cohort level tracks rational welfare, or does it instead treat aggregate behavior as noise that the mechanism corrects—thereby smuggling in an objective standard of rationality? How does it relate to liberal neutrality: is it defensible only if there is no fact of the matter about optimal savings, or precisely because there is such a fact and the regulator has epistemic authority? And where is the design philosophically underdetermined—could a perfectionist liberal, a mild communitarian, and a libertarian paternalist all endorse the same mechanism for incompatible reasons? I am especially interested in whether the discrete opt-out windows commit the design to a particular theory of responsibility, and whether the cohort-level feedback loop quietly replaces individual preference aggregation with something closer to a group-agent model of decision.
