After a long argument with a friend about whether consciousness is genuinely constituted by a second-order representation or whether that view collapses awareness into an internal filing system that can operate in complete absence of what it claims to file, I want a seminar simulation that makes the structural vulnerability visceral rather than textual. I run an upper-level philosophy of mind seminar — 14 students, one 75-minute session, no laptops — and I want participants to encounter what I have been calling the "empty-target asymmetry": the possibility that a higher-order monitoring protocol can be fully formed, sincerely executed, and intersubjectively validated even when there is no corresponding first-order mental state for it to represent.

Design the activity. Divide the session into three rounds with specific rules, materials, and a payoff scheme that uses only credibility points, not money. In Round One (20 minutes), form triads: one Experiencer, one Monitor, one Evaluator. Give each Experiencer a hidden card — some describe a specific qualitative first-order state (e.g., a diffuse chromatic afterimage, a vague somatic unease), others are blank "no-state" cards. Give each Monitor a visible Report Card describing a higher-order judgment that either matches, mismatches, or denies the hidden state. Crucially, include "ghost" pairings (a detailed Report Card with a blank Experiencer card) and "mute" pairings (a detailed first-order card with a Report Card reading "no conscious experience of this kind"). The Monitor presents only the Report Card; the Experiencer remains silent. The Evaluator assigns a consciousness score (1–5) and records it, using only the report and any permitted behavioral observation.

In Round Two (20 minutes), rotate roles so every student serves once as Monitor. Introduce the payoff: Evaluators earn credibility points for inter-rater consistency, not for matching hidden reality, since hidden reality is unrevealed until later. This should drive convergence on report-based norms rather than tracking invisible first-order states. Use physical envelopes or screens to prevent peeking; include an explicit penalty (loss of all points for that round) for attempting to access hidden cards.

In Round Three (15 minutes), reveal all hidden cards. Each triad must defend or revise its original scores under one hard constraint: appeals to "what it feels like from the inside" are disallowed, since in the simulation participants have no direct access to others' first-order states.

Then provide the debrief: map the ghost pairings onto misrepresentation or empty-target problems for actualist higher-order views; map the mute pairings onto unconscious first-order states that lack higher-order registration; map the Evaluators' report-driven convergence onto the risk that consciousness attribution becomes a social-bureaucratic procedure rather than tracking an independent mental reality. Include an honest note on what will predictably go wrong the first time — likely attempts to invent behavioral tells that betray the hidden card, or to peek at envelopes — and specify the structural blocks (physical barriers, penalty rules, a facilitator check) that prevent them. Finally, state frankly which feature of the idea the activity cannot reproduce: it cannot replicate the phenomenal presence that first-order theories treat as primitive; it can only reproduce the epistemic and ontological dilemmas that arise once consciousness is treated as a second-order achievement.

Make sure the rules and incentives are arranged so that participants run into the problem — especially the discomfort of treating a ghost report as fully legitimate and ignoring a mute first-order state — before the facilitator names it as a problem for higher-order theory. The debrief should come only after Round Three, and the facilitator should not lecture on Rosenthal-style views or on the distinction between actualist and dispositionalist accounts until after the simulation is complete.
