After a long argument with a friend about Marx’s account of machinery, she pressed me with a chain that seems to show his theory of technology is self-refuting. I want to keep (1) and (2) if I can. (1) For Marx, value is determined by socially necessary labor time, and surplus value is extracted solely from living labor. (2) In the Grundrisse he describes capital’s drive to develop science and automation until knowledge and fixed machinery—the general intellect—become the dominant productive forces, reducing direct labor to a supervisory residue. (3) If living labor is minimized, the substance of value should wither and surplus value should tend toward zero. (4) Yet Marx insists capital must constantly expand surplus value or face crisis and collapse. (5) So his own account predicts that capital’s technological dynamism will necessarily destroy the condition of its existence. (6) But capitalism has not collapsed despite deep automation; it operates profitably in sectors where direct labor is minimal. (7) Therefore the theory proves too much: it entails capitalism should already have self-destructed. Which step should I reject, and what does giving it up cost? I have a vague sense that something slides between material productivity and value productivity, or between the unit value of commodities and the global mass of surplus value, but I cannot locate the slip.
