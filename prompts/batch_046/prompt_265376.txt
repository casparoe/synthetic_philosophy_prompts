I am a novelist preparing an episode for a small podcast series on language, power, and institutional knowledge. My guest is an unnamed philosopher-psychiatrist who defends a rigorous, updated version of Thomas Szasz's argument: that what we call mental illness is not a literal disease of the brain but a metaphor for conflicts in living, and that modern psychiatry's turn toward dimensional diagnosis—scoring personality traits and emotional states along continuous spectra—does not salvage its scientific credentials but rather exposes its normative skeleton more clearly. By replacing categorical walls with gradients, he argues, the clinic converts moral judgment into a bureaucratic metric of deviance.

Please draft eight to ten interview questions that stay deep in this conceptual terrain. I want no biographical softballs, no requests for reading lists, and no appeals to citation. Each question should anticipate the reply he is most likely to give and carry a follow-up that makes the standard defense harder. In particular:

First, ask how the shift from categorical to dimensional diagnosis changes the force of the myth claim, with a follow-up keyed to his likely response that continuous scales merely render the hidden moral architecture visible: press him on whether any quantitative measure of human suffering can avoid being a disguised normative judgment.

Second, ask what he makes of reproducible neuroimaging and psychopharmacological findings if mental illness is not a brain disease. Follow up by asking whether his thesis is falsifiable—whether there exists any empirical discovery that could genuinely overturn it, or if it is structured to absorb every biological correlation as mere accompaniment rather than cause.

Third, ask on what ethical ground involuntary psychiatric commitment can rest if diagnosis is metaphorical rather than medical. Anticipate the reply that imminent danger to self or others justifies coercion, and follow up by asking whether that criterion reintroduces medical legitimacy through the back door, transforming a moral judgment about risk into a clinical mandate.

Fourth, ask how he distinguishes metaphorical mental conditions from literal neurological disorders such as advanced dementia or delirium tremens. Follow up by asking whether that boundary is ontologically sharp or merely pragmatic, and whether relying on pragmatism undermines the radical nature of his claim.

Fifth, ask about diagnostic performativity: whether psychiatric labels, once institutionalized, generate the very behaviors they describe through social feedback and bureaucratic expectation. Follow up by asking whether this looping effect contradicts the assertion that mental illness is purely metaphorical, given that metaphors can construct the realities they purport merely to name.

Sixth, ask what actually occurs in the therapeutic encounter if there is no underlying disease to treat. Anticipate the answer that therapy is a contractual negotiation of problems in living, and follow up by asking how that differs from secular moral counseling or philosophical consultation, and whether the distinction collapses once medical authority is withdrawn.

The tone should be formal, precise, and appropriate for a novelist who treats ideas as dramatic structures rather than museum pieces. Please do not include quotation marks around any real texts, and avoid asking the guest to cite specific works, dates, or authors.
