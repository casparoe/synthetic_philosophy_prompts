Below are my judgements about whether particular shapes or symbols count as the lowercase letter 'a', considered in the spirit of the Letter Spirit problem—what makes every 'a' an 'a'? A standard double-bowl printed 'a' is unambiguously one. The single-bowl handwritten form common in British school script is also an 'a', despite lacking the upper enclosed region. A conceptual typeface glyph that replaces the bowl with a hollow square, while preserving the ascender, right stem, and connecting bar in their usual proportions, still strikes me as an 'a', albeit at the boundary of recognisability. Yet a symbol geometrically congruent to that glyph, used solely as a corporate logo and never embedded in alphabetic text, does not strike me as the letter 'a' at all. A stencil that removes material to leave the negative-space outline of an 'a' counts, I think, as an instance of the letter; a cloud formation that happens to match that outline does not. In a children's alphabet book, a distorted form with the bowl open at the top—resembling a 'u' with a left ascender—appears in the word "cat"; I read it as a misprinted 'a', but in isolation I would be inclined to call it a malformed 'u'. Lastly, a symbol in an invented script that possesses the topology of an 'a'—one enclosed region, one right ascender—but is scaled to be twice as wide as it is tall and is assigned to represent the phoneme /o/, strikes me as not being an 'a'.

Is there a coherent principle—perhaps combining family resemblance, intended alphabetic function, and context-sensitive recognition—that captures these verdicts without collapsing them into contradiction? Which clause of such a principle is motivated by which case above? What does it predict for a case I have not listed: a perfectly formed mirror-image 'a', with its bowl on the left, used consistently throughout a left-handed handwriting manual to denote the sound /æ/? And if simplicity demands it, which of my judgements should be overturned?
