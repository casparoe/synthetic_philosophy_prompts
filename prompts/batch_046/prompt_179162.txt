I'm trying to stabilize my position for a philosophy-of-mind discussion group and need a consistency check before presenting. Here is where I stand:

(1) I reject the Cartesian theater in its classical form: there is no central, spatiotemporally localized "place" where all conscious experience is presented to a single inner observer.
(2) I broadly accept Dennett's multiple drafts model: conscious content is fixed in parallel, distributed processes, and there is no canonical version of experience that serves as the "real" stream.
(3) Nevertheless, I hold that there is a genuine phenomenal present with temporal thickness—roughly 200 to 500 ms—within which sensory modalities are bound into a unified field, and that this binding is not merely a post-hoc narrative construction.
(4) I think this binding is implemented by distributed predictive-coding circuits that stabilize a temporary global workspace, but without any single "viewer" or homunculus.
(5) I take the explanatory gap to be an epistemic artifact of applying third-person, object-oriented concepts to first-person processes, rather than an ontological mystery.
(6) I am inclined to think that an artificial system implementing (3) and (4) but lacking biological evolutionary history and linguistic self-reference could still possess phenomenal consciousness.

Which of these commitments genuinely pull against each other, and which only appear to? In particular, does (3) smuggle a theatrical structure back in despite (1) and (2), and does (6) conflict with (5) given that attributing consciousness to an alien system seems to require precisely the conceptual translation I claim creates the gap? Rank the tensions by severity, identify the least costly revision for each, and say which single change would clean up the most conflicts overall. Do not treat all conflicts as equally serious, and do not invoke bibliographic authority—just trace what each commitment commits me to.
