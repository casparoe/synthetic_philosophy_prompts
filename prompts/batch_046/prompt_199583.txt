I'm working through my own position for an upcoming metaphysics seminar and want to make sure the pieces actually fit together before I defend them. Here is where I stand on conceivability and possibility: I hold that positive conceivability—being able to imagine a scenario in full detail without detecting a contradiction—provides a defeasible but real guide to metaphysical possibility, though never an absolute guarantee. I also accept the familiar Kripke-style observation that a posteriori identities can fool us here: water failing to be H2O is conceivable, yet impossible, because the true essential nature is hidden from our ordinary concepts. Applying that same framework to consciousness, I take the positive conceivability of philosophical zombies as evidence that physicalism is false, since I do not think there is a hidden essential physical nature of consciousness that would make zombie worlds impossible in the way water-worlds are. At the same time, I find the phenomenal concept strategy persuasive—our phenomenal concepts are partial or perspectival, so they can make physicalism seem false even when it is true, which is precisely why zombies feel so vividly conceivable. I restrict this conceivability-based reasoning to metaphysical possibility alone: in mathematics, I think that if a basic axiom were actually inconsistent, that contradiction would eventually manifest in derivation and thus not be positively conceivable in advance, yet I still believe mathematicians ought to maintain practical epistemic humility and proceed as if ZFC might turn out inconsistent. Finally, I want to say that consciousness supervenes on physical states across all actually nearby worlds, but I deny that this supervenience is metaphysically necessary in the strongest sense, treating it instead as a deep contingent regularity.

My worry is that these commitments pull in opposite directions. The phenomenal concept strategy in my fourth point seems to explain away the zombie intuition that I use in my third point to attack physicalism; if phenomenal concepts are partial, then zombie conceivability might just be an artifact of those concepts rather than a reliable guide to possibility. I also wonder whether my treatment of mathematics creates a hidden tension: if I claim that inconceivability of contradiction is a good indicator of mathematical impossibility because inconsistencies eventually surface, does that commit me to being far more confident about ZFC's consistency than my call for epistemic humility permits? And am I implicitly relying on an unjustified domain split—using conceivability for consciousness but rejecting it for mathematics—without a principled reason for the difference? Please audit this set of commitments: rank the tensions by severity, distinguish the ones that are only apparent from the genuine ones, say what the least costly revision would be and exactly what it costs me, and present everything in continuous prose only, without headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, written as you would in a long, careful letter.
