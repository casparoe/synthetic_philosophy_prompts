In software architecture, we routinely accept what are often called "leaky abstractions" — simplified interfaces over complex underlying systems that do not literally represent the lower-level reality, but that we justify because they produce correct behavior at the boundary we care about. Meanwhile, I have been reading about Milton Friedman's "F-twist": the claim that unrealistic assumptions in economic or scientific models are not a defect, because the purpose of a model is predictive success rather than descriptive realism.

Compare these two justifications for accepting unrealistic representations. Specifically:

- Does the software-engineering defense of leaky abstractions rely on exactly the same logic as Friedman's F-twist — that predictive or interface-level success is sufficient to excuse descriptive falsehood — or does it implicitly require something Friedman rejects, such as modular decomposability or local causal transparency?
- Consider how each framework responds to catastrophic failure originating in the hidden layer: in software, an abstraction leaks when underlying conditions violate an invariant the interface assumed; in science, a model with unrealistic assumptions might continue predicting well in familiar domains yet fail suddenly in novel ones. Are these failure modes genuinely analogous, and does one framework have a better account of why hidden falsehood eventually matters?
- Which of the two positions is more defensible as a general epistemology for complex systems, and what would be the strongest objection to extending the winner from its home domain into the other?

Please say what would change your mind: identify the specific argument or distinction — for instance, about whether predictive success requires some minimal structural correspondence, or about whether the failure modes are genuinely comparable — that would cause you to reverse your judgment about which framework is stronger.
