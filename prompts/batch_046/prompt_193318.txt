I am a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy or earth science, and next week I am running a forty-five-minute lunch session for colleagues—mostly backend developers and product managers—on the Anthropocene not as a climate slogan but as a contested historical period. The core idea I want them to grasp is that selecting a start date—whether the 1610 Orbis hypothesis tied to the Columbian Exchange, the 1784 Industrial Revolution marker, or the 1950 Great Acceleration—is not merely an empirical measurement like calibrating a sensor, but involves an institutional decision about who has the authority to declare a new epoch, specifically the International Commission on Stratigraphy rather than the climate scientists who propose the change. To carry this, I want to use one central analogy: treating a stratigraphic boundary decision as analogous to declaring a breaking change in a long-running API, where the “changelog” is written in sediment, ice cores, and radionuclides rather than documentation, and where the “maintainers” are geologists voting on a Golden Spike rather than activists or engineers. I need a teaching plan, not the lecture itself, and it must specify: first, the exact sequence in which the pieces must be introduced so that the authority question lands only after the audience understands that boundary selection is partly normative and not purely factual; second, what the API analogy must include and exactly where it will actively mislead—particularly the misconception that Earth history has a single “release manager” or that the decision is democratic—and the precise point in the sequence to break the analogy and correct it; third, the misconceptions they will arrive with or form—conflating the Anthropocene with climate change in general, treating the start date as a straightforward measurement like reading a thermometer, and assuming environmental activists named and defined the epoch—with instructions on exactly where in the arc each should be anticipated and defused; fourth, one concrete scenario or visual comparison, such as contrasting the Crawford Lake sediment core proposal with a deprecated API endpoint that still exists in old logs, that carries both the physical evidence and the institutional vote; fifth, the single check or question I can pose at the end to determine whether they have understood that the dispute over the Anthropocene’s start is fundamentally about who decides, not only about carbon curves or nuclear fallout; and sixth, what I should openly refuse to explain at this level—such as formal GSSP ratification procedures, specific plutonium decay chains, or the full history of the Quaternary Subcommission—and how to state that refusal without undermining credibility. Do not provide a bibliography, reading list, or citations; I have no access to academic libraries in this context and the audience will not either.
