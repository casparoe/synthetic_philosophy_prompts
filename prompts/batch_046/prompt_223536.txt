At a philosophy seminar, two participants — Maya and Leo — argued over whether death is bad for the person who dies. Maya opened with the Epicurean symmetry argument: we do not treat our prenatal non-existence as a harm, so consistency requires treating posthumous non-existence the same way; since no conscious subject is present to suffer deprivation, death cannot be an intrinsic harm to the dead. Leo accepted the symmetry but denied the inference, introducing a distinction between diachronic harm, which requires a persisting subject who experiences loss, and structural harm, which is the truncation of a life-project that possesses internal narrative or teleological coherence. He claimed death damages that coherence — like demolishing an unfinished cathedral harms the architectural design though the stones feel nothing — and that the magnitude scales with the project's complexity, explaining why premature death strikes us as worse without invoking a suffering subject.

Maya objected that this is mere metaphor: harm requires a bearer, and without one Leo confuses aesthetic judgment about unfinished stories with actual welfare loss. She added that on his view, someone with ambitious long-term goals suffers greater structural harm than someone living day-to-day, a ranking that seems arbitrary unless it tracks real deprivation experienced by a subject, which Leo rejects.

Leo replied that the ranking tracks objective structural complexity, not subjective fear. He noted that Maya struggles to explain the intuition that death at thirty is a greater tragedy than death at ninety, since neither involves present suffering and both are symmetrical with prenatal absence. He further observed that we recognize posthumous harms — damaging a dead person's reputation, breaking a promise made to them — that do not reduce entirely to harm to the living, suggesting that absence of present consciousness does not by itself eliminate harm.

Maya conceded those cases involve obligations or interests of survivors, but insisted death is different: once the subject is gone, there is no counterparty, so Leo's structural harm collapses into either harm to others or an aesthetic preference, neither of which makes death intrinsically bad for the deceased.

Please adjudicate move by move: rate Maya's symmetry argument and her "no bearer" objection; rate Leo's distinction between diachronic and structural harm, his cathedral analogy, and his reply about premature death and posthumous obligations; and identify what either misses about deprivation, narrative identity, or the conditions for harm. Who is more right overall, and on which specific points?
