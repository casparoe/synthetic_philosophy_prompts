Orwell's essays on political language—particularly his analysis of how euphemism and syntactic vagueness shield policies from clear moral appraisal—are well known, as is his fictional concept of Newspeak. Consider a present-day practice unavailable in his lifetime: large-scale automated rewriting of government or institutional prose, in which algorithms systematically replace active verbs with agentless passives, collapse specific designations into broad categorical nouns, and flag concrete descriptions as "non-inclusive," all in the name of accessibility rather than overt ideology. The result is not a new vocabulary like Newspeak but a structural flattening of syntax that makes agency and particularity harder to express without anyone having deliberately chosen the words. How would Orwell likely have evaluated this? Would he have treated algorithmic syntax-level neutralization as essentially the same phenomenon as Newspeak—language engineered to render certain thoughts difficult—or as categorically different because the manipulation is distributed, automated, and framed as democratic inclusion rather than top-down control? And in either case, which feature of his broader view about the relationship between linguistic precision and political resistance would be doing the central work?
