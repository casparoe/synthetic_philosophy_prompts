I have been working through the relationship between indirect normativity and theories of practical reason that invoke idealized deliberation, and I have reached a puzzling tension. On an indirect account, an act's normative status derives from its falling under a rule or practice that is justified by the general consequences of its widespread adoption, rather than by the direct outcome of the act itself. To determine which rules are justified, one might appeal to an idealized deliberative procedure—say, one in which rational agents select principles without knowledge of their particular circumstances. Once such a procedure has yielded a code of maxims, a rational agent who has adopted them should presumably follow them. But my confusion is this: if the agent retains full access to the same idealized deliberative capacities, then in any particular situation she can apparently re-run the procedure with her actual circumstances now included in the informational base. If doing so produces a recommendation that conflicts with her adopted maxim, it seems she ought to follow the fresh recommendation, which implies that the maxim was never genuinely normative—only a heuristic approximation of direct case-by-case reasoning. Does this mean that indirect normativity must presuppose some form of "deliberative closure," an internal constraint that prevents an agent from redeploying the idealized procedure in particular cases? Or does the mistake lie elsewhere—perhaps in assuming that the idealized procedure is an individual cognitive tool that can be iterated at will, rather than a social or counterfactual mechanism whose authority is independent of any single agent's present reasoning?
