Construct a clinical thought experiment that separates three positions on the authority of an advance directive: (a) the psychological-continuity view, which holds that the directive binds only if sufficient narrative and evaluative continuity remains between the authoring self and the present patient; (b) the contractualist view, which treats the directive as a promise made by the competent earlier self to its future counterpart, binding regardless of present psychological divergence; and (c) the present-interest view, which holds that any directive is advisory and should be overridden whenever honoring it conflicts with the patient’s current, expressed welfare interests. Constraints: the setting must be a real-world hospital or hospice with no speculative technology; the patient’s cognitive change must be specific, medically documented, and consist in a reversal of long-held values rather than in generic memory loss or reasoning failure; and the scenario should be presentable in one paragraph. Engineer the case so that (a) permits overriding the directive because continuity is broken, (b) requires honoring it because the promise stands independently, and (c) demands overriding it on welfare grounds but for a different reason than (a). After presenting the scenario, demonstrate the reasoning of each position, identify the precise structural feature that forces the split between (a) and (b), and explain how a defender of (b) might argue that (a) misidentifies the kind of continuity that matters for promise-keeping. Finally, assess whether removing the value-reversal feature collapses the case into a standard dementia scenario in which (a) and (b) converge, and note what that implies about the robustness of the distinction.
