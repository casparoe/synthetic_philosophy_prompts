There's this evaluation rubric we use at the research fund I work for — every proposal has to include an inside-view narrative (why this specific team and method will work) and an outside-view base rate (how often similar projects succeed in general). The instructions say applicants must "integreate" both, but here's the weird part: if the inside view is optimistic and the outside view is gloomy, you're supposed to split the difference, except if you're a "recogized domain expert," in which case you can weight your inside view up to 80 percent and still score full points for epistemic modesty, as long as you mention the base rate somewhere. Meanwhile we penalize people who just default to the outside view and ignore their own reasoning — we call that false modesty — but we also penalize non-experts who cling to their inside view when the reference class is terrible. What theory of rationality or knowledge does this practice actually presuppose? Is it assuming expert intuition is a special kind of evidence, or that modesty is mainly a social signal? And if we really thought inside and outside views were just two streams of evidence to be Bayes-updated, shouldn't the weighting depend on track record rather than expert status? I want you to reconstruct the implicit philosophy, distinguish what's strictly required from what just fits well, and say whether the parts are actually consistent. There's also this niche framing I've been messing with: that epistemic modesty isn't about lowering confidence in one model, but about distributing credence across multiple reference classes instead of averaging them into one number. Does our rubric make any sense under that view, or does it collapse?
