As a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy, I have been trying to understand the long-running dispute between endurantists and perdurantists about how material objects persist through time. The endurantist picture treats an object as fully present at each moment of its existence, which to me resembles an immutable database snapshot taken at a single timestamp; the perdurantist picture treats it as a four-dimensional structure made of temporal parts, which feels closer to an append-only event log or a stream of incremental state deltas. Despite decades of argument, neither side seems to concede, and the debate repeatedly returns to the same thought experiments—variations on the ship of Theseus, fission and fusion cases—without apparent progress. My working hypothesis is that the disagreement is not a substantive metaphysical conflict but a framing mismatch imported from different modeling conventions: one side demands that identity conditions be evaluable at an instantaneous slice, while the other insists that identity is only meaningful across an extended interval. Is that diagnosis correct? Or is there a hidden conceptual question—perhaps about whether temporal boundaries are real, or about what properly counts as a "part"—that is actually doing the work beneath the surface? I want a diagnosis of the disagreement's structure, supported by what each side actually says, rather than a survey of positions. Specifically: is this a verbal dispute about the word "object," a substantive disagreement masked by ambiguous examples, or a case in which the two sides are answering different questions—one about ontology, one about explanatory adequacy—under a single heading? And if your diagnosis is right, what would count as genuine progress: a new formal distinction, a reconceptualization of the data, or an argument that the question itself is ill-posed? I am particularly interested in whether injecting an engineering distinction—complete state snapshots versus incremental version deltas—helps clarify the philosophical issue, or whether it merely projects an irrelevant metaphor onto a problem that should be resolved on its own terms.
