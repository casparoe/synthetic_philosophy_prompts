Two philosophers of mind are debating what pain asymbolia reveals about whether pain is intrinsically bad. (1) The first argues that the condition is decisive: patients experience the full sensory profile—sharp, burning, localized sensations—yet report no unpleasantness and show no avoidance behavior; therefore the badness of ordinary pain must belong to a separable affective or evaluative component rather than to the sensation itself. (2) The second replies that this misdescribes the phenomenon: asymbolic patients still rate stimuli as harmful and display reflexive withdrawal; what they lack is only the motivational drive, not the negative evaluation, so the badness remains intact but behaviorally disconnected. (3) The first responds that if badness can be present yet behaviorally inert, it cannot play the explanatory role evolutionary accounts assign it—explaining why organisms avoid damage—making it a dangling evaluation with no functional purpose. (4) The second counters that evolutionary explanations require only statistical correlations across populations, not universal motivation, and that pathological dissociation is precisely the exception that confirms the rule linking negative evaluation to avoidance. (5) The first then presses a linguistic point: if the sensory profile can occur without badness and without motivation, why classify that state as "pain" at all rather than as an intense tactile sensation? If "pain" is reserved for the bad kind, then asymbolia is not pain lacking badness but simply not pain, collapsing the argument into a semantic stipulation rather than a metaphysical discovery.

Please adjudicate the dispute move by move. For each of the five moves, rate it from clearly mistaken to clearly correct and briefly justify your rating. Then say which side is closer to the truth about whether pain's badness is separable from its sensory character, and identify what both disputants are overlooking about the relation between evaluation, motivation, and the taxonomy of sensory states.
