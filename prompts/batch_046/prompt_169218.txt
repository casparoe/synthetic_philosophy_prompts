This struck me as puzzling. If family resemblance is genuinely anti-essentialist, then what explains our ability to group them together at all? It seems there must be some hidden relational structure—perhaps a shared form of life or a common pattern of justification—that operates like an invisible backbone, allowing us to see the resemblance without appealing to a common property.

This led me to a more specific question about mathematics. In my other course on the foundations of mathematics, we're discussing whether set-theoretic and category-theoretic foundations are describing the same mathematical reality or playing entirely different language games with the same symbols. But here's where I get confused: doesn't family resemblance still require that we can identify some thread—however loose—that connects members of the family? My core question is whether Wittgenstein's framework allows for any hidden unifying mechanism—perhaps not a shared essence, but something like a shared grammar of justification or a common form of life—that explains why certain language games cluster together while others remain isolated. If the latter, then it seems cross-disciplinary reasoning—say, applying statistical methods developed in one language game to interpret historical data in another—would be nothing more than metaphorical projection, which feels wrong but I can't articulate why without smuggling in the very essentialism Wittgenstein rejects. Can you help me see where the confusion lies?
