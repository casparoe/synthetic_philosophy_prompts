I am working through formal epistemology this semester, and I have become fixated on what seems like a structural limitation in Jeffrey conditionalization that my instructor dismissed too quickly. The canonical formulation holds that when an agent receives evidence too ambiguous to determine a unique outcome—say, a blurry measurement that shifts her credences over a partition {E_1, ..., E_n} without identifying which cell obtains—she should preserve her prior conditional probabilities P(H | E_i) and update only her marginal probabilities over the partition, replacing them with observed values q_i. This works cleanly when the partition is fixed and exhaustive.

But I want to press a case that introduces what I will call a nested evidential structure. Imagine the agent is not merely uncertain about which E_i obtains within a given partition, but is genuinely uncertain about whether {E_i} is even the correct partition of the state space. She might assign nonzero credence to a finer partition {F_j} or a coarser one {G_k}, and her ambiguous data might bear differently on these alternative frameworks. If she applies Jeffrey's rule using the middle partition, she must already treat the conditional probabilities P(H | E_i) as well-defined and stable, which seems to presuppose that she has already settled the question of which partition structures her beliefs. If she hasn't, then distributing new credences q_i over {E_i} looks circular: she uses a partition-dependent update rule to revise beliefs that include doubts about the partition itself.

So here is my confusion, which I hope you can untangle. Does Jeffrey conditionalization implicitly require that the conditioning partition lie outside the scope of the agent's uncertainty—making it inapplicable precisely when model uncertainty is present? Or can the rule be extended to cases where the partition is itself an object of credence, without collapsing into ordinary Bayesian conditioning on some higher-order proposition like "the correct partition is {E_i}"? I want a careful answer that explains where the false presupposition lies—if it does lie—in my framing of the problem, and that addresses whether Jeffrey's framework was ever intended to handle this kind of cross-partition ambiguity. Please respond in American English.
