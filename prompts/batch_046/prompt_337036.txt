As a novelist accustomed to tracing how characters undermine the resolutions they once swore to keep, I have noticed a recurring structure in discussions of constitutional precommitment and personal Ulysses contracts for which I lack a name, and I want to know whether the literature already provides one. A polity, or an individual, binds itself with a hard rule: no deficit spending outside declared wars, no suspension of due process except during invasion, no alcohol after noon. The constraint bites precisely because circumstances change. Rather than formally repealing the rule, which would confess failure, the bound party accumulates interpretive qualifications: the rule governs only ordinary fiscal conditions, applies solely to genuine emergencies, or defines a drink with ever-narrower precision. Each qualification is defended as fidelity to the original intent; the text remains untouched and is still invoked in ceremonial rhetoric. Yet cumulatively these qualifications hollow out the constraint, preserving its symbolic authority while draining its restrictive force—turning a mast to which one is tied into a decorative pillar. I see analogues in constitutional history and in addiction narratives alike. Is there an established concept for this slow, hermeneutic erosion of a self-binding rule—distinct from ordinary auxiliary hypotheses, from formal amendment, and from simple hypocrisy? If such a concept exists, how does it differ from immunizing strategies in the philosophy of science? If it does not exist, what boundary conditions should govern it, and by what term should we call the pattern?
