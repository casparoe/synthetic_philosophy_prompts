I retired some years ago from a career in experimental physics, and in the leisure of retirement I have begun to read seriously in political philosophy, joining a small discussion group of former scientists who meet monthly. Our conversations have repeatedly circled back to the paradox of voting — the Downsian observation that, in large electorates, the instrumental value of casting a single ballot is negligible, whilst the costs of becoming informed are not — and to a frustrating stalemate that I should like your help in diagnosing. On one side, several members argue that the paradox collapses once voting is understood as an expressive or symbolic act: the voter derives value not from altering the outcome, but from affirming an identity or allegiance, and this expressive utility outweighs the informational costs. On the other side, others contend that this reply saves the rationality of the individual voter only by abandoning the normative justification for universal suffrage itself; if voting is merely expressive, they ask, why should the state expend resources on an institution that produces no decisive collective outcome and serves chiefly as a theatre for private sentiment? Both camps, I should emphasise, accept the same empirical premises — the vanishing probability of being pivotal, the non-trivial burden of political knowledge — yet after months of discussion neither has moved an inch. Is this disagreement genuinely about whether expressive goods can constitute rational reasons for action, or is it in part a verbal dispute about the word 'rationality', used in an instrumental sense by one party and in an aretaic or expressive sense by the other? Or are the two sides in fact addressing different questions — one about the psychology of individual motivation, the other about the institutional legitimacy of democracy — whilst believing themselves to be disputing a single thesis? I should be grateful for a diagnosis supported by what each side actually maintains, together with your assessment of whether a precise conceptual distinction — say, between reasons that justify an action and reasons that merely explain or motivate it — or some empirical result about voter behaviour could actually break the deadlock, or whether the impasse is structural and no progress should be expected.
