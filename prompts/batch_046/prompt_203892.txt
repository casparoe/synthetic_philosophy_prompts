I'm preparing my job-market talk for an interdisciplinary workshop on "Boundaries of Mechanism" hosted jointly by a history of science department and a philosophy of mind program. The audience will be roughly half historians of computing who treat Turing's 1950 paper on machine intelligence as a carefully dated artifact rather than a living argument, plus several philosophers of mind who are broadly physicalist but hostile to anachronistic "reading in," and one or two quantum information theorists who will be in the room because the workshop theme touches on locality and information transfer. I want to defend a deliberately non-obvious thesis: that Turing's brief dismissal of extrasensory perception in the imitation-game framework was not a rhetorical aside but a structurally load-bearing boundary condition. Specifically, I argue that he treated ESP not as a mystical exception to materialism but as a potential counterexample to the assumption that cognitive processes are necessarily information-theoretically local—an assumption he was already complicating in his later morphogenetic work on pattern formation. From this I draw out a reconstructed "proto-informationalist" Turing: if non-local information transfer existed, whether through purported telepathy or through quantum correlations misinterpreted as signals, the correct response would not be to abandon mechanism but to revise the substrate conditions of computation itself. I know this sounds like I'm projecting quantum information theory backward onto a mid-century logician, so I need precise ammunition for the Q&A.

Please draft my speaker-prep sheet. Order the objections by how damaging they are likely to be in this specific room—not generic philosophy-of-mind objections, but the ones these historians and these physicists will actually raise, phrased in their respective vocabularies. For each objection, tell me: who is likely to ask it (historian, philosopher, or physicist), whether I should concede part of it up front, where I should hold the line, a concise reply suited to about ninety seconds, and the weakest point in that reply that I should defend rather than hope they miss. Include the objection I'm secretly hoping nobody asks—the one that exposes the anachronism in my reconstruction most painfully. I do not need bibliographies, citations, or reading lists; this audience cares about conceptual coherence, not bibliography. Do not quote Turing verbatim unless you have fetched the exact passage; otherwise paraphrase his remarks on ESP and the imitation game without quotation marks. Keep the focus on the argumentative dynamics of the room, not on summarizing standard interpretations of the 1950 paper.
