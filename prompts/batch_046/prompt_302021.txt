I'm a high school senior delivering the opening remarks at tomorrow's rededication of our school's 1920s planetarium dome, rebuilt after collapse using about sixty percent original bricks, beams, and the original brass telescope mount, but with new steel supports and a redesigned interior. The audience includes alumni who knew the old dome, science faculty, local preservationists, and current students. I want the speech — polished but accessible, no more than 350 words — to use the special composition question, particularly the tension between mereological essentialism and functional continuity, as its actual structure: it should let me honor alumni attachment without asserting that this is literally the same object, and welcome newcomers without treating the dome as a mere heap of reused parts. Please do not cite philosophers by name or provide a bibliography. Instead, work the idea through concrete language: treat the original brass mount as a kind of "mereological anchor" that complicates both answers, and include one unexpected analogy — perhaps to a school debate team that has replaced every member and every trophy but kept the same charter and meeting room — to show why both "it is the same dome" and "it is an entirely new building" miss something important. The speech should leave that tension genuinely unresolved but productive for the occasion.
