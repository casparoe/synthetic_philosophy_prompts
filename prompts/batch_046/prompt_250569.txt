I am designing a ninety-minute session for an upper-level seminar in moral and political philosophy, with twelve students, and I need a structured classroom activity or game that makes participants experience the structural force of the utility-monster problem through coherence constraints rather than through intuition alone. Please respond entirely in continuous prose, without headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, as one might write in a formal letter. The deliverable should be a runnable simulation with roles, rules, a timeline, and a debrief: students should form a collective decision body that must adopt allocation rules for three successive hypothetical societies, each with a different profile of welfare-to-resource conversion, and they must maintain transitive, context-independent preferences across all three societies in order to avoid collective inconsistency; through negotiation over these rules, with a small real stake such as a shared bonus point or collective choice of the next seminar text, they should discover that the only consistent rule assigns all resources to whichever individual has the highest conversion rate, and they should defend or resist this outcome before anyone names it as the utility monster. Do not include any bibliographies, reading lists, citations, or verbatim quotations from texts; do not reference obscure theories or figures that a small language model would be unlikely to have properly memorized. What the students should come away seeing is that the utility monster can be generated from inside the logic of rational aggregation, not merely imported as an external counterexample, and that resisting it requires either breaking coherence or introducing a non-aggregative moral constraint that the rules themselves do not supply.
