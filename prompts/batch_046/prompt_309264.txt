I help manage a small cooperative repair workshop with one other partner. We share a collection of expensive, specialized tools, and each of us can either maintain them meticulously or use them carelessly, since neglect is difficult to trace to a single individual. To foster trust without formal contracts, we adopted an unconventional practice: we both maintain a shared, real-time reasoning journal in which we record our decision-making process before we act—stating why we think a particular course is justified, what outcomes we expect, and how we weigh our partner's likely response. My partner recently argued that because these journals make our reasoning fully transparent, we function as symmetrically reasoning agents facing a one-shot interaction. She claims that when two agents can verify they share the same decision structure and can model each other's reasoning with high fidelity, they will naturally converge on mutual cooperation—even without repetition, enforcement, or external commitment—because each can see that the other will reach the identical cooperative conclusion from the same premises. She therefore proposes that we abandon our informal maintenance rotation and rely entirely on this transparency to sustain cooperation.

I find this unconvincing. Knowing how someone thinks does not, by itself, change the material payoff from defecting, and transparency might even make strategic exploitation easier by allowing me to predict exactly when my partner will be too distracted to notice damage. At the same time, I worry I am conflating transparency about reasoning with transparency about incentives, and that I may be missing how visibility of reasoning genuinely transforms the strategic structure rather than merely revealing it. Is her argument sound? Does making reasoning processes visible actually alter the rational calculus in one-off cooperation problems, or is she assuming that epistemic access can do the work of strategic binding?
