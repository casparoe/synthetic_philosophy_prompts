At a philosophy discussion group last week, two members got into a sustained argument about wrong-kind reasons in aesthetic judgment. I want you to adjudicate their exchange point by point, say who is more right overall, and note what both are missing.

Leah opened by saying that if someone finds a painting beautiful mainly because owning it signals high status, that is a clear wrong-kind reason for an aesthetic judgment. The harmony of color and form can be right-kind reasons; social signaling cannot. Marcus pushed back: aesthetic practices are partly social institutions, so the norms that govern them—including norms of prestige and recognition—are internal to the practice and therefore supply right-kind reasons, not wrong-kind ones. He compared this to scientific testimony: relying on expert reputation is not a wrong-kind reason for belief, because science is a social practice structured by reputation.

Leah replied that Marcus was erasing the distinction altogether. If any reason internal to a practice counts as right-kind, then pragmatic reasons for belief—like believing in free will because it makes you happier—could also be defended as internal to the practice of living a meaningful life. That would dissolve the wrong-kind reasons problem rather than solve it. Marcus answered that there is still a boundary: right-kind reasons must be governed by norms that are genuinely reason-responsive within the practice, not merely instrumentally useful. Expert reputation tracks truth in science; happiness tracks nothing in belief-formation. But prestige in art might track aesthetic value if the art-world's norms are genuinely reason-responsive—though he admitted he was not sure how to show that without circularity.

Leah then offered a harder case. Suppose an art market is thoroughly corrupted: prices and prestige are driven by speculation and money laundering, with no connection to aesthetic quality. In that context, citing prestige as a reason for admiration seems wrong-kind, even though prestige is still internal to the practice. So Marcus's appeal to practice-dependence fails: either prestige is right-kind only when it tracks value (in which case we are back to an independent standard), or it is always right-kind by virtue of being internal (in which case corruption does not matter, which is implausible). Marcus conceded that corruption complicates his view, but said Leah now owes an account of what makes a reason track value in aesthetics without collapsing into subjectivism or reintroducing the very practice-dependence she rejected.

They turned to me and asked me to referee. Please go through the exchange move by move: rate each step from clearly mistaken to clearly right, briefly justify each rating, and say who is more right overall. In particular, address whether Marcus's practice-internal defense genuinely saves wrong-kind reasons from collapse, whether Leah's corruption case succeeds in undermining it, and whether either of them has given a stable account of where the line falls in aesthetic judgment. Also, please say how confident you are in what you claim, and single out the specific point you are least sure of.
