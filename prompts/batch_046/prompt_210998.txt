You are a professional philosopher of history with expertise in ancient historiography and contemporary epistemology of testimony. Before you construct anything, take a deep breath and think step-by-step: first outline the core distinctions from source criticism—Herodotean separation of direct observation from reported hearsay, Thucydidean demands for precision and cross-examination of witnesses, and modern critical historiography’s separation of source, interpretation, and inference—then consider which of these distinctions must survive simplification for a non-specialist practitioner, which can be dropped without damage, and where judgment is genuinely unavoidable.

I am a PhD student in history and philosophy of science, serving as an editorial referee for a small peer-reviewed venue that publishes reconstructed experimental protocols drawn from pre-modern technical texts—alchemical recipes, mechanical treatises, and medical formularies—where direct replication is impossible and every step depends on textual transmission. I need a reusable decision procedure that my co-editors can run in about fifteen minutes per submission, without web access, without expecting bibliographic verification, and without relying on highly specialized archival knowledge. The procedure must be grounded in the tradition of source criticism running from Herodotus and Thucydides through modern critical historiography, but translated into operational steps a referee can apply repeatedly.

Please construct a flowchart-in-words: the ordered questions to ask about any submission, what counts as a sufficient answer at each stage, where the procedure returns a hard reject or provisionally reject, where it hands the decision to a human editor because the issue is one of values or institutional risk rather than epistemic warrant, and which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure. The procedure should explicitly distinguish between epistemic inheritance—faithful transmission of a practice through a chain of witnesses—and epistemic contamination—interpolated interpretation, translation drift, analogical reconstruction bias, or modern framing effects.

Then apply the procedure to three submissions:

1. A reconstructed thirteenth-century metallurgical protocol derived from a single Latin translation of an Arabic manuscript, where the translator is known to have substituted local material terms for unfamiliar ones.
2. A proposed pneumatic device reconstructed solely from Roman secondary summaries of a lost Greek original, with no direct textual witness and conflicting measurements in the summaries.
3. A well-funded and institutionally popular reconstruction of an ancient therapeutic practice that passes initial transmission checks but relies on a modern analogical interpretation of ambiguous dosage terms—an outcome my board wants to accept but the procedure should evaluate honestly.

For each case, show the path through the procedure, including the point where it yields an answer I will not like. Finally, state clearly what the procedure cannot do—where it will misfire, which false positives and false negatives are unavoidable, and which questions it deliberately leaves open. Begin with a brief brainstorming outline of the key distinctions, then present the procedure, then the worked cases, then the limitations.
