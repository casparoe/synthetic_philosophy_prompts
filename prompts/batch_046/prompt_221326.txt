As a high school student attempting to clarify my own thinking about rhetoric and ethics, I have been examining a series of concrete situations to determine where legitimate persuasion ends and manipulation begins. I would like to know whether my intuitions cohere around a single principle, or whether some must be abandoned.

Here are my verdicts. First, when a debate partner accurately summarizes my position before arguing against it, I regard this as legitimate persuasion, even though she has selected the framing. Second, when an advertiser designs a checkout interface so that the cancellation option is rendered in a color nearly identical to the background, I regard this as manipulation, even though no false claim is made. Third, when my counselor tells me that enrolling in the harder advanced course will make me the kind of person who does not back down, I feel this is manipulation: she is not supplying information about the course itself, but attaching the decision to my self-concept in a manner that is difficult for me to examine from the outside. Fourth, when a physician, knowing that I am risk-averse, describes a treatment by emphasizing its alignment with my previously stated priority of maintaining mobility, I regard this as legitimate persuasion, even though she is selectively highlighting one feature among many.

My intuitions become unstable, however, with the following pair. Fifth, a nonprofit fundraiser sends me a letter that opens with a photograph of a specific child and states that this child will not eat tonight unless I respond, even though the donation supports a general program and the child in the photograph is not in immediate danger. I feel this is manipulation, because the emotional anchor is fabricated in its urgency. Sixth, a climate activist tells me that if I do not change my consumption patterns, I am complicit in harm to future generations, which also attaches moral identity to a specific behavioral choice using an emotional frame. I am inclined to call this legitimate persuasion, but I cannot articulate why the fundraiser's emotional anchoring crosses a line that the activist's moral framing does not.

I have tentatively proposed a principle built around two notions I will call frame-locking and value-hijacking. Frame-locking occurs when a persuader restricts the conceptual or emotional space within which the target evaluates an option, so that alternatives become invisible or unthinkable rather than merely unattractive. Value-hijacking occurs when a persuader invokes values that the target genuinely holds, but applies them in a context where their relevance is contested or obscured, thereby making refusal feel like a betrayal of the self rather than a disagreement about application. On this view, manipulation requires both elements together; legitimate persuasion either expands the frame or applies values within a transparent domain.

Can this principle, or any precise repair of it, accommodate all six verdicts? In particular, does it correctly distinguish case five from case six, and does it explain why case three counts as frame-locking while case four does not? If the principle must be significantly revised or abandoned, which of my verdicts—especially the unstable pair in cases five and six—should be overturned, and why? Please state the resulting account precisely, indicate which clause is motivated by which case, predict its verdict on a case I have not listed, and say honestly which of my original verdicts it cannot accommodate.

The unlisted case is this: a teacher redesigns a grading rubric so that students are rewarded precisely for the skills she wants them to practice, knowing that students will adapt their behavior to maximize grades rather than to learn for its own sake, but she never misrepresents the rubric's criteria. Is this manipulation, legitimate persuasion, or a different category altogether?
