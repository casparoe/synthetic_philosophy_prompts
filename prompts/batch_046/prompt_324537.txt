hey, i'm a phd student in political philosophy/ethics and i'm trying to map my actual intuitions about dirty hands in politics, not just repeat the standard Walzer story. here's my gut verdicts on cases, and they're kind of all over the place.

1. a mayor orders riot cops to use excessive force, stops a pogrom against a minority neighborhood, but three bystanders get badly hurt. she should do it, her hands are dirty, and she should carry real guilt after. classic case, i'm on board.

2. same exact scenario, but she runs a cold cost-benefit calc, feels zero guilt, and moves on. i actually think this is worse, not cleaner—like she failed to respect the moral cost. so is guilt part of what makes it dirty hands, or is that mixing psychology with morality?

3. a diplomat secretly arms rebels to overthrow a dictator, knowing the rebels will ethnically cleanse a minority region once they win. she succeeds, long-term maybe more lives saved, but the cleansing happens. she feels crushed. my gut says her hands are dirtier than case 1, but i can't tell if that's because she's more causally responsible or because the harm feels more 'intended' in some fuzzy way.

4. a senior civil servant implements a deportation policy she thinks is unjust, but it's democratically passed and she could resign. she stays, does her job efficiently, feels constant conflict. i think her hands are dirty too, but it's institutional complicity rather than direct authorization—though i keep going back and forth on whether she's just a cog and not really dirty at all.

5. a campaign manager forges signatures to get a genuinely good reform candidate on the ballot, without the candidate knowing. it works, he wins and does good. the manager feels guilty and eventually confesses. sometimes i think this is dirty hands because it's corruption of democratic procedeure; other times i think it's not dirty at all, since there's no state violence or serious harm, just cheating for a good end.

6. a prime minister authorizes torture of one detainee, gets useful intel, saves lives. then she lies about it to parliament—not to protect lives, but to protect her goverment. i think the torture makes her hands dirty, but the lie is just regular corruption, not dirty-hands corruption. but that split feels suspicious.

is there a coherent principle that captures all six, or close? i especially want to know what distinguishes case 2 (no guilt) from case 1, case 5 (procedural cheating) from case 4 (institutional compliance), and whether case 3's distant-but-intended harm is a seperate category. also, what does your principle predict for a case i haven't listed—say, a bureaucrat who 'pre-dirties' her hands by secretly planting false evidence to prevent a much worse policy from being enacted? please say which of my verdicts you'd have to overturn and why. and please don't just hand-wave with Walzer—i've actually read him, i'm looking for something that handles these distinctions.
