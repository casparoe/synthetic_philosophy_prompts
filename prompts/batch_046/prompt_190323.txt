hey all — working through Dennett's brain-transplant puzzle (the Hamlet/Yorick one) for a seminar on self-location and I'm stuck comparing it to Locke's prince/cobbler case in Essay II.xxvii. Roughly, Locke imagines the soul of a prince entering a sleeping cobbler's body, carrying all the prince's consciousness with it, while the cobbler's own soul departs. Locke says the person is now the prince, since personal identity is sameness of consciousness, not sameness of substance.

But Dennett's split feels different. Hamlet's brain gets copied into a backup and then into a new body, so we have two streams with identical consciousness-states at the moment of copying. Locke's criterion seems to say both are Hamlet, since both extend the same consciousness backward. That feels like a reductio, not a feature.

What I'm really stuck on is whether Locke's text actually blocks the split. He calls person a forensic term — appropriating actions and merit — and he says consciousness makes every one to be what they call self. The phrase every one sounds distributive, but maybe there's a hidden indexical in the idea that the same self now reflects on past actions, tying consciousness to a single spatiotemporal path? Or does the forensic aspect require singular agency?

So my question: if we take Locke seriously and then imagine perfect duplication (not just migration), does his view collapse into allowing one person in two bodies? Is Dennett's copy-proceedure exploiting a tension already inside Locke's substance-independance? Or am I misreading Locke's sameness of rational being as allowing branching?

Sorry for the ramble — been staring at this for three days. Any help unpacking whether Locke's criterion is implicitly linear/non-branching would be huge.
