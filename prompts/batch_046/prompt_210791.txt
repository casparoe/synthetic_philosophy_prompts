I'm moderating a philosophy-of-science podcast interview with an invented philosopher of biology, Dr. Elena Voss, who defends a position she calls constrained exaptationism. The view accepts Gould and Lewontin's spandrels critique in principle—acknowledging that some traits are architectural byproducts rather than direct adaptations—but argues that pure spandrels are far rarer than the critics suggest because nearly all byproducts are eventually co-opted. She distinguishes scaffolded adaptation (where a trait's spandrel origin remains visible in its developmental modularity and can be detected via phylogenetic comparative methods that track correlation decay across clades) from integrated exaptation (where co-option has so thoroughly rewritten dependency relations that the original byproduct history is erased). She claims modern comparative methods can identify these categories without smuggling in Panglossian optimality assumptions, and she applies the framework to classic cases such as the mammalian middle-ear ossicles and the panda's radial sesamoid.

Please draft eight to ten interview questions that press on the mechanics of this view rather than asking her to restate it. Specifically include:
- a question on the practical distinction between scaffolded adaptation and integrated exaptation, with a follow-up that anticipates her appeal to developmental modularity and asks what empirical result would show that modularity is reconstructed rather than discovered;
- a question on whether her view collapses into standard adaptationism once she allows that almost all spandrels become exaptations, plus a follow-up pressing whether she can identify any trait that remains a pure spandrel with zero co-opted function;
- a question on how she answers Gould and Lewontin's charge that adaptationists construct just-so stories, with a follow-up challenging whether phylogenetic correlation decay avoids that charge without implicitly assuming optimality;
- a concrete case question—using either the mammalian middle-ear ossicles or the panda's pseudo-thumb—asking which category she assigns and why, followed by a challenge asking what observation could overturn that specific classification;
- and a final closing question asking what single empirical finding (not a philosophical objection) could cause her to abandon constrained exaptationism altogether.

Organize your response in these sections:
1. Position summary (about 80 words): a concise restatement of Voss's key distinctions as you will treat them in the questions.
2. The questions (about 350–450 words): Q1 through Q8–Q10, each consisting of the main question, the anticipated reply in brief, and the follow-up.
3. Interview strategy (about 100 words): brief notes on which replies pose the deepest threat to her framework and why.

Keep the tone appropriate for a formal podcast. Do not include requests for citations, bibliographies, or reading lists, and avoid generic biographical questions.
