Consider Bernard Williams's integrity objection, often summarized as the charge that there is one thought too many when a husband saves his spouse only after running an impersonal calculation. Now extend that idea through the following argument.

(1) If it is genuinely wrong for an agent to interpose a mediating calculation between himself and his spouse, then it is wrong to interpose any mediating thought that is not identical to the direct relational reason—she is my spouse.

(2) Every moral theory that justifies partiality—whether deontological (special obligations), virtue-theoretic (loyalty as a character trait), or contractualist—interposes just such a mediating thought: a general principle that licenses the attachment rather than the bare attachment itself.

(3) Therefore, if Williams's objection succeeds, every moral justification of saving one's spouse involves one thought too many.

(4) But consider a conflict case: one's spouse and one's child are both drowning, and one can save only one. Here unmediated attachment pulls with equal directness in both directions, so attachment alone cannot determine which life to save without being arbitrary.

(5) In such cases some mediating consideration—perhaps a prior explicit promise to the spouse, perhaps comparative vulnerability, perhaps a principle of fair rotation—is unavoidable if the choice is to be reflectively defensible rather than a coin flip.

(6) Hence Williams's objection, applied consistently, implies that in any conflict of attachments no reflective moral agent can justifiably choose, which is absurd.

(7) So either the objection applies only to utilitarianism—in which case its force depends on a feature unique to utility-maximization, not on mediation per se—or it applies universally and destroys the possibility of justified partiality altogether.

I am reluctant to accept (7), and I want to keep (1), since it seems to capture what makes the utilitarian case repugnant: not that reasoning occurs, but that reasoning of the wrong kind replaces the direct bond. Yet if I hold (1) and (4)-(5), I seem forced to reject (6) or deny that conflict cases require mediation, both of which look costly. Where exactly does the argument slip? Which premise should be rejected, and what does rejecting it cost? In particular, is there a principled distinction between the mediation Williams condemns and the mediation required in (5), or is that distinction merely ad hoc?
