Write one 90-minute essay question for an upper-level seminar exam in formal epistemology (graduate or advanced undergraduate). Use the Judy Benjamin scenario: Judy knows she is either on the Red or Blue team and is either in the Mountains or the Valley. Her prior is P(Red) = P(Blue) = 1/2, with P(Mountains | Red) = 0.9 and P(Mountains | Blue) = 0.2; she also assigns prior conditional confidence P(Mountains | Blue) = 0.2. She then receives a fully credible message with content Blue → Mountains. The question should ask students to apply three update rules to this case and evaluate them: (a) Jeffrey conditioning combined with Adams’ thesis (probability of a conditional equals the corresponding conditional probability), updating P(Mountains | Blue) to 1 while leaving P(Mountains | Red) at 0.9 and fixing the marginals P(Red) and P(Blue) at 1/2; (b) Lewis-style imaging, shifting all probability mass from Blue-and-Valley worlds onto the nearest Blue-and-Mountains worlds according to a similarity ordering, leaving Red-worlds untouched; (c) a “non-revisionary imaging” variant in which the similarity ordering is anchored entirely to Judy’s prior unconditional probabilities and is not itself revised upon learning the conditional. For each rule, students must compute the resulting joint distribution over (Team, Location) and the new marginals P(Red), P(Blue), P(Mountains), P(Valley). They should then compare the results—explicitly noting where (a) and (b) coincide in the unconditional marginals and where they differ in internal distribution within the Blue region, and explaining why this coincidence is not general—before turning to whether (c) blocks any unwanted shift in P(Blue). Finally, they must evaluate which rule, if any, respects the intuition that learning Blue → Mountains should not affect Judy’s belief about which team she is on, addressing whether (c) genuinely solves the problem or merely relocates it to the prior anchoring of the similarity metric.

Provide a model answer of roughly 600 words that performs the calculations correctly, states the resulting distributions, and makes the critical evaluation explicit. Then provide a marking scheme built around five must-hit points (total 20 marks), with the marks each is worth and a one-line note on how candidates typically fail that point. The difficulty should come from requiring precise computation together with conceptual diagnosis: a student who only summarizes Adams’ thesis or describes imaging in the abstract should not be able to earn high marks. Do not ask for bibliographies, citations, or reading lists. Avoid verbatim quotations; if you mention a specific philosopher’s claim, paraphrase it without quotation marks. Do not rely on highly obscure formalisms that a well-trained student could not be expected to know without access to sources; present the non-revisionary imaging rule fully within the prompt itself.
