I've been working through Aristotle's sea-battle problem without formal training, and I think I've found a version of it that quantum mechanics doesn't actually resolve. The classic worry is that if it's true today that there will be a naval battle tomorrow, then tomorrow's battle is unavoidable—nothing could stop it. Now, quantum physics gives us genuinely branching outcomes: imagine a particle that will be measured tomorrow and can come up spin-up or spin-down with equal physical chance. Before the measurement, I hold a 50 percent belief in each result. But once spin-up is observed, it seems I can say, "it was always going to be spin-up," which makes my prior confidence look like I was wrong about the future being open. So does replacing "it will happen" with "it has a 50 percent chance" really dissolve Aristotle's problem, or does it just relocate it—meaning that whichever branch becomes actual was always the one destined to occur, and that assigning probabilities to open futures is therefore systematically misleading about what was genuinely fixed all along?
