Here is an argument that seems to collapse Kant’s indirect-duty view of animals into something absurd, and I cannot find the fault line. (1) For Kant, direct moral obligations extend only to beings possessing rational nature; animals, lacking autonomy, are not ends in themselves but mere things. (2) Consequently, cruelty to animals is wrong only instrumentally—specifically, because it cultivates dispositions that tend to erode our respect for human dignity. (3) If the wrong is genuinely indirect in this causal-instrumental sense, then severing or reversing the causal pathway should eliminate or invert the wrongness: where cruelty produces no damage to rational nature, there is no Kantian reason against it. (4) Now imagine a condition of perfect normative insulation: a psychological and institutional architecture that completely decouples one’s treatment of animals from one’s treatment of humans, so that cruelty to animals serves as a controlled catharsis that actually strengthens one’s benevolence toward rational beings. (5) Within such insulation, cruelty to animals threatens no rational nature and may actively preserve it. (6) Therefore, on Kant’s framework, animal cruelty inside normative insulation should be permissible, and perhaps even instrumentally encouraged. (7) Yet it still seems wrong to torture an animal even if the torturer is guaranteed never to mistreat a person and in fact becomes more compassionate toward people as a result. (8) So the indirect-duty account must fail: either animals possess direct moral standing after all, or there is some wrong-making feature of cruelty independent of spillover effects. Where exactly does the argument go wrong? I am inclined to defend (1) if I can, but I want to know which step I must abandon and what abandoning it costs—whether it forces me to grant animals direct status, or whether it merely requires complicating the psychology of moral corruption in some other way.
