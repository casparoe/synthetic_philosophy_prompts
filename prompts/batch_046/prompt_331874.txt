I have been trying to formalize Arendt's thesis that the banality of evil is not primarily a psychological claim about motivation but a phenomenological one: Eichmann's distinctive failure was not hatred but a kind of bureaucratic thoughtlessness, an inability to think from the standpoint of anyone other than himself within the machinery he served. This suggests that the moral horror lies in a specific absence of imaginative judgment rather than in malicious intent. But I worry that if we take this seriously, the concept proves too much. Consider the following argument.

(1) For Arendt, what distinguishes Eichmann's evil from ordinary wrongdoing is that he acted within a system that rendered moral judgment unnecessary, and he personally lacked the imaginative capacity to see his actions from the perspective of their victims.

(2) If the absence of this imaginative perspective-taking is the defining feature of banal evil, then any harmful action performed without it—regardless of scale, institutional context, or intent—exhibits the same moral structure.

(3) A surgeon, working a double shift and mechanically following a checklist, administers a fatal dose because she never pauses to imagine the patient's terror; she is negligent, but she exhibits precisely the failure of imaginative perspective-taking that Arendt identifies.

(4) By (1) and (2), her negligence is not merely culpable carelessness but an instance of the same banal evil.

(5) Yet this collapses the moral distinction between catastrophic bureaucratic complicity and professional negligence; we do not treat a tired surgeon as belonging to the same category as an organizer of genocide.

(6) Therefore, Arendt's criterion either applies so narrowly that it is ad hoc—restricted to large-scale bureaucratic harm without independent justification—or it applies generally and obliterates distinctions we should preserve.

I am inclined to keep (1) but I cannot see how to restrict it without abandoning its explanatory power. Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost the theory if I still want to preserve Arendt's core insight about Eichmann?
