At a philosophy-of-science reading group, two members—Nadia and Eli—got into a prolonged disagreement about a case they had both encountered in a medical-ethics discussion. The case involves a senior researcher, Dr. Park, who tells an institutional review board, "We do not know that this gene-therapy protocol is safe for pediatric use," but later, in an informal hallway conversation with a postdoc, says, "Of course we know it's safe enough for Phase II; the toxicity profile is well within bounds." Nadia argued that this pair of utterances is best explained by epistemic contextualism: the word "know" operates with different evidential standards in a high-stakes regulatory context than in a low-stakes collegial chat, so both statements can be literally true. Eli countered that this misdiagnoses the phenomenon. On his view, it is not the conversational setting that shifts the meaning or the standard of evaluation, but the practical stakes facing the subject—Dr. Park—at the time. In the boardroom, lives and legal liability are on the line, so subject-sensitive invariantism demands stronger evidence before Dr. Park counts as knowing; in the hallway, those stakes are absent, so the same body of evidence now suffices. The word "know" means the same thing in both contexts; what changes is whether its invariant, stakes-sensitive conditions are met.

Nadia then pressed a refinement she called "silent stakes." Imagine, she said, that during the hallway conversation Dr. Park is actually being secretly recorded by a regulatory auditor, and if he makes an unwarranted claim about safety he will face professional ruin—so his practical stakes are just as high as in the boardroom, yet neither he nor his interlocutor is aware of it, and the conversational tone remains casual. The contextualist, Nadia claimed, can consistently say that in this conversation the standards for "knowing" remain low, so Dr. Park's assertion is appropriate and true by the lights of the context, even though hidden high stakes mean he fails to meet the stricter conditions that subject-sensitive invariantism would impose. Eli replied that this is precisely where the two views diverge, and that Nadia's resolution is the wrong one. If the stakes genuinely are high—regardless of whether they are conversationally salient—then subject-sensitive invariantism correctly predicts that Dr. Park does not know, and therefore should not assert it. The contextualist, by tying the epistemic standard to the conversational context rather than to the subject's actual practical situation, allows Dr. Park to be epistemically justified in saying "we know" even when he is in grave practical danger of being wrong. Eli concluded that this shows pragmatic encroachment is not merely a semantic theory about how "knows" is used; it is a substantive claim about the conditions for rational action, and contextualism empties it of that normative force by making knowledge depend on what the audience happens to be treating as important.

Nadia's final response was that Eli is conflating two distinct questions. The semantic or epistemic question is what conditions make "S knows that p" true in a given context of evaluation. The normative question is what S is rationally permitted to assert or act on. Even if Dr. Park's hidden stakes mean he ought not to speak carelessly, that is a matter of practical rationality, not of the truth-conditions of knowledge ascriptions. Contextualism can accommodate the normative point separately—by saying that responsible speakers should track stakes—without collapsing the epistemic standard into the subject's hidden psychological or practical environment. She added that Eli's own view risks a strange result: if Dr. Park genuinely does not know in the hallway because of silent high stakes, then in that very conversation he is uttering a falsehood, yet everyone present—including himself—would correctly judge his utterance as appropriate. That, she said, makes knowledge too detached from the norms of conversation that govern when "I know" can be sincerely and correctly said.

They asked me to referee. Please go through their exchange step by step and say who is right about each specific claim: whether the boardroom-hallway shift is better explained by contextualism or by subject-sensitive invariantism; whether the silent-stakes case genuinely separates the two views or is being misapplied by one side; whether pragmatic encroachment necessarily carries normative weight for action or can be separated from conversational semantics; and whether Nadia's worry about falsehood in casual conversation is decisive. Then say who is more right overall, and identify at least one important distinction or consideration that both of them seem to be missing—perhaps something about the knowledge norm of assertion, or about how "stakes" are individuated, or about a third position that avoids both frameworks—without treating that omission as fatal.
