I've been thinking about Aristotle's sea battle thing and the open future debate, but I want to push a slightly weirder version of it. I think the right way to defend an open future isn't through bivalence failure or branching metaphysics—those feel like they've been done to death in every phil of logic course. Instead, I'm drawn to this idea that "openness" is really about the continuity conditions of our predictive models: the future is open not because reality literally branches, but because our present epistemic state doesn't contain enough fixed points to determine a unique trajectory without collapsing the very distinction between prediction and determination. So the position is: future contingents lack determinate truth values not because the world is ontologically unfinished, but because assigning them truth values would require treating our current models as already containing the outcomes they merely forecast, which breaks the modal separation between what we anticipate and what we cause.

Tell me what would actually count against this—not just the standard "but then you can't plan" objection, but deeper stuff. What formal results in logic or decision theory would be awkward? Which intuitions about agency or causation does it risk? Would a defender of this view have to concede anything about the law of exluded middle applying at the model-level rather than the world-level? And rank the defeaters: which ones are genuinely fatal (maybe something about retrocausation or self-fulfilling prophecy structures) and which are just annoying adjustments? I'm particularly curious whether this collapses into some kind of anti-realism about the future that its defenders wouldn't want, or if it can stay metaphysically quiet. I was discussing this with a friend over coffee and we got stuck on whether this is just epistemicism with extra steps.
