As a novelist reconstructing a seventeenth-century merchant's consciousness for a historical novel, I keep encountering the maxim that "the historian is always a prisoner of her own moment," sometimes sharpened into the claim that "we cannot help but judge the dead by the standards of the living." In my writing group this gets invoked both to defend inserting modern moral frameworks into Restoration-era plots and to condemn such framing as inevitable corruption. Before I settle on a narrative ethics, I need the slogan disambiguated. Separate the trivial fact that historians live in the present from stronger theses: that present conceptual schemes render past mental states literally untranslatable; that normative evaluation of historical actors is necessarily illegitimate; that archival selection is structurally biased toward present concerns; and the metaphysical variant—borrowed from philosophical presentism about time—that past events lack ontological standing independent of current representation. For each reading, indicate whether it is a platitude, defensible, false, or self-undermining, and specify which version my interlocutor is likely wielding when she uses the line to justify contemporary gender norms in a period plot. Lay the readings out as a menu I can apply to my manuscript's problem of anachronism, rather than as a single answer, and tell me which ones actually support the use she puts the slogan to.
