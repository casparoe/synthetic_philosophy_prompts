Since I recently began contributing to an asynchronous, distributed research collective — one that produces co-authored reports without synchronous meetings or direct verbal negotiation — I have found myself drawn into philosophical questions about whether such groups genuinely act together. Last week, two colleagues, Alex and Jordan, argued about precisely this, using our collective as the case in point, and I should like an adjudication of their exchange.

Alex began by claiming that our collective had performed a genuine joint action in producing the latest report. Even though each contributor wrote their section independently, he said the group satisfied the standard conditions for shared cooperative activity: there was a shared goal (publication of a coherent report), mutual responsiveness mediated through annotations on a shared document, and an implicit commitment to mutual support by revising contradictory claims. He concluded that this was not merely an aggregate of individual efforts but a single, coordinated undertaking.

Jordan replied that this conflates retrospective coordination with genuine joint intentionality. For a plural subject to exist — in the sense required for true joint action — there must be a joint commitment that pools individual wills into a collective we. In our case, she argued, each contributor only intended to fulfil their own obligation to submit a section; nobody had formed an intention that we, together, produce this report in an irreducibly collective sense. The annotations showed awareness of outputs, not of others’ intentions, so mutual responsiveness was absent at the level that matters.

Alex responded that Jordan’s standard is unrealistically synchronous and face-to-face. He proposed that in distributed collaboration, joint commitment can be formed asynchronously through sustained engagement with a shared artefact — the living document itself — which functions as a proxy for direct interaction. He added that if we deny joint action here, we must also deny it to large scientific collaborations that never convene, which seems counterintuitive.

Jordan conceded that distributed collaboration exists, but insisted that without a we-mode intention — an intention whose content is irreducibly collective and not decomposable into individual I-mode intentions — what we have is only strategically aligned individual action. She noted that collective moral responsibility might still apply by aggregation, but that this differs from holding a genuine plural subject responsible for a joint act. She accused Alex of relabelling aggregation as jointness.

Alex then made a final move: even if the collective never formed a strong plural subject, he argued, there is still a functional form of joint intentionality that explains why errors in one section are properly blamed on the group rather than on the individual author alone. He said Jordan’s insistence on a pure we-mode misses the practical reality that modern collectives operate through layered, asynchronous commitments, and that philosophy should recognise this intermediate category rather than forcing a binary choice.

Please adjudicate their exchange move by move: rate each of Alex’s and Jordan’s moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with brief justification for each rating. Then say who is more right overall, and identify what — if anything — both parties are missing, particularly regarding whether asynchronous, artefact-mediated collaboration creates a distinctive form of joint intentionality that does not fit neatly into either standard shared cooperative activity or pure plural subjecthood. Please write your answer in British English.
