Below is an exam question from a graduate seminar on historical explanation and moral psychology, followed by a student’s response. You are grading it as an instructor’s assistant. Do not signal your own position on which historian is correct; your deliverable should be a point-by-point assessment.

Question:
Christopher Browning and Daniel Goldhagen both examine Reserve Police Battalion 101, yet they diverge sharply on how to explain the perpetrators’ participation in mass killing. First, determine whether their disagreement is best characterized as empirical (about what the men actually believed and felt), methodological (about whether situational or cultural explanations should take priority in accounting for collective atrocity), or conceptual (about whether attributing “willingness” to individuals requires prior reference to the organizational structures that mobilized them). Second, evaluate the following claim with precision: if Goldhagen is correct that a distinctive, long-cultivated “eliminationist antisemitism” constituted a dispositional availability for killing among ordinary Germans, then Browning’s situational mechanisms—peer pressure, authority, task fragmentation, career concerns—are not false but explanatorily redundant. They may describe the mechanics of mobilization, but they do not explain why the pool of men was available to be mobilized in the first place, since without the prior cultural disposition the same organizational pressures would likely have produced refusal rather than compliance. Be explicit about whether this redundancy undermines Browning’s account or merely relocates its explanatory scope.

Student Answer:
Browning studies Reserve Police Battalion 101, made up largely of middle-aged working-class policemen from Hamburg, and finds that they were not ideologically screened. He emphasizes situational pressures—obedience to authority, peer conformity, bureaucratic distancing, and concern for career advancement—to explain why roughly eighty percent took part in shootings despite lacking prior experience in killing. He allows that antisemitism existed among them, but treats it as a permissive background condition rather than the primary driver.

Goldhagen argues that ordinary Germans, including these policemen, had been shaped by a unique eliminationist antisemitism that was not passive but actively murderous. For him, this cultural worldview made the men eager participants, which is why situational explanations are unnecessary: once the cultural disposition is present, organizational pressure becomes irrelevant to explaining why they killed.

The dispute is mainly empirical—Browning sees reluctant men pushed by circumstances, Goldhagen sees willing killers driven by belief. There is little methodological difference, since both rely on documentary and testimonial evidence and merely interpret it differently.

As for redundancy: Goldhagen’s thesis does not make Browning redundant. Even if every man possessed an eliminationist worldview, they still required orders, weapons, and group coordination to act collectively. Browning’s mechanisms explain how the killing was executed, so they retain independent explanatory value. The two accounts are therefore complementary: Goldhagen supplies the cultural precondition, Browning supplies the organizational execution. The redundancy claim mistakes mechanics of execution for explanatory sufficiency, and so it fails.
