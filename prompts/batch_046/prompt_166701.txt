I organize a monthly metaphysics workshop for about eight people—mostly researchers and engineers with some philosophy background—meeting for 90 minutes with no assigned reading. I want a structured activity that makes the group feel the force of presentism, not through the generic slogan that "only present things exist," but through the specific cross-temporal grounding problem: if future states do not exist, what makes a present promise about them binding, or even truth-evaluable? Design a contract-game with clear rules and payoffs. Split participants into pairs with assigned ontological roles: Presentists may only invoke presently existing entities and mental states when formulating agreements, while Eternalists may reference future events and temporal relations as existing relata. The negotiation should involve real, small stakes—say, allocating a shared resource across time-steps—so that Presentists discover they cannot enforce contracts that depend on future conditions without either collapsing into eternalist ontology or rendering the contract content-empty, while Eternalists discover that their framework dissolves the asymmetry that makes present commitment feel urgently binding rather than merely relational. The idea should emerge from what breaks in the negotiation itself, not from any facilitator framing; do not name presentism, eternalism, truthmakers, or the grounding objection until the debrief. In the debrief, map specific breakdown moments onto corresponding arguments in the metaphysics of time, predict exactly what evasion will occur—most likely Presentists reconstruing promises as present dispositions or intentions, and how to handle that move without merely declaring it illegitimate—and be explicit about which feature of eternalist ontology, probably the B-theoretic static block with its elimination of genuine temporal passage, this physical simulation cannot reproduce and must be supplied verbally. No lecture up front, and please do not include bibliographies or citations.
