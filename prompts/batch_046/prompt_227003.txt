As a retired molecular biologist recently turned to philosophy of medicine, I will present at an interdisciplinary colloquium—clinical psychiatrists with DSM-5 working-group experience, philosophers of science specializing in mechanistic explanation and natural kinds, and a medical anthropologist skeptical of biomedical hegemony—my view that Szasz’s anti-psychiatric thesis, mental illness as myth, should be reconstructed not as an ontological denial of suffering but as a failure of cross-scale diagnostic coherence: psychiatric categories lack the stable mechanistic signatures across molecular, behavioral, and social levels that mature natural kinds such as infectious diseases display. I analogize this to the historical abandonment of phlogiston, not because mental states are unreal, but because the explanatory framework fails multi-level integration. Give me the objections in the order they will come, phrased as each faction would phrase them: first the psychiatrist defending pragmatic diagnostic validity, then the mechanist challenging my appeal to natural kinds without specifying a causal mechanism, then the anthropologist charging scientism. For each, specify what I should concede, what I should hold firm, and the weakest point in my reply. Include the objection I am secretly hoping nobody raises—namely, that my coherence criterion, applied consistently, would also exclude conditions like hypertension or chronic pain that I clearly wish to preserve. Conclude with a brief strategy for defending that boundary without appearing ad hoc, given only fifteen minutes for questions.
