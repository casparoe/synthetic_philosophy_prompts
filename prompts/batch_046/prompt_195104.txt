I'm delivering the opening remarks this Thursday evening at the interdisciplinary memorial symposium for Dr. Amara Okonkwo, held in the university's botanical garden auditorium. The room will be deliberately mixed: her family, who hold conventional religious hopes without being doctrinaire; her graduate students, some of whom have confided that they feel guilty for feeling relieved that an exhausting mentorship has ended; colleagues from cognitive ecology and philosophy of science; and university administrators who never knew her personally. I want these remarks to be genuinely philosophical rather than decorative—specifically, I want Zhuangzi's account of death to do structural work in what I say, not merely provide a poetic image. Rather than relying on the familiar butterfly-dream story, I want to draw on the less commonly emphasized idea that death is a radical dispersal in which the boundary between a self and its environment collapses, a kind of returning to the root that implies no personal survival, no location, and no continued consciousness. At the same time, I want to navigate the tension in the text between the social practice of ritual mourning and the ethical stance of refusing to treat death as a harm to the dead—what is often characterized as no mourning—because both the family's need for ceremony and the students' complicated guilt seem to sit exactly in that tension. The philosophy should shape the address: I must avoid offering false metaphysical comfort about her watching over us or being at peace somewhere, and I must avoid dismissing grief as irrational, yet I need to be able to speak honestly about her research into fungal decomposition networks as a form of transformation rather than erasure, without reducing her life to her data or flattening everything into a generic sentiment of universal connection.

Please write a speech of about seven to eight minutes, suitable for opening the symposium, that uses this framework to structure what can and cannot be said. The tone should be accessible to non-philosophers in the room—especially the family—but should not betray the conceptual rigor for the colleagues and students present. In particular, let the distinction between ritual acknowledgment and metaphysical claim organize the middle of the address, and let the idea of dispersal without personal survival frame the conclusion, so that the audience leaves with a sense of continuity that is ecological and intellectual rather than spiritual in any conventional sense.
