I just finished a long argument with a friend about whether Kurosawa's Rashomon is merely illustrating that everyone has their own truth, or whether it actually dramatizes the Quine-Duhem problem that any body of evidence is compatible with competing theories once we allow adjustments in auxiliary hypotheses. I want to read the film through that philosophical lens—not as generic epistemic humility, but as a study in evidential underdetermination. The woodcutter, the priest, Tajomaru, and the medium each present narratives that seem empirically adequate with respect to the shared sensory events in the grove, yet they diverge radically because of different background commitments about honor, gendered credibility, and human nature. Does the film treat these narratives as genuinely underdetermined by the data, or does it smuggle in a hidden tie-breaker—perhaps narrative coherence, moral symmetry, or the commoner's cynical realism—that makes one theory dominate? I am especially interested in whether the woodcutter's final revised account functions like a scientist saving a theory by revising auxiliaries, and whether the commoner's intervention at the gate breaks the symmetry in a way that parallels the pessimistic meta-induction about scientific change. I also want you to say where the analogy to science breaks down: in scientific underdetermination we eventually appeal to predictive success or simplicity to constrain theory choice, but in Rashomon all predictions are retrospective and all simplicity judgments are aesthetic or moral. Point to specific scenes—the medium's possession sequence, the bandit's boastful retelling, the wife's shifting testimony—where the philosophical idea either illuminates a formal feature of the scene or resists being mapped onto it. Finally, does Rashomon ultimately add something to the philosophical debate that standard textbook examples of underdetermination miss—perhaps about the role of testimony, shame, and self-deception in maintaining theoretical commitments?
