Consider a philosopher working within Douglas Hofstadter's framework of the self as a strange loop—an I that is not a fixed substance but a dynamic pattern of self-reference capable, in principle, of being instantiated across different substrates and distributed among other minds. The philosopher has offered implicit rulings on the following five cases.

First, a patient who has undergone complete corpus callosotomy: despite hemispheric disconnection, she reports a single, continuous first-person perspective. The philosopher rules that one I remains intact; the loop is resilient to internal partition so long as global integration via subcortical structures and shared embodied interaction persists.

Second, a perfect digital emulation of a brain running in isolation on silicon with complete neural fidelity but no causal connection to its original body or social world. The philosopher rules that this is not the same I but only a copy or shadow; severing the loop's embodied and relational dynamics breaks persistence regardless of pattern fidelity.

Third, a long-term intimate partner who, over decades, gradually internalizes a deceased spouse's characteristic judgments, emotional responses, narrative habits, and decision heuristics, to the point that mutual friends experience her as carrying both personalities within one continuous life. The philosopher rules that the deceased's I partially persists within hers; distribution across brains is possible when one loop becomes causally embedded in another's dynamics over sustained time.

Fourth, craniopagus twins with shared thalamic and parietal tissue and continuous inter-brain neural traffic, yet each reports a private, unshared first-person perspective with distinct autobiographical centers. The philosopher rules that two distinct I's remain; shared physical tissue is insufficient for loop merger if first-person boundaries stay intact.

Fifth, a whole-brain transplantation into a new body, with all prior social relationships and environmental interactions continuing. The philosopher rules that the same I travels with the brain; substrate continuity within one biological loop preserves identity even when the peripheral body changes.

Now consider a new case. A patient with extensive cortical damage receives an implantable synthetic neural lattice that bidirectionally links her remaining tissue to a healthy donor's intact hemisphere. Over months, the interface generates a single continuous feedback loop spanning both brains. Both subjects report a unified first-person perspective that includes autobiographical fragments and preference structures drawn from both original persons, yet they retain separate bodies, separate peripheral sensory streams, and largely separate social networks outside the laboratory.

Predict what this philosopher should say: is there now one I distributed across both brains, has one I been destroyed and replaced by an unstable hybrid, or do two I's persist in pathological fusion? Identify the implicit rule that best explains the five earlier rulings—perhaps causal-loop integration, social-relational embodiment, or pattern-fidelity-with-boundary-preservation—and articulate at least one rival rule that fits the track record equally well. Finally, specify the further case that would best distinguish these competing rules.
