I chair a municipal Board of Commemoration and Archive that reviews proposals quarterly for permanent public remembrance—monuments, named spaces, curriculum mandates, archival preservation, or deliberate omission—within a mid-sized city with limited curatorial staff and no in-house historians. Any recommendation that binds the municipality or commits public funds requires our board’s sign-off, and staff have roughly two hours per proposal to prepare a reasoned recommendation. I want a reusable decision procedure that translates the tension between Margalit’s ethics of memory—especially his distinction between thick-relation ethical obligations to specific victims and shared political memory as collective identity-building—and Rieff’s contrarian case for strategic forgetting into an operational protocol that non-philosophers can run. The procedure must preserve those core distinctions without requiring staff to consult the original texts.

Design the protocol as an ordered sequence of tests or questions, each with a specified sufficient answer, a clear stopping condition yielding either “no/defer,” “modify,” or “refer to board,” and explicit flags marking where the issue is one of values rather than facts and therefore cannot be resolved by staff judgment alone. It must handle proposals that differ in form (material monument, living archive, compulsory curriculum, intentional silence), in stakeholder structure (direct survivors, descendants, unaffiliated citizens), and in evidentiary status (well-documented, fragmentary, actively contested).

Then apply the procedure step-by-step to three proposals:

1. A citizens’ group requests a heroic bronze monument in the central square commemorating leaders of a 1930s labor strike that involved violent suppression by local authorities; the archival record is fragmentary and contested, descendants of both strikers and authorities remain active in the neighborhood, and the group demands an unqualified, permanent commemoration.

2. A museum petitions for permanent public display and open digital archiving of personal artifacts belonging to victims of a recent industrial disaster; the victims’ families have formally requested that the artifacts be destroyed or permanently sealed, arguing that public display perpetuates trauma and converts private grief into spectacle.

3. A school board proposes a compulsory curriculum unit on a local figure who built major public-health infrastructure but also designed and enforced racially exclusionary policies; the proposal involves neither monument nor archive, only mandatory classroom study for all secondary students.

For each case, show the procedural path, state the staff recommendation the protocol generates, and identify the precise step where judgment supersedes procedure. Include at least one case—specify which—where the protocol outputs a recommendation against remembrance (or in favor of forgetting/omission) that conflicts with the common intuition that remembering is always morally preferable to forgetting; explain exactly which conceptual feature—Margalit’s thick-relation obligation or Rieff’s harm-to-the-present condition—drives that reversal.

Finally, specify three limitations: a class of proposals the procedure systematically mishandles; one value judgment it cannot eliminate despite being dressed in procedural form; and one kind of scenario in which running the procedure at all would be ethically inappropriate. Do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; do not invoke theoretical frameworks outside the Margalit-Rieff tension unless strictly necessary for the procedural logic; and keep all descriptions within realistic municipal governance constraints.
