I'm drafting a scene for my novel where two philosophy grad students are arguing in a bar in Chicago, and one of them — she's kind of insufferable but brilliant — keeps pushing this argument about friendship and value that sounds airtight but ends somewhere totally wrong. I want the dialogue to be real, not like a textbook, so I'm trying to map out the steps she uses, and I need to know where the crack is.

Here's her reasoning: (1) G.E. Moore thought the only intrinsic goods are things like deep friendship and the experience of beauty, and he said their value depends on organic unity — the value of a whole being more than just the parts summed. (2) In friendship, what's valuable isn't just me plus you separately; it's the relational pattern, the way our mental states are arranged around each other. (3) So anything that breaks up that pattern — disagreement, distance, or just the fact that we're two bodies — is a kind of defect that lowers the good. (4) If that's right, then the best version of friendship wouldn't be two people at all; it would be one consciousness containing everything both people used to feel, arranged with perfect internal harmony. (5) But then Moore's view seems to recommend dissolving every friendship into a merged mind, wich is obviously crazy — friendship needs a you that's not me. (6) So either Moore was wrong about organic unity applying to people, or he's stuck with this bizarre merger conclusion.

My character insists the argument is sound and that we're just sentimental about separation. I want her to be wrong but in an interesting way. Which step should I have her reject, and what does giving that up cost her? Does she have to abandon organic unity entirely, or can she tweak it? And please write your answer in American English — I'm keeping the novel's voice in Chicago-style English and I don't want British spellings sneaking in.
