I am looking for an established concept, if one exists, that captures a specific argumentative configuration I have observed in historiographical controversies—particularly in debates over the Holocaust, the Armenian genocide, and the Nanjing Massacre. The configuration works as follows. In one instance, a researcher dismisses extensive survivor testimony about mass executions in a particular district on the grounds that no contemporaneous administrative order bearing an explicit kill directive has survived, treating the absence of that single documentary type as decisive. In another instance, the same researcher accepts ambiguous logistical records—say, a railway requisition form or an incomplete budget line—to argue that large-scale population displacement was purely administrative and therefore not genocidal, without demanding the same explicit directive standard. The asymmetry is structurally consistent: evidence that would increase the estimated scale or the intentionality of violence is held to an almost forensic, often unattainable archival standard, while evidence that diminishes either is admitted under far looser conditions of inference. The posture is presented not as advocacy but as rigorous methodological skepticism, and it rarely, if ever, produces conclusions that amplify culpability—only ones that reduce it. I understand the distinction between legitimate historiographical revisionism and outright denialism, and I recognize that genuine archival discoveries can legitimately revise casualty figures downward. What I am asking about is this precise pattern—the directional calibration of evidentiary thresholds themselves as the mechanism of diminution. Does it have a specific name in the literature on historical denial or critical historiography, or is it best understood as a subtype of denialism, a failure mode of source criticism, or a phenomenon that requires a new term? I care less about a bare label than about the boundaries: how this differs from ordinary skeptical source work, whether it can in principle be practiced in good faith, and what exactly distinguishes it from cases in which scholars genuinely revise estimates after uncovering new documents that uniformly affect all sides of the evidentiary ledger.
