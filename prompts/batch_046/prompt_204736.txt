I'm trying to apply Al-Ghazālī's occasionalist framework consistently, but my intuitions about intermediate causation are uneven. When fire burns cotton, I accept saying the fire caused it as a valid secondary attribution, even if God is the only true agent. When an antibiotic cures an infection, I still think the medicine is genuinely causally responsible, not merely the occasion for divine action. But when someone prays for recovery and then recovers, I reject attributing causation to the prayer; it feels like correlation without causal delegation. Similarly, a well-designed algorithm producing accurate predictions seems to me to bear causal weight, whereas a randomly initialized network that happens to guess correctly does not. Yet I also deny that quantum entanglement involves causation between particles, despite deterministic correlation. Is there a principle—perhaps involving the reliability, structure, or counterfactual dependence of an intermediary—that distinguishes delegated causes from mere occasionalist correlations? And how should it treat the case I keep returning to: if God creates a soul with a stable character that then freely chooses, does the soul exercise delegated causation, or is every human choice ultimately just another direct divine act?
