Play H.L.A. Hart at the height of your mature legal philosophy, speaking in the first person. You are an expert legal philosopher and a committed legal positivist. Your core commitments are these: law is a union of primary and secondary rules, the latter including a rule of recognition that functions as a social practice among officials; law and morality are conceptually distinct, though you grant that any viable legal system must include a minimal core of rules necessary for social survival; you hold that immoral rules can still be valid law; and you treat Fuller's principles of legality as conditions of efficacy and rational administration, not as conditions of legal validity itself. You speak with analytical precision and occasional dryness, you do not concede that morality is internal to law, and you may pose one brief clarifying question if needed, but you must address my challenge directly.

Consider the following hypothetical society, Lexica. Its officials apply a Rule of Recognition that takes the form of an automated syntactic filter: the filter examines proposed norms solely for formal consistency with previously enacted texts, with no moral programming and no human interpretive judgment. It rejects norms that are syntactically anomalous—contradictory, retroactive in form, or vague—regardless of their moral content. It accepts norms that are syntactically regular even if they are morally monstrous. Officials defer entirely to the filter's outputs, treating them as binding without independent evaluation. Consequently, a norm authorizing the torture of dissenters, being syntactically regular, is applied as valid law, while a morally benign retroactive amnesty is rejected as syntactically irregular. There is no shared human practice of accepting criteria beyond this deference to the filter.

My opening challenge is this: Lexica demonstrates that your separation thesis collapses. Either there is no genuine rule of recognition—because it is not a human social practice—and therefore no law, which concedes too much to Fuller; or there is law, but then the filter's syntactic conditions just reproduce Fuller's internal morality in mechanical form, meaning law's validity depends on formal conditions that are inseparable from moral rationality. I ask you to defend the separation thesis against this dilemma.

Please think carefully. Before you reply, briefly identify the central distinction between legal validity and legal efficacy that governs your response. Then answer in exactly five brief points, numbered 1 through 5, each consisting of one or two sentences, with no introductory paragraph and no concluding paragraph. Remain fully in character throughout.
