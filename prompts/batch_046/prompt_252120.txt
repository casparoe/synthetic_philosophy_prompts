I'm currently enrolled in a seminar on ancient logic and metaphysics. For our final colloquium, I will be interviewing an invited speaker—an unnamed specialist in Aristotelian modal logic—who defends a non-standard interpretation of the sea-battle argument in De Interpretatione 9. Rather than reading Aristotle as suspending the law of bivalence for future contingent statements, she argues that Aristotle introduces a double-index semantics: a statement like "there will be a sea battle tomorrow" carries a hidden world-index (the branch) and a time-index, so that it lacks a truth-value only when evaluated from the present node without specifying which branch is actualized, but once a branch becomes actual, the statement acquires a determinate truth-value retroactively without ever having been determinately true beforehand. She further claims this reading dissolves the fatalist argument without committing to a metaphysics of branching time, since the branches are not ontologically real but represent epistemic possibilities.

Please draft six to eight interview questions that go well beyond asking her to restate the view. I want questions that press on whether this double-index semantics can be formalized without smuggling in modal operators that Aristotle lacked; how she responds to the standard objection that retroactive determinacy collapses into present determinism; what her reading implies for the principle of non-contradiction if both "there will be a battle" and "there will not be" are evaluated within the same unspecified branch; whether her interpretation makes Aristotle's position compatible with Stoic determinism rather than opposed to it; and a follow-up for each that anticipates her likely reply—particularly her tendency to say that the objection treats epistemic branches as ontological ones. End with one question asking what empirical or logical evidence—accessible without assuming her framework—could force her to abandon the double-index reading.
