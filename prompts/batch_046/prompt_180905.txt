I'm defending a thesis at our philosophy of mind colloquium that applies Hofstadter's tripartite distinction among frame messages, outer messages, and inner messages—drawn analogously from Gödel, Escher, Bach—to the meta-problem of consciousness. I argue that phenomenal reports lack a stable "inner message" of raw qualitative content separable from framing conditions; rather, the framing context—linguistic practice, theoretical commitments, and introspective training—partially constitutes the content of the report itself. From this I draw a middle conclusion: the hard problem is not an explanatory gap between physical processes and an independent phenomenal datum, but a frame-level confusion about how introspective reports are interpreted and decoded. This is meant to split the difference between illusionism and full-blooded realism about the hard problem.

The audience is a research-university philosophy of mind seminar: mostly physicalist philosophers (some eliminativists, some non-reductive physicalists), two cognitive scientists who study introspection and metacognition, and one philosopher of language with contextualist leanings. They have no specialist background in formal systems or in Hofstadter's work, they are skeptical of mysterian or "explanatory gap" rhetoric, and they generally treat the hard problem as either a pseudo-problem or a live but tractable challenge for physicalism.
