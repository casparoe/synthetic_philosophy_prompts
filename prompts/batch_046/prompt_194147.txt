I'm drafting a chapter on epistemic constraints on ontological commitment, and I've walked myself into an argument that dissolves ordinary object-reference entirely—though I want to preserve the intuition that ontology shouldn't outstrip our epistemic reach. Here is the chain: (1) For any object O to be included in our ontology, there must be some criterion of identification that allows us, at least in principle, to distinguish O from every other object. (2) Call a property "epistemically insulated" if it is necessarily such that no observer in our epistemic position could ever detect its presence or absence. (3) For any ordinary object O—a specific electron, tree, or particle—we can construct a "shadow-twin" O* that is identical to O in every non-insulated property, but differs from O in at least one insulated property (for instance, a precise quantum configuration that decoheres before measurement, or a microstructural feature permanently below any physical detection threshold). (4) Because the difference between O and O* lies entirely in insulated properties, our criterion of identification cannot distinguish them. (5) By (1), if we cannot distinguish O from O*, then O and O* are not two objects in our ontology; they are one. (6) But this applies not just to pairs, but to arbitrarily large sets of shadow-twins for every putative object, so for every object we claim to refer to there is actually only one "cluster" corresponding to all its shadow-twins. (7) Therefore, our ontology contains no numerically distinct specific objects—only maximally specific accessible-property clusters. (8) Yet scientific practice and ordinary language presuppose that we can re-identify the same specific object over time. (9) I reject (8) as absurd. So one of (1)–(6) must fail.

I am inclined to keep (1) and (3), since the epistemic constraint seems reasonable and the metaphysical possibility of shadow-twins seems unproblematic. But I cannot locate the precise slip: is there an equivocation in (1) between "distinguishable by us" and "distinguishable in principle"? Does (5) illegitimately apply an epistemic criterion of identification to metaphysical identity? Or does (3) already beg the question by assuming we can specify a difference that is, by definition, inaccessible? If I reject (1), do I have to embrace a full-blown metaphysical realism that permits entirely epistemically opaque entities? Or is there a middle position—perhaps a distinction between reference-fixing and re-identification—that lets me preserve both the intuition and ordinary object-talk? Which step should I give up, and what does giving it up cost?
