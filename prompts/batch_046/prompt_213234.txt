I serve on an interdisciplinary government board that reviews predictive models—mostly in epidemiology and climate-adaptation economics—before they are adopted for policy. Contractors frequently defend starkly unrealistic assumptions, such as homogeneous populations, perfect information, or the absence of strategic behavior, by appealing to the philosophical claim that descriptive falsehood is permissible if predictive output is accurate. Our experience, however, is that predictive success in one observational regime often collapses once the model is used to design an intervention that changes the very conditions the assumptions suppress. I need a reusable decision procedure—ordered questions, sufficient-answer criteria, explicit stop points for rejection, and clearly marked judgment-call nodes—that my board can run in a ninety-minute session without philosophical training, to determine whether a given model’s unrealistic premises are tolerable for the specific policy use proposed.

The procedure should be built around a distinction I want you to treat as central: “predictive insulation,” where an unrealistic feature operates only inside the model’s internal mechanism and does not interact with the policy lever, versus “intervention exposure,” where the policy acts directly on the very feature the model misrepresents. The latter should be treated as disqualifying unless the contractor supplies a separate counterfactual robustness check that survives a defined stress test.

Please deliver:
1. The step-by-step procedure in sequence, stating at each step what counts as a sufficient answer, what answer stops the process with a rejection, and where the board must sign off on a value judgment rather than a factual claim.
2. Explicit flagging of the points where the procedure will misfire—where predictive accuracy is ambiguous, where robustness checks depend on expert opinion, and where risk tolerance rather than model quality drives the decision.
3. Application of the procedure to three cases: (a) an epidemiological model assuming uniform social mixing that predicts infection peaks well but is being used to justify targeted school closures; (b) a climate-adaptation crop-yield model assuming rational actors with perfect foresight that predicts regional outputs well but is being used to set insurance premiums that will alter planting behavior; and (c) one case where the procedure yields an unwelcome answer—specifically, a model with an excellent predictive history that should nonetheless be rejected for this board’s purpose. For each case, show where the procedure stops or continues, which step is decisive, and what the board must record.
4. A clear statement of what the procedure cannot do: for instance, it cannot generate new predictions, resolve disagreements about acceptable risk, or substitute for domain-specific empirical validation.

The procedure should be honest about its dependence on the underlying philosophical reasoning—namely, that predictive success can justify unrealistic premises—without requiring board members to read philosophical texts. It must accommodate members with mixed scientific backgrounds and include a one-paragraph summary they can cite in a formal rejection or approval memo.
