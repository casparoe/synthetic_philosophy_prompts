You serve on an interdisciplinary review board that funds archaeological and anthropological projects. The board must sort every proposal into one of two methodological tracks—"collapse analysis" or "reconfiguration analysis"—because the two tracks use different evidentiary standards, comparison groups, and reporting obligations. The theoretical problem is well known: some scholars treat rapid societal transformation as "collapse" (a systemic failure across institutional domains), while others treat the same evidence as "reconfiguration" (a selective transformation in which lost functions are replaced, decentralized, or simply become invisible in the record). Your board needs an operational procedure that a reviewer without specialized theoretical training can apply in under thirty minutes, using only the material claims made in the proposal itself.

Write a reusable decision procedure. It should take the form of an ordered checklist with explicit decision nodes. Define three diagnostic axes—demographic discontinuity, institutional discontinuity, and symbolic-material discontinuity—and for each specify what counts as sufficient evidence of discontinuity versus mere change, including the "replacement condition" (the presence of a functional substitute, however decentralized or informal, counts against discontinuity on that axis). State the aggregation rule that maps axis scores onto the two tracks, and include an explicit "asymmetric collapse" branch for cases in which one axis shows severe discontinuity while another shows clear replacement or expansion. Identify the exact points where the procedure stops and hands the decision to human judgment—particularly where archaeological visibility is poor or where the proposal makes causal claims that the checklist cannot evaluate.

Then apply the procedure to three cases described only by their standard archaeological profiles: (1) the Late Bronze Age Eastern Mediterranean (palatial economies, writing systems, and settlement hierarchies around 1200 BCE); (2) the Classic Maya lowlands during the Terminal Classic (divine kingship, monumental inscription, and urban cores between approximately 800 and 900 CE); and (3) Rapa Nui (Easter Island) during the period of deforestation and moai cessation (approximately 1600–1800 CE). For each, run the checklist axis by axis and state the track assignment. One of these applications must produce a result that runs against the popular narrative—state that result clearly and explain why the procedure produces it despite the conventional label. Finally, list three specific things the procedure cannot do—such as determine causation, assess normative significance, or resolve debates about archaeological invisibility—and flag any proposal feature that should trigger an automatic referral to expert panel review regardless of the checklist outcome.

The deliverable should be a single document: the procedure first, then the three worked applications, then the limitations. Do not include a bibliography, reading list, or citation request. Do not ask the reviewer to consult external literature. Phrase all nodes as questions that can be answered from the proposal text and standard comparative knowledge.
