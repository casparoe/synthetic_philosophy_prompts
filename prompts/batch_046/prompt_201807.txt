Two philosophers of mind, Ravi and Elena, were debating Erwin Schrödinger’s later philosophical essays on consciousness and their explicit debt to Advaita Vedanta. Both accept that Schrödinger denies the plural of consciousness and affirms a singular, undivided awareness underlying all apparent individual minds, but they disagree sharply on whether this is a literal ontological identity claim or a transcendental ground-level unity.

Ravi argued that Schrödinger advances a literal numerical-identity thesis: there is one consciousness, and what we call my experience and your experience are not two experiences supported by a shared substrate but one and the same experience erroneously partitioned by the illusion of separate bodies. He claimed that Schrödinger treats the multiplicity of minds analogously to the multiplicity of colors produced by a prism—an artifact of the apparatus of embodiment, not a feature of consciousness itself. Therefore, if two people report an identical qualitative state—say, the same shade of crimson—there are not two crimson-experiences belonging to two subjects, but one crimson-experience that the single consciousness is having through two bodily vantage points.

Elena replied that Ravi conflates metaphysical ground with empirical subject. She granted that Schrödinger adopts the Vedantic view that Atman and Brahman are non-dual, but insisted he treats this as a transcendental unity of being, not as the denial of token-distinct mental lives. On her reading, embodied perspectives generate genuinely different streams of consciousness—numerically plural, even if they share an ultimate ground—just as two waves are numerically distinct motions even if both are motions of the same ocean. She added that Schrödinger’s own scientific practice treats other minds as independent variables, which would be incoherent if he literally believed only one mind existed behind all brains.

Ravi countered that Elena’s “waves on one ocean” metaphor smuggles in multiplicity that Schrödinger explicitly rejects. He noted that Schrödinger writes that consciousness is never experienced in the plural, and he takes this not as a phenomenological limitation but as an ontological discovery. If Elena is right, then the unity of consciousness becomes a decorative metaphysical add-on with no consequences for how we individuate minds, which Ravi thinks betrays the radicalness of the Vedantic influence.

Elena then introduced a thought experiment: imagine a hypothetical case of perfect inter-subjective overlap, where two individuals in sustained empathic union report not merely similar but indexically identical first-person narratives—each saying I am seeing crimson with no distinguishable perspective-marker. She asked whether Schrödinger’s view gives us any reason to treat these as two reports from one consciousness rather than two reports from two consciousnesses that happen to be qualitatively and indexically indistinguishable. She claimed that without a verificationist criterion for numerical identity, Ravi’s interpretation collapses the distinction between same consciousness and indistinguishable consciousness, which is a metaphysical overreach.

Ravi responded that Elena is demanding a criterion of identity appropriate only to empirical objects, whereas consciousness, on the Vedantic view, is precisely that which is not an object and therefore not subject to such criteria. He concluded that Elena saves empirical multiplicity only by domesticating Schrödinger’s monism into a harmless background ontology.

Finally, give an overall verdict: is Schrödinger’s unity of consciousness best understood as a literal numerical identity of all minds, as a transcendental unity of ground, or as something else?
