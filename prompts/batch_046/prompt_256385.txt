In Keynes's well-known essay imagining a future of greatly reduced work and widespread leisure, I notice a recurring argumentative pattern that I cannot name. The argument proceeds in two steps that seem to pull in opposite directions. First, it treats material scarcity as the primary obstacle to human flourishing: once economic needs are satisfied, people will naturally devote themselves to wisdom, art, and agreeable living. Second, it insists that the very conditions of scarcity have so corrupted human psychology—fostering greed, status anxiety, and an obsessive attachment to accumulation—that people will continue laboring pointlessly even when abundance makes work unnecessary. The result is a strange double vision: abundance is expected to restore capacities for the good life, yet scarcity is said to have destroyed those capacities so thoroughly that abundance alone may not revive them. I encounter this same structure in modern post-work debates that invoke Keynes. Does this specific tension have an established philosophical or economic label—something that distinguishes it from ordinary optimism about progress, or from deterministic claims that material conditions simply shape culture? If there is no standard term, how should one precisely delimit the phenomenon: what counts as an instance, and where does it collapse into a simpler contradiction or into a different fallacy altogether?
