Design a realistic, one-paragraph thought experiment set in an ordinary workplace or household context—no science fiction, no perfect predictors, no lottery structures—in which an agent exhibits weakness of will (akrasia) driven by hyperbolic discounting, but where the standard smaller-sooner versus larger-later reward structure is entirely absent. Instead, engineer the case so that the hyperbolic curve applies to the immediate psychological cost of exerting self-control: the agent’s best judgment identifies a course of action that yields the same objective payoff regardless of when it is taken, yet she avoids it because the present burden of willpower feels disproportionately large compared to the diffuse future benefit of having acted well. Present the scenario. Then verify that it meets the specifications: state her all-things-considered judgment, the reasoning method behind it, the action she takes, and the nearby possible worlds in which the distortion is absent. Next, show how a hyperbolic-discounting model explains the failure without relying on temporal proximity of outcomes, and contrast this with a Davidson-style divided-self account—specifying exactly where the two explanations diverge. Conclude by identifying the structural feature that makes the case work, describing what collapses if you revert to conventional reward-based hyperbolic discounting, and explaining how a defender of the Davidsonian view could attack the scenario by denying that the failure is genuinely akratic.
