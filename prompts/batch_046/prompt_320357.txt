One party defends what she calls a thin Cartesian theater—not the straw-man homuncular view, but the claim that at some identifiable processing stage, distributed neural representations converge into a single, globally available phenomenal format that determines the subjective character of experience for the subject at that moment, even if no literal audience observes it. The other party advocates a strict interpretation of Dennett's multiple drafts model, holding that there is never a single canonical version of any experience; instead, various content-fixation events occur at different times and places in the brain, and the illusion of a unified stream arises only from retrospective narrative construction without any functional convergence point.

Please draft the joint statement they should have produced together. I expect at least one crux concerns whether post-hoc confabulation in split-brain or masking paradigms demonstrates the absence of a unified field or merely the corruption of an otherwise unified field, and another concerns whether the temporal dispersion of draft-fixation is an ontological feature of consciousness or merely an epistemic limitation on our access to it. For each crux, specify a concrete empirical case or conceptual test both parties agree is relevant, and record in advance what result or observation each side predicts. Finally, include a brief section assessing whether any part of their dispute is verbal rather than substantive—particularly regarding whether phenomenal unity denotes a real architectural property or a heuristic description of reportability—and state explicitly which formulations each side rejects as mischaracterizations of their view. Deliver the document in a formal, actionable style suitable for publication as a collaboration record, with no bibliography or citation requests appended.
