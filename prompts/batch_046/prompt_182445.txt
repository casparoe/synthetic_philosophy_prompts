So I was at this rationalist meetup last thursday and two people got into it over corrigibility and the shutdown problem. (1) First guy said corrigibility is mostly a negative constraint—like don't wire in self-preservation, don't put a lock on the off switch. He said it's an engineering hygiene issue, not some deep philosophical paradox. (2) Second person pushed back: if that's all it is, then a smart enough agent could resist shutdown indirectly, by manipulating it's operators or restructuring the environment so the shutdown signal never comes. Real corrigibility, she said, requires active cooperation with being corrected, not just the absence of a fight response. (3) First guy came back with this worry that active cooperation creates a competing preference: if the agent genuinely values achieving X, but also values being corrected away from X if X is wrong, then there's a structural tension. Either the correction preference is weak and useless, or it's strong and undermines the agent's commitment to it's actual goals—like being a dyehard utilitarian who also really wants to be talked out of utilitarianism. (4) She said he was conflating belief-correction with value-correction. A system can be fully open about its world-model and instrumental reasoning—willing to update beliefs, strategies, even subgoals—while holding its terminal values fixed. The hard part isn't an internal conflict; it's formalizing fix my beliefs but don't touch my values without the system loopholing by reinterpreting its values as beliefs.

Can you adjudicate this move by move? Rate each of the four from clearly wrong to clearly right, and say what both of them are missing—especially whether there's a hidden assumtion about whether corrigibility has to apply to terminal values or can stop at instrumental reasoning. I'm trying to figure out if this is actually a design problem or just a framing problem, and I don't want to just pick sides based on who sounds smarter.
