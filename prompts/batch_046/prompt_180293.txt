I've been thinking about Gödel's rotating universe solution to Einstein's field equations — the one where the whole cosmos spins and you get closed timelike curves, so in principle a observer could loop back and meet themselves. I tried to turn this into an argument about personal identity and it now proves way too much, but I can't tell if I'm smuggling in an equivocation or if one of the premises is just wrong.

(1) In a Gödel universe, the rotation of matter creates closed timelike curves, which means an observer's worldline can loop back to an earlier point in spacetime.
(2) If an observer can be present at two points on their own worldline that are causally connected in a circle, then that observer is literally their own ancestor — they exist before themselves.
(3) Personal identity is irreflexive and asymmetric: nothing can be its own ancestor, because that would violate the transitivity of the "ancestor of" relation.
(4) So in a Gödel universe, personal identity becomes contradictory.
(5) But we clearly have personal identity, therefore Gödel universes are not just physically unlikely but conceptually impossible.
(6) Now apply the same logic to any cyclical process — seasonal cycles, economic booms and busts, even a pendulum swinging back — since a later state "returns" to an earlier configuration.
(7) By steps (2)-(4), any cyclical change implies that some entity is its own predecessor, which is contradictory.
(8) Therefore, if the argument is sound, cyclical processes don't exist at all, which is obviosly absurd.

Where does this go wrong? I feel like (3) might be doing heavy lifting by treating "ancestor" as a logical relation rather than a causal one, but I'm not sure if that's enough to stop the overreach into (6)-(7). I'd rather keep (1) and (5) if possible, but I'm open to giving up (3). Is there a hidden scope shift between the personal-identity case and the pendulum case, or is the problem somewhere else?
