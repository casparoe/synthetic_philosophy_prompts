I am leading a discussion section next Thursday for an upper-level seminar on intergenerational justice, and I need a teaching plan—not the explanation itself—for one conceptual distinction I have been struggling to articulate clearly since a conversation with a colleague who works in estate law. The students have read Rawls's account of the basic structure and Dworkin's separation of brute luck from option luck, but they have not encountered Nozick, nor any legal history of trusts, nor the empirical literature on intergenerational elasticity. I want them to grasp why inheritance taxation is structurally different from income taxation: not as a penalty on accumulated savings, but as a correction for what I will call compound temporal proximity to capital—the way dynastic wealth operates as a sequential option that rewrites the social opportunity set of each new generation before any labor-market exchange takes place.

The carrying example I have in mind compares a timber dynasty organized through a generation-skipping perpetual trust, in which capital is legally shielded from estate taxation and market liquidation across four generations, with a single-generation artisan cooperative in which members liquidate and redistribute capital upon retirement and no intergenerational transmission mechanism exists. Before I commit to this example, I need to know what properties it must possess and what it must lack—should the cooperative retain any nominal inheritance feature to make the comparison sharper, or would that muddy the structural point?—and I need the precise order of introduction: what conceptual dependency must be secured first (the temporal asymmetry of opportunity, the distinction between flow and stock, or the legal form of the trust?), what example detail carries the load, and at what point the students should be asked to apply the framework themselves rather than observe it.

I also need a misconception map. I expect they will treat inheritance tax as a punishment for success, conflate dynastic capital with large personal savings, or interpret equal opportunity as requiring identical starting wealth rather than the absence of compound structural advantages. For each, I need the point in the sequence where it should be headed off—before the example is introduced, during its exposition, or only after an initial application attempt—and exactly what language to use without sounding like I am defending a specific tax regime. Additionally, I want one check-for-understanding question that distinguishes genuine comprehension of the structural correction from mere repetition of vocabulary like "compound proximity," plus an explicit statement of what to leave out at this level—perhaps the mathematics of elasticity coefficients, or the specific provisions of Scandinavian wealth-tax designs—together with the sentence I should actually say in the room to mark that boundary. Please do not include any bibliography, citation list, or suggested readings; the students are restricted to the primary texts already assigned.
