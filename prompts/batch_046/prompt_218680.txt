I'm a doctoral student in moral philosophy organizing a small interdisciplinary workshop next month on whether comparing mass atrocities is conceptually coherent or morally obscene. The occasion is the opening remarks, about ten minutes, delivered to a room divided evenly between scholars of genocide studies and philosophy of history and descendants and community representatives for whom these events are living family history rather than academic material. I need the speech to do structural work: it should not merely welcome attendees but establish what kind of conversation is permitted here and what kind is forbidden, not out of politeness but out of the nature of comparison itself. To that end, please build the remarks around a distinction I have been developing between what I call competitive suffering, in which comparison becomes a zero-sum contest over victim status, and structural comparison, in which we examine institutional mechanisms and patterns of violence without weighing lives against one another. The remarks must make clear that this workshop pursues only the second form, yet they must also admit that the boundary between them is permanently unstable, that any structural comparison can slide into competitive suffering, especially for those present who carry one particular atrocity in personal memory. Because of this instability, there are specific things the remarks must not say: no numerical ordering of death tolls presented as if it resolves moral significance, no phrase like the worst genocide, no reassurance that comparison is harmless when conducted with sufficient scholarly care. At the same time, the remarks must justify why this workshop is happening at all, why silence is not the only ethically permissible response, and they must do so without resolving the tension I have described. Please write this as continuous prose only, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, in the manner of a formal letter read aloud, suitable for an audience in which philosophical precision matters but must never come at the expense of human presence. The philosophical problem should shape every sentence, so that by the end the audience understands exactly what has been permitted and what has been refused, and why that refusal is itself part of the inquiry.
