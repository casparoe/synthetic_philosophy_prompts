I am preparing a graduate seminar for students whose training is almost entirely in analytic philosophy of science and epistemology, and I wish to devote one session to the Nyāya theory of the pramāṇas—not as historical taxonomy, but as a live account of how knowledge is structured. Please design the explanatory arc for a single fifty-minute session: first, the precise sequence in which pratyakṣa, anumāna, upamāna, and śabda must be introduced so that the independence of each becomes philosophically intelligible rather than merely listed; second, the single carrying example that should thread through the entire session, with an explicit assessment of whether the classic inference of fire from smoke on a distant hill is adequate or whether a case involving linguistic testimony about an unseen object better illuminates the specific work done by śabda and upamāna; third, the misconceptions these students will almost certainly import—especially the tendency to treat all cognition as either direct observation or probabilistic inference, and to read pramāṇa through an internalist theory of justification—together with the exact point in the sequence at which each should be intercepted and defused; fourth, the final diagnostic question I should pose to determine whether they have genuinely grasped why Nyāya treats upamāna as irreducible to anumāna rather than as a subspecies of it; and fifth, the genuinely hard problem I should leave deliberately unresolved at the close—specifically, whether yogipratyakṣa collapses the boundary between pratyakṣa and anumāna—without offering them a resolution. The audience has no background in Sanskrit philosophical texts, so do not assume familiarity with the Nyāyasūtra or with later commentators, and do not ask for a bibliography or further readings.
