I'm preparing to lead a discussion section for first-year computer science graduate students who have just implemented a recursive-descent parser generator for a toy language. They are confident that they understand self-reference because they have handled mutual recursion and bootstrapping, and they have encountered Hofstadter's distinction between tangled hierarchies and strange loops only in passing, treating it as a poetic gloss on practices they already master. I need a teaching plan—not the explanation itself—for how to move them from that complacency to a precise grasp of why Escher's Drawing Hands exemplifies a tangled hierarchy rather than mere recursion, and specifically what the inviolate level is and why it is not simply "the root of the call stack." The students will arrive thinking that if two hands draw each other, the situation is symmetric and therefore either paradoxical or resolvable by adding a meta-level; they will not yet see that the physical paper and the artist's hand form a level that participates in neither loop.

Plan the explanatory arc in dependency order: which idea must be firmly in place before the image can even be introduced, what properties the carrying example must possess and what it must lack (and why Drawing Hands succeeds where their parser does not), and exactly where in the sequence each predictable misconception should be intercepted. I anticipate three misconceptions arising in this order: first, that "tangled hierarchy" means "infinite regress of meta-languages" (which they will raise immediately upon seeing mutual reference); second, that the artist's hand is simply "level n+1" in the same stack (which they will propose as a tidy resolution); and third, that the inviolate level is just an implementation detail that could be eliminated by making the system fully self-contained. For each, specify the point in the arc where it should be headed off and the brief counter-example or distinction that does the work without derailing the session. Then propose one diagnostic question I can ask at the end—something they could not answer correctly if they still held misconception two—that reveals whether they understand that the inviolate level lies outside the hierarchy rather than above it. Finally, state explicitly what should be omitted at this stage—perhaps the formal logical analogues to Gödel's construction or the full analysis of strange loops—and explain why leaving it out protects the core distinction rather than merely simplifying it.
