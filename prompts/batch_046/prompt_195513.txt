Consider the following track record of Professor V., a philosopher of science working within a broadly Bayesian framework, regarding whether old evidence—data that existed prior to a theory's formulation—can genuinely raise that theory's posterior credibility.

1. The bootstrapping case: a quantitative law is inferred inductively from dataset D, and D is subsequently cited as independent confirmation. Professor V. rules that there is no confirmation.

2. The epistemic-novelty case: theory H is derived in 2010 from structural first principles, with no access to dataset D collected in 1980 and archived in an obscure repository. In 2024 a researcher discovers D and finds that it falls within H's predicted range. Professor V. rules that D confirms H.

3. The partitioned-archive case: a predictive model is calibrated on climate records from 1950–1980 and then applied to records from 1980–2000 drawn from the same historical archive. Professor V. rules that the 1980–2000 records confirm the model, but the 1950–1980 calibration records do not.

4. The redescription case: an old astronomical observation is translated into the vocabulary of a new cosmological theory and presented as a successful prediction, yet the theory's parameters could have been adjusted post hoc to accommodate any value within the observed range. Professor V. rules that no quantitative risk was taken and therefore there is no confirmation.

5. The first-principles match case: in 2020 a physicist derives a specific constant from fundamental theory without knowing that an 1890 experiment had measured exactly that value; the 1890 record is discovered in 2023. Professor V. rules that the old measurement confirms the theory.

Now consider a new case. In 2028, biologist Dr. R. formulates evolutionary model E using only contemporary genomic data and structural biological principles. She then combines E with an auxiliary assumption A—concerning the error profile of 1990s sequencing instruments—to derive a precise quantitative prediction P about a genetic marker. Unbeknownst to Dr. R., assumption A is false: the instruments actually exhibited a different error structure. However, an entirely unrelated arithmetic mistake in her computation exactly cancels the distortion that A introduces, so the numerical value in P is correct. Meanwhile, a forgotten 1995 study had measured the same marker and recorded value V, identical to P. Dr. R. had no knowledge of the 1995 study, and her derivation of P did not depend on knowing V. In 2030 the 1995 archive is digitized and the match is noted.

What verdict should we predict Professor V. will deliver? Identify at least two competing rules implicit in her track record—one that emphasizes epistemic novelty and structural independence from the evidence, and another that requires the soundness of the reasoning chain that produces the prediction. For each rule, state whether it predicts confirmation in Dr. R.'s case, and identify which of her earlier cases serves as the closest analog for each rule. Give your best-guess prediction with explicit reasoning, and specify the single further hypothetical case that would best discriminate between these two rules. If her precedents genuinely underdetermine the outcome, state what philosophical principle she would be adopting with each possible verdict.
