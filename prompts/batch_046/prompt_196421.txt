I just finished a long and exhausting argument with a friend about consciousness and want an audit of whether the commitments I ended up defending actually hang together, ordered by severity with the cheapest revision noted for each. Here is roughly my position: (1) I hold a non-propositional higher-order view—phenomenal consciousness requires an actual, occurrent awareness-relation directed at a first-order state, not a propositional thought about it; (2) I maintain that this awareness-relation must always have an intentional object, so a "targetless" higher-order state is incoherent, since awareness that is not awareness-of collapses into mere neural dynamics; (3) I reject first-order representationalism on empirical grounds, specifically the no-report paradigm literature distinguishing feedforward sensory processing (which can occur unconsciously) from recurrent or re-entrant dynamics (which appear necessary for consciousness); (4) I think split-brain cases show not two streams of consciousness but one unified consciousness with fragmented access, because the awareness-relation is essentially unitary: right-hemisphere states that fall outside the left-hemisphere monitor's targeting are unconscious, yet they remain states of the same subject rather than constituting a second conscious being; and (5) I treat consciousness as a natural kind with empirically specifiable conditions of realization—those recurrent dynamics constrain what can enter the awareness-relation—while denying that this amounts to reduction, since the relational structure has modal and normative features that outstrip any particular neural substrate. I can see several potential fractures. First, (1)'s demand for an actual occurrent relation seems to sit uneasily with (3): if recurrent dynamics can occur without report or behavioral access, am I not treating the presence of those dynamics as sufficient, which smuggles in the mechanistic criterion I claim to reject? Second, (2) and (4) together seem to commit me to saying that right-hemisphere guided behavior in split-brain patients is unconscious, but if it guides action in ways functionally indistinguishable from conscious control, does that not undermine my claim that only targeted states are phenomenally conscious? Third, is the tension between (3) and (5) only apparent—since dynamics can constrain realization without reducing the relation—or is there a hidden reductionism in saying the empirical conditions "specify" the kind?
