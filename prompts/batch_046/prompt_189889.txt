In my advanced economics seminar we have been arguing for months about Herbert Simon’s concept of satisficing—selecting an option that clears an aspiration threshold rather than searching for a global optimum. One faction maintains that satisficing is merely optimization with an explicit search-cost term folded into the utility function; once you formalize the cost of acquiring information, stopping rules become derivable from standard expected-utility maximization. The opposing faction insists that this reduction misses the structural point: in true satisficing, the decision-maker does not possess a complete preference ordering over the full choice set, and the aspiration level itself is adaptively revised by experience rather than being a fixed parameter in a utility calculus.

What frustrates me is that the disagreement has not budged despite shared reference to identical laboratory findings—job-search experiments, consumer-choice protocols, and the same formal stopping-rule models. Both sides acknowledge the data; both sides reject the other side’s interpretation of what the data mean for rationality. I am trying to determine whether this is a hidden empirical dispute about cognitive architecture—perhaps whether neural value-computation involves a discrete good-enough gate rather than continuous comparison—or a normative disagreement about what rationality requires, or even a verbal conflict in which optimization and rationality carry different commitments for economists versus cognitive scientists.

There is an additional framing I recently encountered that I do not fully understand but that seems relevant: treating the acceptance of a satisfactory option not as the termination of a search process but as a kind of contractual commitment to that option, which renders the decision structurally irreversible in a way that utility-maximization, with its constant openness to trade-offs, is not. If this framing is coherent, does it provide a genuine third position, or does it merely relocate the same disagreement to the question of whether reversibility is constitutive of rational choice?
