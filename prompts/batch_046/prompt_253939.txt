I'm working through Sor Juana Inés de la Cruz's 1691 letter, the Respuesta a Sor Filotea de la Cruz, with a small reading group, and we've gotten stuck on the section where she defends her study of secular philosophy, natural science, and theology against ecclesiastical objections. In the passage, she argues that if God granted her the specific capacity for reason, then suppressing that capacity through enforced ignorance is not true humility but rather a waste of a divine gift; she emphasizes that her study was always conducted in private, never interfering with her religious obligations, and she points out that many male scholars pursued the same texts without facing censure. She also invokes St. Catherine of Alexandria—celebrated for defeating philosophers in public debate—to ask why a woman's use of reason is praised in hagiography yet forbidden in contemporary practice. I have summarized this from our shared translation, but the underlying structure of the reasoning remains unclear to me.

What confuses me is whether her defense is primarily theological, social, or some hybrid that risks internal tension. She seems to claim both that reason is a God-given talent that must not be buried, and that her particular exercise of it was harmless because hidden and subordinate to devotion. But if the defense depends on privacy and non-interference, does that not concede the very gendered boundary she is challenging? In other words, is the private-public distinction a strategic concession to her audience, or is it essential to her argument that women can study only so long as they do not teach or speak? Our group is divided: some think she is making a universal claim about women's intellectual equality, others think she is carving out a narrow exception for herself based on exceptional vocation.

I'd like you to help me understand the passage by explaining the structural relationship between her theological claim about not wasting God-given talents and her social observation about the gendered double standard in access to texts. In particular, please clarify whether the private-study condition weakens or actually strengthens her broader position. Additionally, I have been wondering whether framing her defense through the lens of what contemporary philosophers call epistemic injustice—where a group's capacity to know is systematically devalued because of social identity rather than evidence—illuminates her reasoning or instead imposes an anachronistic framework that obscures her specifically Baroque theological commitments. Could you address that tension as well? A model that can reason through conceptual distinctions should be able to do this without consulting external bibliographies.
