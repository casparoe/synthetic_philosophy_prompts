In the philosophy of action reading group I coordinate, our treatment of Parfit's hitchhiker scenario has reached an impasse that feels deeper than ordinary disagreement. One camp holds that the case dissolves upon analysis: once the driver has arrived and received the rescue, the causal efficacy of paying is nil, so withholding payment is ex post rational, which in turn makes the prior promise irrational to issue, leaving us with nothing more than a standard intertemporal conflict between present and future selves. The opposing camp maintains that this analysis presupposes an implausible atomism about agency: the rational agent is a temporally extended entity capable of binding her future stages through credible dispositions or commitments, and recognizing this is precisely what allows her to secure the rescue in the first place; to deny it is to render long-term cooperation inexplicable. We all accept the payoff structure, we all accept that Parfit intended the example to illustrate how rationality might require being disposed to act against one's immediate best reasons, and we have all worked through the standard discussions of self-defeating rationality. Nevertheless, after several years neither side has shifted, and the same moves are replayed. What kind of disagreement is this? Are we arguing about the correct interpretation of rationality—moment-relative versus agent-relative—or about whether decision theory should allow for commitment mechanisms that are not reducible to present expected utility? Is there a hidden formal question about the representation of agents in extensive-form games, or are we facing a bedrock divergence in intuitions about whether a future self can genuinely be constrained by a present decision? Given your diagnosis, what would count as progress: a refined formal model of diachronic commitment, an empirical study of how people actually honor promises in one-off interactions, a conceptual distinction that separates two questions we have been running together, or a reconceptualization of the problem itself? Or is the stalemate a sign that the question is ill-posed, and if so, why?
