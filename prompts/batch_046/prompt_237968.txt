Write a deadpan, satirical “Annual Moral Audit Report” of approximately 600 words, styled after the technical language of a corporate accounting firm auditing the ethical books of a fictional democratic administration. The target is the rationalist-technocratic framing of the dirty-hands problem in politics—the view that morally compromising acts, such as deception, coercion, or betrayal of allies, can be rendered as quantifiable line items in a public-interest ledger, with a politician’s ethical credibility treated as a depreciable capital asset that can be leveraged for state purposes. To keep the exercise away from well-worn regurgitation, inject the conceptual devices of an “integrity amortization schedule” and a “moral leverage ratio”: the politician’s remaining trust capital functions as collateral against which dirty-hand transactions are leveraged, with depreciation recognized on a straight-line basis over an electoral term and impairment tested against polling data and media sentiment. The voice must remain entirely bureaucratic and deadpan—no winking, no explicit moral condemnation—so that the absurdity arises solely from the precision with which corruption is rendered in audit jargon. Include mock line items such as goodwill impairment from broken promises, contingent liabilities for unauthorized surveillance, and depreciation of trust capital. By the end, a reader should feel the tension between the audit’s internal coherence and the grotesqueness of treating moral stain as portfolio risk, without the text ever breaking character to announce that tension. Do not cite philosophers, provide bibliographies, quote real texts, or refer to named living persons; the satire should stand on its own conceptual logic and depend only on widely familiar notions of political ethics and basic accounting vocabulary.
