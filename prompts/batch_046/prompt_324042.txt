I'm putting together the final exam for my upper-level political philosophy seminar, and I keep circling back to that Chesterton fence idea—y'know, don't tear down a fence if you don't understand why it's there. But I don't want students to just parrot Burke and Chesterton about tradition being wise. Instead, I want them to grapple with functional opacity: the idea that long-standing procedural rules can solve coordination problems in ways that are invisible to the people who currently maintain them, and that this invisibility creates a special burden of proof for democratic reformers who want to abolish them. The exam should ask them to evaluate whether Chesterton's fence survives if we learn the original builder was wrong about the fence's purpose, or if the fence now serves a completely different function—essentially forcing them to seperate epistemic humility from mere inertia.

Please write one essay question for a 75-minute exam (expect 800-1000 words), then a model answer of about 600 words, and a marking scheme. The scheme should name exactly what seperates an A answer from a B—specific moves like distinguishing functional opacity from habit, engaging Burke's argument from prescription without treating it as blind obedience, and addressing the democratic objection that reformers can't be expected to solve invisible problems. List the must-hit points with marks, and flag the typical mistakes—like conflating Chesterton with a general 'old is good' claim, or assuming Burke rejects all rational reform. Don't include a bibliography or citation request; I want the prompt to work for a model that can't search. And please keep it in the weeds: maybe ask them to apply the argument to a specific institutional case, like abolishing an arbitrary-seeming procedural check in administrative law that no current legislator can justify, so they can't just recite the standard summary of conservative thought.
