I'm taking a philosophy-of-mind course this semester, and next week I'm leading a fifty-minute discussion section for first-year undergraduates on the view, associated with Hofstadter and Sander's Surfaces and Essences, that analogy-making—not logic or statistical pattern-matching—is the fundamental mechanism of cognition. Rather than summarizing the book, I want to structure the session around a specific comparative framing: using the well-documented failure modes of large language models in analogical reasoning as a negative mirror. The goal is for students to see that these systems reproduce superficial similarities with fluency yet systematically miss the deep structural alignments that characterize human analogical thought, and then to derive from that contrast what makes analogy constitutive of cognition rather than decorative.

Plan the explanatory arc. First, lay out the dependency order: which idea must be in place before I can introduce the negative mirror, and which idea must come only after students have seen why surface matching is insufficient. Second, identify the single concrete scenario that should carry the session—it needs to display both a genuine analogical mapping and a superficial statistical resemblance that could mislead—and specify what properties it must possess and what it must avoid (for instance, whether it should involve physical objects, social situations, or abstract structures). Third, map the misconceptions the audience will predictably form in sequence: likely that analogy is merely finding similarities, that comparing language models demonstrates machine cognition, that essence refers to some hidden metaphysical property, and where in the arc each should be headed off. Fourth, give me one end-of-section check question that reveals whether students understand analogy as category construction rather than comparison between pre-existing categories, not just whether they can repeat the vocabulary. Fifth, be explicit about what I should openly refuse to explain at this level—for instance, formal models of analogical mapping, the mathematics of vector spaces in language models, or debates about machine consciousness—and explain how leaving each out protects the conceptual dependency structure. Finally, please separate what is broadly settled in cognitive science and philosophy about analogy's central role from what remains genuinely contested—for example, whether analogical reasoning competes with or subsumes rule-based and statistical mechanisms—and do not present any contested position as the consensus view.
