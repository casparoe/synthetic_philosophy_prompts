I have been reading through several contemporary applications of Stoic ethics—particularly in cognitive-behavioral therapy manuals and popular self-help frameworks that adapt Epictetus’s dichotomy of control—and I keep encountering a specific pattern that seems to sit awkwardly between healthy acceptance and plain emotional suppression. A practitioner is invited to classify what is within their control (present judgments, volitions) and what is not (past events, other people’s actions, external outcomes). The difficulty arises when someone applies this distinction retroactively to a past trauma or a long-standing grievance: they classify not only the original historical event, but also their current memory of it, their present emotional reaction, and any impulse to interpret or narrate it, as belonging entirely to the "not in my control" category. By treating the present act of cognitive engagement with the past as itself an attempt to control the uncontrollable past, they justify avoiding any further examination of their current interpretation, effectively sealing the episode off from revision.

I have seen this move made explicitly in guided journaling exercises and in conversations where Stoic language is blended with trauma-informed rhetoric, but I am unsure whether philosophy or clinical psychology has named this particular failure mode. Is there an established concept—perhaps within Stoic scholarship, in the literature on experiential avoidance, or in the philosophy of mental health—for this retroactive collapse of the present-judgment/past-event boundary? I am not asking for a bare label; I want to know how precisely it differs from standard emotional suppression, from the therapeutic misuse of acceptance, and from the ordinary Stoic exercise of distinguishing present assent from external outcomes. If no standard term captures exactly this configuration, I would welcome a precise carving of its edges, including what it is not, and whether it represents a corruption of the dichotomy itself or a predictable structural consequence of applying it to historical memory.
