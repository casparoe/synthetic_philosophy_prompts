I am drafting the final exam for an upper-level seminar on Buddhist metaphysics and personal identity. I need one 90-minute essay question that students cannot answer by reciting standard expositions of anatta, the five-skandha analysis, or the chariot analogy. To force original conceptual work, the question should center on a constructed tension—call it the divided-stream scenario—in which a single mental continuum, through an extreme but causally continuous transformation that involves no substantial substrate, bifurcates into two non-communicating experiential streams. Each stream retains partial mnemonic and karmic traces from the original, yet nothing underlying connects them. Students must determine whether classical Buddhist accounts of continuity, which rely on impermanent skandhas linked by causal conditions, can accommodate this division without implicitly reintroducing a substantial self, and whether the case instead demands a revision toward a relational or process-based view of personal continuity.

Please provide:
(a) The exam question exactly as it should appear to students;
(b) A model answer that articulates three essential analytical moves—first, a skandha-level decomposition of both post-bifurcation streams showing no shared substance; second, an evaluation of whether causal conditions (such as contiguity and qualitative similarity among mental moments) can preserve karmic accountability without numerical identity; and third, a reasoned judgment on whether the scenario undermines strict momentariness in favor of a more relational account—plus a brief identification of the hidden fallacy of substituting functional unity for a metaphysical self;
(c) A marking scheme totaling 20 points that assigns specific marks to each of the three moves, includes a distinct point for recognizing and avoiding the self-substitution fallacy, and lists, for each component, the one-line typical failure mode that should cost marks.

The prompt must not ask for bibliographies, reading lists, or citations, and the question should be answerable without reference to specific texts by page or passage.
