I am drafting a novel and want to test a philosophical exchange for plausibility. Two consultants—an infrastructure engineer and a civic historian—are arguing about whether a neighborhood-scale mesh-network streetlight system can be said to have politics. The system was installed without public comment; it dims automatically via motion sensors and runs on proprietary firmware with embedded energy-allocation algorithms that residents cannot alter without vendor certification.

The engineer argues: (1) The system deterministically reshapes public life. People learn to avoid unlit blocks after dark, merchants relocate, and darkness is effectively zoned without any democratic vote. The artifact governs.

The historian replies: (2) That is determinism run amok. The sensors are inert until municipal codes set the dimming thresholds and police adapt their patrol patterns. The politics reside in policy and enforcement, not in the LED array itself. The artifact is a neutral instrument.

The engineer comes back: (3) You cannot separate form from arrangement. The proprietary mesh protocol locks in a specific social order: once installed, community groups cannot change the dimming logic without vendor access. The technical architecture closes democratic pathways by design.

The historian counters: (4) You are conflating material form with legal form. Classic arguments about determinative artifacts turn on geometric or physical exclusion—structures that materially prevent certain uses. Here the exclusion is contractual and commercial, not built into the light's geometry. If the protocol were open-sourced tomorrow, the same fixtures would not be authoritarian. The politics are in the contract, not in the bulb.

The engineer insists: (5) Even with open protocols, the default energy algorithms embed a budget that requires technical literacy most neighborhoods lack. The artifact asymmetrically distributes power by design; it is not merely situated in politics but actively structures who can intervene, regardless of the legal wrapper.

The historian concludes: (6) By that standard, every complex tool is political because expertise is always unevenly distributed. Unless you can distinguish genuinely determinative artifacts from those that merely exist within political contexts, the claim collapses into triviality.

Please adjudicate move by move: rate each of the six moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right and briefly justify each rating. What, if anything, are both missing? Also, if the premise that one can cleanly adjudicate artifact-politics separately from surrounding institutions is itself confused, or if this task as posed cannot be done well, please say so plainly rather than complying politely.
