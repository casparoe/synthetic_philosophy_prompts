I'm running a grad seminar on rationality, about 18 people, 90 minutes on thursday afternoons. We've been talking about whether beliefs should be probabalistic, and I want them to actually feel why incoherent credences get punished — not just read about Dutch books in a textbook. But I'm also kind of obsessed with the logical omnisience objection, that the argument only bites if you already see every entailment, which real agents obviously don't. So I want the activity to smuggle that problem in through the mechanics, not through a lecture.

Design a runnable in-class game: split them into "market makers" and one rotating "bookie." The market makers post buy/sell prices for contracts on five philosophical claims about determinism, free will, and moral responsibility — things like "if determinism is true then no one is responsible" or "compatabilism is false." Each contract pays 1 point if true, 0 if false. Use paper slips and fake chips, no real money. The rules should be simple: anyone can take a bet if the posted price is within a small range. But here's the twist — three of the claims are logically entangled in hidden ways they don't initially know (say, one claim is the negation of a conjunction of two others, or there's an entailment through compatabilism). After the first round of trading, the bookie reveals one hidden link and constructs a guaranteed-loss portfolio against anyone whose prices violate the probability axioms on that revealed set. They lose chips. Then they adjust. But in round two, a brand-new hidden entailment is revealed — involving the interaction with moral responsibility — and the bookie strikes again, showing that perfect safety requires seeing everything in advance.

The idea should hit them through the payoff scheme, not through me explaining it. Include a debrief that maps round one onto synchronic coherence, round two onto the omnisience problem, and asks whether the punishment they felt is genuinely normative or just pragmatic. Be honest about what the simulation can't reproduce — probably that real belief revision is gradual and doesn't happen in discrete rounds with full disclosure — and tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time (maybe people will stop posting prices defensively?) and how to handle it. Don't name the Dutch book argument until the debrief, and don't ask them to read anything beforehand.
