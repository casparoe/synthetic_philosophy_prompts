I've been trying to make sense of Hofstadter's suggestion that an "I" can be distributed across brains — that after someone dies, their pattern of selfhood might genuinely persist in the minds of those who loved them. But I keep hitting what feels like a contradiction: if my wife's self really lives on inside me, why don't I ever experience her thoughts, memories, or preferences as intruding on my own first-person perspective? To sharpen the worry, imagine what I call an "echo-host" — a brain that carries the complete structural pattern of another person's self but lacks the recursive loops that would let it generate an independent consciousness. If Hofstadter is right that a self can migrate like software, wouldn't an echo-host feel as if it were being inhabited or possessed by that foreign "I"? So is the mistake in thinking that a distributed self must share a single stream of consciousness, or is Hofstadter actually describing something more like a dormant copy that never executes on the new hardware? Take a definite position: does the view entail any phenomenological merging, and what exactly separates an echo-host from the original person?
