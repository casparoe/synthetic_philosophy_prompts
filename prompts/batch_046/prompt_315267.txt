At a recent philosophy-of-science reading group, two regular participants—whom I will call Alex and Blake—debated Einstein’s stance on free will and moral responsibility in relation to his Spinozist determinism and his idea of a cosmic religious feeling. I listened without taking sides, and they asked me to relay the exchange for outside adjudication.

Here is the back-and-forth as it actually occurred.

Alex opened by arguing that Einstein’s determinism is hard and total: since every mental state is physically embedded in a causally closed universe governed by field equations, there is no ontological room for anyone to have genuinely done otherwise. On this reading, Einstein’s occasional references to human ethical responsibility are at best pragmatic fictions—useful for social coordination but metaphysically hollow, since praising or blaming a person for an action necessitated by prior physical conditions is structurally analogous to praising a planet for its orbit.

Blake replied that this collapses a distinction Einstein actually maintained, namely between causal determination and ethical agency. Blake noted that Einstein consistently treated consciousness and foresight as real features of the world rather than as epiphenomenal shadows. For Einstein, being morally responsible does not require escaping physical law; it requires only that one’s behavior be guided by one’s own rational evaluation of consequences. Blake therefore read Einstein as an implicit compatibilist: determinism governs physical dynamics, while responsibility governs reason-responsive agency.

Alex pushed back that Blake is merely relabeling the causal chain. If every rational evaluation is itself the deterministic output of neural processes, then saying an action flows from one’s reasoning describes the mechanism of determination without adding any normative significance. Without a branch point where the causal chain could genuinely diverge—what libertarians require—Einstein’s ethics reduce to describing how complex deterministic systems feel from the inside.

Blake answered that Alex is importing a libertarian premise Einstein rejected, but more importantly, Blake argued that Alex misses the integrative function of Einstein’s cosmic religion. In Blake’s reading, Einstein’s sense of cosmic harmony implies that ethical striving is not an interruption of deterministic order but an expression of it. Human beings come to understand universal law and align themselves with it rationally; this alignment is what Einstein means by freedom. Moral evaluation is therefore not about counterfactual branching but about whether a person’s world-line manifests rational alignment with cosmic order.
