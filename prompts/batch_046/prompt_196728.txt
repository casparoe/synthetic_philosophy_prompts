Consider the institutional practice now standard in the development of large language models, in which a system's conduct is shaped by aggregating thousands of anonymous, disinterested raters' pairwise judgments of model outputs. These judgments are compressed into a single scalar reward function that serves as the authoritative specification of helpful or harmless behavior, with no individual rater bearing responsibility for any particular outcome and with no expectation that raters engage in sustained reflective deliberation about the cases they evaluate. What philosophical commitments about ethical authority, individual responsibility, and the nature of collective judgment does this practice presuppose? Specifically, does it implicitly adopt a conception of the public as an abstract quantitative entity that acquires normative authority precisely through its anonymity and scale—akin to the leveling force that Kierkegaard identified in the modern press and the crowd—or can it be reconstructed on premises consistent with his insistence that genuine ethical authority requires the passionate, reflective commitment of the single individual, and that the crowd functions as an abstraction that dissolves rather than constitutes responsibility? In addressing this, distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what merely fits it well, and identify any internal tensions—such as whether the mechanism simultaneously depends on individual moral intuition at the point of rating and denies the authority of that intuition at the point of aggregation—while also considering whether this form of mediated judgment represents a genuinely novel structure or an extension of the press-like flattening of individual voices into uniform statistical noise.
