I am a doctoral student working in comparative cultural memory, and I have become preoccupied with a structural pattern that appears in societies where the political order responsible for large-scale violence dissolved or was radically transformed—such as the post-Confederate American South or Republican Turkey after the Ottoman collapse—rather than being defeated, reconstituted, and compelled into democratic succession, as was the case with West Germany. In these settings, the past is not primarily managed through suppression or silence of the kind often associated with postwar Japan; instead, the society generates robust commemorative institutions—museums with standardized pedagogical narratives, school curricula, annual public rituals, and monument landscapes—that closely mirror the formal architecture of Vergangenheitsbewältigung. Yet the moral vector is inverted. The same practices of archival display, spatial memorialization, and civic pedagogy are deployed not to integrate perpetrator guilt and historical responsibility, but to institutionalize counter-narratives of collective victimhood, heroic defense, or irrecoverable cultural loss. The phenomenon is not mere denial, because denial can be passive or discursive; this is an active, structurally parallel mnemonic inversion. Nor is it exactly competitive victimhood, since the latter typically describes inter-group claims over suffering, whereas here a single collective appropriates the institutional form of perpetrator-memory culture precisely to avoid occupying the perpetrator position. I have seen this echoed in heritage museums that replicate the exhibit logic of Holocaust education centers but commemorate “Lost Cause” valor, and in commemorative rituals that borrow the solemn form of genocide remembrance to frame national victimization. Is there an established concept in transitional justice or memory studies for this specific institutional isomorphism coupled with moral inversion? If the literature addresses it only as a subspecies of denial, state-sponsored mythmaking, or generic memory politics, I would ask for the closest established terms, the precise ways in which my description diverges from each, and—should no adequate term exist—a proposed name together with boundary conditions that distinguish it from both straightforward denial and from legitimate commemorative practices by genuinely victimized minorities.
