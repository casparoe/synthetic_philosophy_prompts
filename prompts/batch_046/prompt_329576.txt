I'm preparing a podcast interview with a philosopher who defends a modified coherence argument for expected utility maximization. Her central claim is that standard Dutch Book arguments for transitivity, completeness, and independence only hold for idealized agents with perfect recall and static preference sets. For bounded, real-world agents, she proposes adding a "revisability-cost axiom": an agent's preference ordering at time t must be constrained not only by internal consistency but by a penalized divergence measure from her ordering at t-1, where the penalty scales with the cognitive cost of revision. She argues this explains why rational agents resist certain money-pump sequences without invoking external norms, and that the penalty parameter should be endogenous to the information structure—cheap when new evidence genuinely simplifies the decision problem, expensive when it merely introduces noise.

Please draft eight to ten interview questions that go well beyond asking her to restate the view. I want at least one question on how the revisability-cost axiom interacts with the independence axiom when an agent faces a compound lottery whose resolution reveals information that makes preference revision cheap; include a follow-up keyed to her likely reply that the cost function is endogenous to the information structure. Another should press whether her framework collapses coherence into a form of conservatism about prior preferences—anticipate her answer that conservatism is rational only when the divergence penalty tracks genuine learning costs, and ask how she distinguishes learning costs from mere inertia. A third should ask what happens to the normative force of the argument if the penalty parameter is agent-relative: does coherence become subjective in a way that undermines the Dutch Book's universal bite? Follow up assuming she appeals to a shared baseline of cognitive architecture. Finally, include a question about whether her view can accommodate well-documented preference reversals—such as those in addiction or framing effects—without either excusing them as rational (when revision is cheap) or pathologizing them (when revision is expensive). Keep every question specific to the mechanics of her position, avoid generic biographical prompts, and do not ask for citations, bibliographies, or reading lists.
