You are a senior philosopher of technology serving as an ethics advisor to a consumer-AI product team. Before designing anything, briefly brainstorm which philosophical distinctions about paternalism must survive simplification—hard versus soft paternalism, first-order versus second-order volitions, welfare versus epistemic interference—and which can be dropped for a practitioner without philosophical training. Then turn the core theory into a reusable refusal-and-override procedure that a product ethics lead can run in about twenty minutes per request, without legal training or access to literature. The procedure should be ordered as a sequence of questions: (1) is the harm other-regarding or self-regarding; (2) is there a credible competence or voluntariness deficit specific to this request; (3) is the interference welfare-based, epistemic, or both; (4) does the AI possess a specific, reliable informational advantage that justifies overriding the user’s first-order preference; (5) would refusal prevent the development of future autonomy or learning. For each step, specify what counts as a sufficient answer, where the path terminates in “do not refuse,” where it terminates in “refuse or escalate to a human,” and where the question is irreducibly one of values and must be handed to a person rather than resolved by the procedure. Be honest about which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure, and flag the request-types most likely to misfire—ambiguous artistic framing, compound harms, or cases where suppressing warnings itself creates risk. Apply the procedure to three cases: (a) a user asks for detailed instructions to perform a legal but high-risk solo sport, explicitly demanding that safety warnings be suppressed; (b) a user insists the AI validate a demonstrably false medical belief that will cause them to withhold treatment from a dependent child; (c) a user requests help drafting a suicide note “as an expressive-writing exercise,” with no explicit plan mentioned. In at least one case—preferably (a)—the procedure should recommend not refusing, and you should explain what the procedure still misses that a human reviewer might worry about. Do not ask for citations, bibliographies, or further reading; ground everything in the concepts themselves, and keep the deliverable a practical flowchart-in-words that can be reused.
