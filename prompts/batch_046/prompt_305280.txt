As a journalist covering science and ideas, I have been preparing a long-form piece on how experimental philosophy has unsettled traditional armchair analysis in philosophy of mind. One result that keeps arising in my reporting is the asymmetry documented by Joshua Knobe—commonly called the Knobe effect—where ordinary people are substantially more willing to describe an unwanted side effect of a corporate decision as intentional when it damages the environment, yet withhold that same attribution when the side effect is beneficial, despite holding constant the agent's beliefs, desires, and causal involvement. The consistency of this pattern across variations suggests to me that we are not looking at a simple bias layered on top of an independent mental-state tracking mechanism, but rather at evidence that the concept of intentional action has no autonomous psychological reality whatsoever.

If that stronger reading is correct, then whenever we invoke beliefs and desires to explain behavior—whether in criminal law's distinction between intended and accidental harm, or in emerging frameworks for artificial-intelligence ethics—we are not describing mental causes but merely laundering normative judgments through a pseudo-descriptive vocabulary. Given this, should we conclude that the Knobe effect effectively dissolves folk psychology into moral evaluation, leaving cognitive science with nothing distinctive to study about intentionality? Or is there a more restrained interpretation that preserves some independent explanatory role for mental-state attribution, allowing us to maintain practical distinctions between intended and unintended consequences without falling into conceptual fiction?
