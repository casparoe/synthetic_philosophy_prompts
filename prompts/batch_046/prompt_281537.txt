Design a 75-minute final-exam essay question for an upper-level philosophy of language seminar on Davidson’s radical interpretation. The question must not be answerable by summarizing the principle of charity or the thesis of translation indeterminacy alone. Instead, frame it around this constructed case: an interpreter attempts radical interpretation of a speaker who (i) has a systematically inverted color spectrum relative to the interpreter, (ii) employs a private, non-observable vocabulary for “moral residue” that the interpreter can access only through the speaker’s emotional vocalizations, and (iii) uses a non-monotonic reasoning pattern in practical inference that violates local consistency while preserving global coherence. The exam prompt should ask students whether Davidson’s framework can accommodate such a speaker without collapsing into extreme holism or an implausible mentalistic access, and specifically whether charity should prioritize doxastic consistency over perceptual accuracy, or vice versa, given this triadic tension. Provide the exam question itself (with framing sufficient for students), a model answer of approximately 700 words, and a marking scheme out of 20 that distinguishes an A answer from a B answer by naming the specific philosophical moves each must contain—for example: distinguishing global rationality constraints from local belief ascription; assessing whether Quinean indeterminacy is exacerbated or dissolved by the emotional-access channel; identifying the precise conflict between perceptual charity and doxastic charity; and evaluating whether non-monotonic reasoning undermines the constitutive role of logical consistency in interpretation. For each required move, include a one-line note on how candidates typically fail it. Do not ask for bibliographies, citations, or reading lists.
