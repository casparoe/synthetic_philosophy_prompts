Hey — I'm a journalist who writes about science and ideas, and I've been chewing on this old Friedman twist about unrealistic assumptions in models. You know the move: a model can be totally unrealistic and still be good science if its predictions hold up. I've got gut reactions to a bunch of cases and I want to know if there's actually one principle behind them, or if I'm just being inconsistent.

Here are my verdicts:

1. Newton treating planets as point masses with zero volume. Totally fine. The unrealism doesn't leak into orbital predictions.

2. The economics model where firms are perfect profit-maximizers with infinite computing power. Okay for comparative statics, but not okay if you use it to predict actual entry by small businesses. Same unrealistic assumption, different verdict — depends on what you're predicting.

3. Climate models that average rainfall over 100km grid cells, smoothing away mountains and coastlines, then use that to forecast local frost in a specific valley. Not okay. The smoothing is exactly where the prediction lives.

4. McCulloch-Pitts neurons — binary on/off — used to study logical computation in the brain. Fine. The brain isn't digital, but for logic the unrealism is harmless.

5. The SIR model with homogenous mixing. I think it's okay for predicting whether an outbreak crosses the R>1 threshold in a big city. But using the same model, same unrealistic mixing, to predict the cluster size at a specific wedding? That feels wrong. So my verdict flips with the question being asked.

6. There's this idea I picked up at a workshop — call it "performative leakage" — where a model assumes people don't react to being modeled, and that very assumption changes behavior. Like a traffic algorithm that assumes drivers don't reroute, so they do, and the prediction collapses. I think that's not okay, even if the math is elegant.

7. A niche systems-biology case: a model includes a "ghost parameter" for cellular intent — no physical referent, just keeps the differental equations tractable. It doesn't change quantitative predictions, but it makes biologists interpret mechanisms as if cells have goals. I think that's okay mathematically but dangerous interpretively — split verdict.

So is there one principle? Maybe it's about whether the unrealistic part is "isolated" from the prediction target? Or maybe it's about whether the modeled system stays independent of the model itself?

What would your principle say about using an infinite-population evolutionary game theory model to study cooperation in a real community of about 200 people?

Also be honest: which of my verdicts — especially the split ones in case 2, 5, and 7 — would you have to drop to keep the principle simple? I don't need citations, just your reasoning.
