I am preparing a 90-minute graduate seminar in philosophy of psychiatry (twelve students) on Ian Hacking’s looping effects, and I want a participatory simulation that makes the loop tangible before anyone names it. Design an in-class activity in which students experience how a psychiatric classification reshapes the very behaviors it measures, and how clinicians’ awareness of that reshaping feeds back into the stability of the category.

Please include the following. First, invent a stylized but clinically plausible diagnostic category for the exercise—analogous to a social-cognitive withdrawal disorder but not a real DSM entry—together with a brief three-criterion rubric that clinicians will apply. Second, divide or rotate students between two roles: “subjects,” who receive ambiguous behavioral profiles and are then assigned a classification, and “clinicians,” who apply the rubric to observed behavior in a structured task. Specify exactly how the assignment works, whether roles rotate, and how many rounds occur.

Third, embed a reflexive mechanism in the rules: the assigned label must alter the subject’s available actions or incentives in the subsequent observation task—for instance, through a disclosure protocol or modified interaction payoff—so that the behavioral change is produced by the rules themselves, not explained as “this is the loop.” Fourth, structure the timeline so that classification happens, then behavior is observed under the altered conditions, then clinicians re-apply the rubric with full knowledge that the subjects know their labels. Fifth, provide a debrief that maps each phase onto Hacking’s framework (initial categorization, induced behavioral change, revised clinical observation, and the instability of the kind), and be explicit about which feature of looping effects—such as long-term institutional identity formation or historical path dependence—the simulation cannot reproduce and must be supplied verbally.

Sixth, state what will predictably fail on the first run (for example, moral discomfort with labeling peers, failure to notice the behavioral shift, or conflation of role-play with real diagnosis) and how to correct it. Seventh, ensure no lecture precedes the activity and that the term “looping effects” is withheld until the debrief; the idea should emerge from the incentives and rules. The activity must be runnable with only paper, cards, and a whiteboard, require no digital tools, and avoid any request for bibliographies, citations, or reading lists. Finally, inject one somewhat niche conceptual twist—such as a diagnostic criterion that references the subject’s awareness of being observed for the condition—so that the exercise is not reducible to standard textbook descriptions of self-fulfilling prophecy.
