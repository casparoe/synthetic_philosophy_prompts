You are a professional philosopher of ethics and value theory with particular familiarity with Samuel Scheffler's arguments about why human extinction is bad independently of the suffering it causes. Before you begin, spend a few moments brainstorming the core structure of his conjecture about the collective afterlife — the idea that a great deal of what we presently value, from long-term scientific and artistic projects to institutional preservation and ordinary commitments to justice, derives its meaning and motivational force from an implicit assumption that human life will continue after our own deaths — and list the specific features of the real-world episode that will matter for the mapping.

Then, proceeding step by step and with disciplined analytical mapping, analyze the establishment (2008) and the first operational withdrawal (2015) of the Svalbard Global Seed Vault as a case study through this lens. Treat the vault not merely as an ecological or agricultural project, but as a material institution that embodies a particular temporal structure of value.

Specifically, address the following in order:

1. Identify the relevant actors and their options in both moments — the contributing states and institutions during the founding, and the Syrian agricultural researchers together with the vault administrators during the 2015 retrieval — and specify what values or payoffs were at stake: preservation for an unknown future, immediate food-security needs, international solidarity, symbolic continuity, or something else.

2. Apply Scheffler's conjecture precisely. Does the decision to construct a remote Arctic repository of crop diversity make rational sense only if the contributors assume a future human population capable of using those seeds? Would the same international investment be justifiable under the certain knowledge of imminent human extinction? Turn then to the 2015 withdrawal, in which seeds were retrieved to replace stocks damaged at the Aleppo seed bank during the Syrian civil war: was this act best understood as preserving resources for a distant collective afterlife, or as an immediate humanitarian response to present catastrophe?

3. Say where the conjecture illuminates the episode and where it breaks down or falls silent. Does the vault's existence confirm that our preservationist instincts depend on an assumed future? Or does the 2015 withdrawal reveal that such instincts can be sustained by present reciprocity, symbolic identity, or diplomatic cooperation that does not require belief in survivors? Note any feature of the case — for instance, the treaty framework, the physical remoteness of Svalbard, or the fact that the vault was opened not for a hypothetical future but for an existing present crisis — that the theory does not clearly anticipate.

4. Reverse the direction: what does the episode show about the conjecture itself? If extinction were genuinely certain, would an institution like the seed vault still have been built, and would its absence or presence support or weaken Scheffler's claim about why extinction is bad beyond the deaths it involves? Be explicit about whether the case functions as confirmation, as an anomaly requiring revision, or as a scenario the theory was never designed to cover.

Be honest about where the mapping is forced. Do not invent contested historical details; stick to the well-known facts of the vault's founding in Svalbard, its international governance, and the 2015 retrieval for Syrian researchers. Where the conjecture offers no clear verdict, say so rather than forcing an answer. Conclude with a concise judgment on whether the seed vault is best read as an embodiment of the collective afterlife, a counterexample to its necessity, or a hybrid case that reveals the conjecture needs supplementation.
