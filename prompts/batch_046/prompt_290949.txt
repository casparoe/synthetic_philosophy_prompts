I've been arguing with a friend about what variety of free will is actually worth wanting—not just metaphysically real, but the kind you'd defend if someone said "actually humans are just algorithms." My gut reactions on cases are all over the place, and I want to see if there's a hidden principle behind them. Someone picking chocolate over vanilla: yes, they've got it, determinism be damned. Someone signing a false confession because they're being tortured: clearly no. The addict who quits smoking through brutal willpower: I'd say yes, they have the valuable kind, even if their dopamine system made failure likely. But then there's a hypothetical AI that computes the globally best action every time with zero internal deliberation—no branching, no randomness—do I care if it has free will? I think not, but I'm not sure if my reason is that it lacks alternative possibilities, or that it lacks some kind of narrative ownership over the choice.

There's also the OCD sufferer who resists washing their hands versus a casual person who washes without thinking—the first feels freer, even though the physical outcome is identical, so it can't just be about absence of external constraint. And the person brainwashed from childhood who now genuinely endorses the ideology—do they have free will worth wanting in their political choices, or does the origin story poison it? I keep oscillating between tracking moral responsibility, psychological health, and this vague idea of "counterfactual stability"—would the choice hold up if they reflected harder? Is there one principle that sorts all these, maybe centered on reflective endorsement under counterfactual reasoning? Or do I need separate accounts for moral, psychological, and political freedom? And whatever the principle is, what should it say about the case I'm stuck on: a person with perfect predictive knowledge of their own future actions, who still reports feeling like they're genuinely deciding in the moment?
