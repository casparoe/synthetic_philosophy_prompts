Consider the following reconstruction of a study partner's argument about the Nyāya fourfold scheme of pramāṇa. (1) Nyāya defines a pramāṇa as a cognition that is yathārtha (correspondent to its object) and abādhita (not contradicted by a stronger cognition). (2) Śabda (testimony) is valid only if the speaker is āpta—one whose prior utterances have consistently yielded yathārtha results. (3) That consistency is itself established either by direct observation of past outcomes (pratyakṣa) or by inferential generalization about the speaker's reliability (anumāna); hence śabda never generates knowledge independently but only transmits knowledge originally grounded in perception or inference. (4) Upamāna (comparison) operates only when one already perceives the upamāna (the object of comparison) and grasps the sādṛśya (similarity relation), so it too reduces to prior pratyakṣa. (5) Therefore only pratyakṣa and anumāna are genuinely independent pramāṇas; śabda and upamāna are derivative instruments. (6) But Nyāya-sūtra lists all four as independent, each with a distinct viṣaya (domain): śabda covers supersensory realities such as dharma and apavarga, while upamāna covers relations inaccessible to the other three. (7) If they are truly independent, they must be able to yield knowledge without prior grounding in pratyakṣa or anumāna—yet (3) and (4) show they always require such grounding. (8) So either the taxonomy is redundant, or the independence condition is false and all knowledge ultimately collapses into perception and inference. (9) If the collapse is complete, then metaphysical entities—ātman, dharma, apavarga, and the very conditions of mokṣa—have no independent pramāṇa, since none are perceptible in the ordinary sense and inference about them depends on premises that themselves require testimonial or analogical support. (10) Hence the argument proves too much: it defends the parsimony of the scheme only by destroying the metaphysical commitments Nyāya was built to secure, including the possibility of yogaja pratyakṣa (yogic perception) being classified as a non-derivative source. I want to preserve both the independence of śabda and the legitimacy of upamāna, but I am willing to concede that one of them is derivative if that saves the metaphysical framework. Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? In particular, does the flaw lie in treating the conditions for applying a pramāṇa as conditions for its epistemic ground, or is there a scope shift between "independent source of cognition" and "independent domain of cognition"? Lay out the diagnosis precisely, and price the alternative repairs.
