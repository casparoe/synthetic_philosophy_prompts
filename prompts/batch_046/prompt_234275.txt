As a novelist drafting an essay on moral character for a literary quarterly, I have sketched an argument that maximal moral goodness—interpreted not as optimal welfare production or perfect Kantian duty, but as what I call complete moral luminosity, the total absence of unacknowledged ambivalence in every ethical choice—would yield a personality so narratively flat that it could not sustain the interior conflict necessary for either fiction or fully human selfhood. I distinguish this third interpretation from standard utilitarian and deontological accounts, then propose that if such luminosity were achievable it would eliminate the hidden tensions that generate moral development. Before I revise the draft, I would welcome precise comments on three matters: first, whether my distinction genuinely isolates a separate conception of moral excellence or merely renames an existing virtue-theoretic ideal of transparency; second, whether inferring moral undesirability from narrative flatness commits a category error that confuses aesthetic taste with ethical evaluation; and third, whether there is any coherent way to defend the desirability of moral sainthood that avoids both the utilitarian demand for constant self-sacrifice and this luminosity that erases psychological depth. I am not seeking citations or reading lists, but rather an assessment of whether the conceptual architecture holds together and whether the injection of this luminosity framework adds anything philosophically substantive rather than merely decorative.
