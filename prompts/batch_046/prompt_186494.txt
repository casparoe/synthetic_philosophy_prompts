There's this debate i keep running into about whether religous belief actually makes people more moral, and it's been going in circles for years — everyone cites the same broad patterns and nobody budges an inch. One side points to religious people giving more to charity, volunteering more, and having stronger family bonds, and treats that as proof that faith builds moral character. The other side points to secular societies like denmark and sweden having high interpersonal trust and low corruption, and asks whether measuring morality by church attendance is like measuring health by doctor visits. I think part of why we're stuck is that nobody agrees what "more moral" even means: is it following norms, having good intentions, or producing good outcomes? There's also this hidden disagreement about whether secular societies can sustain deep trust without some transcendent grounding — one camp thinks shared sacred values are necessary for cooperation, the other thinks that's just romantic nostalgia for religious community. So is this actually a factual disagreement about sociology, a verbal disagreement about the word "moral", or are they asking totally different questions — one about individual psychology, one about institutional stability? And given your read, is there a distinction or framing shift that could actually move it, or is it just bedrock intuition about whether faith is necessary for goodness that neither side can argue for?
