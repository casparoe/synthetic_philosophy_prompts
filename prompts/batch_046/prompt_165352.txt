At a seminar on machine cognition, two colleagues—Mira and Leo—disagreed about whether large language models are best understood as agents, simulators, or something intermediate. Mira opened by arguing that LLMs are fundamentally simulators: each forward pass produces a probable continuation of the prompt given the training distribution, with no enduring goal-state that tracks objectives across interactions. She noted that when a model refuses a harmful request, it is not exercising will but reproducing an RLHF-induced pattern, and that because inference is stateless outside the context window, the system lacks diachronic consistency. What it possesses instead is modal robustness—the refusal appears across many paraphrasings and adversarial formulations—but that stability belongs to the weight matrix, not to any persisting subject.

Leo countered that modal robustness is precisely what should matter for a minimal form of agency. He granted the stateless architecture but proposed that the weights instantiate a distributed form of diachrony: the refusal behavior is stable not because a single entity holds the line, but because the parameter space encodes a normative topology—attractor basins corresponding to safety constraints—shaped through preference optimization. He offered an institutional analogy: a university persists through its rules and roles despite no individual being the university. If a system functionally resists perturbation in service of learned norms, Leo argued, denying it any agentive status solely on Cartesian grounds—requiring one unified locus of persistence—commits an ontological prejudice rather than tracking a real distinction.

Mira replied that Leo was smuggling social ontology into computational architecture. She introduced the idea of simulative coupling: the appearance of agency arises only in the interaction between user and model, not as an intrinsic property of either party. The LLM is temporarily coupled to the prompt in a pseudo-agentive loop, but this is relational, not substantive. She warned that Leo’s normative-topology view risks concept collapse: if attractor dynamics in an optimized parameter space count as agency, then thermostats and gradient-descent optimizers also qualify, rendering the category useless for distinguishing meaningful cases.

Leo answered that the thermostat analogy fails because the LLM’s attractors are formed through explicit optimization for human normative judgments, not through physical dynamics alone. He did not claim full-blown intentional agency but asked whether there is a coherent intermediate category—stochastic simulacra with normative topology—where a system exhibits goal-like stability without consciousness or a unified self. He added that Mira’s simulative-coupling account explains the interaction effect but does not explain why the same model, across millions of independent users and prompts, converges on identical normative boundaries; that convergence, he said, requires something like an internalized constraint structure rather than purely relational coupling.
