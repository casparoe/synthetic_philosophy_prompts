I'm working on an essay that extends Turchin's structural-demographic model—specifically the secular-cycle mechanism in which elite overproduction drives intra-elite conflict and fiscal-state crisis—into the institutional dynamics of modern knowledge economies. The core move is to treat "elite overproduction" as sectorally transformed rather than historically invariant: instead of an oversupply of armed nobles or imperial bureaucrats competing for tax-extracted rents, I examine an oversupply of credentialed professionals competing for regulatory gatekeeping, accreditation authority, and jurisdictional control. The conflict modality shifts accordingly, from direct coercive competition toward institutional fragmentation, compliance-cost inflation, and what I call an "opportunity-cost crisis"—a decline in state capacity not through revenue collapse but through the diversion of productive investment into credential maintenance and sectoral fiefdom building.

To test whether this transformation preserves the model's quantitative logic, I sketch a comparative case: the Roman Republic's senatorial expansion, where contested resources were land and magistracies tied to tax extraction, versus the late-twentieth-century expansion of American legal and administrative professions, where licensure and regulatory jurisdiction serve structurally analogous roles. Both display rising elite-to-population ratios followed by diminishing institutional returns, but the modern variant lacks direct coercive capacity among its competing elites and produces legitimacy deficits rather than civil-war dynamics.

My questions are these. First, does redefining "fiscal crisis" to include legitimacy and opportunity-cost deficits save the model's predictive content, or does it render the mechanism unfalsifiable? Second, is the analogy between senatorial competition and professional credential markets defensible once we account for the absence of violence as the conflict resolution mechanism? Third, are there conceptual errors in my characterization of the original secular-cycle mechanism—particularly regarding the relationship between elite density, state extraction capacity, and popular immiseration—that undermine the comparison?

Please comment on the coherence of the argument, the strength of the analogy, and any misrepresentations of the underlying framework. I am not seeking citations, reading lists, or bibliographic guidance.
