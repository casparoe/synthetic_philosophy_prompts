Design a decision tree that sorts philosophical positions on rational choice between genuinely incommensurable values—values that share no common metric and neither dominates the other. The root question should ask whether incommensurability entails that the options are incomparable (no rational choice is possible) or merely that comparison requires a different form of practical reasoning. From there, branch on three further issues: whether hard choices leave a rational residue (regret that persists even after a justified decision), whether selecting one value partially undermines the other through value leakage, and whether the agent's own act of commitment can generate a reason that resolves the tie. Each leaf should be a named or unnamed position—include, for example, a view that accepts rational resolution via commitment, a view that treats the choice as arbitrary but permissible, a view that denies any resolution exists, and a view that appeals to second-order values to break the tie. At each branch node, phrase the diagnostic question as a concrete scenario: e.g., a researcher who must choose between a career path that maximizes intellectual discovery and one that maximizes direct humanitarian impact, with no shared scale of success. Note any combinations of answers that appear unoccupied and briefly diagnose why (incoherent, unexplored, or unattractive). Flag pairs of leaves that the tree cannot distinguish. Keep the instrument to no more than six branch nodes, direct it to a graduate philosophy audience, and do not include requests for bibliographies, reading lists, or specific citations.
