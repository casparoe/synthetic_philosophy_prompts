I am revising the discussion notes for a graduate seminar I teach on metaphysics and theology in the Islamic Golden Age, and I have sketched a brief interpretive argument about al-Ghazālī’s occasionalism that I intend to present before asking students to respond. The standard reading, I think, treats his denial of necessary causation in Tahāfut al-Falāsifa as largely defensive: a way to secure conceptual space for miracles by insisting that God alone is the true cause and that apparent natural causes are merely occasions. In my draft, however, I push the interpretation in a more epistemological direction. I suggest that al-Ghazālī’s distinction between what occurs by custom (ʿāda) and what occurs by intrinsic necessity (ḍarūrī) does not only relocate causal efficacy to divine volition; it also raises a largely ignored problem about modal knowledge. If our belief that fire burns cotton rests solely on repeated conjunction rather than on any genuinely grasped necessary connection, then our judgments about what must happen, what could not happen, and what is merely contingent are sustained by a kind of epistemic illusion maintained only through divine habit. I then ask whether this makes al-Ghazālī closer to a radical skeptic about natural knowledge than is usually acknowledged, and whether occasionalism can survive as a live metaphysical option if it is paired with this stronger claim about the limits of our modal access.

Would you comment on these notes and on the interpretive thesis itself? Specifically, I want to know whether this reading is textually defensible or whether it improperly imports modern concerns about modal epistemology into a theological context where they do not belong. I am also interested in whether comparing al-Ghazālī to Hume’s regularity theory is genuinely illuminating, or whether it obscures the specifically Ashʿarī theological commitments that motivate the position. If you see internal tensions—for instance, between denying that we know necessary connections in nature while still allowing that we know necessary truths in logic or mathematics—I would appreciate your pointing them out. I am looking for conceptual criticism and suggestions for sharpening the framing, not for bibliographic recommendations, and I am particularly keen on ways to preserve the theological specificity of the original view while exploring its epistemological implications.
