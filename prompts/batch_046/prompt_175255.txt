A fellow doctoral student in philosophy of mind recently argued for epiphenomenalism by comparing phenomenal consciousness to the waste heat radiated by a combustion engine: it is a real, measurable byproduct of neural machinery, yet it exerts no causal influence back on the pistons or the control loop, just as qualia are said to exert no influence on behavior. She extends the analogy to phenomenal judgment, claiming that a report like "I am in pain" is comparable to a thermal-camera image that registers the engine's heat without the heat ever affecting the camera's sensor mechanism, which she takes to show that the accuracy of first-person phenomenal reports is unexplained and therefore paradoxical on an epiphenomenalist view. I want a precise audit of this comparison. Respond in exactly five numbered items, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion: (1) the explicit correspondence table, mapping combustion engine, waste heat, thermal-camera sensor, and external engineer onto brain processes, phenomenal properties, judgment mechanisms, and third-person observers; (2) the points where the heat-engine mapping breaks down, particularly regarding whether the system that generates the byproduct can access or represent it; (3) any normative or causal assumptions the analogy quietly imports, such as whether a judgment's reliability requires direct causal interaction with its target; (4) an assessment of whether the paradox of phenomenal judgment survives once those disanalogies and hidden premises are exposed; and (5) a brief suggestion of a more apt analogy, if one exists, for the relationship between non-causal phenomenal properties and judgments about them.
