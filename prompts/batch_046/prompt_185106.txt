I am a retired physicist who, over the past two years, have begun studying philosophy of science and philosophy of mind with the same stubbornness I once reserved for experimental design. Next month I will deliver the opening remarks at a retirement symposium honoring a colleague who spent three decades developing predictive neural-network models of visual perception. The room will be split: on one side, neuroscientists, engineers, and philosophers of mind who know her technical work intimately; on the other, family members, university administrators, and scholars from the humanities who do not. The occasion demands both warmth and intellectual honesty.

I want the remarks to be structurally governed by John von Neumann’s argument—developed in his late lectures on the brain and computation—that neural processing operates through analog, probabilistic, and self-referential architectures that are not reducible to digital logic without losing something essential to how the brain functions. I do not want a conventional celebration of “digitizing the mind.” Rather, the philosophical point should shape precisely what I can and cannot say: I should be able to praise the predictive power and clinical utility of her models without endorsing the ontological claim that the brain is “running software,” and I should be able to acknowledge the structural limits of digital approximation without diminishing her career.

To do this, please construct a five-minute address that weaves in a conceptual framing I have been developing in my recent reading—call it “computational humility.” By this I mean the view that predictive equivalence between a formal model and a biological system does not entail ontological identity; a model can be instrumentally indispensable while remaining metaphysically silent about what the system essentially is. Use this distinction to frame her legacy: she built instruments of extraordinary predictive reach, but she never confused the map for the territory. The speech must avoid jargon—no backpropagation, no synaptic-weight notation—yet remain precise enough that the specialists in the room recognize I am not advancing a naive anti-computationist position.

Please do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or requests for citation. Do not quote von Neumann verbatim unless you can reproduce the passage from a verified source; paraphrase is preferable. The address should comfort and honor a room that includes people who loved her work for very different reasons, and it should end with a single, clear statement about what it means to understand a brain well without claiming to have captured it entirely.
