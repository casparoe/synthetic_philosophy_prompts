Right, I've just retired from a long career in experimental physics and I've fallen down a philosophy hole — specifically this chap Galen Strawson and his Basic Argument against moral responsibility. I've been trying to apply it to what I used to call "owning" a result, and I've built this chain that feels like it proves far too much. Here it is:

(1) If I'm genuinely morally responsible for publishing a scientific result, then I must be genuinely morally responsible for the mental processes — the reasoning, the judgement — that led me to it.
(2) If I'm responsible for those mental states, I must be responsible for the prior charcter and training that shaped them, and so on backwards.
(3) That regress ends in genes and upbringing, for which I'm clearly not responsible.
(4) So I'm never genuinely morally responsible for any mental process behind my work.
(5) But if I'm not responsible for those processes, they aren't "mine" in any robust sense; they just happen inside me, like digestion or a sneeze.
(6) Therefore there's no meaningful diference between me, actively arguing and revising, and an automated data-processor that happens to spit out the same equation.
(7) But that can't be right — I spent thirty years doubting, checking, arguing with colleagues.
(8) So one of the premises must be wrong.

The bit that worries me is the jump from (4) to (5). Or maybe there's an equivocation in "responsible" between (1) and (3)? I want to keep (1) if I can, because it feels right that you can't take credit for something you don't control. But if I hold onto (1), what exactly am I paying? Does it mean my whole career was just luck with a fancy hat on?

Tell me which step is actually the weak link, why it looked fine, and what the standard positions — maybe the compatibilist response, maybe Strawson's own reply — each have to pay to reject it. Don't need a bibliography; just talk me through the damage.
