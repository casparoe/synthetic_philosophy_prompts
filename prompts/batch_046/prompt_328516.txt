I've been trying to formalise an argument about transformative experience that seems to prove far too much, and I'd like help locating the exact misstep. The argument runs as follows.

(1) On Paul's account, an experience is epistemically transformative if it yields phenomenal knowledge unavailable beforehand, and personally transformative if it restructures your core preferences.
(2) Where both apply, your pre-choice self cannot assign reliable utilities or rank alternatives, since the evaluator who would experience the outcome is not the same agent as the one making the choice.
(3) But this condition is not restricted to dramatic cases like parenthood or gender transition; it also holds for apparently minor choices — accepting a research post that reshapes your social world, taking a psychedelic that alters your ethical intuitions, or committing to a cause that rewrites what you find salient.
(4) Therefore, for almost any non-trivial life decision, rational preference-ordering is impossible; all one can do is leap.
(5) If so, then multi-year planning, career coherence, and even stable moral identity are irrational; the only consistent stance is perpetual openness to being rewritten.
(6) That conclusion strikes me as intolerable — I want to preserve space for deliberate, long-range practical reasoning — yet I am reluctant to deny that major transformations genuinely change what one values. Which premise should I reject? Is the error in extending Paul's framework from "large" to "small" transformations, or in assuming that rational choice requires a stable evaluator across the boundary?

Please say how confident you are in your diagnosis and single out the point you are least sure of.
