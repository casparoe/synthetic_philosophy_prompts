I run a 90-minute graduate seminar on cultural heritage ethics with about 12 students. Design a structured role-play activity that makes participants feel the conceptual force of what I will call reversal-justice versus relation-justice: the idea that returning an object can aim either to undo a specific wrongful transfer or to establish a new custodial bond that did not exist before. Use the Benin Bronzes and Parthenon Marbles only as shared background references, not as cases to resolve. The scenario should center on a hypothetical artifact with a documented violent seizure in 1897 and a documented good-faith purchase in 1965, with students assigned to three roles—chain-of-custody owner, world-steward trustee, and relational-custodian—who must jointly decide its fate. Build the rules, hidden-incentive role cards, and a collective decision protocol with small real stakes (e.g., points that determine a shared reward) so that the group discovers for itself that undoing the 1897 event does not settle the 1965 claim, and that framing the museum as trustee rather than owner transforms what "return" means. Include a timeline for the 90 minutes, a debrief that maps each negotiation phase onto reversal-justice and relation-justice, an honest account of what the activity cannot reproduce—specifically, the actual plural legal claims of Nigerian and Greek state parties—so I can supply that in discussion, and a prediction of what will go wrong the first time I run it with instructions for handling it. No lecture or naming of the philosophical positions should occur until after the role-play concludes.
