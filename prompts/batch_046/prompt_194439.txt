I've been trying to understand consciousness by comparing two ideas I came across. One distinguishes access consciousness—being able to talk about or reason with information—from phenomenal consciousness, the raw subjective feel that might exist even when you cannot report it. The other is global workspace theory, which holds that you are conscious of something precisely when it is broadcast widely across brain networks so that multiple systems can use it together. To test how they diverge, I imagined a case I call split-broadcast: a person feels a sharp pain in the left hand, automatically withdraws it, remembers the event, and protects the hand afterward, yet whenever they try to state that they are in pain, the signal reaches speech and belief-forming systems too late or not at all, so they sincerely deny feeling anything and never form the belief that their hand hurts. Comparing these two frameworks, does global workspace theory have to classify this person as not conscious of the pain, since full broadcasting to verbal report never occurs? Or can it allow that broadcasting is partial—reaching action and memory modules but not language—while still counting as conscious? And how does the access-phenomenal distinction treat the same situation? Which framework gives the more coherent account of split-broadcast, and does either framework force us to deny that there is a subjective feel present in that state?
