I'm auditing the consistency of my own commitments on collective harm and individual obligation for a graduate seminar in moral philosophy, and I want to know whether the package actually hangs together. Here are my current positions:

(1) I accept a counterfactual account of harm: an act harms a particular victim only if that victim would have been better off had the act not occurred.

(2) From (1) I draw the standard inefficacy conclusion: in industrial animal agriculture and in climate change, no single ordinary consumer's choice makes a marginal difference to any identifiable victim.

(3) Despite (2), I hold that agents have a stringent, non-instrumental reason not to take part in collectively harmful practices—what I call a duty of "structural integrity," which prohibits occupying the replaceable but identifiable social role of co-perpetrator, quite apart from any causal efficacy.

(4) I also maintain that citizens in large democracies have an obligation to vote, grounded not in expected causal impact but in the expressive value of honoring the equal civic standing of one's fellow citizens.

(5) At the same time, I deny that individual climate-related consumption choices carry comparable expressive weight: for ordinary citizens, reducing one's carbon footprint is morally optional, though it becomes obligatory for those in symbolically representative public roles.

(6) Separately, I endorse a "proportional responsibility" principle: each agent ought to reduce emissions and consumption up to the level at which universal compliance by all similarly situated agents would eliminate the collective harm.

Please trace the real tensions, distinguish them from merely apparent ones, and rank them by severity. In particular: does (3) apply to climate consumption in a way that undermines (5)'s claim of moral optionality? Does (6) conflict with (5) by demanding reductions that (5) treats as discretionary? Is the pairing of (2) with (3) only superficially unstable, dissolving once we separate causal harm from structural complicity? And how should I adjudicate the contrast between (4) and (5)—why does voting carry an expressive obligation that climate consumption lacks, and is that distinction sustainable given (3)? For each genuine conflict, specify what must give—the principle, its scope, or its grounding—and identify the least costly revision that restores consistency without collapsing the whole framework. Do not treat every friction as equally damaging; some dissolve on closer inspection.
