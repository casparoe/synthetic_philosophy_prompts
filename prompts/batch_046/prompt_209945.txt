I've recently been asked to advise a memorial committee weighing whether to retain Thomas Jefferson's name on a university hall, and a line of reasoning I encountered has left me unable to blame any historical figure without seeming to blame everyone now. (1) To blame an agent for act X requires that the agent had epistemic access to X's wrongness through moral resources actually available in their historical context—not merely that counter-arguments existed somewhere, but that the agent could have integrated them without abandoning the social ontology they inhabited. (2) Jefferson, embedded in an eighteenth-century slaveholding society, could not have adopted the abolitionist framework as a live option without dissolving the economic, legal, and interpersonal structures that constituted his practical identity; the framework was therefore not epistemically available to him in the relevant sense. (3) So Jefferson is not blameworthy for slaveholding—though he may be less praiseworthy than if he had transcended his horizon. (4) By parity, present-day citizens lack epistemic access to the moral frameworks future generations will take for granted—say, one treating industrial animal agriculture as structurally continuous with chattel slavery—because those frameworks depend on conceptual innovations not yet integrated into our common practical reasoning. (5) Therefore we are not blameworthy for actions that future generations will condemn. (6) Generalizing, no agent is ever blameworthy for actions consistent with their era's dominant moral ontology, since every agent is confined to the epistemic horizon of their time. (7) But this makes moral blame impossible in principle, which is absurd: we do blame people, and we sometimes blame historical figures precisely for failing to transcend their horizons. (8) So (1) must be false. Yet if I reject (1) and allow blame without full epistemic access, I seem committed to holding people responsible for failing to achieve moral insights they literally could not have reached. Where does the argument slip—between (2) and (3), between (4) and (5), or in the generalization to (6)? If I must preserve (3)—that Jefferson's blame is severely mitigated, not erased—while avoiding (6), which premise should I surrender, and what does each alternative cost?
