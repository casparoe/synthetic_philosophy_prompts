Two philosophers at a workshop argued about whether historical atrocities can be morally ranked. (1) Alex said ranking is obscene: quantifying suffering collapses incomparable qualitative differences into a false metric, and treating the Holocaust as comparable to other mass killings reduces a unique rupture in humanity to a large murder. (2) Blake replied that we constantly rank harms—triage, sentencing, resource allocation—so refusing to rank atrocities abandons moral reasoning; if one event killed ten million and another killed a hundred thousand, the scale difference genuinely matters. (3) Alex countered with a distinction between magnitude and moral phenomenology: some evils leave a "stain" that transforms the moral universe differently than mere body count, so genocide is not just a bigger murder but a different kind of wrong entirely. (4) Blake said this "stain" is mystical without intersubjective criteria, allowing anyone to declare their own trauma incomparable; refusing ordinal comparison becomes moral solipsism that protects one's community rather than reasoning about harm. (5) Alex clarified that comparison is not the problem—ranking is: we can discuss atrocities along multiple dimensions without constructing a single scale, just as we evaluate paintings without assigning "beauty points"; forcing a ranking imposes a false decision procedure where none belongs. Please adjudicate move by move: rate each of the five moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right and briefly justify each rating. Respond as exactly five numbered items, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion.
