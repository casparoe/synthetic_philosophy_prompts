As a novelist drafting a work that alternates between a contemporary clinical narrative and a pre-modern cosmology in which mental states are treated as publicly observable substances, I require a reusable decision procedure grounded in Davidson's radical interpretation. The aim is to determine, during revision, how to render a character's utterance across these frames—whether by literal translation, invented neologism, or metaphorical framing—without consulting outside sources. Structure the procedure as an ordered checklist that applies the principle of charity, the holism of belief and meaning, and interpretive triangulation between speaker, interpreter, and shared world. For each step, specify what counts as a sufficient answer, where the procedure mandates a rendering, and where it must halt and defer to authorial judgment because the tension is between rational coherence and dramatic irony rather than interpretation itself. Then apply it to three cases: first, the physician's claim that "melancholy is a black stone lodged in the heart"; second, the patient's statement that "my anxiety is a separate person living in my chest"; and third, a belief I intend to present as irrational superstition but which the procedure forces me to treat as rationally coherent within the character's web of commitments—state precisely what rendering this produces and why I find it undesirable. Conclude by specifying what the procedure cannot do: which translation errors it will fail to catch, and why Davidson's framework provides no criterion for preserving narrative voice when charity conflicts with aesthetic design.
