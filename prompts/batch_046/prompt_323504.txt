I have been trying to work through a puzzle on my own about how we see the world, though I have no formal training in philosophy. The problem is usually introduced as the argument from illusion: if I can hallucinate an apple that feels exactly like seeing a real one, then there must be some inner picture or mental content shared between the two cases, and from this people conclude that even genuine seeing is really just acquaintance with that inner content rather than with the apple itself. Recently I came across a response called disjunctivism, which denies there is any such shared element; on this view, veridical perception is a direct openness to the object, while hallucination is an entirely different kind of state that only mimics the real thing from the outside, with no hidden common factor behind both. I keep thinking about how two poems might share an identical rhyme scheme on the page yet have nothing whatsoever in common inside them, and I wonder whether the mistake in the argument is assuming that similarity on the surface proves identity in the core.

Could you write a sonnet about this idea, letting the form carry part of the philosophical weight? I would like the octave to build the intuitive picture of a hidden shared core present in both seeing and seeming-to-see, the volta at line nine to overturn that assumption by denying the common factor outright, and the closing couplet to say precisely what is lost when we insist on finding something identical inside the two experiences. It should be accurate enough that someone familiar with the debate recognizes the move rather than just seeing keywords, and it should avoid turning into a plain summary.
