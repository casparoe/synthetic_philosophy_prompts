I run an upper-level graduate seminar in moral philosophy with sixteen students, meeting for a single ninety-minute session; the group has completed introductory ethics but has not yet read anything systematically on global rescue obligations. I want a structured in-class simulation—not a lecture or Socratic discussion—that makes the students feel, through their own binding choices, the pressure of the argument that physical proximity and emotional salience do not diminish a duty to rescue. Rather than staging the standard pond case as a narrated thought experiment, I want you to build the tension into a custom mechanism: a “Rescue Ledger” board divided into three zones—Near (rich sensory detail: named individuals, photographs, audio descriptions), Middle (moderate descriptive information), and Far (bare statistical descriptions: mortality rates, cost-per-life figures)—with each student receiving a fixed budget of ten physical tokens representing available moral resources. In Round One, students allocate tokens freely across zones with no knowledge of conversion rates, working individually but with allocations revealed publicly; in Round Two, a hidden “efficiency table” is revealed showing that one token in the Near zone saves exactly one life, one token in the Middle zone saves four lives, and one token in the Far zone saves twenty lives. From that point forward, allocations are made in pairs under a ninety-second timer, are fully binding, and unspent tokens are converted into a real, small-dollar donation to an effective charity at session’s end, so that retaining resources carries a tangible opportunity cost. The rules should be arranged so that students discover for themselves—without the facilitator naming the philosophical thesis in advance—that their own prior commitments to save the vividly imaginable, nearby life commit them, once efficiency is revealed, to redirect resources away from that very life toward statistical lives they cannot visualize. Include explicit rules, materials needed, any assigned roles, the round-by-round timeline, the payoff structure, and precise instructions for how the facilitator introduces each round without lecturing.

After the simulation, provide a debrief that maps each round onto the corresponding premises of the rescue argument—the principle of equal moral consideration, the irrelevance of distance and emotional accessibility, and the problem of demandingness—and that requires students to articulate exactly where they resisted the logic and at what moral cost. Be explicit about what will predictably go wrong the first time this is run: for example, emotional attachment to the Near-zone cards that leads students to reject the efficiency table as morally illegitimate rather than merely descriptive, attempts to game the timer by refusing to commit, or collective refusal to treat the charity donation as a genuine stake. Offer concrete ways to handle each failure without breaking the frame of the activity. Finally, include an honest note about which feature of the actual moral obligation the simulation necessarily fails to reproduce—I take it to be the open-ended, unbudgeted, and recurring nature of real-world rescue demands, as opposed to a one-time allocation of a fixed token pool—and indicate precisely what the facilitator must supply verbally or structurally to compensate for that absence.
