Consider the variant of negative utilitarianism that assigns lexical priority to the reduction of suffering below a specified magnitude before any positive welfare gains become morally admissible—commonly called lexical-threshold negative utilitarianism. Now suppose we combine this framework with a strong incommensurability thesis regarding affective experience: physical pain, existential dread, and social degradation are not merely quantitatively distinct but belong to phenomenologically discrete registers that resist cardinal translation onto a unified scale. Explain the conceptual instability this introduces. In particular, does the lexical ordering apply globally—demanding that every harm-register fall below a single, cross-domain boundary—or does it apply locally within each register, allowing inter-register trade-offs so long as no individual domain exceeds its own calibrated ceiling? Furthermore, if the thresholds themselves must be fixed through intersubjective negotiation rather than introspective comparison, does this pluralistic restructuring preserve the determinacy that lexical negative utilitarianism promises as a decision procedure, or does it collapse the view into a family of context-sensitive, domain-specific moral heuristics? Develop this tension in detail, attending specifically to whether incommensurability forces a restructuring of the lexical framework rather than a mere refinement of its metrics.
