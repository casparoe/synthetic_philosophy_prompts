I'm a PhD student in philosophy of mind working on narrative theories of personal identity, and I have become stuck on a recurring pattern in how people talk about severe psychological discontinuities—traumatic brain injury, religious conversion, prolonged contemplative practice, or radical value shifts. Rather than claiming strict continuity or announcing themselves an entirely different person, they often preserve the present self as what Daniel Dennett describes as a center of narrative gravity, but they treat the pre-discontinuity self in a very specific way: not as a direct predecessor or ancestor-self, but as a kind of satellite narrative orbiting near the present center. Past events are still narrated in the first person—someone might say they wanted to be a musician—but the motivational center is described as elsewhere: that was what the old system wanted, or that framework was running through this body. Meanwhile, future-directed intentions are pulled tightly into the present center's orbit. So there is an asymmetry: the present self exerts strong gravitational pull backward onto past events, reclassifying them as belonging to a different orbital center, but the past exerts almost no counter-pull on the present.

I can illustrate with two cases. A patient with severe frontal-lobe damage describes her pre-injury career ambitions not as abandoned goals but as goals that body had programmed, using first-person possession but third-person agency. A long-term practitioner after an intensive retreat describes his pre-retreat ethical framework as the narrative that was running through this body, again retaining ownership of the biography while displacing the center of agency. In both cases, the speaker does not adopt a fully episodic or no-self stance of the sort Galen Strawson discusses, nor do they maintain standard narrative continuity of the kind Alasdair MacIntyre or Marya Schechtman defend. Instead, they perform what looks like a retroactive decentering: the present self remains the sole gravitational center, but past mental states are sorted into an outer orbit—still part of my story but not part of my center.

My question is whether this specific configuration—this asymmetric orbital restructuring in which the present narrative center subordinates past agency without severing biographical ownership—has an established name in the literature on narrative identity, clinical phenomenology, or in extensions of Dennett's own metaphor. Is it simply an informal application of the center-of-gravity idea, a variant of narrative self-constitution, or perhaps related to the narrative distance sometimes discussed in trauma studies? Or does it misapply existing categories? If it is genuinely unnameable, I want a term that captures both the retention of first-person biography and the displacement of past agency, together with clear boundary conditions: for instance, how it differs from ordinary autobiographical revision, from dissociative identity presentations, and from Strawsonian episodic discontinuity. I am not looking for a bibliography or reading list, but for the conceptual carving itself—what the phenomenon is, what it is not, and where its edges lie.

Please conclude with a line beginning "Verdict:" that states either the established term you think best fits (with a brief justification in the same line) or the coined term with its definition, followed by a one-sentence summary of whether this is an established concept, a misapplication, or a genuinely novel pattern.
