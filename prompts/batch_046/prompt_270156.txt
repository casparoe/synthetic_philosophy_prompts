Philosophers of personal identity and scholars of early Buddhism have been replaying the same moves for decades: does the anattā (no-self) doctrine align with analytic reductionism about persons, or does it dismantle the very framework that makes reductionism coherent? The familiar positions are well mapped—some read the Abhidharma traditions as anticipating a bundle theory via the five skandhas, others treat Buddhist continuity (santāna) not as a metaphysical relation substituting for the self but as a purely conventional device for tracking karma across lives, which makes comparison with Western personal-identity theory a category mistake. Yet neither side shifts the other, and the literature keeps returning to the chariot analogy, the distinction between conventional and ultimate truth, and whether narrative coherence can be rebuilt without a substrate. My working hypothesis is that the stall is not about whether a self exists, but about what job a theory of personal identity is meant to perform—whether it must ground moral accountability, preserve phenomenological unity, or merely describe conventional designation—and that the two sides are therefore answering different questions (ontological reduction versus soteriological continuity) under a single heading. I want a diagnosis rather than a survey or adjudication. Is this disagreement substantive, verbal (centered on what counts as a person), or driven by hidden disagreements about which phenomena—karmic retribution, first-person experience, or conventional coherence—are non-negotiable fixed points? Are the sides using incompatible criteria for theoretical success, or is there an empirical or formal question masked beneath the conceptual exchange? And given your diagnosis, what would actually count as progress: a distinction that cleanly separates ontological from normative continuity, a formal model of conventional designation that avoids smuggling in a substrate, or an argument that the question is ill-posed because Buddhist and analytic frameworks have incommensurable aims? Please engage the positions concretely and say honestly whether anything—conceptual, formal, or experimental—should be expected to resolve it.
