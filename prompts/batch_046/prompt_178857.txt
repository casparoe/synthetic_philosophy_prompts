I have been thinking through, without any formal training, a pair of decision puzzles that sit at the edge of what people call rational choice. The first involves twenty strangers who must each, in complete isolation and without communicating, decide whether to send a telegram; if exactly one sends it by noon the next day, that person receives an enormous sum, but if the number is anything else—zero, two, or more—no one receives anything and cooperation is forbidden. The second is a related experimental setup sometimes called the luring lottery, in which participants face repeated rounds where symmetry-breaking seems advantageous in hidden subsets of cases, testing whether they can maintain aligned reasoning even when a direct reward for defection is present.

From my own reflections, I think the standard treatments miss an asymmetry that matters in practice: real groups never share identical reasoning capacities, and if each participant weights the others not equally but according to how likely they are to overthink or second-guess, the neat symmetric probability collapses into a hierarchical cascade. I am going to interview a guest—an unnamed researcher who defends the view that superrational coordination can survive this kind of asymmetry, provided participants adopt a specific stability threshold rather than uniform randomization—on my small podcast about practical reasoning. He tends to reply that asymmetry can be internalized into a weighting function, that the lure in the lottery is merely a noisy perturbation correctable by the same threshold, and that in real-world analogues—such as distributed climate pledges or vaccination commitments—the common knowledge of symmetry can be replaced by credible institutional signaling.

I need eight to ten interview questions that go well beyond asking him to restate his view. Specifically, I want at least one question each on: how his stability threshold handles the case where one participant knows she is the least likely to be predicted correctly by the others; whether the luring lottery's explicit reward for defection in hidden rounds undermines the threshold or simply shifts its parameters; and what he would say to someone who argues that real-world collective-action analogues fail precisely because people lack the common knowledge of symmetry that these puzzles assume. For each of these three, include a follow-up keyed to the answer he is most likely to give—namely, that the weighting function absorbs the disparity, that the perturbation is bounded, and that institutional signaling can substitute for common knowledge. The remaining questions should press on the mechanics of his position: for instance, whether the threshold requires participants to know the distribution of reasoning capacities in advance, what happens when two participants apply different weighting schemes, and whether there is any empirical result from actual human experiments with these puzzles that he thinks counts against his view rather than just showing human irrationality. Please avoid generic biographical questions and keep the focus entirely on the content of his defense.
