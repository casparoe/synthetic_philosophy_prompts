I have been thinking through the statue-and-clay problem on my own, without formal philosophical training, and I want to present my view next Thursday to a small public philosophy discussion group that meets in a library community room. My thesis is what I call an interaction-lock model: the statue is neither strictly identical to the clay nor a separate object that displaces it; rather, it is a temporary functional stabilization of the clay that exists only while a specific physical and social context is actively maintained—display conditions, protective handling, naming practices, and an institutional framework that treats reshaping as damage rather than change. If that context is broken—say, the clay is melted and remolded into a bowl—the statue does not persist in hidden form or survive as a scattered temporal part; it unlocks, and only the clay remains. This differs from standard constitution views because I deny that the statue carries intrinsic persistence conditions independent of that interaction environment, and it differs from four-dimensionalism because I do not treat it as a spatiotemporal part with its own boundaries; the boundary is relational, not intrinsic.

The audience will be about ten people with very different commitments. There is a ceramicist who thinks constantly about material transformation, vitrification, and whether a fired vessel is still the same mud; a retired mechanical engineer who is a strict physicalist and holds that if two entities share exactly the same atoms at exactly the same place and time, they are one entity, full stop; a graduate student who recently read the classic puzzle in an introductory philosophy text and remembers the standard answers involving coincident objects and temporal parts; and a museum volunteer who cares about conservation ethics and will hear my view as licensing destructive restoration.
