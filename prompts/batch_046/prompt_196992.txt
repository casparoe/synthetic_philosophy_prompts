I'm taking a graduate seminar on the ethics of war and conscientious refusal, and my classmates keep circling a puzzle about how modern conscription systems classify objectors. In most jurisdictions that still recognize conscientious objection, the exemption applies only to those who oppose all wars on moral or religious grounds—the absolute pacifists. A draftee who objects solely to the present conflict, or to a specific type of warfare, is generally denied exemption and must either serve or accept criminal punishment, even though the same legal systems routinely permit selective refusal in other domains: a physician may decline to perform abortions without being expelled from medicine entirely, and a pharmacist may refuse to stock a particular drug without losing professional standing altogether. I want to understand what philosophical commitments this institutional asymmetry quietly embeds. Does the practice presuppose that conscience is only authentic when universalized, so that selective refusal is not genuinely conscientious but a disguised political judgment? Or does it rest on a particular theory of democratic obligation—namely, that citizens may not unilaterally judge the justice of a particular war without undermining the collective decision procedure?

Reconstruct the implicit theory of moral integrity and political authority that makes the distinction coherent, distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what it merely accommodates, and say whether it can be defended on grounds other than universal pacifism. Then explain what would have to change in institutional design if we treated selective war-refusal symmetrically with selective medical refusal—would we need to abandon the category of conscientious objection altogether, or could we preserve it on revised grounds? In particular, is there a consistent view that licenses the physician's selective refusal but denies the soldier's, or does the current arrangement turn out to be philosophically unstable once we press on the comparison?
