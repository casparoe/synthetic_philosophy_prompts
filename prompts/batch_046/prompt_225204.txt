I hold the view that the legal category of genocide, as originally formulated by Raphael Lemkin in his 1944 study and subsequently codified in the 1948 Convention, has undergone a structural contraction that is not merely diplomatic but conceptually self-defeating. Specifically, the exclusion of political groups and the near-elimination of cultural destruction from the Convention's definition—driven in part by state interests in preserving sovereignty—transformed genocide from a broad diagnostic of group destruction into an ultra-narrow forensic standard. The result is what I would call a moral black hole effect: because the term retains overwhelming rhetorical gravity, its legal unavailability for intermediate or structural forms of group violence—forced assimilation, systematic deprivation of livelihood, targeted demographic restructuring—does not merely leave those acts unnamed; it implicitly downgrades their moral status by comparison, since the law treats them as something less than genocide. I want to know what would genuinely count against this view. Which empirical cases or legal developments show that the narrowed definition has been effective precisely because of its narrowness—securing convictions, triggering intervention obligations, or preventing escalation? Which conceptual arguments show that broadening the category would collapse it into uselessness or violate nullum crimen sine lege? Is there a risk that my position has been insulated by qualification—e.g., that any failure of the Convention to apply is taken as proof of the narrowing problem—such that it is unfalsifiable? If so, is that insulation fatal for a claim that is meant to be both descriptive and normative? Sort the defeaters by how decisive they would be, distinguish evidence that is merely awkward from evidence that undermines the core mechanism, and include what defenders of the current legal framework would themselves concede as damaging rather than only what critics say.
