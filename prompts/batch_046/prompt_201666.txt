Design a decision scenario built to the following specifications. The setting must be realistic—such as a hospital triage board or a municipal resource-allocation office—confined to a single paragraph describing the situation, and must involve no demons, omniscient predictors, exotic physics, or technologies unavailable to present-day institutions.

The case should concern an agent distributing a scarce but divisible resource (for example, doses of an antiviral or bandwidth for emergency response) among a fixed population. The welfare or survival outcome of any allocation depends on pairwise interactions among recipients: assigning resources to individual A improves outcomes for B and C but degrades them for D and E in a globally coupled pattern that is not decomposable into independent individual effects. Crucially, stipulate that evaluating the total welfare of any proposed allocation requires solving an optimization problem for which no polynomial-time algorithm can approximate the global optimum within any constant factor—so that computing even a near-optimal policy scales exponentially with the size of the population, and practical computation is therefore restricted to heuristic or rule-based methods.

Engineer the case so that three normative positions deliver clearly distinguishable verdicts:
1. An unbounded rationalist (or act-utilitarian) position, which demands the globally optimal allocation regardless of the computational cost required to identify it.
2. A bounded-obligation or feasible-utilitarian position, which holds that moral requirements are limited to policies computable with available resources; consequently, this view permits adopting a polynomial-time heuristic even when it is known to achieve welfare arbitrarily far below the global optimum.
3. A procedural-deontological position, which rejects welfare optimization altogether in favor of a fair lottery or first-come-first-served rule, on the grounds that the intractability reveals a category mistake in treating justice as an optimization problem.

After presenting the scenario, walk through the reasoning each position gives, explicitly identifying the structural feature—the combination of globally coupled welfare effects with a constant-factor inapproximability barrier—that causes the bounded-obligation view to peel away from both the unbounded rationalist and the proceduralist. Then explain what happens if that feature is removed: if welfare becomes linearly separable so that the optimal allocation is computable in polynomial time, does the disagreement collapse back into the standard consequentialist-versus-deontological conflict, or does a distinct disagreement survive? Finally, include an honest assessment of how a defender of the unbounded rationalist position would attack the bounded-obligation view—whether by denying that computation costs constrain moral obligation, or by arguing that the intractability is irrelevant at realistic population sizes.

End with a one-line summary beginning "Core feature:" that identifies the single element of the case doing the philosophical work.
