In my graduate seminar on moral obligation, the debate over supererogation has stalled in a way that suggests a hidden structural disagreement rather than a dispute about cases. One camp maintains that genuinely supererogatory acts require a bipartite normative architecture—strict obligations versus non-obligatory ideals—while the other treats all moral reasons as scalar, holding that "beyond the call of duty" is merely a pragmatic label for actions whose costs exceed what morality can legitimately demand of an agent. They converge on every canonical example—heroic rescue, anonymous organ donation, excessive charitable giving—yet neither side has modified its position in years. My working hypothesis is that the stall is not about moral ontology but about formal framework: one side implicitly models "requirement" on deontic logic, the other on a decision-theoretic calculus of agent-costs, so the examples function as Rorschach tests rather than shared data. But I also wonder whether both sides are circling an unmade distinction between directed reasons—reasons that apply specifically to an agent in virtue of role or relationship—and undirected general reasons, with supererogation arising precisely when a directed reason lacks the corresponding enforcement entitlement that generates obligation. Diagnose the disagreement: is it verbal, about the term "obligation"; substantive but buried in competing formal representations; or a clash of bedrock intuitions about the limits of moral demand? And given your diagnosis, is there a conceptual distinction—perhaps involving the structure of directed reasons or the separation of reasons-for-action from reasons-for-enforcement—that could resolve it, or should we treat the question as ill-posed?
