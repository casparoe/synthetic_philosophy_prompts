I was arguing with my roommate last night about vagueness and I think I've convinced myself of this weird hybrid view: the sorites paradox isn't really about missing boundaries or hidden knowledge. Instead, predicates like "bald" or "tall" are more like measuring instruments that come with an implicit resolution dial — what I'm calling a granularity parameter. In casual chat the dial is loose, but in a courtroom deciding disability benefits it gets cranked tight, and there's always a fact of the matter at that setting. The paradox only happens because philosophers illegitimately keep sliding the input (one hair at a time) while pretending the dial hasn't moved, untill they reach absurdity. So vagueness isn't in the world or in language; it's in the mismatch between a fixed evaluation and a shifting measurement context.

But I need to know what would actually kill this, not just annoy it. What specific linguistic intuitions, formal results about supervaluationism or three-valued logic, or practical cases — say, legal age cutoffs or medical diagnosis thresholds — count as real defeaters? Would a committed defender concede that if we can always post-hoc adjust the granularity parameter to save the view, then it's unfalsifiable in a bad way? And are there predictions this makes that rival theories don't — like that high-stakes contexts should always be able to eliminate vagueness by fixing the dial — that turn out false? Rank them from fatal to merely awkward, and be honest about whether the view has already been insulated into uselessness. I want to know if I'm defending something substantive or just redescribing contextualism with extra gears.
