I'm preparing a feature on how modern states calibrate the visibility of their citizens, and I keep returning to a comparative puzzle. Germany's constitutional right to informational self-determination treats personal data as an extension of human dignity; the American system of mandatory campaign-finance and property disclosure treats opacity as a potential corruption of civic trust; and China's social-credit architecture treats dispersed administrative infractions as nodes in a unified reputational graph. Together they seem to assign citizens different "opacity budgets"—the quantum of information one may withhold from the state before civic standing is affected. What philosophical commitments about personhood, civic virtue, and the epistemic authority of the state does this triad presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit normative framework, distinguish what it strictly requires from what it merely accommodates, and say whether the three regimes share a common root or rest on mutually exclusive conceptions of the boundary between the private and the political. Finally, indicate what each jurisdiction would have to alter if it adopted a consistently applied single theory of informational sovereignty.
