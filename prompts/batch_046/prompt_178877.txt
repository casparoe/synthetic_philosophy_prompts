Having spent my career in experimental physics before turning to philosophy, I find myself puzzled by the conceptual architecture underlying Bernard Williams's and Kwame Anthony Appiah's discussions of moral revolutions—particularly the disappearance of dueling and chattel slavery. The standard framing suggests these norms collapsed either because material interests shifted or because moral arguments finally prevailed. Yet there seems to be a more subtle conceptual possibility: that honor in these contexts functioned not merely as a coordination device among interests, but as a kind of epistemic commitment to maintaining visible, intersubjectively verifiable status. If that is right, then the abandonment of dueling might involve not just a cost-benefit recalibration but a conceptual rupture in what counts as credible evidence of personal worth. Could you explain this conceptual distinction—between interest-driven dissolution and epistemic-status rupture—in the context of how norms like dueling or slavery actually end? Please separate what is settled in the philosophical literature from what is genuinely contested, and avoid presenting any one interpretation as the unanimous view of scholars who work on this.
