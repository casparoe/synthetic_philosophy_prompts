In the course of drafting a novel set in the long aftermath of human extinction, I have encountered a conceptual difficulty that I hope you can help me resolve. The narrative requires that certain actions—say, the unnecessary destruction of a self-sustaining ecosystem or the corruption of an archived mathematical proof—remain somehow wrong or irrational even after every possible agent, observer, and beneficiary has vanished. This raises the problem of free-floating rationales: reasons that appear to exert normative force without being anchored in any reasoner. I am not asking for a survey of existing literature, but for a rigorous conceptual explanation. Please clarify the distinction between reasons that are essentially relational—existing only as connections between an agent and a consideration—and those that might be genuinely object-dependent, inhering in states of affairs themselves. In particular, examine whether the notion of an orphaned rationale—a reason that survives the extinction of all agents capable of being guided by it—is philosophically coherent, or whether it collapses into a confusion between normative language and descriptive language. How should one think about the boundary between legitimate free-floating normativity and the mere anthropomorphic projection of agent-centered categories onto an empty world? I want an analysis that is precise about where the conceptual fault lines lie, particularly regarding whether rationality itself can be attributed to non-agential structures, or whether the absence of reasoners necessarily dissolves the very possibility of error.
