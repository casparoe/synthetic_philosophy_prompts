I'm drafting an essay for my applied ethics seminar on whether Garrett Hardin's lifeboat metaphor remains coherent if we reject its implicit assumption of a single captain with unilateral authority over admission. I argue that Hardin treats carrying capacity as simultaneously physical and political: the captain decides who boards, guided by long-term preservation rather than immediate compassion. I then introduce a "distributed lifeboat" model—several small vessels linked only by radio, with no central command, varying resource levels, and different local norms about rescue. I ask whether this structure dissolves Hardin's argument (there is no unified "we" to preserve) or merely multiplies it (each vessel faces the same calculus independently). I also consider the objection that real-world migration crises already resemble this distributed model more than a single ship, which might make Hardin's framework descriptively obsolete even if normatively interesting. I am not asking for a bibliography, but I would appreciate comments on whether the distributed model is philosophically coherent, whether I fairly represent Hardin's implicit authority structure, and whether the descriptive-obsolescence objection actually undermines the normative claim. Suggestions on sharpening the distinction between physical carrying capacity and political boundary-setting would also be welcome.
