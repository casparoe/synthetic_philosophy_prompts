I've been reflecting on my tendency to abandon long-term plans for immediate comfort, and I recently came across hyperbolic discounting—the pattern where people don't just devalue future rewards smoothly but drop them sharply as the reward draws near, which makes preferences flip over time. That sounds a lot like what philosophers call akrasia, or weakness of will, but I'm not sure whether the economic model actually explains the philosophical problem or simply replaces it with a curve.

Specifically, I'm wondering about the conceptual tension between treating these time-inconsistent preferences as a kind of rational bargaining among different temporal selves, and treating akrasia as a failure within one continuous agent who makes an all-things-considered judgment yet fails to execute it. If hyperbolic discounting gives each momentary self its own legitimate utility function, is there still a coherent sense in which my later self ought to obey my earlier resolution? Or does that framing secretly rely on a normative idea of diachronic authority that the discounting model itself cannot justify? I would like an explanation of how these frameworks interact—particularly whether hyperbolic discounting eliminates any need for a concept of willpower, or whether it can be integrated with an Aristotelian view that practical reasoning can break down independently of how strongly one prefers the immediate option.
