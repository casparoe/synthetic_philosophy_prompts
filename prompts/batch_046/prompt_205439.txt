As a journalist covering philosophy, science, and the architecture of ideas, I am preparing a feature on how moral-philosophy examinations are being redesigned to test structural reasoning rather than canonical recall. For this piece, I need a polished draft of a final-exam essay question — complete with a model answer and a marking scheme — intended for an upper-level undergraduate seminar in normative ethics. The assessment parameters are strict: candidates have ninety minutes, the total value is twenty-five marks, and the answer should be impossible to produce by merely summarizing well-known objections.

The domain is demandingness objections to consequentialism, but I want the prompt injected with a non-canonical framing so that memorization of familiar debates is useless. Specifically, present students with a scenario of intergenerational moral bookkeeping: an agent faces a choice between a high-cost rescue that depletes the very resources she had reserved to discharge future welfare obligations toward her descendants, and a lower-cost partial rescue that preserves those reserves. Ask candidates to evaluate whether distributing the moral cost across a temporal chain of obligations strengthens or weakens the standard demandingness objection, and require them to identify the implicit principle that distinguishes permissible self-sacrifice from impermissible over-demand when the bearer of cost and the beneficiary of rescue are not the same moral subject at the same time.

The model answer must isolate the structural crux: conventional demandingness objections assume a synchronic, agent-centered budget of costs, and applying them diachronically either collapses into unmodified aggregation — thereby erasing the objection's force — or covertly imports a non-consequentialist threshold without independent justification. The marking scheme should be organized around five must-hit analytical moves, each assigned a specific point value (summing to twenty-five), together with a one-line note on the characteristic mistake that should cost marks for that move — for example, conflating temporal distribution with probabilistic risk dilution, or invoking a proportionality condition without addressing how the moral subject shifts across generations.

Please do not include requests for bibliographies, reading lists, or citations, and avoid relying on hyper-obscure literature that a well-prepared student without web access could not be expected to have internalized. Write in a formal, precise register suitable for publication.
