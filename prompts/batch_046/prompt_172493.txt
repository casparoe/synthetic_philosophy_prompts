I am defending a modified version of Dennett's multiple drafts model—specifically a "no-final-editor" account—at an interdisciplinary philosophy-of-mind seminar. My thesis is that conscious experience is not produced by a Cartesian theater in which representations are presented to a central observer, but rather by distributed, parallel narrative drafting across cortical regions: competing temporal slices of perceptual information are continuously revised and retroactively edited, with no single unified "stream" of consciousness and no convergence zone that binds them into one canonical display. Earlier drafts can be overwritten without leaving traces accessible to introspection, and apparent perceptual continuity—especially across saccades—is not the result of the brain "filling in" missing information but of post-hoc confabulation among incompatible draft representations of pre- and post-saccade scenes.

The audience will be: ten analytic philosophers of mind (with commitments spread across global workspace theory, higher-order theories, and a minority sympathetic to direct realism or naive realism), two cognitive neuroscientists who work on predictive processing and are skeptical of eliminativist rhetoric but open to distributed architectures, and one senior philosopher of mind who defends a "Cartesian materialist" view—that there must be some neural substrate functioning as a literal presentation space for unified conscious content. The department values conceptual precision, empirical responsibility, and resistance to what they regard as mysterian or anti-scientific moves; they will not be impressed by armchair speculation that ignores known psychophysics.
