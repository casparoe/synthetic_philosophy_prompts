My metaphysics professor has offered verdicts on the modal and epistemic status of a range of statements. So far she holds that "water is H2O" is necessary a posteriori; that "the actual gravitational constant in our world has the value G ≈ 6.67430 × 10⁻¹¹ N·m²/kg²" is necessary a posteriori; that "Sherlock Holmes lives at 221B Baker Street" is contingent and fiction-dependent; that "Mount Everest is the tallest mountain on Earth" is contingent a posteriori; that "the solar system contains eight planets" is contingent a posteriori; that "Neptune is the planet responsible for the perturbations in Uranus's orbit" is contingent a posteriori; and that "the actual cause of the Permian-Triassic extinction was large-scale volcanism in the Siberian Traps" is necessary a posteriori.

Next week she will evaluate the statement "Thomas Edison is the actual inventor of the phonograph." Predict her verdict on whether this identity is necessary or contingent, and whether its modal status is knowable a priori or only a posteriori. Then identify the implicit theory of singular reference that best explains the pattern above—specifically, whether she treats all successful singular terms as rigid designators, or whether she restricts rigidity to natural-kind terms and descriptions prefixed by "actual," leaving ordinary proper names descriptively anchored. Explain which of her earlier verdicts most strongly supports your attribution and which ones remain neutral between the two interpretations. Give a best-guess prediction for the Edison case with your reasoning, and then propose the single further statement that would most sharply distinguish the two theories—for example, an identity claim linking two ordinary proper names with no "actual" modifier. State the competing predictions for that further case and say exactly what result would settle the dispute.
