I consult on governance design for small-scale intentional communities—typically fifty to two hundred members—who want decision rules that respect both collective welfare and individual sovereignty without collapsing into pure majoritarianism or pure atomism. This came up after a long discussion with a friend who runs a fifty-person co-op in Vermont and is trying to draft a charter; several clients who read widely in social-choice theory have adopted the framing of Sen's liberal paradox: there exists a minimal sphere of personal choice—say, over one's diet, intimate associations, or sensory environment—that should be immune even to unanimous collective preference, yet the collective also wants to honor something like the Pareto principle, so that if every member prefers state A to state B on a communal resource, A should prevail. The theoretical tension is well-known, but my clients need it operationalized: they need an actual procedure a mediator can run in real time when a proposal comes in that might straddle both domains.

Please construct a reusable decision procedure—structured as an ordered sequence of diagnostic questions with operational criteria for each answer, explicit stopping points, and clearly marked boundaries where judgment must substitute for derivation—that a non-philosopher community mediator could execute on a whiteboard in under thirty minutes. The procedure should determine, for a given proposal, whether it activates a protected personal sphere, whether it generates a Pareto-relevant collective preference, and what to do when both conditions apply but point in opposite directions. Include: the exact questions in sequence; what counts as a sufficient answer to move to the next step; where the procedure terminates with a clear "no intervention" or "collective choice"; and the precise point at which the mediator must hand the decision to a normative judgment because the conflict is irreducible rather than factual. Be explicit about what the procedure cannot do—for instance, whether the minimal sphere is defined by subjective claim or by an ex ante community charter—and do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; the mediator will not have library access.

Then apply the procedure to two concrete proposals. First, a unanimous vote to mandate noise-canceling headphones in all shared workspaces to reduce sensory overload, where one member asserts that auditory processing differences make compliance a medical burden rather than a preference, and the others treat this as a simple collective-efficiency measure. Second, a proposal—supported by every other member—that all romantic partnerships be disclosed to the collective budget committee for "resource-transparency auditing," which one member refuses on privacy grounds, arguing that intimacy falls within the protected sphere regardless of budgetary relevance. In at least one case, the procedure should yield a recommendation that the collective will find unacceptable, and you should flag exactly where the procedure fails and a values-based decision must take over. The answer should be in American English.
