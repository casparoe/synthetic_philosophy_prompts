I have no formal academic training, but I have been working through Al-Ghazālī's occasionalism on my own and trying to apply it consistently. From my reading, he seems to rule that God is the direct cause of every effect, while the things we call causes are only present occasions. The cases I take him to treat this way include: fire placed near cotton producing burning; a sharp blade cutting fabric and producing separation; a person drinking water and then thirst being quenched; the sun rising and daylight spreading; and a person deciding internally to move and then the limb moving—except that here he adds something called "acquisition" (kasb), so the act is genuinely mine even though God creates both the decision and the motion.

I see two patterns that could organize these rulings. Pattern One: God is the sole direct cause in every single case, and "acquisition" is only a special moral label reserved for human voluntary acts, leaving fire, water, and blades all on the same footing. Pattern Two: God acts through stable "customary" sequences (ʿādāt), and human voluntary acts form one specially protected custom in which the mental-to-physical link is never broken; other sequences are also customs, but they are not protected and can be interrupted, as in a miracle.

Now I want to test these patterns with a case I invented. Imagine bacteria engineered with a synthetic gene circuit and placed in a human gut. The circuit detects an artificial chemical—one that never occurs in nature—and in response the bacteria manufacture a drug that cures a specific disease. The chemical is introduced, the bacteria respond, and the patient recovers. No conscious agent is involved at the moment of cure, but the entire chain was designed by scientists and depends on a non-natural trigger.

If Al-Ghazālī treats this exactly like fire and cotton—so the chemical is merely the occasion and God creates the cure directly—then Pattern One looks right, because the artificial origin makes no difference to direct divine causation. But if he treats it differently because the sequence was invented rather than observed as a long-standing natural regularity—perhaps saying there is no established custom here, or that artifacts never had inherent powers for occasionalism to deny—then Pattern Two looks better, since the status of the sequence depends on whether it belongs to a stable, ordained pattern rather than to a designed mechanism.

Please predict Al-Ghazālī's verdict on whether the bacteria or the chemical genuinely cause the cure.
