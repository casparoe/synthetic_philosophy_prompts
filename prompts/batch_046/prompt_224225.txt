I'm giving a seminar talk next tueday in the comparative history dept — mostly people who do microhistory and bottom-up social history, plus a couple of big-name state-formation scholars. My thesis is that microhistory doesn't actually escape elite archives; it just hides the dependency behind a single protagonist. I call it archival ventriloquism — the historian lets the peasant speak, but only through documents generated by the state court or the church. So I'm arguing we should stop pretending microhistory is methodologically distinct from macro-political history and instead admit that all bottom-up work is still structurally tethered to top-down record-keeping.

The audience cares deeply about agency, resistance, and not reducing people to statistics. They also know the literature — Ginzburg, Levi, maybe some James Scott — but they're not philosophers of history.

Prepare me for the Q&A. Order the objections by how much damage they do. Phrase them like a defensive microhistorian would phrase them, then like the state-formation people. Tell me what to concede upfront, what reply has to land, and where my own argument is weakest. And include the objection I'm hoping nobody asks — maybe about whether I'm just smuggling in a totalizing narrative through the back door, or if I'm saying we should just go back to writing about kings.
