In debates about whether a social rule can be justified to every person it touches, I keep running into a specific argumentative pattern that feels different from the familiar worry about summing small harms. I will describe two concrete cases where it shows up.

First, imagine a public-health measure that saves thousands of lives by imposing on each citizen an almost imperceptible risk—say, a water treatment that eliminates a deadly pathogen but carries a one-in-a-million chance of a serious reaction for each of the million people served. Critics often object that anyone who might suffer that reaction has legitimate grounds to reject the rule: being forced to bear even a minuscule risk for the collective good is something one could reasonably refuse. Defenders reply not by weighing the total lives saved against the total risk, but by insisting that because each person’s share is so tiny, there is simply no valid complaint to be made. They treat the objection as having disappeared entirely, not as being present but outweighed.

Second, consider a fiscal scheme that takes an imperceptible amount—rounding down every bank account by a fraction of a penny—from each of ten million people, then uses the accumulated sum to fund an elite luxury project that benefits only a few. Here too, defenders might argue that since no individual suffers a meaningful loss, no one can reject the rule. Yet the total extraction is substantial and the purpose seems illegitimate. The odd feature is that the moral assessment seems to hinge on whether the burden is concentrated enough to register in a single life.

What distinguishes this from ordinary aggregation is that the defenders are not comparing aggregates or trading off totals; they are claiming that below a certain per-capita threshold, the standing to object dissolves altogether. Is there an established name for this particular move—the disappearance of individual objection when harm or extraction is dispersed across a large group? If there is no precise term, can you coin one and define it sharply, distinguishing it both from standard aggregation problems and from simple utilitarian weighing, and indicate whether it applies symmetrically to distributed benefits and to distributed burdens? I am not looking for a bare label; I want to know exactly what the phenomenon is, what it is not, and where its edges lie, especially in cases where the dispersed effect is a risk rather than a definite loss.
