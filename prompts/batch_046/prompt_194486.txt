Student answer (approximately 450 words):

Descartes' cogito in Meditation II is the foundation of his epistemology. He writes that the proposition that he is and exists is necessarily true whenever he conceives it. This establishes existence, but philosophers disagree about what exists. Hintikka claims the cogito is performative: by doubting, one demonstrates existence in the very act, a point Augustine already made in De libero arbitrio with the claim that if one is deceived, one must exist, using exactly the same performative rather than inferential structure. Because of this performative nature, the I in I think is merely grammatical, like the it in it rains, so there is no substantial bearer of thought.

Still, Descartes does call himself a res cogitans. He reaches this after the wax argument, where he realises that he grasps the wax through pure intellect rather than sense. From this he infers that his essence is thought. But this inference depends on a hidden premise that every act requires a substantial bearer, a principle Descartes derives from his rejection of occasionalism in Meditation III, where he proves God exists and thereby rules out that thoughts could occur without a real substance causing them. He also imports scholastic hylomorphism into the wax argument to supply the metaphysical framework for substance.

Therefore, on the performative reading the cogito only proves that thinking occurs, not that I am a thinking substance. Descartes overcomes this only by combining the wax argument with the anti-occasionalist argument of Meditation III, together with hylomorphic metaphysics. So the move from I think to res cogitans is not licensed by the cogito alone.
